Death of cells, organs and even whole plants is a regular part of plant life histories: vessel elements die as they mature, and only then become water-conducting pipes; before autumn leaves die they transport more than half of the nitrogen and phosphorus in the leaf back to the stem; annual crops die as whole plants after setting seed, committing the material accumulated over a lifetime to the seeds. These deaths are not the passive result of wear and tear but developmental processes controlled by genes and carried out according to a program. This chapter discusses three closely related processes: senescence (Sections 24.1–24.3), programmed cell death (Section 24.4) and abscission of organs (Section 24.5), and finally their application to the postharvest preservation of agricultural products (Section 24.6).

## 24.1. Concept, Types and Significance of Senescence

### 24.1.1. Concept

**Definition 24.1.1 (Plant Senescence)** The genetically programmed process in which, late in development, the physiological functions of the cells, organs or whole plant of a plant gradually decline, cellular components are broken down and transported in an orderly manner, and death finally results, is called **plant senescence**.

"Senescence" here differs from two concepts in animal biology, and the distinctions must be made:

| Concept | Meaning | Relation to plant senescence |
|---|---|---|
| Organismal aging ([7.5.37](/7.5.37)) | With increasing age, damage accumulates, function declines and mortality rises | Senescence of plant organs is not the result of accumulated damage; leaves can be induced to senesce while "young"; the meristems of perennial plants may show almost no senescence |
| Cellular senescence ([7.3.25](/7.3.25)) | Animal cells permanently exit the cell cycle but remain alive | Senescing plant cells have already stopped dividing; plant senescence ends in death |
| **Necrosis** | Nonprogrammed death caused by extreme stimuli (physical injury, poisons, freezing), with destruction of the plasma membrane and leakage of contents | Senescence is an orderly, energy-consuming process, and cells remain intact until death so that materials can be recovered |
| **Programmed cell death** | Cell death controlled by a genetically encoded program | The last stage of leaf senescence is a form of programmed cell death; but for most of senescence the cells are alive, and early senescence is reversible |

Early senescence is reversible: when a yellowing tobacco leaf is topped, or treated with cytokinin, it can recover its green color (regreening). This shows that senescence is a regulated developmental program and not an irretrievable collapse.

### 24.1.2. Types

By the death of the plant and its organs, plant senescence can be divided into four types:

| Type | Characteristics | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Whole-plant senescence | The whole plant dies after flowering and seed set | Annuals and biennials; monocarpic perennials such as bamboo and agave |
| Shoot senescence | The aboveground part dies every year, and roots and underground stems live for many years | Perennial herbs (peony, Jerusalem artichoke, perennial grasses) |
| Synchronous leaf senescence | All leaves senesce and fall together every year, and stems and roots live for many years | Temperate deciduous trees ([7.12.23](/7.12.23#sec-23.7)) |
| Progressive senescence | Stems and roots live for many years, and leaves and reproductive organs senesce and fall in succession | Evergreens; annual crops during vegetative growth (lower leaves senesce in order) |

By the number of times a plant flowers and sets seed in its lifetime, plants are divided into monocarpic and polycarpic plants ([7.12.29](/7.12.29#sec-29.1)). Monocarpic plants senesce and die as whole plants after flowering and seed set; this whole-plant senescence is called monocarpic senescence and is discussed specifically in Section 24.3.

The lifespans of individual plants differ enormously: some desert annuals live only a few weeks from germination to death; individuals of bristlecone pine (*Pinus longaeva*) can live more than 4800 years; and the "individuals" of clonally propagating plants (such as quaking aspen clones in North America and Posidonia seagrass in the Mediterranean) can be even older. Trees generally do not show animal-style senescence: in many tree species the rate of carbon accumulation continues to increase as the tree grows larger (Stephenson et al., 2014), and their deaths are mostly caused by external factors (drought, pests and diseases, windthrow).

### 24.1.3. Biological Significance

Senescence is a means by which plants adapt to the environment and reproduce:

1. Recovery and redistribution of nutrients. Proteins, nucleic acids and chlorophyll–protein complexes in senescing organs are broken down, and nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, sulfur and some micronutrients are transported to young organs, seeds, tubers or storage tissues for reuse (Section 24.2.4). Senescence of petals shifts nutrients to the developing ovules; senescence of leaves supplies most of the nitrogen of the seeds.
2. Adaptation to unfavorable environments. Temperate deciduous trees shed their leaves before winter, greatly reducing transpiring surface and avoiding water loss when root uptake is difficult in winter; tropical deciduous trees shed leaves in the dry season by the same principle.
3. Elimination. Shaded lower leaves cannot cover their photosynthetic costs, and their senescence and abscission let the plant concentrate resources on well-lit leaves; senescence and abscission of organs infected by pathogens is also a defense.

## 24.2. Leaf Senescence

### 24.2.1. The Course of Senescence

Leaf senescence usually begins after the leaf is fully expanded, and its rate varies with the environment. Within the cell senescence proceeds in a certain order:

1. The first to change is the **chloroplast**: thylakoids swell and break down, and lipids accumulate in plastoglobuli (osmiophilic granules), which increase in number and size; the chloroplast changes into a *gerontoplast*. A gerontoplast resembles a chromoplast but still retains its envelope, and for most of senescence remains intact and capable of regreening.
2. Ribosomes in the cytoplasm decrease, and the endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi apparatus break down.
3. The nucleus and mitochondria change last and remain functional late in senescence, supplying energy and gene expression for degradation and transport.
4. Finally the tonoplast ruptures and the cell dies.

The color change of autumn leaves is the outward manifestation of this process: after chlorophyll degrades, the previously masked carotenoids show their yellow; some plants (maple, smoke tree, Chinese tallow) also synthesize anthocyanins de novo, turning the leaves red.

### 24.2.2. Physiological and Biochemical Changes during Senescence

| Change | Content |
|---|---|
| Proteins | Fall markedly: synthesis weakens and degradation accelerates. Rubisco is the most degraded protein: in leaves of C3 plants Rubisco makes up about half of the soluble protein and 20%–30% of leaf nitrogen, and its degradation is the main source of nitrogen remobilization |
| Nucleic acids | RNA content falls and DNA falls more slowly; expression of most genes declines, but expression of a group of senescence-associated genes rises (Section 24.2.5) |
| Photosynthesis | The photosynthetic rate begins to fall before chlorophyll clearly decreases: Rubisco decreases, electron transport and photophosphorylation are hindered, and then the chloroplast breaks down and chlorophyll degrades |
| Respiration | Mitochondria change less than chloroplasts; the respiration rate generally falls with senescence, but many leaves show a brief rise in respiration late in senescence, related to a peak of ethylene and similar to the **climacteric** of fruit; the respiratory substrate shifts from sugar to amino acids; oxidative phosphorylation is gradually uncoupled |
| Membranes | Degradation and peroxidation of membrane lipids, decreased membrane fluidity, increased permeability and ion leakage |
| **Reactive oxygen species** | Free radicals and reactive oxygen species (superoxide anion, hydrogen peroxide, hydroxyl radical, singlet oxygen) increase, while the capacity of scavenging systems such as superoxide dismutase, catalase, peroxidase, ascorbate and glutathione declines; lipoxygenase activity rises and catalyzes peroxidation of membrane lipids |
| Sugars | Soluble sugars in the leaf often increase (rather than decrease), and sugar accumulation itself can promote senescence (Section 24.3) |

The time course of leaf senescence can be described by several reversed logistic curves (Figure 1). Let an index (chlorophyll, protein, nitrogen, photosynthetic rate) have the value 1 in the mature leaf and finally fall to $y_\infty$:

$$
y(t) = y_\infty + \frac{1 - y_\infty}{1 + \mathrm{e}^{(t - t_{50})/s}},
\tag{24.1}
$$

$t_{50}$ is the time to fall to half (relative to the part that can fall) and $s$ determines the steepness of the decline. The $t_{50}$ of the indices come in this order: photosynthetic rate earliest, soluble protein and Rubisco next, chlorophyll later. When the leaf yellows (visible senescence), most of its photosynthetic capacity has already been lost and most of its nitrogen has already been exported.

### 24.2.3. Degradation of Chlorophyll

Chlorophyll must be broken down safely in senescence: free chlorophyll and its colored intermediates produce singlet oxygen on absorbing light and are strongly photosensitizing toxins, so the cell cannot let them accumulate. The degradation pathway of chlorophyll is called the PAO pathway (named for its key enzyme), and its products are collectively called phyllobilins (Figure 2):

1. Chlorophyll b is converted to chlorophyll a by chlorophyll b reductases (NYC1 and NOL) and 7-hydroxymethyl chlorophyll a reductase (HCAR). Chlorophyll b can therefore be degraded only after passing through chlorophyll a.
2. The magnesium ion is removed, producing pheophytin a. The catalyst of this step is the STAY-GREEN (SGR) protein, which is a "reverse reaction" enzyme of magnesium chelatase (Mg-dechelatase, Shimoda et al., 2016); SGR also dissociates chlorophyll–protein complexes (especially LHCII), releasing the apoproteins for degradation by proteases.
3. Pheophytinase (PPH) removes phytol, producing pheophorbide a. Older textbooks held that this step was done by chlorophyllase before removal of magnesium; it is now known that chlorophyllase is not required for chlorophyll degradation in senescence, and it is PPH that acts.
4. Pheophorbide a oxygenase (PAO) opens the porphyrin macrocycle, producing the red chlorophyll catabolite (RCC), which RCC reductase (RCCR) immediately reduces to the primary fluorescent chlorophyll catabolite (pFCC). The PAO step makes the degradation product lose its green color and phototoxicity and is the truly irreversible step of the pathway.
5. FCC is exported from the plastid, modified in the cytoplasm, imported into the vacuole by ABC transporters and converted nonenzymatically in the acidic vacuole into nonfluorescent catabolites (NCC), which are stored in the vacuole and not recycled. The breakdown of chlorophyll therefore does not recover its nitrogen (each chlorophyll molecule has only 4 nitrogen atoms, and chlorophyll nitrogen is only about 2% of leaf nitrogen); its significance lies in detoxification and in allowing the proteins that bind chlorophyll to be degraded.

Stay-green mutants stay green during senescence. Mendel's pea cotyledon color trait (yellow *I* dominant over green *i*) is the *SGR* gene: green-cotyledon pea cultivars carry a mutant allele of *SGR* (Armstead et al., 2007; Sato et al., 2007). Stay-green is of two kinds: those in which only chlorophyll degradation is blocked and photosynthetic function declines as usual are called "cosmetic" stay-green (such as the *sgr* and *nyc1* mutants); those in which the whole senescence program is delayed and photosynthesis continues longer are called "functional" stay-green. Some functional stay-green cultivars of sorghum and maize are drought tolerant and higher yielding late in grain filling, and are one of the goals of breeding.

### 24.2.4. Protein Degradation and Nutrient Remobilization

Chloroplast proteins (mainly Rubisco) are degraded by several routes:

- ATP-dependent proteases in the chloroplast (Clp, FtsH, Deg and others) degrade thylakoid and stromal proteins; this step begins inside the chloroplast.
- Rubisco-containing bodies (RCBs) bud from the chloroplast and are delivered by autophagy to the vacuole for degradation; under starvation or high-light damage whole chloroplasts can also be engulfed by the vacuole (chlorophagy). Leaves of autophagy-deficient *atg* mutants senesce prematurely and send less nitrogen to the seeds (Section 24.4.4).
- Senescence-associated vacuoles (SAVs) are small, acidic, cysteine-protease-rich vacuoles that appear in senescing leaves and degrade stromal proteins; vesicles induced by chloroplast vesiculation protein (CV) carry thylakoid and stromal proteins out of the chloroplast.

The amino acids produced by degradation (mainly as glutamine and asparagine), phosphate, potassium and part of the iron, zinc and sulfur are transported through the phloem to young leaves, fruits and seeds ([7.13.11](/7.13.11)). Elements that cannot be reused (calcium, boron and others, [7.13.4](/7.13.4)) remain in the senescing leaf. The extent of remobilization is measured by the *resorption efficiency*:

$$
\mathrm{RE} = \frac{N_{\mathrm{green}} - N_{\mathrm{senesced}}}{N_{\mathrm{green}}} \times 100\%,
\tag{24.2}
$$

where $N_{\mathrm{green}}$ and $N_{\mathrm{senesced}}$ are the nutrient contents of mature and fallen leaves (they should be expressed per unit leaf area to avoid error from the loss of dry weight during leaf senescence). In global compilations for woody and herbaceous plants, the resorption efficiency of nitrogen and phosphorus averages about 50% (Aerts, 1996); nitrogen resorption is slightly more efficient in deciduous than in evergreen plants, while phosphorus resorption shows no clear difference; in nutrient-poor habitats plants rely more on prolonging leaf lifespan than on raising resorption efficiency to conserve nutrients. In cereal crops 60%–90% of the nitrogen in the grain comes from remobilization of nitrogen accumulated in vegetative organs before flowering.

**Example 24.2.1** Mature leaves of a deciduous tree contain $2.2\ \mathrm{g\,N\,m^{-2}}$ and fallen leaves $0.95\ \mathrm{g\,N\,m^{-2}}$; the leaf area index is 5. Find the nitrogen resorption efficiency and the amount of nitrogen recovered per hectare per year. If the tree needs $11\ \mathrm{g\,N\,m^{-2}}$ (per ground area) for new leaves each year, what proportion of the need is met by resorption?

$\mathrm{RE} = (2.2 - 0.95)/2.2 = 57\%$. The leaf nitrogen per unit ground area is $2.2 \times 5 = 11\ \mathrm{g\,m^{-2}}$ and the amount recovered is $1.25 \times 5 = 6.25\ \mathrm{g\,m^{-2}}$, that is $62.5\ \mathrm{kg\,N\,ha^{-1}\,a^{-1}}$; this meets $6.25/11 = 57\%$ of the need for new leaves, and the remaining about $4.75\ \mathrm{g\,m^{-2}}$ must be taken up from the soil. Remobilization during senescence is an important part of the nitrogen economy of plants and also a link in the internal cycling of plants in the nutrient cycle of ecosystems (7.19).

:::

Studies in wheat illustrate the trade-off between senescence and grain quality: *NAM-B1* (also called *GPC-B1*) is a NAC transcription factor gene, and the functional allele of wild emmer wheat causes earlier leaf senescence and raises the protein, zinc and iron content of the grain; most cultivated wheats carry a nonfunctional allele. Lowering expression of *NAM* genes by RNA interference delays leaf senescence by several weeks and lowers protein, zinc and iron content of the grain by more than 30% (Uauy et al., 2006). Delaying senescence (stay-green) favors yield and earlier senescence favors nutrient remobilization and grain quality, and breeding has to choose between them.

### 24.2.5. Senescence-Associated Genes

Senescence is an active process with many changes in gene expression. In *Arabidopsis* leaf senescence thousands of genes change markedly in expression. Genes whose expression is up-regulated during senescence are called *senescence-associated genes* (SAGs); those down-regulated are called senescence-downregulated genes (SDGs), and there are also genes expressed only at a particular stage of senescence. The products of SAGs include proteases, nucleases, lipases, cell wall degrading enzymes, glutamine synthetase and asparagine synthetase (nitrogen remobilization), transporters, autophagy proteins, antioxidant enzymes and components of hormone synthesis and signaling. The cysteine protease gene *SAG12* is expressed only in developmental senescence and is often used as a senescence marker gene.

In regulation, the two most important classes of transcription factors are NAC and WRKY:

- The NAC family members ORE1 (ANAC092), NAP (ANAC029) and others promote senescence. Expression of *ORE1* increases with leaf age and is repressed by miR164, and the ethylene signaling component EIN2 represses miR164: EIN2 promotes *ORE1* both directly and indirectly by lowering miR164, forming a feed-forward loop that switches *ORE1* on only in sufficiently old leaves (Kim et al., 2009).
- WRKY53 is an early regulator of senescence, induced by hydrogen peroxide and linked to reactive oxygen signaling through MAP kinase pathways; it activates the transcription of many *SAG*s and also regulates itself by negative feedback.

Leaf age is thus encoded as the "capacity to senesce": young leaves do not senesce even when treated with ethylene, and only leaves that have reached a certain age respond to signals that induce senescence such as ethylene, abscisic acid and darkness.

### 24.2.6. Hormonal Regulation of Senescence

| Hormone | Effect | Evidence and mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| **Cytokinin** | Delays senescence | Kinetin markedly delays yellowing of detached leaves and causes nutrients to be transported to the treated region (Richmond and Lang, 1957); cytokinin content falls in senescing leaves; in tobacco with the cytokinin synthesis gene *IPT* driven by the *SAG12* promoter, cytokinin is synthesized as soon as senescence begins and senescence is delayed, forming a self-regulating feedback loop (Gan and Amasino, 1995) |
| **Auxin** | Delays senescence (weaker effect) | Represses expression of some *SAG*s; *Arabidopsis* overexpressing the auxin synthesis gene *YUCCA6* shows delayed senescence |
| **Gibberellin** | Delays leaf senescence in some plants | Dandelion, Alstroemeria and others; gibberellin synthesis inhibitors (growth retardants) sometimes also delay senescence, the effect varying among plants |
| **Ethylene** | Promotes senescence | Exogenous ethylene or ACC promotes senescence of leaves and flowers; ethylene-insensitive mutants (*etr1-1*, *ein2*, the latter being *ore3*) show delayed senescence; the ethylene synthesis inhibitor AVG, the ethylene action inhibitors silver thiosulfate (STS) and 1-MCP delay senescence of flowers |
| **Abscisic acid** | Promotes senescence | Abscisic acid increases in senescing leaves; it promotes degradation of macromolecules and remobilization of nutrients and increases ethylene production, indirectly promoting abscission; drought-induced senescence acts mainly through abscisic acid |
| **Jasmonic acid** | Promotes senescence | Exogenous methyl jasmonate induces premature senescence of leaves, with a more marked effect on old leaves; requires the receptor COI1 |
| **Salicylic acid** | Promotes senescence | Salicylic acid in senescing leaves increases rapidly when chlorophyll begins to fall; plants defective in salicylic acid synthesis or signaling cannot express some *SAG*s |
| **Strigolactone** | Promotes senescence | The *Arabidopsis* *ore9* mutant (delayed senescence) is a mutation of *MAX2*, the gene of the F-box protein of strigolactone signaling |

The leaf can be viewed as an integrating decision maker: cytokinin and "survival" signals such as sugar and light maintain leaf function, ethylene, abscisic acid, jasmonic acid, salicylic acid and "senescence" signals such as darkness, drought and pathogens push it into the senescence program, and leaf age determines its sensitivity to these signals.

### 24.2.7. Effects of the Environment on Senescence

- Light: light delays leaf senescence and darkness accelerates it (detached leaves yellow within a few days in the dark); red light delays the loss of chlorophyll and far-red light cancels the effect of red, showing that **phytochrome** is involved; light reaching lower leaves of a plant has a high proportion of far-red and accelerates their senescence; the transcription factors PIF4 and PIF5 accumulate in the dark and promote dark-induced senescence. Another effect of light is to maintain the supply of sugar and ATP through photosynthesis.
- Temperature: both low and high temperature accelerate senescence, possibly related to disturbed calcium transport, accelerated protein degradation and decline of chloroplast function.
- Water: drought promotes senescence, strengthens protein degradation, raises the respiration rate, damages the lamellar structure of chloroplasts and inhibits photophosphorylation.
- Nutrition: deficiency of nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium and others causes premature senescence of old leaves, which is why deficiency symptoms of mobile elements appear first in old leaves ([7.13.4](/7.13.4)); allocation of nutrients from old tissues to new organs also accelerates senescence of old leaves.
- Other: pathogen infection, pollutants such as ozone, shading and flooding can all induce senescence.

## 24.3. Whole-Plant Senescence and Theories of Senescence

### 24.3.1. Monocarpic Senescence

Monocarpic plants senesce and die as whole plants after flowering and seed set. One of their features is that removing the reproductive organs can delay senescence greatly. If the pods of soybean are removed continuously, the plant can remain in vegetative growth for several years under suitable conditions and grow into an almost woody form; removing flowers or young fruits of sunflower and pea can also delay senescence of the plant. In *Arabidopsis*, after flowering the shoot apical meristem stops producing new flowers ("global proliferative arrest"), and this arrest is controlled by the balance of FUL and AP2-type transcription factors and by signals from the developing seeds.

Why does fruit development cause death of the whole plant? Several explanations have been proposed:

1. The nutrient diversion theory: reproductive organs are very strong **sinks** that monopolize the allocation of materials in the plant, so that vegetative organs lack nutrients and senesce and die. This theory has two difficulties: supplying plenty of nutrients to plants in the reproductive stage does not prevent senescence; and in dioecious spinach male plants flower but do not set fruit and still senesce and die after flowering, and removing male flowers delays senescence as much as removing female flowers. Later measurements showed, however, that the nutritional demand of male flowers early in development actually exceeds that of female flowers, so this objection does not fully hold; and carbohydrates in senescing leaves often increase, so what is truly "taken away" is not carbohydrate but nitrogen and other nutrients. The loss of nitrogen and accumulation of sugar raise the C/N ratio of the leaf, and the latter is related to the start of senescence.
2. The hormonal regulation theory: during vegetative growth, cytokinin synthesized in the roots is transported to the leaves, promotes protein synthesis and delays senescence; after flowering and seed set, cytokinin synthesis in the roots decreases and flowers and fruits become centers of vigorous metabolism that intercept cytokinin; at the same time flowers and seeds produce abscisic acid, ethylene and other hormones that promote senescence and are transported to the vegetative organs. In *Arabidopsis* the transcription factor *AtMYB2*, expressed at the base of the stem, represses cytokinin synthesis and branching, and the *myb2* mutant has many shoots and delayed senescence.

The two are in fact two aspects of the same process: fruit development changes the source–sink relations of the whole plant and thereby the balance of nutrients and hormones.

### 24.3.2. Theories of Senescence

There are several more general theories of the cause of senescence:

| Theory | Content | Assessment |
|---|---|---|
| Free radical damage theory | Senescence is the result of accumulation of free radicals and reactive oxygen species, which damage membrane lipids, proteins and DNA; the capacity of scavenging systems declines and lipoxygenase activity rises during senescence | Reactive oxygen species do increase during senescence and are signals of it (inducing WRKY53 and others); but antioxidants can only partly delay senescence, and reactive oxygen species are more a component of the senescence program than its fundamental cause |
| DNA damage and error theory | Errors in gene expression and protein synthesis accumulate and beyond a threshold cause cellular malfunction (Orgel's "error catastrophe") | Lacks experimental support; senescing leaves can accurately synthesize large amounts of new proteins (products of SAGs) |
| Nutrient diversion theory | See Section 24.3.1 | Applies only to monocarpic senescence and is not the initial cause |
| Hormone balance theory | The balance of hormones that promote and delay senescence determines the course of senescence | Consistent with many experiments; a regulation and not a "cause" |
| Genetic program (spatiotemporal control) theory | Senescence is controlled by nuclear genes according to the temporal order and spatial position of leaf development (SAGs, SDGs) | The framework of modern molecular research; the processes described by the other theories are components or inputs of this program |

In sum, senescence of plant organs is a program in which developmental age sets the "capacity," hormone and environmental signals determine the "timing," a network of transcription factors executes it and the purpose is nutrient recovery; it differs in nature from animal aging driven by accumulation of damage ([7.5.37](/7.5.37)).

## 24.4. Programmed Cell Death and Autophagy in Plants

### 24.4.1. Characteristics of Plant PCD

Programmed cell death in plants and animals has features in common and also marked differences. Plant cells have cell walls, and dead cells cannot be engulfed and cleared; plants have no typical **caspases**, nor the Bcl-2 family or apoptotic bodies of animal apoptosis. The executioners of plant PCD are:

- Vacuolar processing enzymes (VPEs): cysteine proteases in the vacuole with caspase-1-like activity (cleaving the YVAD sequence), which carry out degradation around the time of tonoplast rupture;
- Metacaspases: cysteine proteases similar in structure to caspases but cleaving substrates after arginine or lysine, involved in vessel differentiation, death of suspensor cells and the hypersensitive response;
- The β1 subunit (PBA1) of the 26S **proteasome**, which shows caspase-3-like activity and takes part in fusion of the tonoplast with the plasma membrane;
- Papain-like cysteine proteases (XCP1/2, RD21 and others), the subtilisin-like "plant aspartate-specific proteases" (phytaspases) and nucleases (such as BFN1).

Plant PCD has two main morphological types (van Doorn et al., 2011):

| Type | Process | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Vacuolar cell death | The vacuole gradually enlarges and engulfs the cytoplasm; the tonoplast ruptures, hydrolases are released and the protoplast is digested (autolysis), leaving the cell wall | Differentiation of vessels and tracheids, aleurone layer, suspensor, senescence of petals |
| Necrotic-like cell death | Proceeds quickly, the protoplast shrinks, the plasma membrane ruptures early, tonoplast rupture is late or inconspicuous, and the cell collapses without autolysis | Hypersensitive response; death caused by some abiotic stresses |

Besides these two, **autophagy**, which occurs in large amounts in senescing cells, can maintain cell survival and can also take part in death (Section 24.4.4). The process of programmed cell death can be divided into three stages: initiation (generation and transmission of death signals such as reactive oxygen species, Ca²⁺ and hormones), execution (activation of proteases and changes in permeability of mitochondria and the tonoplast) and degradation and clearance (enzymatic breakdown of substrates, DNA fragmentation). Biochemical features of PCD include DNA cleavage into fragments of certain lengths, chromatin condensation and vacuolation.

### 24.4.2. Programmed Cell Death in Development

| Example | Process and regulation |
|---|---|
| **Vessel elements** and tracheids | In the in vitro differentiation system of *Zinnia* mesophyll cells, cells complete thickening of the secondary wall and autolysis in succession within about 3 d; the NAC transcription factors VND6 and VND7 are master switches of vessel differentiation and activate secondary wall synthesis and death-executing genes such as *XCP1* and *BFN1*; after tonoplast rupture the protoplast is rapidly degraded, and the cell wall remains as a water-conducting channel (**xylem**) |
| **Aleurone layer** | When cereal seeds mature all cells of the starchy endosperm die and only the aleurone layer survives; at germination the aleurone layer synthesizes and secretes hydrolases under the action of gibberellin, supplies nutrients to the embryo and then undergoes PCD (promoted by gibberellin, delayed by abscisic acid) ([7.13.14](/7.13.14)) |
| Megaspores | Of the four megaspores formed by meiosis of the megaspore mother cell, three degenerate and die and only one develops into the embryo sac |
| **Tapetum** | Degenerates at the proper time in microspore development to supply exine material and nutrients to the pollen; degeneration that is too early or too late causes pollen abortion, and many male sterilities (including cytoplasmic male sterility) are related to abnormal timing of tapetum PCD ([7.12.28](/7.12.28)) |
| Suspensor, root cap, anther dehiscence | The suspensor dies late in embryo development; the outer cells of the root cap die and are shed continuously; death of cells at the line of anther dehiscence opens the anther locule |
| **Aerenchyma** | Under flooding and oxygen deficiency, cortical cells of roots and stems die under induction by ethylene, forming air-filled spaces (lysigenous aerenchyma, [7.13.27](/7.13.27)) |
| Abortion of organs in unisexual flowers | Abortion of stamens in the maize ear and of pistils in the tassel ([7.13.22](/7.13.22#sec-22.7.4)) |
| Perforation of leaves | Cells in specific regions of leaves of lace plant (*Aponogeton madagascariensis*) and *Monstera* die during development, forming holes |
| Self-incompatibility | Self pollen tubes of poppy undergo PCD through PrsS–PrpS signaling ([7.13.23](/7.13.23#sec-23.4.2)) |

### 24.4.3. The Hypersensitive Response

When a plant is infected by an incompatible pathogen (carrying an effector recognized by a plant resistance protein), the cells around the infection site die rapidly, forming a local necrotic lesion that confines the biotrophic pathogen in dead cells; this is the **hypersensitive response**. It is accompanied by a burst of reactive oxygen species, Ca²⁺ influx, accumulation of salicylic acid and expression of defense genes, and can induce systemic acquired resistance throughout the plant. Cell death in the hypersensitive response is of the necrotic type, and the executioners include VPEs and metacaspases; its immunological mechanisms (effector-triggered immunity, resistosomes) are in [7.13.25](/7.13.25).

### 24.4.4. Autophagy

Autophagy is a conserved mechanism by which eukaryotic cells degrade cellular components using lysosomes or vacuoles ([7.3.11](/7.3.11)). Plants have macroautophagy and microautophagy but no chaperone-mediated autophagy; *Arabidopsis* has about 40 *ATG* genes, and the core mechanism is the same as in yeast and animals. The physiological roles of plant autophagy include:

- Nutrient recovery: under starvation (carbon or nitrogen starvation) and in senescence, autophagy degrades cytoplasm, ribosomes and chloroplasts (the RCB pathway and chlorophagy) and recovers nutrients. *atg* mutants die quickly under carbon or nitrogen starvation, their leaves senesce prematurely, and remobilization of nitrogen to the seeds measured by ¹⁵N tracing is reduced.
- Removal of damaged organelles and protein aggregates: chloroplasts damaged by high light and peroxisomes and mitochondria damaged by oxidation can all be removed by selective autophagy.
- A dual effect on cell death: in nonsenescent tissues autophagy maintains cell homeostasis and suppresses unnecessary death; the premature senescence of *atg* mutants and the spread of cell death in the hypersensitive response are caused in part by accumulation of salicylic acid (autophagy limits salicylic acid signaling by negative feedback, Yoshimoto et al., 2009). In vessel differentiation and tapetum degeneration, on the other hand, autophagy takes part in executing death.

## 24.5. Abscission

### 24.5.1. Concept and Types of Abscission

**Definition 24.5.1 (Abscission)** The process by which organs such as leaves, flowers, fruits, seeds or branches separate from the plant body is called **abscission**.

Abscission is a means of self-regulation of plants:

- Normal abscission: caused by senescence or maturity, such as the fall of leaves in autumn, abscission of ripe fruits and seeds, and abscission of petals and sepals after pollination.
- Physiological abscission: caused by the physiological activity of the plant itself, such as shedding of flowers and fruits when nutrition is insufficient or source and sink are out of balance (physiological fruit drop of apple and citrus, shedding of squares and bolls of cotton). The shedding rate of cotton squares and bolls often reaches 60%–70% and is an important factor limiting yield.
- Stress abscission: caused by drought, high temperature, low temperature, flooding, pests and diseases, air pollution and so on.
- Timely abscission eliminates weak vegetative organs and aborted flowers and fruits and is an adaptation to the environment; but excessive shedding of crop flowers and fruits lowers yield, and seed shattering (such as that of wild rice) was the first trait selected against in crop domestication ([7.13.28](/7.13.28)).

### 24.5.2. Development of the Abscission Zone and Cell Biology of Abscission

Abscission occurs at the **abscission zone** at the base of the organ ([7.12.23](/7.12.23#sec-23.7)). The abscission zone consists of several layers of closely packed, small, unlignified cells; it is formed early in organ development but is activated only at abscission. The process of abscission can be divided into four stages:

1. Differentiation of the abscission zone: the abscission zone forms at the base of the petiole, pedicel or fruit stalk (the abscission zone of the tomato pedicel requires MADS-box genes such as *JOINTLESS*; tomato with the *jointless* mutation has no "joint" in the fruit stalk, the stalk does not come off with the fruit at harvest, which is convenient for mechanical harvest and processing).
2. Acquisition of sensitivity to ethylene: the cells of the abscission zone are originally insensitive to ethylene (maintained by auxin from the organ) and become sensitive after the organ senesces and auxin decreases.
3. Activation: cells of the abscission layer have prominent nucleoli, increased RNA and more endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi apparatus and vesicles, and synthesize and secrete cell wall hydrolases: cellulase (β-1,4-glucanase), polygalacturonase, pectin methylesterase, xyloglucan endotransglucosylase/hydrolase and **expansin**; the **middle lamella** and primary wall are broken down, the cells swell and round up and are loosely arranged, and finally the organ breaks along the abscission layer under gravity and wind, tearing the vessels and tracheids of the xylem. The petiole abscission zone of bean has two cellulase isozymes: the pI 4.5 isozyme is present throughout development, whereas the pI 9.5 isozyme forms only during abscission and moves to the middle lamella, and its synthesis is promoted by ethylene (and abscisic acid).
4. Formation of a protective layer: the cell walls of the cells on the stem side of the break surface deposit suberin and lignin, forming a protective layer that is later replaced by periderm, leaving a leaf scar.

Abscission is a process that requires energy and new protein synthesis: oxygen deficiency, respiratory inhibitors and protein synthesis inhibitors all inhibit abscission.

### 24.5.3. Hormonal Control of Abscission

First auxin. Auxin produced in the leaf inhibits abscission of the leaf. Addicott et al. (1955) proposed the auxin gradient theory: what determines abscission is not the absolute content of auxin but the relative auxin concentration on the two sides of the abscission zone. When the concentration on the distal side (organ side) is higher than on the proximal side (stem side), the organ does not abscise; when the difference between the two sides is small or absent, the organ abscises; when the concentration on the distal side is lower than on the proximal side, abscission is accelerated. The classic experiment is as follows: if the blade is cut off and the petiole kept, the petiole soon abscises; if auxin is applied to the cut end of the petiole, abscission is delayed; if auxin is applied to the proximal side of the abscission zone (the stem side), abscission is instead accelerated. The theory can be written as a dose–response relation (Figure 3A): let $r = A_{\mathrm{distal}}/A_{\mathrm{proximal}}$; the probability of abscission is

$$
p = \frac{1}{1 + (r/r_{50})^h},
\tag{24.3}
$$

with $r_{50} \approx 1$ and $h > 1$ reflecting the steepness of the response. Equation (24.3) is merely a summary of experimental regularities, but it explains why leaf senescence (decline of auxin synthesis), leaf injury or shading all promote abscission. The effective auxin concentration must be appropriate: at higher concentrations auxins induce ethylene synthesis and instead promote abscission (used for flower and fruit thinning, [7.13.13](/7.13.13)).

**Ethylene** is the most direct inducer of abscission. In the nineteenth century it was noticed that trees under street lamps lit by coal gas lost many leaves, and the active component in the gas was later confirmed to be ethylene. Ethylene promotes RNA and protein synthesis in abscission zone cells, induces the synthesis and secretion of cellulase and polygalacturonase and stimulates swelling of the cells proximal to the abscission layer; it also inhibits transport of auxin to the abscission zone and promotes inactivation of auxin, so that auxin in the abscission zone decreases and the sensitivity of the zone to ethylene is raised further. Osborne (1978) proposed that there are ethylene-responsive target cells in the abscission layer. The ethylene-insensitive *etr1-1* mutant of *Arabidopsis* shows delayed abscission of floral organs; tomato with a mutation in an ethylene receptor gene, "never ripe" (*Never ripe*), has flowers that also do not abscise easily.

This interaction can be summarized as an "auxin–ethylene" balance model: in an intact leaf the continuous supply of auxin makes the abscission zone insensitive to ethylene; senescence, injury and stress lower the auxin supply from the leaf and increase ethylene, so that the abscission zone becomes sensitive; ethylene further lowers auxin transport, forming positive feedback, and abscission is triggered.

**Abscisic acid** was first isolated in studies of the abscission of cotton bolls ("abscisin II"), from which it got its name. Its content is low in young fruits and young leaves and highest near abscission; the short days of autumn promote synthesis of abscisic acid, which is thought to be one of the signals of seasonal leaf fall. Abscisic acid can inhibit auxin transport in the petiole, promote secretion of cell wall hydrolases, promote ethylene synthesis and raise the sensitivity of organs to ethylene; but in most plants the direct effect of abscisic acid on abscission is weaker than that of ethylene, and it acts mainly indirectly by promoting senescence and ethylene synthesis.

**Gibberellin** promotes ethylene synthesis and indirectly promotes abscission; **cytokinin** content is very low when fruits abscise, and it delays senescence and inhibits abscission. Under certain conditions gibberellin and cytokinin can antagonize abscisic acid and ethylene and inhibit senescence and abscission of organs.

### 24.5.4. The IDA–HAE/HSL2 Peptide Signal

Floral organs (sepals, petals, stamens) of *Arabidopsis* abscise after pollination, and the genetics of this process has revealed a peptide signaling pathway (Butenko et al., 2003):

- *INFLORESCENCE DEFICIENT IN ABSCISSION* (*IDA*) encodes a secreted small peptide precursor, and the processed IDA peptide (a dozen or so amino acids, containing hydroxyproline) is expressed in cells of the abscission zone; floral organs of the *ida* mutant do not abscise and remain on the growing silique, whereas overexpression of *IDA* causes premature abscission of floral organs.
- The receptors of IDA are two leucine-rich-repeat receptor-like kinases on the plasma membrane of abscission zone cells, HAESA (HAE) and HAESA-LIKE2 (HSL2), which bind SERK-type coreceptors; the *hae hsl2* double mutant likewise does not abscise.
- The activated receptors turn on a MAP kinase cascade (MKK4/5–MPK3/6), which relieves repression of cell separation by KNOX-type transcription factors such as BP (KNAT1) and induces expression of genes for cell wall remodeling enzymes (polygalacturonase, XTH, expansin), so that abscission layer cells separate.

The IDA–HAE/HSL2 pathway and the ethylene pathway are parallel and interact: in ethylene-insensitive mutants abscission of floral organs is delayed but still eventually occurs, whereas the *ida* mutant does not abscise even in the presence of ethylene. IDA-type peptides also take part in separation of cortical cells when lateral roots emerge and in abscission of organs of tomato, citrus, soybean and others, showing that "cell separation" is controlled by a conserved peptide signal.

### 24.5.5. Quantifying Abscission: Time Course of Break Strength

The course of abscission in the abscission zone is often quantified by the break strength (the force required to pull the organ off at the abscission zone). When petiole abscission zone explants of bean, cotton and others are placed in ethylene (for example $10\ \mu\mathrm{L\,L^{-1}}$), the break strength falls rapidly after a lag phase (Figure 3B), which can be approximated by

$$
F(t) = F_{\min} + (F_0 - F_{\min})\,\mathrm{e}^{-k(t - t_{\mathrm{lag}})}, \qquad t > t_{\mathrm{lag}},
\tag{24.4}
$$

The lag phase $t_{\mathrm{lag}}$ corresponds to the time for abscission zone cells to acquire sensitivity and begin to synthesize hydrolases, and the rate constant $k$ reflects the increase of hydrolase activity. When auxin is applied to the distal end of the explant, the lag phase is prolonged and $k$ decreases; in air (without ethylene) the explant also abscises gradually with its own senescence, but much more slowly. When the break strength falls below a certain threshold, the organ drops under its own weight.

**Example 24.5.1** A petiole explant has initial break strength $F_0 = 300\ \mathrm{g}$ (in grams-force) and $F_{\min} = 10\ \mathrm{g}$; in ethylene $t_{\mathrm{lag}} = 8\ \mathrm{h}$ and $k = 0.12\ \mathrm{h^{-1}}$. If the organ abscises when break strength falls below $30\ \mathrm{g}$, find the time of abscission. If auxin applied to the distal end makes $t_{\mathrm{lag}} = 24\ \mathrm{h}$ and $k = 0.06\ \mathrm{h^{-1}}$, what is the time of abscission?

From $10 + 290\,\mathrm{e}^{-0.12(t-8)} = 30$, $\mathrm{e}^{-0.12(t-8)} = 20/290$, $t - 8 = \ln 14.5/0.12 = 22.3\ \mathrm{h}$, $t = 30\ \mathrm{h}$. With auxin, $t - 24 = \ln 14.5/0.06 = 44.6\ \mathrm{h}$, $t = 69\ \mathrm{h}$; abscission is delayed by about 38 h, of which the prolonged lag phase contributes 16 h and the lowered rate about 22 h.

A: dose–response representation of the auxin gradient theory (Eq. 24.3, $r_{50} = 1$, $h = 3$): the organ abscises when the ratio $r$ of auxin on the distal to the proximal side of the abscission zone is less than 1 and is retained when it is much greater than 1; leaf senescence and reduced auxin supply lower $r$. ### 24.5.6. Environmental Factors

- Temperature: both too high and too low temperatures accelerate abscission of organs. Biochemical reactions accelerate as temperature rises ($Q_{10} \approx 2$, the **temperature coefficient**), and high temperature also causes water deficit and indirectly promotes leaf abscission; low temperature (frost) injures leaves and increases ethylene.
- Water: seasonal drought makes trees shed leaves, reducing transpiration. In drought the activity of indoleacetic acid oxidase increases, diffusible auxin decreases, cytokinin content falls and ethylene and abscisic acid increase, all of which promote abscission. Flooding makes roots oxygen-deficient, ACC is transported to the shoot and converted to ethylene, causing epinasty and abscission of leaves.
- Light: with plenty of light the carbohydrate content is high and organs do not abscise easily; with insufficient light (cloudy or rainy weather, planting too dense) the carbohydrate content is low and flowers and fruits abscise easily. Shortening day length is one signal of autumn leaf fall of deciduous trees (through abscisic acid and growth cessation).
- Oxygen: a high concentration of oxygen promotes ethylene synthesis and abscission; low oxygen inhibits abscission (abscission is an aerobic process).
- Mineral nutrition: deficiency of nitrogen, zinc, boron and calcium can all cause shedding of flowers and fruits.
- Other: air pollutants (ozone, sulfur dioxide), pests and diseases and mechanical injury induce ethylene and promote abscission.

## 24.6. Postharvest Physiology and Preservation

Harvested fruits, vegetables and cut flowers are still living organs; they continue to respire and transpire and to undergo ripening and senescence, while losing the ability to obtain water and assimilates from the mother plant. The aim of postharvest preservation is to slow these processes while avoiding physiological injury. The main factors that determine the effect of preservation are temperature, ethylene, gas composition and humidity.

Temperature is the most important factor. The rates of respiration and senescence vary exponentially with temperature and can be described by the temperature coefficient $Q_{10}$ (the factor by which the rate increases for a 10 °C rise in temperature, [7.13.12](/7.13.12)). If the rate of deterioration at a reference temperature $T_0$ is $r_0$ and the storage life is $L_0$, then

$$
r(T) = r_0\,Q_{10}^{(T - T_0)/10},
\qquad
L(T) = L_0\,Q_{10}^{-(T - T_0)/10}.
\tag{24.5}
$$

The $Q_{10}$ of most fruits and vegetables is between 2 and 3 and is often higher in the low temperature range of 0–10 °C. If the storage life at 20 °C is 7 d and $Q_{10} = 3$, it is 21 d at 10 °C and 63 d at 0 °C (Figure 4). Rapid precooling after harvest and a complete cold chain are therefore the most effective means of preservation. But fruits and vegetables of tropical and subtropical origin (banana, mango, cucumber, tomato, sweet potato) suffer **chilling injury** below a certain temperature (about 10–13 °C) ([7.13.27](/7.13.27)), expressed as browning and pitting of the skin and failure to ripen normally, and their optimum storage temperature is above the chilling temperature.

Next is ethylene. Ethylene accelerates ripening of climacteric fruits ([7.13.23](/7.13.23#sec-23.10.2)), yellowing of leafy vegetables (broccoli, spinach), wilting and petal abscission of cut flowers, sprouting of potatoes and development of bitterness in carrots. Adding the effect of ethylene to Eq. (24.5) gives

$$
r(T, E) = r_0\,Q_{10}^{(T - T_0)/10}\left(1 + \frac{aE}{K_{\mathrm{e}} + E}\right),
\tag{24.6}
$$

$E$ is the ethylene concentration in the storage environment, $a$ is the maximum factor by which ethylene can increase the rate of deterioration, and $K_{\mathrm{e}}$ is the half-saturation concentration. Many fruits and vegetables respond markedly to $0.1$–$1\ \mu\mathrm{L\,L^{-1}}$ of ethylene, so climacteric fruits (which release ethylene) should not be stored together with ethylene-sensitive vegetables and flowers. Methods of controlling ethylene include ventilation, removal of ethylene by absorption or catalytic oxidation with potassium permanganate, blocking ethylene receptors with 1-MCP (apple, pear, banana, cut flowers), inhibiting ethylene synthesis with AVG, and treating cut flowers such as carnation with silver thiosulfate (STS) to inhibit the action of ethylene and extend vase life.

![Effects of temperature and ethylene on the storage life of fruits and vegetables (Eqs. 24.5 and 24.6, illustrative parameters: storage life 7 d at 20 °C without ethylene; ethylene effect $a = 2$, $K_{\mathrm{e}} = 0.3\ \mu\mathrm{L\,L^{-1}}$). The larger $Q_{10}$, the more marked the extension of life by cooling; $1\ \mu\mathrm{L\,L^{-1}}$ of ethylene shortens storage life to about 40%. In the shaded region (below about 10 °C), fruits and vegetables of tropical origin suffer chilling injury, and storage life does not continue to lengthen as temperature falls](./plots/postharvest.py)

Gas composition and humidity are also important. Lowering $\ce{O2}$ and raising $\ce{CO2}$ (controlled-atmosphere storage and modified-atmosphere packaging) inhibits respiration and the synthesis and action of ethylene; too low $\ce{O2}$ causes anaerobic respiration and off-flavors and too high $\ce{CO2}$ causes injury, and the suitable gas composition varies with the species ([7.13.12](/7.13.12#sec-12.12)). The storage environment should maintain high relative humidity (90%–95% for most fruits and vegetables) to reduce water loss and wilting, but too high a humidity favors growth of pathogens.

There are also some specific measures: leafy vegetables and cut flowers can be treated with cytokinin (such as 6-benzylaminopurine) to delay yellowing; sucrose (a respiratory substrate), fungicides (to prevent microbial blocking of vessels) and acidifiers are added to the vase solution of cut flowers; apples are dipped in calcium chloride after harvest to strengthen the cell wall and reduce softening and physiological disorders; potatoes and onions are kept from sprouting by methyl naphthaleneacetate, maleic hydrazide or low temperature, prolonging dormancy ([7.13.20](/7.13.20)); climacteric fruits such as banana and tomato can be harvested at the mature-green stage, transported cold and ripened with ethylene after arrival at the market.

**Example 24.6.1** A batch of apples has a storage life of 7 d at 20 °C without ethylene, with $Q_{10} = 3$. (a) What is the approximate storage life under refrigeration at 2 °C? (b) If $0.6\ \mu\mathrm{L\,L^{-1}}$ of ethylene ($a = 2$, $K_{\mathrm{e}} = 0.3\ \mu\mathrm{L\,L^{-1}}$) accumulates in the cold store because of mixed loading, to what does the life shorten?

(a) $L = 7 \times 3^{(20-2)/10} = 7 \times 3^{1.8} = 7 \times 7.22 = 51\ \mathrm{d}$. (b) Ethylene increases the rate by a factor of $1 + 2 \times 0.6/0.9 = 2.33$, and life falls to $51/2.33 = 22\ \mathrm{d}$, less than half of the original. This shows that controlling ethylene (ventilation, ethylene removal, 1-MCP) is as important as refrigeration.

## Questions

1. A wheat cultivar has a flag-leaf nitrogen content of $2.8\ \mathrm{g\,m^{-2}}$ at flowering, falling to $0.9\ \mathrm{g\,m^{-2}}$ at maturity; the flag-leaf area is $1.2 \times 10^4\ \mathrm{m^2}$ per hectare (leaf area index 1.2). Find the nitrogen resorption efficiency of the flag leaf and the amount of nitrogen supplied by the flag leaf to the grain per hectare. If the grain yield is $7\ \mathrm{t\,ha^{-1}}$ with 2.0% nitrogen, what proportion of grain nitrogen is supplied by resorption from the flag leaf? Discuss the advantages and disadvantages of "stay-green" cultivars for yield and quality.

   **Answer** $\mathrm{RE} = (2.8 - 0.9)/2.8 = 68\%$. The flag leaf supplies $1.9 \times 1.2 \times 10^4 = 2.28 \times 10^4\ \mathrm{g} = 22.8\ \mathrm{kg\,N\,ha^{-1}}$. The grain contains $7000 \times 0.020 = 140\ \mathrm{kg\,ha^{-1}}$ of nitrogen, so the flag leaf contributes about 16%; the rest comes from other leaves, stems and sheaths and root uptake after flowering. The leaves of stay-green cultivars photosynthesize for longer and the filling period is extended, which favors grain weight and yield, especially in environments with drought in the filling period; but remobilization of nitrogen, zinc and iron from the leaves is reduced or delayed, and grain protein and micronutrient contents may fall (as in the *NAM-B1* example), and if low temperatures occur at maturity, failure of the leaves to senesce in time may delay harvest. Breeding must balance these according to the objective (yield or quality) and compensate by cultural measures such as applying more nitrogen after flowering.

2. Explain the following facts: (a) detached leaves placed in the dark yellow within a few days, but the region treated with kinetin stays green and amino acids from other regions are transported to the treated region; (b) leaves of *SAG12::IPT* transgenic tobacco show delayed senescence but the plants are otherwise nearly normal; (c) the pea *sgr* mutation (green cotyledons) does not affect yield, whereas functional stay-green in sorghum can raise drought tolerance.

   **Answer** (a) In the dark photosynthesis stops, the supply of sugar and ATP decreases, transcription factors such as PIFs accumulate and the senescence program starts; detached leaves also lose cytokinin from the roots. Kinetin, as a cytokinin, maintains protein synthesis and chloroplasts and inhibits expression of SAGs; at the same time it makes the treated region a "sink" (raising invertase and other activities) that attracts nutrients from other regions, the "nutrient mobilization" effect of cytokinin. (b) The *SAG12* promoter is activated only when senescence begins, so *IPT* is expressed and cytokinin synthesized only in leaves that are beginning to senesce, and when senescence is suppressed expression of *SAG12* falls again, forming a self-limiting negative feedback: the cytokinin level is just maintained at what is needed to prevent senescence and does not accumulate to excess in young tissues, so the side effects of excess cytokinin (loss of apical dominance, inhibited roots) do not appear. (c) SGR is the Mg-dechelatase in chlorophyll degradation, and *sgr* only blocks degradation of chlorophyll (cosmetic stay-green), while photosynthetic function, protein degradation and nutrient remobilization proceed as usual, so seeds and yield are normal and only the color differs. Functional stay-green delays the whole senescence program, so the leaves still maintain photosynthesis and water balance late in filling and can continue to supply assimilates to the grain in drought, which raises drought tolerance and yield.

3. Using Eq. (24.4) and the parameters of Example 24.5.1, calculate the break strength of the explant after 20 h in ethylene. If after 12 h of ethylene treatment the explant is moved into air (and assuming that $k$ immediately falls to 0.02 h⁻¹), when will the organ abscise? What does this suggest about whether the decision to abscise is reversible?

   **Answer** At $t = 20\ \mathrm{h}$: $F = 10 + 290\,\mathrm{e}^{-0.12 \times 12} = 10 + 290 \times 0.237 = 78.7\ \mathrm{g}$. At 12 h $F(12) = 10 + 290\,\mathrm{e}^{-0.48} = 10 + 179.4 = 189.4\ \mathrm{g}$; thereafter $F = 10 + 179.4\,\mathrm{e}^{-0.02(t-12)}$, and falling to 30 g takes $t - 12 = \ln(179.4/20)/0.02 = 2.19/0.02 = 110\ \mathrm{h}$, so abscission takes place only at about 122 h (compared with 30 h in continuous ethylene). This shows that removing ethylene early in the activation stage can greatly delay abscission, so the decision to abscise is reversible up to a certain stage, whereas after the cell wall has been extensively degraded abscission is irreversible. In practice, once the cells of the abscission zone have synthesized hydrolases in quantity, the enzymes continue to act even when ethylene is removed, so $k$ does not fall immediately and the real delay would be less than this calculated value.

4. Compare the differentiation of vessel elements with cell death in the hypersensitive response with respect to morphology, speed, executioners and biological significance. Why is plant programmed cell death said to differ from animal apoptosis?

   **Answer** Death of vessel elements is of the vacuolar type: the cell first completes deposition of the secondary wall (controlled by VND6/7), the central vacuole accumulates hydrolases and enlarges, and after tonoplast rupture the protoplast autolyzes within a very short time, leaving a hollow cell with a secondary wall; the process lasts several days, the executioners include XCP1/2, BFN1 and metacaspases, and its significance is the formation of a water-conducting channel. The hypersensitive response is of the necrotic type: it occurs within hours after recognition of the pathogen, accompanied by a burst of reactive oxygen species and Ca²⁺ influx, the protoplast shrinks and the cell collapses, tonoplast rupture is late, the executioners include VPEs, metacaspases and the proteasome, and its significance is to confine biotrophic pathogens. Compared with animal apoptosis, plant cells have cell walls and dead cells cannot be engulfed, and the protoplast is cleared by vacuolar autolysis; plants have no typical caspases, Bcl-2 family or apoptotic bodies, and different proteases such as VPEs and metacaspases act; and plant PCD is often aimed at leaving useful dead cell structures (vessels, fibers, seed coat).

5. A batch of bananas is to be shipped from Hainan to Beijing, and transport takes 4 d. Design a plan for harvest, transport and sale and explain the physiological basis of each step.

   **Answer** (a) Harvest at the mature-green stage (the fruit has reached physiological maturity and has not yet entered the climacteric), when the response of the fruit to ethylene is still low and it withstands transport. (b) Precool as soon as possible after harvest and transport at 13–14 °C: low temperature lowers respiration and ethylene synthesis (the $Q_{10}$ effect), but it must not fall below about 12 °C, otherwise chilling injury occurs, the skin turns black and the fruit cannot ripen normally. (c) During transport and storage, ventilate or absorb ethylene with potassium permanganate, treat with 1-MCP if necessary and use suitable modified-atmosphere packaging (lower $\ce{O2}$, higher $\ce{CO2}$) to prevent premature start of the climacteric, which is irreversible once started ([7.13.23](/7.13.23#sec-23.10.2)). (d) Keep humidity high to prevent water loss; handle gently to reduce mechanical injury (injury induces ethylene). (e) On arrival at the market, treat in a ripening room with about $100\ \mu\mathrm{L\,L^{-1}}$ of ethylene (or ethephon) at 15–20 °C and high humidity for a day or two to induce system 2 ethylene and the respiratory climacteric, so that the fruit turns yellow and sweet together (starch hydrolyzed to sugar) before going on sale.
