Plants are sessile and cannot avoid drought, flood, cold, heat or salinity as animals can; they can only adjust their own physiology, metabolism and form in place. Once light, water, $\ce{CO2}$, $\ce{O2}$, temperature, or soil nutrients and toxic ions depart from the suitable range, plant growth and yield decline; worldwide, crop losses due to abiotic stress far exceed those due to pests and diseases. This chapter first gives the concepts of stress, adaptation and acclimation and the general effects of stress on metabolism; it then describes how plants perceive stress and pass the signal to the genome; next it discusses the protective mechanisms common to all stresses: osmotic adjustment, antioxidation, molecular chaperones and adjustment of membrane lipids; it then treats drought, flooding, salinity, temperature and heavy metal stress in turn; and finally it covers approaches to improving stress resistance. The chapter uses results from many earlier chapters: water potential and its components are in [7.13.2](/7.13.2), transpiration and xylem water transport in [7.13.3](/7.13.3), ion transport across membranes in [7.13.5](/7.13.5), photoprotection and photoinhibition in [7.13.7](/7.13.7), stomatal movement in [7.13.10](/7.13.10), fermentation under anoxia in [7.13.12](/7.13.12), and abscisic acid and ethylene in [7.13.15](/7.13.15); the morphology and structure of plants adapted to drought, salinity, wet and cold environments are in [7.12.32](/7.12.32).

## 27.1. The Concept of Stress

### 27.1.1. Adversity and Stress

**Definition 27.1.1 (Stress)** Environmental conditions that prevent a plant from realizing its genetically possible maximum growth and reproductive potential are called **stress** (adversity); stress caused by abiotic factors is called abiotic stress, and stress caused by biotic factors (pathogens, herbivores, competitors; the previous two chapters) is called biotic stress.

The word "stress" is borrowed from mechanics: J. Levitt likened the action of the environment to stress and the resulting change in the plant to strain; strain may be reversible ("elastic", recovering when the stress is removed) or irreversible ("plastic", causing injury). Whether a given condition constitutes stress depends on the plant: $5\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ is harmless to winter wheat but is chilling injury to rice.

Plants follow different developmental strategies at different levels of stress. Under suitable conditions vegetative growth is completed according to the genetic program before the transition to reproductive growth; under stress vegetative growth may end early and the plant flowers and sets seed before reaching normal size, so that the plant is small with few leaves and yield falls accordingly. This trade-off between vegetative and reproductive growth is related to life history: annual plants must complete the life cycle in one season and tend to adjust metabolism and development to produce as many viable seeds as possible under the conditions of that season; perennials tend to secure storage reserves to survive to the next season, even at the cost of reduced seed production that year. Rice can tolerate moderate drought without obvious effect on growth, while severe drought strongly inhibits vegetative growth.

### 27.1.2. Adaptation and Acclimation

Plants cope with stress in two ways on different time scales.

- Adaptation: genetic change fixed in the whole population by natural selection over many generations. For example, serpentine soils have low water content, few macronutrients and many heavy metals, and most plants cannot grow on them, but populations genetically adapted to serpentine can often be found not far from related plants on normal soil; transplant experiments show that only they can grow and reproduce on serpentine, and crossing experiments show that the adaptation is stably inherited. Populations of Yorkshire fog (*Holcus lanatus*) on arsenic-contaminated mining sites in southwestern England carry a genetic variant that reduces arsenate uptake, while this variant is rare in populations on uncontaminated soil.
- Acclimation: reversible physiological or morphological adjustment of an individual without genetic change; if a plant becomes more tolerant of the same stress after experiencing it once, this is called acclimation or hardening (domestication of crops is a different matter, see [7.13.28](/7.13.28)). Hardening off of seedlings in horticulture is an example: seedlings raised indoors are placed outdoors for some time each day and are moved completely outdoors only after several days. Activation of the SOS pathway (Section 27.5.3) by salt-intolerant glycophytes in saline conditions to extrude $\ce{Na+}$ is also acclimation.

The two often act together: the adaptation of Yorkshire fog only reduces arsenic uptake and cannot stop it completely, and the arsenic that does enter must still be detoxified by an acclimation mechanism common to all plants, the synthesis of the metal-binding peptides phytochelatins (Section 27.7.2). Epigenetic mechanisms can prolong the duration of the acclimation response and even pass it in part to progeny.

### 27.1.3. Modes of Stress Resistance

**Definition 27.1.2 (Stress Resistance, Stress Escape and Stress Tolerance)** The capacity of a plant to resist and endure adversity is collectively called **stress resistance**. A plant that completes its life cycle before the adversity arrives, or survives it in dormant organs, and so does not meet the adversity in the course of growth and development, shows **stress escape**; the ability of a plant to survive and grow in the midst of adversity is called **stress tolerance**.

Ephemerals of deserts germinate, grow, flower and set seed only in the few weeks of the rainy season and survive the dry season as seeds, a typical case of stress escape. Stress tolerance in turn includes two kinds of mechanism: one keeps the tissue itself free or relatively free of the stress, for example deep roots and stomatal closure keep leaf water potential high in drought, called avoidance (as in "dehydration avoidance"); the other lets tissue continue to function while under the stress, for example protoplasm that is not damaged at low water potential, called tolerance (as in "dehydration tolerance"). Section 27.4 illustrates these three modes with drought.

### 27.1.4. Effects of Stress on Metabolism

Different stresses have their own primary effects, but the secondary effects overlap greatly (Table 27.1).

| Stress | Primary effects | Secondary effects |
|---|---|---|
| Water deficit | Lower water potential, cellular dehydration, increased hydraulic resistance | Slower cell and leaf expansion, lower metabolic activity, stomatal closure, inhibited photosynthesis, altered carbon partitioning, destabilized membranes and proteins, toxicity from raised ion concentrations |
| Salinity | Lower water potential, ion toxicity | As for water deficit; nutrient imbalance (uptake of $\ce{K+}$ and $\ce{Ca^2+}$ inhibited) |
| Flooding and soil compaction | Oxygen deficiency (hypoxia, anoxia) | Inhibited respiration, enhanced fermentation, energy deficit, toxic substances produced by anaerobic soil microbes |
| High temperature | Destabilized membranes and proteins | Inhibited photosynthesis and respiration, protein aggregation |
| Chilling | Membrane destabilization (phase transition) | Membrane dysfunction, metabolic imbalance, photoinhibition |
| Freezing | Lower water potential, cellular dehydration, mechanical damage by ice crystals, intracellular freezing | As for water deficit |
| Heavy metals and toxic elements | Displace or interfere with the binding of cofactors to proteins and DNA | Metabolic disorder, accumulation of reactive oxygen species |
| Strong light | Photoinhibition, impeded repair of photosystem II | Formation of reactive oxygen species, reduced $\ce{CO2}$ fixation |

Almost all stresses lead to accumulation of **reactive oxygen species**: temperature changes alter membrane fluidity and disrupt the coupling between different complexes of the photosynthetic and respiratory electron transport chains, so that electrons are passed directly to $\ce{O2}$; enzymes have different temperature optima, and temperature change makes different steps of a metabolic pathway lose coordination and toxic intermediates accumulate. Reactive oxygen species on the one hand cause damage and on the other are signals that start acclimation (Section 27.2.2).

At the metabolic level there are also some common changes: biosynthetic enzyme activities fall and hydrolase activities rise, and polysaccharides and proteins are broken down to soluble sugars and amino acids; **abscisic acid** increases rapidly; proline accumulates. Respiration responds differently to different stresses: it declines gradually under freezing, high temperature, salinity and flooding; it first rises and then falls under chilling and drought; and it rises markedly upon pathogen infection ([7.13.25](/7.13.25)).

Water is the plant's most abundant and also most limiting resource. About 97% of the water a plant takes up is lost to the atmosphere by transpiration, about 2% is used for cell volume increase, and only about 1% is used in metabolism (mainly photosynthesis).

Stresses in the field often occur at the same time. Drought and high temperature almost always occur together, with the most serious consequences: in 1980–2004 losses of United States agriculture due to drought plus heat were about 6 times those due to drought alone. The physiological effect of combined stress is not a simple sum of the two stresses: at high temperature plants increase stomatal conductance and lower leaf temperature by transpiration, whereas in drought they close stomata; when both occur together the stomata close and leaf temperature is 2–5 °C higher than under heat alone, which can be lethal. In *Arabidopsis*, drought plus heat induced several hundred transcripts that neither single stress induced. Some combinations may be beneficial: drought closes stomata and may reduce the entry of ozone.

## 27.2. Stress Perception and Signaling

### 27.2.1. Perceiving Stress

Stress alters the stability of proteins and RNA, ion transport, the coupling between reactions and so on, and these primary perturbations can themselves serve as signals. There are roughly five classes of mechanism for perceiving stress: physical perception (such as the mechanical effect of the plasma membrane shrinking away from the cell wall in drought), biophysical perception (changes in protein structure or enzyme activity), metabolic perception (accumulation of by-products when coupling is disturbed, such as reactive oxygen species under strong light), biochemical perception (proteins that evolved specifically to sense a particular stress) and epigenetic perception (changes in chromatin structure). Known or proposed sensors include:

- Temperature: in *Arabidopsis*, a cyclic nucleotide-gated calcium channel senses temperature increase and is required for heat acclimation; rice COLD1 interacts with a G protein and senses low temperature; thermal reversion of phytochrome B makes it a temperature receptor as well ([7.13.16](/7.13.16#sec-16.9.2)).
- Osmotic: the osmosensitive calcium channel OSCA1 in the plasma membrane opens under hyperosmotic conditions; the histidine kinase ATHK1 is also thought to take part in osmosensing.
- Salt: the sphingolipid glycosyl inositol phosphoryl ceramide (GIPC) in the outer leaflet of the plasma membrane binds $\ce{Na+}$ and causes $\ce{Ca^2+}$ influx (this response is lost when MOCA1, the enzyme that synthesizes GIPC, is absent).
- Energy: the SnRK1 kinase senses energy depletion during stress and activates hundreds of stress-responsive transcripts.
- Oxygen: the stability of group VII ethylene response factors (ERF-VII) depends on $\ce{O2}$, sensing hypoxia through the N-end rule pathway ([7.13.12](/7.13.12)).
- Unfolded proteins: the ER transmembrane sensor IRE1 is required for heat tolerance.

### 27.2.2. Calcium, Reactive Oxygen Species and Regulons

In early stress, rises in cytosolic $\ce{Ca^2+}$ and reactive oxygen species are signals common to many acclimation pathways. $\ce{Ca^2+}$ binds **calmodulin**, calcium-dependent protein kinases (CPKs), calcineurin B-like proteins (CBLs) and the kinases that bind them (CIPKs), and can also bind some transcription factors directly, thereby altering transcription. The plasma membrane NADPH oxidase RBOH produces superoxide outside the cell, reactive oxygen species activate calcium channels, and $\ce{Ca^2+}$ in turn phosphorylates and activates RBOH through CPKs (the N terminus of RBOH also has two EF hands that bind $\ce{Ca^2+}$ directly), so that the two signals form a positive feedback. MAPK cascades integrate the signals of different stresses such as temperature, oxidation, salt and osmotic stress, and drought: they share components of the same MAP3K–MAP2K–MAPK modules, so the signal of one stress influences the responses to other stresses.

Long-distance transmission of stress signals likewise involves a self-propagating wave of reactive oxygen species: when one leaf is subjected to heat, cold, salt or strong light, each cell in turn activates its own RBOHD, and reactive oxygen species travel through the whole plant at about $8.4\,\mathrm{cm\,min^{-1}}$, accompanied by calcium waves and electrical signals, so that parts not yet stressed also begin to acclimate; this is called systemic acquired acclimation, corresponding to the systemic acquired resistance caused by pathogens ([7.13.25](/7.13.25#sec-25.5.1)).

At the transcriptional level one transcription factor can bind the promoters of hundreds of genes; a network consisting of a group of transcription factors and their target genes that responds in a coordinated way to a stress is called a **regulon**. Its advantage is that it activates the needed pathways while repressing unneeded or even harmful ones, for example under strong light repressing the genes encoding light-harvesting antenna proteins and activating genes that scavenge reactive oxygen species. Regulons of abiotic stress fall into two classes, ABA-dependent and ABA-independent: the ABA-dependent ones include bZIP-type AREB/ABF (binding the ABA-responsive element ABRE) and MYC/MYB types; the ABA-independent ones include the DREB1 (that is, CBF, low temperature) and DREB2 (drought, heat) types, which bind the dehydration-responsive element DRE/CRT (core sequence A/GCCGAC); NAC-type transcription factors take part in both pathways.

"Retrograde signals" sent from the chloroplast to the nucleus also take part in stress sensing: strong light over-reduces the electron transport chain and reactive oxygen species accumulate, and the chloroplast signals through components such as GUN1 to down-regulate the nuclear gene *Lhcb* that encodes the LHCII apoprotein. Small RNAs and epigenetic modifications add a longer time scale to acclimation: miRNAs and siRNAs regulate gene expression under low temperature, nutrient deficiency, dehydration, salt and oxidative stress (for example under oxidative stress miR398 falls, so that the mRNA of its target Cu/Zn-SOD increases); some changes in DNA methylation and histone modification can be retained through mitosis and meiosis, so that stress "memory" persists for a long time and even affects progeny.

### 27.2.3. Hormones

Hormones are the main integrators of stress responses (synthesis and signaling of each hormone in [7.13.13](/7.13.13)–[7.13.15](/7.13.15)).

- **Abscisic acid**: in drought, ABA in leaves can rise about 50-fold, the most dramatic change in hormone concentration in response to an environmental signal known. ABA comes from synthesis in the leaf itself and hydrolysis of the conjugated ABA (ABA glucose ester) in the vacuole, and in part from ABA synthesized in the root and carried in the transpiration stream (root-sourced signal, [7.13.15](/7.13.15#sec-15.2.3)); it closes stomata, maintains root growth in drought, and induces the synthesis of LEA proteins and osmotic adjustment compounds. When humidity rises ABA is rapidly degraded and the stomata reopen; mutants that cannot synthesize or perceive ABA cannot close their stomata in drought and are called wilty mutants. ABA also takes part in acclimation to cold and salt, but flooding is an exception.
- **Cytokinin**: antagonizes ABA, promoting stomatal opening and delaying leaf senescence; in drought cytokinin falls while ABA rises. In transgenic tobacco in which a senescence- and stress-inducible promoter (*SARK*) drives the cytokinin synthesis gene *IPT*, after 15 days of drought and 7 days of rewatering the leaves stay green and photosynthetically competent, while the wild type wilts severely and its leaves senesce.
- **Ethylene**: accumulates in tissue during flooding, induces aerenchyma and adventitious roots, and regulates internode elongation of deepwater rice and the expression of *SUB1A* (Section 27.4.4).
- **Gibberellin** and DELLA: under stress DELLA proteins accumulate and growth is inhibited, which aids survival; **jasmonic acid** raises the expression of low-temperature-responsive transcription factors; **auxin**, **brassinosteroids** and **salicylic acid** also take part in stress responses, and the regulatory networks of the hormones intersect.

## 27.3. Common Protective Mechanisms

### 27.3.1. Osmotic Adjustment

When the soil water potential falls because of drought or salinity, a plant can keep taking up water only if it lowers its own water potential still further. From $\Psi_{\mathrm{w}} = \Psi_{\mathrm{s}} + \Psi_{\mathrm{p}}$ (Eq. (2.5) of [7.13.2](/7.13.2)), the only way to lower water potential without losing turgor is to lower the **solute potential**.

**Definition 27.3.1 (Osmotic Adjustment)** The process in which cells under osmotic stress actively accumulate solutes, increasing the net amount of solute in each cell and thereby lowering the **solute potential** and maintaining water uptake and turgor, is called **osmotic adjustment**.

Osmotic adjustment must be distinguished from the passive concentration caused by water loss: when cells lose water, the solute concentration rises naturally, but the total amount of solute is unchanged; osmotic adjustment is an increase in the total amount of solute, independent of changes in cell volume. It generally lowers the solute potential by 0.2–0.8 MPa, and more in plants adapted to extreme drought. From the van 't Hoff relation for solute potential (Eq. (2.2) of [7.13.2](/7.13.2)),

$$
\Delta\Psi_{\mathrm{s}} = -\Delta c_{\mathrm{s}}\,R\,T, \tag{27.1}
$$

at $25\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$, $RT = 2.48\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$, so every additional $1\,\mathrm{osmol\,L^{-1}}$ ($1000\,\mathrm{osmol\,m^{-3}}$) of solute lowers the solute potential by $2.48\,\mathrm{MPa}$.

There are two routes of osmotic adjustment. One is to take up, or import from other organs, inorganic ions ($\ce{K+}$, $\ce{Na+}$, $\ce{Cl-}$) while synthesizing organic acids such as malate and citrate to balance the charge; plants on saline soil generally do this, and **halophytes** in particular maintain growth in salinity by $\ce{Na+}$ and $\ce{Cl-}$ accumulated in the vacuole. But high concentrations of ions harm enzymes and membranes, so ions accumulate mainly in the vacuole, separated from cytosolic enzymes and organelles. The other is to accumulate **compatible solutes** in the cytosol: they do not destabilize membranes or interfere with enzyme function even at high concentration, and so balance the low solute potential caused by ions in the vacuole (the water potential balance between cytosol and vacuole shown in the figure below). There are four common classes of compatible solutes: amino acids (proline), sugar alcohols (sorbitol, mannitol, pinitol), quaternary ammonium compounds (glycine betaine) and tertiary sulfonium compounds (dimethylsulfoniopropionate, DMSP); they are all small molecules with zero net charge. Plants of each family often prefer one or two of them, for example Chenopodiaceae and Poaceae mostly accumulate glycine betaine.

Proline accumulation is the most widespread: it is synthesized from glutamate by Δ1-pyrroline-5-carboxylate synthetase (P5CS) and is rapidly oxidized after the stress is removed by mitochondrial proline dehydrogenase ([7.13.12](/7.13.12)). Its role is not only osmotic adjustment: proline interacts with proteins, increasing their hydration and solubility, reducing precipitation, and protecting membrane structure; it scavenges reactive oxygen species; storing nitrogen as proline reduces the toxicity of free ammonia and supplies nitrogen and carbon for protein synthesis after rewatering. This protective action of compatible solutes is called osmoprotection. Synthesizing large amounts of organic solutes consumes carbon and energy, which is one reason osmotic adjustment lowers yield.

**Example 27.3.1 (How Much Osmotic Adjustment Can Proline Contribute?)** In a droughted leaf, proline increases from $1$ to $40\,\mu\mathrm{mol\,g^{-1}}$ fresh weight, and the leaf contains $0.85\,\mathrm{mL\,g^{-1}}$ of water. (a) If proline is distributed uniformly in all cell water, by how much does it lower the solute potential? (b) What if it is all concentrated in the cytosol, which is only 10% of cell water? ($25\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$)

**Solution** (a) The increase of $39\,\mu\mathrm{mol}$ dissolved in $0.85\,\mathrm{mL}$ raises the concentration by $45.9\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}} = 45.9\,\mathrm{mol\,m^{-3}}$; by Eq. (27.1), $\Delta\Psi_{\mathrm{s}} = -45.9 \times 2478\,\mathrm{Pa} = -0.11\,\mathrm{MPa}$. (b) Dissolved in $0.085\,\mathrm{mL}$ of cytosol, the concentration rises by $459\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$ and $\Delta\Psi_{\mathrm{s}} = -1.14\,\mathrm{MPa}$ (estimate for an ideal solution). Averaged over the whole leaf, proline contributes little to the solute potential, and osmotic adjustment of the whole cell relies mainly on ions and sugars in the vacuole; but in the very small volume of the cytosol it can reach concentrations of several hundred millimolar, enough to balance the low solute potential of the vacuole. Evaluating the role of a compatible solute must therefore take compartmentation into account.

### 27.3.2. Antioxidant Systems

The most common reactive oxygen species in plant cells are superoxide ($\ce{O2^{.-}}$), singlet oxygen ($^1\ce{O2}$), hydrogen peroxide ($\ce{H2O2}$) and the hydroxyl radical ($\ce{^.OH}$). Their sources include: the iron–sulfur centers of photosystem I passing electrons to $\ce{O2}$ (Mehler reaction); triplet chlorophyll in photosystem II passing energy to $\ce{O2}$ to give singlet oxygen; glycolate oxidase of photorespiration and β-oxidation of fatty acids in peroxisomes producing $\ce{H2O2}$; the mitochondrial electron transport chain; plasma membrane NADPH oxidase; and the breakdown of ozone in the apoplast. The hydroxyl radical is the strongest oxidant, produced from $\ce{H2O2}$ and $\ce{Fe^2+}$ or $\ce{Cu+}$ by the Fenton reaction; there is no enzyme in the cell that scavenges it, and it can start radical chain reactions with many organic molecules, causing membrane lipid peroxidation (one product, malondialdehyde, is often used as an index of oxidative damage), enzyme inactivation and nucleic acid degradation.

Oxidative stress refers to the state in which the production of reactive oxygen species exceeds their scavenging and they accumulate in the cell. The systems that scavenge reactive oxygen species include enzymatic and nonenzymatic antioxidants, which form a network and are continually regenerated by NAD(P)H produced by photosynthesis or respiration (figure below):

- **Superoxide dismutase** (SOD) both oxidizes and reduces superoxide: $\ce{2O2^{.-} + 2H+ -> O2 + H2O2}$. Plants have Cu/Zn-SOD (cytosol and chloroplast), Mn-SOD (mitochondria) and Fe-SOD (chloroplast).
- **Catalase** (CAT) breaks down in peroxisomes the large amounts of $\ce{H2O2}$ produced by photorespiration: $\ce{2H2O2 -> 2H2O + O2}$.
- The ascorbate–glutathione cycle (Foyer–Halliwell–Asada cycle) is present in chloroplasts, mitochondria, peroxisomes and the cytosol: ascorbate peroxidase (APX) reduces $\ce{H2O2}$ with ascorbate, $\ce{2 Asc + H2O2 -> 2 MDHA + 2H2O}$; monodehydroascorbate (MDHA) is reduced directly by MDHA reductase with NAD(P)H, or disproportionates spontaneously to dehydroascorbate (DHA), which is reduced by DHA reductase with reduced glutathione (GSH), and the oxidized glutathione (GSSG) formed is reduced again by glutathione reductase with NADPH. APX has a much higher affinity for $\ce{H2O2}$ than CAT, so it keeps $\ce{H2O2}$ at a very low level while CAT handles large amounts of $\ce{H2O2}$.
- Peroxiredoxins (Prx) are oxidized by $\ce{H2O2}$ and then reduced by **thioredoxin**; glutathione peroxidase reduces $\ce{H2O2}$ with GSH.
- Nonenzymatic antioxidants: the water-soluble ascorbate (vitamin C, up to about $10\,\mathrm{mM}$ in the chloroplast stroma) and glutathione, the lipid-soluble α-tocopherol (vitamin E, protecting membrane lipids) and carotenoids (quenching singlet oxygen and triplet chlorophyll, [7.13.7](/7.13.7)), as well as flavonoids and proline.

Reactive oxygen species are not merely poisons to be cleared. The scavenging network keeps them at a safe but nonzero level, so that the cell can still use them for signal transduction (Section 27.2.2); overexpressing scavenging enzymes until reactive oxygen species are almost zero in fact weakens the acclimation response.

### 27.3.3. Molecular Chaperones and Protective Proteins

Protein structure is sensitive to changes in temperature, pH and ionic strength. Besides osmotic adjustment to maintain hydration and proton pumps to maintain pH, plants also use **molecular chaperones** to help proteins fold and to prevent misfolding and aggregation. The group of chaperones induced at temperatures above normal growth temperature are called **heat shock proteins**, HSPs for short, first discovered in *Drosophila* and present universally in plants, animals, fungi and microbes; they are also induced by water deficit, wounding, low temperature and salt. The main classes are: HSP70 binds and releases misfolded proteins, HSP60 (chaperonin) forms a barrel-shaped folding chamber, HSP101 disaggregates already aggregated proteins, HSP90 stabilizes signaling proteins, and small heat shock proteins (sHSPs) bind and stabilize denatured proteins and membranes and prevent irreversible aggregation. The *heat shock response* is regulated by heat shock transcription factors (HSFs).

Another class of protective proteins is the LEA (late embryogenesis abundant) protein family, which includes dehydrins. They were first found during the desiccation phase of late seed maturation and also accumulate in salt, drought, cold and freezing; because they are often induced by ABA they are also called RAB (responsive to ABA) proteins. LEA proteins are highly hydrophilic, intrinsically disordered proteins that fold into α helices on drying and can reduce aggregation of desiccation-sensitive proteins, an action called molecular shielding; they also have cryoprotective activity.

### 27.3.4. Adjustment of Membrane Lipids

When temperature falls, a membrane may change from the flexible liquid-crystalline phase to the solid gel phase; the phase transition temperature depends on the composition of the membrane lipids. Saturated fatty acids have no double bonds, their chains can pack tightly and they solidify at relatively high temperature; the cis double bonds of polyunsaturated fatty acids bend the chain so that they cannot pack tightly and solidify at much lower temperature. The melting points of several C18 fatty acids illustrate this: stearic acid (18:0) about $69\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$, oleic acid (18:1) about $13\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$, linoleic acid (18:2) about $-5\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$, α-linolenic acid (18:3) about $-11\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$; palmitic acid (16:0) about $63\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$.

The degree of unsaturation of membrane lipids is often summarized by the double-bond index:

$$
\mathrm{DBI} = \frac{1}{100}\sum_k n_k\,w_k, \tag{27.2}
$$

where $n_k$ is the number of double bonds of the $k$th fatty acid and $w_k$ is its percentage of total fatty acids.

| Stearic 18:0 | 1.9 | 1.1 | 2.9 | 2.2 | 2.6 | 1.6 |

During long exposure to extreme temperature, the composition of membrane lipids undergoes acclimatory change. In cold acclimation the activity of fatty acid desaturases rises and the proportion of unsaturated fatty acids increases, so that membranes remain fluid at lower temperature; conversely, at high temperature the degree of saturation rises so that membranes are not too fluid. In *Arabidopsis* mutants with reduced ω-3 fatty acid desaturase activity, the saturation of chloroplast membrane lipids is raised and the heat tolerance of photosynthesis is improved.

### 27.3.5. Cross-Tolerance

**Definition 27.3.2 (Cross-Tolerance)** The phenomenon in which a plant that has experienced one adversity also shows increased resistance to another adversity is called **cross-tolerance**.

The cause of cross-tolerance is that many stresses induce the same general protective substances: enzymes that scavenge reactive oxygen species, molecular chaperones, osmoprotectants and so on, which remain for some time after the stress is removed, so that the plant is already "prepared" when the second stress arrives. ABA is a common signal linking many stresses; cold hardening also raises drought resistance, and drought hardening also raises freezing resistance. Recovery after acclimation itself carries risk: for example when flooding ends and the roots suddenly regain oxygen, the accumulated reduced substances and the restarted electron transport generate large amounts of reactive oxygen species, so recovery also needs coordination; some plants retain part of the regulatory mechanisms, hormonal state or epigenetic modifications after the stress is removed so that they respond faster when the stress recurs, which is stress memory.

## 27.4. Water Stress: Drought and Flooding

### 27.4.1. Drought Injury

**Definition 27.4.1 (Drought Injury)** The injury suffered by a plant when water loss exceeds water uptake and the tissue has a water deficit is called **drought injury**.

Meteorological drought means prolonged insufficient precipitation; for plants, drought is divided into atmospheric drought (high temperature and low air humidity, excessive transpiration) and soil drought (lack of available water in the soil), and prolonged atmospheric drought also leads to soil drought. In China non-irrigable dryland makes up about half of the cultivated land, and drought is one of the main limiting factors of agricultural production.

The direct manifestation of water deficit is **wilting**: wilting from which the plant recovers when transpiration is reduced is called temporary wilting; wilting that does not recover even when transpiration is reduced is called permanent wilting, at which point the soil water content is close to the **permanent wilting point** ([7.13.3](/7.13.3)). Severe dehydration of protoplasm is the core of drought injury. Of the mechanisms of injury, the mechanical injury hypothesis (when cells lose water the cell wall stops contracting while the protoplast continues to contract, and on rewatering the two expand out of step and tear the plasma membrane) and the sulfhydryl hypothesis (dehydration brings proteins close together, sulfhydryl groups oxidize to disulfide bonds, and on rewatering the proteins cannot return to their original conformation) were proposed historically and explain part of the phenomena; current understanding emphasizes destabilization of membranes and proteins, reactive oxygen species produced by uncoupling of photosynthetic electron transport, xylem embolism and hydraulic failure ([7.13.3](/7.13.3)), and carbon starvation caused by prolonged stomatal closure.

Different physiological processes differ greatly in their sensitivity to water deficit. The most sensitive is cell expansion: a slight fall in turgor markedly slows growth (the Lockhart equation, [7.13.17](/7.13.17)); in sunflower, leaf expansion stops completely under mild stress that hardly affects the photosynthetic rate. As stress worsens, the processes affected in turn are the synthesis of cell wall and protein, stomatal opening, $\ce{CO2}$ assimilation and respiration; accumulation of ABA and proline begins under moderate stress. A reduction of leaf area is therefore the earliest and most universal consequence of drought, and is itself a water-saving response.

The three modes by which plants cope with drought (Section 27.1.3):

| Mode | Mechanism | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Drought escape | Complete the life cycle before drought arrives | Desert ephemerals; early-maturing crop cultivars; drought-induced early flowering |
| Dehydration avoidance (one kind of drought tolerance) | Maintain high tissue water potential: take up more water, lose less | Deep, well-developed roots, increased root-to-shoot ratio (requires ABA); stomatal closure; leaf rolling, adjusting leaf angle (paraheliotropic movement), more trichomes and wax, thicker cuticle; reduced leaf area and leaf shedding (cotton); CAM ([7.13.8](/7.13.8)) |
| Dehydration tolerance (the other kind of drought tolerance) | Tissue maintains function at low water potential | Osmotic adjustment; LEA proteins and dehydrins; antioxidation; "resurrection plants" (such as some species of *Boea* and *Selaginella*) revive after losing more than 90% of their water |

In drought, leaf expansion is inhibited before photosynthesis, and the photosynthate saved is allocated more to the roots, so that the root-to-shoot ratio increases: in maize, at low water potential the roots of the wild type grow much faster relative to the shoot than those of an ABA-deficient mutant, showing that ABA is required for this adjustment. Roots lose turgor and stop growing in dry soil layers and continue growing in moist layers, so the root system concentrates where there is water. Leaf angle also changes: in soybean under mild water stress the terminal leaflet lifts and the two lateral leaflets droop, nearly parallel to the sunlight, reducing light interception; this is entirely different from wilting under severe stress.

Crassulacean acid metabolism is the extreme form of dehydration avoidance: stomata open at night and close by day, the leaf-to-air vapor pressure difference is much smaller in the cool night, so the transpiration ratio of CAM plants is about 50 (molar ratio, [7.13.8](/7.13.8)), only about one eighth of that of a typical C3 plant (about 400). Some succulents (such as the ice plant *Mesembryanthemum crystallinum*) are facultative CAM plants, which under drought or salinity induce PEP carboxylase, pyruvate phosphate dikinase and NADP-malic enzyme and so switch from C3 to CAM ([7.13.8](/7.13.8)).

### 27.4.2. Isohydric and Anisohydric Regulation

Different species regulate leaf water potential differently as the soil dries. Let the transpiration rate be $E$ and the hydraulic conductance from soil to leaf be $K$; by analogy with Ohm's law ([7.13.3](/7.13.3)),

$$
\Psi_{\mathrm{leaf}} = \Psi_{\mathrm{soil}} - \frac{E}{K}, \qquad E = g_{\mathrm{s}}\,\frac{\Delta e}{p}, \tag{27.3}
$$

where $g_{\mathrm{s}}$ is stomatal conductance and $\Delta e/p$ is the leaf-to-air difference in water vapor mole fraction.

**Definition 27.4.2 (Isohydric and Anisohydric Regulation)** The mode of regulation in which, as the soil dries or evaporative demand increases, stomatal closure keeps the minimum leaf water potential roughly constant is called **isohydric** regulation; the mode in which leaf water potential falls together with soil water potential while stomata stay open longer is called **anisohydric** regulation.

Maize, poplar and many conifers lean toward isohydric regulation, and sunflower, barley and many desert shrubs toward anisohydric regulation; in fact this is a continuous spectrum, and the same species also changes with conditions. Isohydric plants control stomata mainly by ABA (root-sourced signal and synthesis in the leaf itself) together with leaf water potential (Tardieu model, [7.13.15](/7.13.15#sec-15.2.3)), avoiding xylem embolism but possibly dying of carbon starvation in prolonged drought; anisohydric plants maintain photosynthesis and growth but risk hydraulic failure and need more embolism-resistant xylem and stronger osmotic adjustment.

**Example 27.4.1** Suppose a plant has $K = 10\,\mathrm{mmol\,m^{-2}\,s^{-1}\,MPa^{-1}}$ and, at a given evaporative demand with stomata fully open, $E = 5\,\mathrm{mmol\,m^{-2}\,s^{-1}}$. (a) If it is isohydric with leaf water potential not below $-1.2\,\mathrm{MPa}$, what are the maximum transpiration rate and relative stomatal conductance when $\Psi_{\mathrm{soil}}$ is $-0.3$, $-0.9$ and $-1.2\,\mathrm{MPa}$? (b) What if it is anisohydric and allows leaf water potential to fall to $-2.5\,\mathrm{MPa}$?

**Solution** By Eq. (27.3), $E_{\max} = K(\Psi_{\mathrm{soil}} - \Psi_{\min})$. (a) $\Psi_{\mathrm{soil}} = -0.3$: $E_{\max} = 10 \times 0.9 = 9 > 5$, stomata are not limited, relative conductance 100%; $-0.9$: $E_{\max} = 3$, stomata must close to $3/5 = 60\%$; $-1.2$: $E_{\max} = 0$, stomata close completely and photosynthesis stops. (b) $\Psi_{\min} = -2.5$: $E_{\max}$ in the three cases is 22, 16 and 13, all greater than 5, so stomata are not limited and the leaf water potentials are $-0.8$, $-1.4$ and $-1.7\,\mathrm{MPa}$. The anisohydric plant can still transpire and photosynthesize as usual at $\Psi_{\mathrm{soil}} = -1.2\,\mathrm{MPa}$, at the cost of leaf and xylem bearing a much lower water potential.

:::

Agriculture uses the responses of plants to mild water deficit to save water: moderate water control at the seedling stage ("promoting the roots and controlling the shoots") to make the root system well developed (seedling hardening by withholding water); and regulated deficit irrigation and alternate partial root-zone irrigation (keeping half of the root system in dry soil, which produces a root-sourced ABA signal that partly closes the stomata, while the other half supplies water to maintain leaf water status), which reduce water use with essentially no loss of yield.

### 27.4.3. Flooding Injury

**Definition 27.4.3 (Flooding Injury)** The injury to plants caused by excess soil water or submergence of the plant, owing to oxygen deficiency and other causes, is called **flooding injury**; injury to upland crops when the soil is saturated but there is no standing water on the surface is often called waterlogging injury.

The fundamental cause of flooding injury is oxygen deficiency. $\ce{O2}$ makes up about 21% of air, about $8.6\,\mathrm{mol\,m^{-3}}$ at $25\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$; water in equilibrium with air has only about $0.26\,\mathrm{mol\,m^{-3}}$ (about $8\,\mathrm{mg\,L^{-1}}$), and flooded soil has less. More important is diffusion: the diffusion coefficient of $\ce{O2}$ in water (about $2\times10^{-9}\,\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$) is ten thousand times smaller than in air (about $2\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$). Once the soil pores are filled with water, the $\ce{O2}$ around the root is used up by roots and microbes within a few hours.

Oxygen deficiency stops aerobic respiration in the root, which turns to fermentation: lactic fermentation acidifies the cytosol, lactate dehydrogenase is inhibited and pyruvate decarboxylase is activated, and metabolism shifts to ethanolic fermentation ([7.13.12](/7.13.12)); each hexose yields only 2 ATP by fermentation, far fewer than by aerobic respiration, enough for root cells to survive a short flood but not to sustain growth. Energy deficit stops energy-consuming processes such as protein synthesis, and ethanol and acetaldehyde accumulate and the cytosol acidifies. Anoxia induces a group of "anaerobic polypeptides", mainly enzymes of glycolysis and fermentation. In flooded soil, aerobic ammonifying and nitrifying bacteria are inhibited, nitrate is denitrified, anaerobic bacteria become active, the redox potential of the soil falls, and toxic substances such as $\ce{Fe^2+}$, $\ce{Mn^2+}$, $\ce{H2S}$ and butyric acid are produced; ethylene synthesized by roots accumulates because it diffuses slowly in water. When the floodwater recedes and oxygen is suddenly restored, large amounts of reactive oxygen species again cause reoxygenation injury.

Flood-tolerant plants have a series of adaptations:

- **Aerenchyma**: wetland plants (such as rice) have constitutive, longitudinally continuous gas spaces in stems and roots; $\ce{O2}$ enters through stomata or lenticels and is carried to the root tip by molecular diffusion (sometimes by convection driven by small pressure differences). Nonwetland plants such as maize induce the formation of lysigenous aerenchyma under oxygen deficiency: hypoxia activates ACC synthase and ACC oxidase in the root tip, and ethylene causes programmed cell death and disintegration of part of the cortical cells, leaving gas spaces ([7.13.15](/7.13.15#sec-15.5.4)).
- Radial oxygen loss barrier: the outer layers of the roots of wetland plants such as rice have suberized and lignified cell walls that prevent $\ce{O2}$ from leaking from the root into the soil, so that the oxygen in the aerenchyma can supply root tips several tens of centimeters away; maize roots leak oxygen, the internal oxygen quickly becomes insufficient to sustain aerobic respiration of the root tip, and this limits the depth to which roots can enter anoxic soil.
- Adventitious roots, hypertrophied lenticels and aerenchyma at the stem base; and organs that can survive for weeks to months without oxygen, such as the embryo and coleoptile of rice and barnyard grass and the rhizomes of bulrush and cattail overwintering in lake mud, which can expand leaves under anoxia and switch to aerobic metabolism when the leaves emerge above the water.

The supply of $\ce{O2}$ in aerenchyma can be estimated with **Fick's law** ([2.5.1](/2.5.1)). Let the $\ce{O2}$ concentration in the gas phase at the root base be $C_0$, the fraction of root volume occupied by gas spaces (porosity) be $\varphi$, the root tissue (including radial loss) consume oxygen uniformly at rate $Q$ ($\ce{O2}$ consumed per unit volume of root tissue per second), and the root tip have no flux. At steady state $D\varphi C'' = Q$, which gives

$$
C(z) = C_0 - \frac{Q}{D\varphi}\Bigl(Lz - \frac{z^2}{2}\Bigr), \qquad L_{\max} = \sqrt{\frac{2D\varphi\,(C_0 - C_{\mathrm{c}})}{Q}}, \tag{27.4}
$$

where $L$ is the root length, $C_{\mathrm{c}}$ is the minimum concentration needed to sustain aerobic respiration, and $L_{\max}$ is the maximum root length at which the root tip still receives enough $\ce{O2}$. $L_{\max}$ is proportional only to $\sqrt{\varphi}$: increasing porosity from 2% to 30% increases the length of root that can be aerated about 4-fold; and a radial oxygen loss barrier that reduces the effective $Q$ is equally effective (figure below).

A: O₂ profiles for roots with porosity φ of 0.02, 0.10 and 0.30, each extending to its maximum length L_max (about 9, 21 and 36 cm) at an oxygen consumption of root tissue of Q = 7.5×10⁻⁴ mol m⁻³ s⁻¹; dots mark the root tips. **Example 27.4.2** A wetland plant has root porosity $\varphi = 0.25$ and oxygen consumption $Q = 1.0\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{mol\,m^{-3}\,s^{-1}}$; an upland crop after flooding has root porosity of only 0.04 (before induced aerenchyma forms), no oxygen loss barrier, and an effective oxygen consumption of $3.0\times10^{-3}$. Estimate the maximum aerable root length of both with Eq. (27.4).

**Solution** Wetland plant: $L_{\max} = \sqrt{2 \times 2.0\times10^{-5} \times 0.25 \times 8.2/1.0\times10^{-3}} = \sqrt{0.082} = 0.29\,\mathrm{m}$; upland crop: $\sqrt{2 \times 2.0\times10^{-5} \times 0.04 \times 8.2/3.0\times10^{-3}} = \sqrt{4.4\times10^{-3}} = 0.066\,\mathrm{m}$. The former is about 4.3 times the latter; the root tips of upland crops quickly become anoxic after flooding, which is why they induce aerenchyma and grow adventitious roots close to the water surface.

### 27.4.4. Two Flooding Strategies of Rice

Rice is the only major cereal cultivated under flooded conditions, but it is also sensitive to complete submergence: ordinary rice cultivars die after about a week of complete submergence. In the rainfed lowlands of South and Southeast Asia, floods often cover the paddies within a few days and recede after one or two weeks ("flash floods"); in the deepwater areas of Bangladesh, Thailand and elsewhere, the floodwater rises slowly in the rainy season and does not recede for months, with depths of several meters. Rice has evolved two opposite strategies (figure below).

- Quiescence strategy: the landrace FR13A from Odisha, India, can tolerate about two weeks of complete submergence, and the gene *SUB1A* at its major locus *SUB1* encodes an ERF-VII transcription factor (Xu et al. 2006). Ethylene accumulating during submergence induces *SUB1A*, which causes the DELLA proteins SLR1 and SLRL1 to accumulate, repressing gibberellin-promoted elongation and at the same time slowing starch degradation; the plant almost stops growing, saves carbohydrate, and resumes growth with the remaining reserves after the flood recedes. By marker-assisted backcrossing, introduction of *SUB1* into high-yielding cultivars such as Swarna has produced submergence-tolerant cultivars that have been released widely in South Asia with essentially unchanged yield traits.
- Escape strategy: deepwater rice elongates its internodes rapidly during submergence, growing 20–25 cm per day, keeping the leaves above the water, and finally the plant can reach several meters in height. Ethylene induces two ERF transcription factor genes, *SNORKEL1* and *SNORKEL2* (Hattori et al. 2009), which raise the action of gibberellin and promote internode elongation; a deepwater-rice allele of the gibberellin synthesis gene *SD1* (the "Green Revolution" semi-dwarf gene) also takes part in this response.

Each strategy suits a different environment: in flash floods, plants that try to elongate exhaust their carbohydrate before emerging, and elongation also makes the stem weak so that it lodges after the flood recedes, so quiescence is more favorable; in slowly rising deep water, quiescent plants remain submerged until they starve, and only escape allows survival. Ordinary lowland cultivars without *SUB1A* elongate to some extent during complete submergence but not enough to emerge, and so fall between the disadvantages of the two strategies.

## 27.5. Salt Injury

### 27.5.1. Saline Soils and Salt Injury

**Definition 27.5.1 (Salt Injury)** The injury to plant growth and development caused by excess salt in the soil is called **salt injury**.

Soils dominated by NaCl and $\ce{Na2SO4}$ are called saline soils, and those dominated by $\ce{Na2CO3}$ and $\ce{NaHCO3}$ are called alkaline (sodic) soils; usually both are present, and together they are called saline–alkaline soils. Evaporation in arid regions makes salt accumulate at the surface, and irrigation with saline water with poor drainage accelerates salinization ([7.13.3](/7.13.3)); it is estimated that about 20% of irrigated farmland worldwide is affected by salinity. Soil salinity is often expressed as the electrical conductivity of a saturated soil extract, $\mathrm{EC_e}$ (unit $\mathrm{dS\,m^{-1}}$), and a soil with $\mathrm{EC_e} > 4\,\mathrm{dS\,m^{-1}}$ is called saline. The solute potential of the soil solution is approximately proportional to the conductivity:

$$
\Psi_{\mathrm{s}} \approx -0.036\,\mathrm{MPa} \times \frac{\mathrm{EC}}{\mathrm{dS\,m^{-1}}}, \tag{27.5}
$$

so the solute potential of a soil solution with $\mathrm{EC_e} = 10\,\mathrm{dS\,m^{-1}}$ is about $-0.36\,\mathrm{MPa}$ ($1\,\mathrm{dS\,m^{-1}}$ corresponds roughly to $10\,\mathrm{mM}$ salt, and seawater is about $50\,\mathrm{dS\,m^{-1}}$).

Salt injury has two components. One is nonspecific osmotic stress: the water potential of the soil solution falls, roots have difficulty taking up water, causing "physiological drought", and seeds fail to germinate or germinate late. The other is specific ion toxicity: normally the cytosol of plant cells contains about $100\,\mathrm{mM}$ $\ce{K+}$ and less than $10\,\mathrm{mM}$ $\ce{Na+}$, and enzymes function optimally in this ionic environment; in salinity the cytosolic $\ce{Na+}$ and $\ce{Cl-}$ can exceed $100\,\mathrm{mM}$, reducing the hydration of proteins and membranes, denaturing proteins and destabilizing membranes. $\ce{Na+}$ denatures proteins more strongly than $\ce{K+}$, and in the apoplast competes with $\ce{K+}$ for the binding sites of transporters and interferes with high-affinity potassium uptake; high concentrations of $\ce{Na+}$ displace $\ce{Ca^2+}$ bound to the plasma membrane, lowering membrane selective permeability so that $\ce{K+}$ and organic solutes leak out (single-salt toxicity and ion antagonism, [7.13.5](/7.13.5)). In most plants $\ce{Na+}$ reaches a toxic concentration before $\ce{Cl-}$, but citrus, grape and soybean are especially sensitive to $\ce{Cl-}$. Salinity also causes oxidative stress and lowers photosynthesis and respiration.

### 27.5.2. The Two-Phase Model and the Maas–Hoffman Model

R. Munns summarized the time course of salt injury in two phases (Munns and Tester 2008). The osmotic phase appears within minutes to days after salt is added, is caused by the low water potential outside the root, and is unrelated to salt accumulation in the tissue: leaf expansion slows and the formation of lateral shoots is inhibited, affecting all genotypes (including salt-tolerant ones). The ionic phase comes later, after days to weeks, when $\ce{Na+}$ accumulates in old leaves to toxic levels and the old leaves senesce and die; if the formation of new leaves cannot keep up with the death of old leaves, the photosynthetic area keeps decreasing and the plant eventually dies. The timing of the ionic phase depends on the ability of roots to exclude $\ce{Na+}$ and on tissue tolerance, so differences between genotypes are mainly expressed in this phase (panel B of the figure below).

The salt tolerance of crops is described in agriculture by the Maas–Hoffman model (Maas and Hoffman 1977): relative yield $Y_{\mathrm{r}}$ (%) is unaffected when soil salinity is below a threshold $a$ and declines linearly with $\mathrm{EC_e}$ above the threshold,

$$
Y_{\mathrm{r}} = 100 - b\,(\mathrm{EC_e} - a), \qquad \mathrm{EC_e} > a, \tag{27.6}
$$

where $b$ is the percentage decline in yield per $1\,\mathrm{dS\,m^{-1}}$ increase, and the yield falls to zero at $\mathrm{EC_e} = a + 100/b$. Parameters of several crops are given in the table below (Maas 1990).

| Crop | Threshold $a$ / ($\mathrm{dS\,m^{-1}}$) | Slope $b$ / (% per $\mathrm{dS\,m^{-1}}$) | $\mathrm{EC_e}$ at zero yield | Tolerance class |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bean | 1.0 | 19 | 6.3 | Sensitive |
| Maize | 1.7 | 12 | 10.0 | Moderately sensitive |
| Rice | 3.0 | 12 | 11.3 | Sensitive |
| Wheat | 6.0 | 7.1 | 20.1 | Moderately tolerant |
| Cotton | 7.7 | 5.2 | 26.9 | Tolerant |
| Barley | 8.0 | 5.0 | 28.0 | Tolerant |

Sugar beet, sorghum, cotton and barley are salt-tolerant crops, wheat is moderately tolerant, rice and maize are more sensitive, and legumes are the most sensitive. Chinese sources sometimes list wheat and rice together as crops with weak salt tolerance, but by the Maas–Hoffman parameters the salt tolerance threshold of wheat is twice that of rice.

**Example 27.5.1** The soil of an irrigation district has $\mathrm{EC_e} = 9\,\mathrm{dS\,m^{-1}}$. (a) What are the relative yields of wheat, maize and barley? (b) What is the approximate solute potential of the soil solution? (c) To keep the relative yield of maize at no less than 80%, to what must soil salinity be reduced?

**Solution** (a) Wheat: $100 - 7.1 \times 3 = 78.7\%$; maize: $100 - 12 \times 7.3 = 12.4\%$; barley: $100 - 5.0 \times 1 = 95\%$. (b) By Eq. (27.5), $\Psi_{\mathrm{s}} \approx -0.036 \times 9 = -0.32\,\mathrm{MPa}$. (c) $100 - 12(\mathrm{EC_e} - 1.7) \ge 80$ gives $\mathrm{EC_e} \le 3.4\,\mathrm{dS\,m^{-1}}$, so salinity must be reduced by leaching with irrigation to about 40% of its original value.

### 27.5.3. Mechanisms of Salt Tolerance

The mechanisms of salt tolerance of plants can be grouped into three classes (Munns and Tester 2008): tolerance of osmotic stress (regulating stomata and growth, osmotic adjustment), exclusion of $\ce{Na+}$ from the shoot, and tissue tolerance of accumulated $\ce{Na+}$ (compartmentation into the vacuole, synthesis of compatible solutes). They depend on a group of transporters in the plasma membrane and tonoplast (figure below).

- Entry of $\ce{Na+}$: $\ce{Na+}$ is charged and can hardly cross the lipid bilayer; it enters root cells through low- and high-affinity routes such as nonselective cation channels and HKT-type transporters, many of which are originally transporters of $\ce{K+}$. Millimolar external $\ce{Ca^2+}$ (the normal $\ce{Ca^2+}$ concentration of the apoplast) raises the selectivity of $\ce{K+}$ transporters and reduces $\ce{Na+}$ uptake.
- The SOS pathway and efflux: the SOS pathway, identified in the *Arabidopsis* "salt overly sensitive" (*sos*) mutants and elucidated by J.-K. Zhu and colleagues, works as follows: the salt-induced $\ce{Ca^2+}$ signal is sensed by the calcium-binding protein SOS3 (CBL4 in roots; SCaBP8/CBL10 in shoots), which binds and activates the protein kinase SOS2 (CIPK24), which phosphorylates and activates the plasma membrane $\ce{Na+}/\ce{H+}$ antiporter SOS1, pumping $\ce{Na+}$ out of the cell using the proton gradient established by the plasma membrane $\ce{H+}$-ATPase. SOS1 expels $\ce{Na+}$ back to the soil at the root epidermis, and in xylem parenchyma cells takes part in long-distance partitioning of $\ce{Na+}$.
- Retrieval of $\ce{Na+}$ from the xylem: HKT1 (*Arabidopsis* AtHKT1;1) on xylem parenchyma cells of the root stele takes $\ce{Na+}$ back from the xylem sap, reducing the $\ce{Na+}$ carried to the leaves; *hkt1* mutants accumulate large amounts of $\ce{Na+}$ in leaves and are hypersensitive to salt. Homologous genes in cereals are important targets of salt tolerance breeding: the rice salt tolerance quantitative trait locus *SKC1* encodes OsHKT1;5; introduction of the *HKT1;5* gene (*Nax2*) from einkorn wheat into durum wheat raised the latter's yield on saline fields by about 25%.
- Vacuolar compartmentation: the V-type $\ce{H+}$-ATPase and $\ce{H+}$-pyrophosphatase in the tonoplast pump protons into the vacuole, and the $\ce{Na+}/\ce{H+}$ antiporter NHX uses this gradient to load $\ce{Na+}$ into the vacuole, which both relieves toxicity in the cytosol and accomplishes osmotic adjustment with vacuolar $\ce{Na+}$ and $\ce{Cl-}$; the cytosol is balanced by compatible solutes. Overexpression of *NHX1* or the $\ce{H+}$-pyrophosphatase gene *AVP1* can raise the salt tolerance of *Arabidopsis* and other plants.
- Maintaining a high $\ce{K+}/\ce{Na+}$ ratio: $\ce{K+}$ channels and high-affinity $\ce{K+}$ transporters keep cytosolic $\ce{K+}$, and in salinity this ratio predicts salt tolerance better than the absolute $\ce{Na+}$ content.

**Halophytes** can complete their life cycle in saline environments (morphology and types in [7.12.32](/7.12.32#sec-32.2.4)). Compared with salt-intolerant glycophytes, the cells of their shoots have stronger vacuolar compartmentation and better limit the net entry of $\ce{Na+}$ into mesophyll cells, so that they can bear the $\ce{Na+}$ carried continuously in the transpiration stream. By the way they handle salt, halophytes fall into three classes: salt accumulators store salt in the vacuole and take up water from saline soil with a very low osmotic potential, such as *Suaeda salsa* and *Salicornia*; salt secretors excrete the salt they take up through salt glands or salt bladders, such as tamarisk (*Tamarix*) and sea lavender (*Limonium*); salt excluders have roots of low permeability to $\ce{Na+}$ and $\ce{Cl-}$ and accumulate few ions, such as reed (*Phragmites*) and tall wheatgrass.

## 27.6. Temperature Stress

### 27.6.1. Chilling Injury

**Definition 27.6.1 (Chilling Injury)** The injury caused to plants by low temperature above freezing (generally 0–15 °C) is called **chilling injury**; no ice forms in the tissue, but physiological function is impaired and, when severe, organs or the whole plant die.

Chilling injury occurs mainly in plants of tropical and subtropical origin: rice, maize, cotton, cucumber, tomato, banana, sweet potato and others. Symptoms include chlorosis and necrosis of leaves, wilting and cessation of growth; when rice meets low temperature at the booting stage, pollen development is blocked and the plant is sterile; chilling-sensitive fruits and vegetables such as banana and cucumber develop browning of the skin and water-soaked spots in cold storage ([7.13.12](/7.13.12)). The core of chilling injury is the membrane: in the membrane phase transition hypothesis proposed by Lyons and Raison, the membranes of chilling-sensitive plants change from the liquid-crystalline to the gel phase at relatively high temperature, cracks appear in the membrane and permeability increases, water-soluble substances leak out and ion balance is destroyed; the activity of membrane-bound enzymes changes while soluble enzymes are unaffected, so metabolic pathways lose balance; the coupling of thylakoid ATP synthase and the activity of proton pumps decline, and energy conversion is impeded. With low temperature plus light, carbon assimilation slows while light absorption continues as before, the electron transport chain is over-reduced, reactive oxygen species are produced and photoinhibition results (in cucumber under chilling and light, photosystem I is especially susceptible to damage, [7.13.7](/7.13.7)). Chilling-resistant plants have a higher degree of membrane lipid unsaturation, and desaturase activity rises when low temperature comes (Section 27.3.4).

### 27.6.2. Freezing Injury and Extracellular Ice

**Definition 27.6.2 (Freezing Injury)** The injury caused to plants by ice formation in the tissue at temperatures below freezing is called **freezing injury**.

Intracellular freezing is almost always lethal: ice crystals destroy biological membranes and organelles and compartmentation no longer exists. But in most cases ice forms first outside the cell: the solutions in the apoplast, vessels and tracheids are dilute, have a higher freezing point, and ice crystals nucleate there first (ice-nucleating bacteria on leaf surfaces, such as the ice nucleation protein of *Pseudomonas syringae*, can start freezing at $-2$ to $-3\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$) and spread rapidly along the xylem.

There is a large water potential difference between extracellular ice and intracellular liquid water. At temperature $T$ the chemical potential of ice is lower than that of liquid water (supercooled water) at the same temperature, and the difference is the Gibbs free energy of melting $\Delta_{\mathrm{fus}}G(T)$; the water potential of ice is

$$
\Psi_{\mathrm{ice}} = -\frac{\Delta_{\mathrm{fus}}G(T)}{\bar V_{\mathrm{w}}} \approx -\frac{\Delta_{\mathrm{fus}}H}{\bar V_{\mathrm{w}}}\cdot\frac{T_0 - T}{T_0}, \tag{27.7}
$$

where $\Delta_{\mathrm{fus}}H = 6.01\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$, $\bar V_{\mathrm{w}} = 18.0\,\mathrm{cm^3\,mol^{-1}}$ and $T_0 = 273.15\,\mathrm{K}$. Substituting gives $\Psi_{\mathrm{ice}} \approx -1.22\,\mathrm{MPa}$ per degree below the freezing point near 0 °C; after taking the heat capacity difference between liquid water and ice into account, the slope decreases slightly at lower temperature and averages about $-1.2\,\mathrm{MPa\,K^{-1}}$ down to $-10\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ (a commonly cited value is $-1.16\,\mathrm{MPa}$ per °C), with $\Psi_{\mathrm{ice}}(-10\,^\circ\mathrm{C}) \approx -11.8\,\mathrm{MPa}$. This water potential is lower than the solute potential of any cell, so unfrozen water in the cell flows out down the water potential gradient and freezes on the extracellular ice crystals until the intracellular solution is concentrated to a solute potential equal to $\Psi_{\mathrm{ice}}$. At this point turgor is zero, and by the Boyle–van 't Hoff relation ([7.13.2](/7.13.2)) the fraction of osmotically active water retained in the cell is

$$
\frac{V}{V_0} = \frac{\Psi_{\mathrm{s0}}}{\Psi_{\mathrm{ice}}(T)}, \tag{27.8}
$$

where $\Psi_{\mathrm{s0}}$ is the solute potential before freezing.

**Example 27.6.1** A cell has solute potential $-1.2\,\mathrm{MPa}$ before freezing. (a) How much osmotically active water does it lose on reaching equilibrium at $-10\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$? (b) Cold acclimation lowers the solute potential to $-2.4\,\mathrm{MPa}$; how much water is lost at $-10\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$? To what temperature must it fall to lose the same fraction of water?

**Solution** (a) By Eq. (27.8), $V/V_0 = 1.2/11.8 = 0.10$, so about 90% of the osmotically active water is lost. Freezing injury is therefore in many respects the same as drought injury. (b) $V/V_0 = 2.4/11.8 = 0.20$, so 80% is lost; to retain only 10% requires $\Psi_{\mathrm{ice}} = -24\,\mathrm{MPa}$, which from panel A of the figure below is about $-21\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$. Doubling the solute potential does not prevent dehydration, but postpones the same degree of dehydration to a much lower temperature, which is one meaning of the accumulation of solutes such as sugars in acclimation.

B: Osmotically active water retained by a cell at equilibrium after extracellular freezing (Eq. 27.8); the three curves correspond to solute potentials before freezing of −1.0, −1.5 and −2.5 MPa (the last corresponding to a cell that has been cold-acclimated and accumulated solutes such as sugars); the dotted line marks −10 °C, and the vertical line marks the temperature of homogeneous nucleation (about −40 °C), at which deeply supercooled tissue freezes spontaneously](./plots/freezing.py)

Injury from extracellular ice comes mainly from dehydration and mechanical stress on the membrane: the cell loses water and shrinks, and the plasma membrane, stiffened at low temperature, detaches from the cell wall and is damaged; in severe dehydration the lipids of adjacent membranes can change from bilayer to hexagonal phase and fail to recover on rewatering. In freezing-resistant cells the plasma membrane invaginates on freezing, enlarging the area for water efflux and increasing water permeability so that water can be expelled in time and intracellular freezing is avoided; cells that are already dehydrated (seeds and pollen) are not easily affected by further extracellular freezing. Plants such as Chinese cabbage and onion survive thawing after intercellular freezing.

Another way to avoid freezing injury is **supercooling**: tissue fluid remains unfrozen below the freezing point. In many tree species of the hardwood forests of southeastern North America, the xylem ray parenchyma cells can supercool deeply; but liquid water undergoes homogeneous nucleation and freezes spontaneously at about $-40\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$, which sets a low-temperature limit of survival for alpine and subarctic tree species that rely on deep supercooling and may also be why the tree line of many mountains lies near the $-40\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ minimum isotherm.

### 27.6.3. Cold Acclimation and Antifreeze Proteins

**Definition 27.6.3 (Cold Acclimation)** The process in which temperate plants, as day length shortens and temperature falls in autumn, undergo a series of physiological and biochemical changes adapting them to low temperature and their freezing resistance gradually increases is called **cold acclimation**.

Changes in cold acclimation include: reduced tissue water content and a higher proportion of bound water; slower metabolism, cessation of growth and entry into dormancy; increased ABA; increased protective substances such as soluble sugars and lipids, which lower the freezing point and buffer excessive dehydration of the protoplasm; increased unsaturation of membrane lipids; and increased expression of freezing-resistance genes and synthesis of antifreeze proteins. Short days and low temperature are the two main environmental signals that induce dormancy and acclimation, and strong light shone on street trees at night in cities extends the day length perceived by the trees and may interfere with their entering dormancy and completing acclimation. The effect of acclimation is large: after one week of acclimation at 4 °C, the LT50 (lethal temperature for half the plants) of *Arabidopsis* falls from about $-5\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ to below about $-10\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$; winter rye can tolerate $-30\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$; and the twigs of some fully acclimated willows and birches can even survive in liquid nitrogen.

At the molecular level, low temperature induces *CBF1*–*CBF3* (that is, *DREB1A*–*DREB1C*) within 15 min; their products bind the CRT/DRE elements in the promoters of *COR* (cold-regulated) genes and activate a CBF regulon of hundreds of genes, including genes encoding LEA proteins, dehydrins and enzymes for the synthesis of compatible solutes (*COR15A*, *RD29A*, *KIN1* and others). *CBF* itself is regulated by transcription factors such as ICE1 and is modulated by the circadian clock and light quality (low R:FR). Constitutive overexpression of *CBF* can give unacclimated *Arabidopsis* considerable freezing tolerance, but the plants are dwarfed and flower late, showing that acclimation also has a cost. Cold acclimation and vernalization ([7.13.22](/7.13.22)) both require low temperature, but the former is a reversible acclimation over days to weeks and the latter is an epigenetic memory of the capacity to flower, and the mechanisms differ.

**Definition 27.6.4 (Antifreeze Protein)** A protein that binds to the surface of ice crystals, prevents or slows the growth and recrystallization of ice crystals, and thereby reduces freeze–thaw injury is called an **antifreeze protein**.

Antifreeze proteins lower the freezing point of a solution below the melting point (thermal hysteresis), by a mechanism different from the colligative lowering of the freezing point by solutes. The thermal hysteresis of plant antifreeze proteins is very small (usually below $1\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$), far less than in polar fishes and insects, and their main role in plants is to inhibit ice recrystallization, that is, to prevent small ice crystals from merging into large ones in freeze–thaw cycles, and to protect biological membranes such as thylakoid membranes. The antifreeze proteins secreted into the apoplast by winter rye at low temperature are homologous to pathogenesis-related proteins (chitinase, β-1,3-glucanase, thaumatin-like protein) and have both antifreeze and antifungal activity ([7.13.25](/7.13.25#sec-25.4.5)), which may be related to the susceptibility of overwintering crops to low-temperature fungi under snow. Sugars, polysaccharides, osmoprotectants and dehydrins also have cryoprotective effects.

### 27.6.4. Heat Injury

**Definition 27.6.5 (Heat Injury)** The injury caused to plants by high temperature is called **heat injury**.

Heat injury is divided into direct and indirect injury. Direct injury occurs quickly: high temperature makes membrane fluidity too high and liquefies membrane lipids, denatures and aggregates membrane proteins and soluble proteins, and changes the secondary structure of RNA and DNA. Indirect injury occurs slowly: at high temperature respiration increases while photosynthesis declines, and beyond the temperature compensation point the plant consumes its reserves and "starves"; synthesis of nitrogen-containing compounds is inhibited and protein degradation intensifies, and ammonia accumulates and poisons the cells. Photosynthesis is especially sensitive to high temperature: Rubisco activase begins to be inactivated above about $35\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$, so that Rubisco cannot maintain its activity; the oxygen-evolving complex of photosystem II is also susceptible to heat damage. The reproductive stage is the most sensitive: when many crops meet temperatures above 35 °C at flowering, pollen development and germination are blocked and seed set falls markedly.

The heat tolerance of plants differs with organ and developmental stage: mature leaves are more heat-tolerant than young leaves, and young leaves more than senescent leaves; dormant seeds are the most heat-tolerant and tolerance declines gradually after imbibition; the riper a fruit, the more heat-tolerant. Cell sap concentration correlates positively with heat tolerance and water content negatively; but succulents with high water content are very heat-tolerant, because their cytoplasm is viscous and has much bound water.

Plants cope with high temperature by: cooling by transpiration (which fails when drought and heat occur together, Section 27.1.4); adjusting leaf angle to reduce light interception, and dense trichomes and a well-developed waxy cuticle reflecting sunlight (though trichomes also reduce the light needed for photosynthesis in cool seasons); leaves on the outside of the canopy being more deeply lobed, with a thinner boundary layer and better heat dissipation; and synthesizing **heat shock proteins**. After pretreatment at a nonlethal high temperature, plants can tolerate temperatures that would otherwise be lethal (acquired thermotolerance), which depends on the heat shock proteins induced by heat shock transcription factors, especially HSP101, which disaggregates protein aggregates; during heat acclimation some of the hydrophilic bonds of proteins break and re-form into more stable bonds, which is also considered a reason for the increase in heat tolerance.

### 27.6.5. Strong Light and Ultraviolet Radiation

When a plant grown in shade with large light-harvesting antennae is suddenly moved into full sunlight, the light energy absorbed exceeds the capacity of photosynthesis to use it, electrons are passed to $\ce{O2}$ to produce reactive oxygen species, and photoinhibition results; the mechanisms of photoprotection (nonphotochemical quenching, the xanthophyll cycle, repair of photosystem II) are in [7.13.7](/7.13.7). Ozone entering the leaf through open stomata is converted to various reactive oxygen species, causing membrane lipid peroxidation and oxidation of proteins and nucleic acids and forming spots of programmed cell death (chlorosis and necrosis) on the leaf; reduction of stratospheric ozone increases the UV-B reaching the ground, which besides affecting photosynthesis also induces reactive oxygen species and DNA damage; plants sense UV-B through UVR8 and induce UV-screening substances such as flavonoids ([7.13.16](/7.13.16#sec-16.7.4)).

## 27.7. Heavy Metals and Toxic Ions

### 27.7.1. Mechanisms of Toxicity

Cadmium, lead, mercury, arsenic (a metalloid), and excess of essential elements such as copper, zinc and nickel are all toxic to plants; in acid soils with pH below about 5, dissolved $\ce{Al^3+}$ is the primary factor limiting root growth, inhibiting elongation of the root tip within minutes to hours. There are three main reasons for the toxicity of heavy metals: they mimic essential elements and replace them, for example $\ce{Cd^2+}$ can replace $\ce{Mg^2+}$ in chlorophyll or $\ce{Ca^2+}$ in calmodulin, disrupting photosynthesis and signal transduction at the same time, and they also enter cells through the channels and transporters that carry essential elements (arsenate enters root cells through phosphate transporters and arsenite through aquaporin-type transporters); they bind the sulfhydryl groups of proteins and inhibit enzyme activity; and they produce reactive oxygen species directly (Fenton reaction) or indirectly, causing programmed cell death.

### 27.7.2. Exclusion and Internal Tolerance

Plants tolerate toxic ions by two basic mechanisms. Exclusion prevents ions from entering cells so that their concentration does not reach the toxic threshold, as in the reduced arsenate uptake of Yorkshire fog in Section 27.1.2; aluminum-tolerant wheat cultivars secrete malate from the root tip through the aluminum-activated malate transporter ALMT1 in the plasma membrane and chelate $\ce{Al^3+}$ outside the root. Internal tolerance is tolerating, compartmenting or chelating ions that have already entered the cell.

Chelation is the binding of an ion to at least two coordinating atoms of one molecule; different coordinating atoms such as sulfur, nitrogen and oxygen have different affinities for different ions. Important chelators synthesized by plants include:

**Definition 27.7.1 (Phytochelatin)** Low-molecular-weight sulfhydryl-containing peptides synthesized from glutathione by phytochelatin synthase, with the general formula $(\gamma\text{-Glu-Cys})_n\text{-Gly}$ ($n = 2$–$11$), are called **phytochelatins**; they bind heavy metal ions such as cadmium and arsenic through the sulfhydryl groups of cysteine.

Phytochelatin synthase is activated by heavy metal ions, so phytochelatins are synthesized rapidly under heavy metal stress, and the metal–phytochelatin complexes formed are transported into the vacuole for storage by ABC transporters in the tonoplast. *Arabidopsis* mutants that cannot synthesize phytochelatins are extremely sensitive to cadmium and arsenic, showing that they are required for tolerance of cadmium and arsenic. Unlike phytochelatins, metallothioneins are small cysteine-rich proteins directly encoded by genes, mainly involved in the homeostasis of copper and zinc. In addition, nicotianamine and histidine act as chelators in the long-distance transport of metals, and active transport of metal ions into the vacuole and out of the cell is also part of internal tolerance.

### 27.7.3. Hyperaccumulators

**Definition 27.7.2 (Hyperaccumulator)** A plant whose shoots can accumulate a trace element at concentrations far higher than in ordinary plants, sufficient to be lethal to most plants, without injury is called a **hyperaccumulator**; commonly used criteria are more than $1000\,\mathrm{mg\,kg^{-1}}$ of nickel or arsenic, more than $3000\,\mathrm{mg\,kg^{-1}}$ of zinc, and more than $100\,\mathrm{mg\,kg^{-1}}$ of cadmium or selenium in leaf dry weight.

About 700 hyperaccumulator species are known, most accumulating nickel and growing on metal-rich soils such as serpentine; in some, the metal in leaves can reach 1% of dry weight or more. Examples are *Noccaea caerulescens* (Brassicaceae), which hyperaccumulates zinc and cadmium, *Arabidopsis halleri*, which hyperaccumulates zinc and cadmium, the Chinese brake fern *Pteris vittata* (Pteridaceae), which hyperaccumulates arsenic, and many species of *Alyssum*, which hyperaccumulate nickel. Hyperaccumulation requires genetic changes that greatly raise the expression of transporters involved in uptake, xylem loading and vacuolar compartmentation: in *A. halleri* the gene for HMA4, the P-type ATPase that loads zinc and cadmium into the xylem, has three copies and is expressed far more strongly than in *Arabidopsis thaliana*, and knocking it down abolishes the hyperaccumulation. The adaptive significance of hyperaccumulation may include defense against herbivores and pathogens (the "elemental defense" hypothesis).

Hyperaccumulators can be used to extract heavy metals from contaminated soil (phytoextraction, a kind of phytoremediation), and even to "farm" metal from nickel-rich soil (phytomining).

**Example 27.7.1** The plow layer of a farm field (depth 20 cm, bulk density $1.3\,\mathrm{t\,m^{-3}}$) has a cadmium content of $5\,\mathrm{mg\,kg^{-1}}$, to be reduced to $1\,\mathrm{mg\,kg^{-1}}$. (a) A cadmium hyperaccumulator is grown, with shoot cadmium content $500\,\mathrm{mg\,kg^{-1}}$ dry weight and annual harvest of dry matter $5\,\mathrm{t\,hm^{-2}}$; how many years are needed? (b) What if an ordinary crop with shoot cadmium content $1\,\mathrm{mg\,kg^{-1}}$ and annual harvest $10\,\mathrm{t\,hm^{-2}}$ is grown instead? (Ignore leaching and atmospheric deposition, and assume that the cadmium content of the plants does not change as soil cadmium falls.)

**Solution** The mass of the plow layer per hectare is $10^4\,\mathrm{m^2} \times 0.2\,\mathrm{m} \times 1.3\,\mathrm{t\,m^{-3}} = 2600\,\mathrm{t}$, and the cadmium to be removed is $2600 \times 10^3\,\mathrm{kg} \times 4\times10^{-6} = 10.4\,\mathrm{kg\,hm^{-2}}$. (a) Annual removal is $5000\,\mathrm{kg} \times 500\times10^{-6} = 2.5\,\mathrm{kg}$, so about $10.4/2.5 \approx 4$ years are needed. (b) Annual removal is $10\,000 \times 1\times10^{-6} = 0.01\,\mathrm{kg}$, so about 1000 years are needed. In reality the uptake by plants also falls as soil cadmium decreases, and the time needed is longer; but this estimate shows why only hyperaccumulators can be used for phytoextraction.

## 27.8. Approaches to Improving Stress Resistance

- Hardening: let plants experience moderate stress at the seedling stage or before the stress arrives, inducing acclimation. Seedling hardening (moderate water control at the seedling stage) raises drought resistance, cold hardening raises cold resistance, and heat hardening raises heat resistance; cross-tolerance (Definition 27.3.2) means that one kind of hardening can often raise resistance to several adversities. Seed priming (imbibing seeds at a controlled water potential before sowing and then drying them back) makes seed germination faster and more uniform and also raises the stress resistance of seedlings.
- Chemical regulation: exogenous ABA, jasmonate, brassinosteroids, salicylic acid and others raise stress resistance; growth retardants (paclobutrazol, chlormequat and others) inhibit gibberellin synthesis, making plants short and sturdy with a larger root-to-shoot ratio and raising drought resistance and lodging resistance ([7.13.15](/7.13.15#sec-15.8)).
- Rational fertilization: phosphorus promotes the synthesis of organic phosphorus compounds, raises the degree of hydration of protoplasm and increases cold resistance; potassium improves sugar metabolism, raises the osmotic concentration of cells and maintains the turgor of guard cells; calcium stabilizes membrane structure; excess nitrogen makes tissue tender and lowers stress resistance.
- Breeding stress-resistant cultivars: using QTL mapping and marker-assisted selection to introduce stress-resistance loci into high-yielding cultivars, with successful examples such as rice *SUB1* (submergence tolerance) and *Saltol* (seedling salt tolerance) and wheat *Nax2* ($\ce{Na+}$ exclusion) and loci for aluminum and boron tolerance; transgenesis and gene editing, such as expressing *DREB1A* from a stress-inducible promoter (rather than a constitutive one) to avoid dwarfing, drought-tolerant maize carrying a bacterial cold shock protein gene and drought-tolerant wheat carrying the sunflower transcription factor *HaHB4*, which have been approved for planting ([7.13.29](/7.13.29)). Many genes that are effective in the laboratory show no effect in the field, and combined stresses in the field and the trade-off with yield are the main difficulties of this research.

## Questions

1. Why are freezing injury, drought injury and salt injury said to "arrive at the same place by different roads" physiologically? What injury does each have that differs from the other two?

   **Answer** What the three have in common is that they lower the water potential environment of the cell and dehydrate it: in drought the soil water potential falls, in salinity the solute potential of the soil solution falls, and with extracellular freezing the water potential of ice is extremely low (Eq. 27.7); in every case water is "pulled" out of the cell, so turgor falls, growth stops, solutes are concentrated, membranes and proteins are destabilized and reactive oxygen species accumulate, and plants respond with similar mechanisms: osmotic adjustment, compatible solutes, LEA and dehydrins and ABA signaling, with cross-tolerance among them. The differences: freezing injury also involves mechanical damage by ice crystals, the lethal risk of intracellular freezing, and recrystallization in freeze–thaw cycles, hence the need for antifreeze proteins, supercooling and water-draining structures of the plasma membrane; salt injury also involves ion toxicity of $\ce{Na+}$ and $\ce{Cl-}$ and nutrient imbalance ($\ce{K+}$, $\ce{Ca^2+}$), hence the need for transport systems such as SOS, HKT and NHX; drought injury also involves failure of whole-plant water transport (xylem embolism) and carbon starvation caused by prolonged stomatal closure.

2. (Calculation) A halophyte grows on soil with $\mathrm{EC_e} = 30\,\mathrm{dS\,m^{-1}}$. (a) Estimate the solute potential of the soil solution. (b) If mesophyll cells are to maintain a turgor of 0.5 MPa and be 0.3 MPa lower in water potential than the soil solution to ensure water uptake (ignoring the pressure gradient in the xylem), what must the solute potential of the cells be at least? What osmotic concentration does this correspond to (25 °C)? (c) If the vacuole makes up 90% of cell water and cytosol and vacuole have equal solute potential, why can the solute in the cytosol not also be NaCl?

   **Answer** (a) By Eq. (27.5), $\Psi_{\mathrm{s,soil}} \approx -0.036 \times 30 = -1.08\,\mathrm{MPa}$. (b) The leaf water potential must be at least $-1.08 - 0.3 = -1.38\,\mathrm{MPa}$ and the solute potential $\Psi_{\mathrm{s}} = \Psi_{\mathrm{w}} - \Psi_{\mathrm{p}} = -1.38 - 0.5 = -1.88\,\mathrm{MPa}$; the osmotic concentration is $c_{\mathrm{s}} = 1.88\times10^6/2478 = 759\,\mathrm{osmol\,m^{-3}}$, about $0.76\,\mathrm{osmol\,L^{-1}}$. (c) In the vacuole $\ce{Na+}$ and $\ce{Cl-}$ can supply most of the osmotic concentration (about 0.4 M NaCl would be needed if it were all supplied by NaCl), but the enzymes and organelles of the cytosol are inhibited when $\ce{Na+}$ and $\ce{Cl-}$ exceed about 100 mM, so the roughly 0.76 osmol/L of solute in the cytosol must be supplied mainly by compatible solutes (glycine betaine, proline, sugar alcohols and so on), while maintaining about 100 mM $\ce{K+}$. Because the cytosol is only about 10% of cell water, the cost of synthesizing these organic solutes is much smaller than accumulating them in the whole cell, which is the economics of the division of labor "ions in the vacuole, compatible solutes in the cytosol".

3. (Calculation) The mesophyll cells of an overwintering plant have a solute potential of $-1.0\,\mathrm{MPa}$ before acclimation and $-2.0\,\mathrm{MPa}$ after. If cells die when they lose more than 85% of their osmotically active water, estimate the lowest temperature tolerated before and after acclimation. (Use the linear approximation $\Psi_{\mathrm{ice}} \approx -1.2\,\mathrm{MPa}$ per °C.) From this result, what changes besides solute accumulation are needed in cold acclimation?

   **Answer** At death $V/V_0 = 0.15$, and by Eq. (27.8) $\Psi_{\mathrm{ice}} = \Psi_{\mathrm{s0}}/0.15$. Before acclimation: $\Psi_{\mathrm{ice}} = -6.7\,\mathrm{MPa}$, corresponding to about $-6.7/1.2 = -5.6\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$; after acclimation: $\Psi_{\mathrm{ice}} = -13.3\,\mathrm{MPa}$, about $-11\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$. Doubling the solute potential lowers the tolerated temperature by only about 5.6 °C, far from explaining the ability of winter rye to tolerate $-30\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$. Acclimation must therefore also raise the degree of dehydration the cell can tolerate (the lethal fraction of water loss itself rises): greater unsaturation of membrane lipids and membrane stability (avoiding the hexagonal-phase transition caused by dehydration), molecular shielding by LEA proteins and dehydrins, vitrification of sugars and membrane protection, inhibition of recrystallization by antifreeze proteins, and structural changes of the plasma membrane and cell wall that let water drain smoothly and avoid intracellular freezing.

4. Compare the molecular mechanisms and ecological conditions of the *SUB1A* and *SNORKEL* flooding strategies. What would happen if *SUB1A* were introduced into deepwater rice?

   **Answer** Both are started by ethylene accumulating during submergence and both act through ERF-type transcription factors, but they act on gibberellin in opposite directions: *SUB1A* causes DELLA proteins to accumulate and represses gibberellin action, so the plant stops elongating and saves carbohydrate, which suits flash floods that recede after one or two weeks; *SNORKEL1/2* enhance gibberellin action and make the internodes elongate quickly, so that the leaves emerge above the water and photosynthesis and aeration are restored, which suits slowly rising deep water that lasts for months. If *SUB1A* were introduced into deepwater rice, it is expected to repress gibberellin-promoted elongation during submergence and to cancel the action of *SNORKEL*, so the plant could not elongate as the water level rises and would remain submerged in deep water and might finally die of carbohydrate exhaustion or oxygen deficiency; this shows that the value of a stress-resistance trait depends on the target environment, and that breeding for flood tolerance must first establish the type of local flood (depth, duration, rate of rise).

5. (Calculation) Use Eq. (27.4) to show: (a) By what factor does the maximum aerable root length increase when root porosity increases from 5% to 20%? (b) If formation of a radial oxygen loss barrier reduces the effective oxygen consumption $Q$ to 1/3 of its original value, what increase in porosity is it equivalent to? (c) Why do wetland plants form aerenchyma continuously behind the root tip rather than only at the root base?

   **Answer** (a) $L_{\max} \propto \sqrt{\varphi}$, and with porosity increased 4-fold, $L_{\max}$ increases by $\sqrt4 = 2$-fold. (b) $L_{\max} \propto 1/\sqrt{Q}$, and reducing $Q$ to 1/3 increases $L_{\max}$ by $\sqrt3 = 1.73$-fold, equivalent to a 3-fold increase in porosity. The oxygen loss barrier is therefore as important as aerenchyma, and rice has both. (c) Eq. (27.4) assumes gas spaces continuous along the whole root length; if some segment has no gas spaces, the $\ce{O2}$ in that segment can only diffuse in aqueous solution, with a diffusion coefficient about ten thousand times smaller, and can hardly supply oxygen to the part downstream. The root is continually extending, and only by forming new aerenchyma behind the root tip at all times can the gas-phase path extend to the region close to the tip, allowing the root to penetrate anoxic soil.
