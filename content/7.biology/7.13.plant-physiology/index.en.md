**Plant physiology** studies how plants stay alive. Plants have no heart, no lungs and no nervous system, and they cannot move, yet they too must acquire and transport materials, convert energy, perceive their environment and integrate their responses to it. The course is organized around a few questions. How do water and minerals enter the root from the soil, and how are they carried to leaves tens of meters above the ground? How is light energy converted into chemical energy, and how is the fixed carbon distributed among leaves, roots, stems, fruits and seeds? How do plants sense light, gravity, temperature, day length and other organisms, and how do they integrate this information through hormones and other signals to decide when to germinate, grow, flower and senesce? And how do plants respond to drought, salinity, cold, pathogens and herbivores, and at what cost? A basic feature of the course is that it is quantitative. Water transport, gas exchange, phloem transport and ion uptake can all be written in the form flux = conductance × driving force; enzymes and carriers obey saturation kinetics, hormone effects follow dose–response curves, and the decisions to grow, flower or remain dormant can be written as the accumulation of a quantity up to a threshold and as switches. Wherever possible, each chapter gives the equations, typical values of the measured parameters and numerical calculations.

The course follows the sequence from the acquisition and conversion of resources to signaling and development, then to plants and their environment, and finally to agriculture. After the introduction, the first five chapters start from the water relations of cells and **water potential**, pass through water uptake by the whole plant, xylem transport and **transpiration**, and reach mineral nutrition, membrane transport of solutes and the assimilation of nitrogen, sulfur, phosphorus and iron. The next six chapters deal with carbon: light absorption and photoprotection, carbon assimilation in C3, C4 and CAM plants, the physiological ecology of photosynthesis in leaves and canopies, the **stoma** as the hub of water and carbon exchange, transport in the **phloem** and the partitioning of assimilates between sources and sinks, and respiration. The third part is about signals: three chapters on **plant hormones** treat auxin, then gibberellins and cytokinins, then abscisic acid, ethylene and the other hormones, followed by photoreceptors and **photomorphogenesis**. The fourth part follows development through the life of the plant: cell expansion and growth kinetics, the formation of vegetative organs, tropisms, seed dormancy and germination, **photoperiodism** and **florigen**, juvenility and **vernalization**, pollination, fertilization and the development of seeds and fruits, and finally senescence, abscission and programmed cell death. The fifth part treats plants and their environment: interactions with pathogenic microbes and plant immunity, relations with herbivores, mutualists and other plants, and abiotic stress. Crop domestication and breeding then apply this physiology to yield and quality. The course summary introduces no new material; it reorganizes the whole course into a system through a unified quantitative framework, the coupling of water and carbon at the stoma, whole-plant budgets of carbon, water and nitrogen, the network of signals and development, and the trade-offs that arise under stress.

Plant physiology lies at the crossroads of many courses. The biochemistry of the light reactions, the Calvin cycle, respiration and nitrogen metabolism comes from [biochemistry](/7.1); membrane transport, the cell wall and cell death come from [cell biology](/7.3); water potential, chemical potential, the photochemistry of photoreceptors and the physics of switches and oscillators come from [biophysics](/7.6). The epigenetic memory of vernalization, heterosis and male sterility connect with [genetics](/7.4) and [molecular biology](/7.2), and auxin patterning and meristems connect with [developmental biology](/7.5). The microbial partners in root nodules and mycorrhizae are treated in [microbiology](/7.11), and the coevolution of plants with pathogens and herbivores and the domestication of crops in [evolutionary biology](/7.10); the morphology and anatomy of leaves, roots, stomata and vascular tissues come from [botany](/7.12), and this course explains the mechanisms behind those structures. In turn, primary production, water use, light-use efficiency, the relations of plants with other organisms and the effects of climate change on plants are the foundation on which [ecology](/7.19) discusses ecosystems. The problems of growth, oscillation and diffusion in the course draw on [ordinary differential equations](/2.4) and [partial differential equations](/2.5).

This course left a deep impression on me. I have often felt that plant physiology is a good test of a student's level in the biology olympiad, because it is so integrative. It requires some understanding of both microscopic mechanisms and quantitative calculation, it requires knowledge of morphology, anatomy and taxonomy as background, and above all it requires a strong enough sense of systems to understand the processes that run simultaneously in roots, stems and leaves, and constrain one another, within a single whole plant. For the same reason, I did not stress quantitative treatment in botany but have made plant physiology as quantitative as possible: knowledge of form and classification is mainly description and comparison, whereas physiological processes can mostly be written as equations with numbers substituted in, and a quantitative result is often a better test of whether one's understanding is correct than a qualitative account.

As for the books, the one I mainly used during my olympiad years was the eighth edition of *Plant Physiology* by Xiaojing Wang, which is the most detailed and the most up to date; I used the fourth edition of *Modern Plant Physiology* by Hesheng Li to check some of the vaguer statements. Among English textbooks, *Plant Physiology and Development* by Taiz and coauthors is the undisputed authority, and for the microscopic molecular and biochemical details, *Biochemistry & Molecular Biology of Plants* by Buchanan and coauthors serves well as a reference book.
