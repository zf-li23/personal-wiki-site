Gibberellins and cytokinins are two classes of growth-promoting hormones discovered after auxin, but their modes of action differ greatly from that of auxin. The most conspicuous effect of gibberellin is to make whole plants taller, above all restoring normal height to genetically dwarf plants, while it has almost no effect on isolated stem segments; it also controls seed germination, bolting and flowering, and fruit growth. Reduced gibberellin synthesis, or insensitivity to it, is the genetic basis of the semidwarf wheat and rice of the twentieth-century "Green Revolution". Cytokinin was discovered in the search for factors that promote cell division in tissue culture; its ratio to auxin determines whether an isolated tissue forms roots or shoots, and in the intact plant it controls bud outgrowth, the size of meristems and leaf senescence.

This chapter treats the discovery and structure, biosynthesis and metabolism, and transport of the two classes, and those parts of their physiological effects that can be described quantitatively: the dose-response of dwarf mutants to gibberellin, a simplified model of DELLA degradation, induction of aleurone α-amylase, and the Skoog–Miller hormone ratio. The gibberellin receptor GID1 and the degradation of DELLA, and the His–Asp phosphorelay of cytokinin, were treated in [7.1.15](/7.1.15#sec-15.5.4) and are used here only in discussing physiological effects. General concepts of hormones and auxin are in [7.13.13](/7.13.13); the roles of gibberellin in seed germination and flowering are developed in [7.13.20](/7.13.20) and [7.13.22](/7.13.22); the roles of cytokinin in meristems and apical dominance are in [7.13.18](/7.13.18) and in leaf senescence in [7.13.24](/7.13.24).

## 14.1. Discovery and Structure of Gibberellins

### 14.1.1. Bakanae Disease and the Discovery of Gibberellin

Bakanae ("foolish seedling") disease of rice had long been recorded in Japan: diseased plants are overgrown, spindly and yellowish, lodge easily, and set little or no grain. In 1926 Eiichi Kurosawa found that the sterile filtrate of a culture of the pathogenic fungus (then called *Gibberella fujikuroi*, now *Fusarium fujikuroi*) alone could make healthy rice seedlings overgrow, showing that the fungus secretes some growth-promoting substance. In 1935 Teijiro Yabuta isolated the active substance and named it gibberellin after the genus name of the fungus; in 1938 Yabuta and Yusuke Sumiki obtained crystals.

Because of the Second World War this work was not appreciated in the West until the 1950s. In 1954 research groups in Britain and the United States isolated gibberellic acid (GA₃) from fungal culture fluid, and it is still the main gibberellin used in production. It was soon found that gibberellin restores normal height to genetically dwarf maize and pea and makes rosette plants bolt, which suggested that higher plants themselves contain similar substances. In 1958 MacMillan and Suter identified GA₁, the first gibberellin of higher plants, in immature seeds of runner bean (*Phaseolus coccineus*), and gibberellin was thereby confirmed as a plant hormone.

### 14.1.2. Structure and Nomenclature

**Definition 14.1.1 (Gibberellin)** Tetracyclic diterpenoid carboxylic acids with an *ent*-gibberellane skeleton built from four isoprene units are collectively called **gibberellins**; they are numbered in order of discovery as GA₁, GA₂, GA₃, and so on.

More than 130 gibberellins are known, but only a few are intrinsically bioactive, mainly GA₁, GA₃, GA₄ and GA₇; most plants use GA₁ and/or GA₄ as the active forms that act as hormones, and the others are their precursors or inactivation products. The numbers reflect only the order of discovery: gibberellins with adjacent numbers are not necessarily related chemically or metabolically.

Several structural features determine activity:

- Number of carbon atoms: C20-GAs retain all 20 carbons (for example GA₁₂, GA₅₃); in C19-GAs C-20 has been lost and a lactone bridge forms between C-4 and C-10 (for example GA₉, GA₂₀, GA₁, GA₄). All active gibberellins are C19-GAs, and C20-GAs are generally inactive.
- The 3β-hydroxyl at C-3 is required for activity (GA₂₀ → GA₁ and GA₉ → GA₄ are 3β-hydroxylations).
- A 2β-hydroxyl at C-2 inactivates the gibberellin (GA₁ → GA₈, GA₄ → GA₃₄).
- Whether C-13 is hydroxylated divides synthesis into two parallel branches: the 13-hydroxylation pathway yields GA₁ (the main pathway in most plants), and the non-13-hydroxylation pathway yields GA₄ (in *Arabidopsis* and others).
- All gibberellins have a carboxyl group at C-7 and are therefore acidic.

## 14.2. Gibberellin Biosynthesis, Inactivation and Transport

### 14.2.1. Three Steps in Three Compartments

Gibberellin synthesis proceeds in three stages, successively in plastids, the endoplasmic reticulum and the cytosol:

1. Plastid: in green tissues, isopentenyl diphosphate (IPP) is made from glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate and pyruvate by the methylerythritol phosphate (MEP) pathway, and four C5 units condense to the C20 geranylgeranyl diphosphate (GGPP); in some nongreen tissues, such as pumpkin endosperm, IPP can also come from the cytosolic **mevalonate** pathway. GGPP is cyclized by *ent*-copalyl diphosphate synthase (CPS) to the bicyclic *ent*-copalyl diphosphate, and then by *ent*-kaurene synthase (KS) to the tetracyclic *ent*-kaurene.
2. Endoplasmic reticulum: two **cytochrome P-450** monooxygenases oxidize in turn: *ent*-kaurene oxidase (KO) oxidizes the C-19 methyl group through the alcohol and aldehyde to the carboxylic acid (*ent*-kaurenoic acid), and *ent*-kaurenoic acid oxidase (KAO) oxidizes C-7 and contracts ring B, forming GA₁₂-aldehyde and GA₁₂. GA₁₂ is the earliest gibberellin formed in all plants and the common precursor of all gibberellins; GA 13-oxidase hydroxylates it to GA₅₃.
3. Cytosol: soluble 2-oxoglutarate-dependent dioxygenases (2-ODDs) carry out the subsequent reactions. GA 20-oxidase (GA20ox) oxidizes C-20 three times in succession and removes it as $\ce{CO2}$, forming the C19 gibberellins GA₉ and GA₂₀; GA 3-oxidase (GA3ox) 3β-hydroxylates at C-3, producing the active GA₄ and GA₁.

In the endoplasmic reticulum: two P450 monooxygenases, KO and KAO, oxidize it to GA₁₂ (the common precursor of all gibberellins), and GA13ox hydroxylates it to GA₅₃. ### 14.2.2. Inactivation and Feedback

The level of active gibberellin is set by the balance of synthesis and inactivation:

- 2β-Hydroxylation: GA 2-oxidase (GA2ox) irreversibly converts the active GA₁ and GA₄ and their immediate precursors GA₂₀ and GA₉ to the inactive GA₈, GA₃₄, GA₂₉ and GA₅₁; another class of GA2ox acts on C20 precursors. This is the most important inactivation pathway.
- Other means of inactivation: methylation (GA methyltransferase GAMT, in developing seeds), 16,17-epoxidation of non-13-hydroxylated gibberellins by rice EUI (a P450), and formation of glucosides or glucosyl esters (possibly also storage and transport forms).

Gibberellin acts back negatively on its own level: when active gibberellin increases, expression of *GA20ox* and *GA3ox* falls and that of *GA2ox* rises; GA-deficient mutants accumulate *GA20ox* and *GA3ox* transcripts. This feedback operates through the DELLA proteins (Section 14.3.1): DELLA promotes expression of *GA20ox*, *GA3ox* and the GA receptor *GID1*, and gibberellin causes DELLA to be degraded, so that expression of these genes declines. As a result, after exogenous gibberellin is applied endogenous synthesis falls, and the endogenous level is much smaller than "one plus one" would predict; conversely, signaling-defective *gai*-type mutants, in which DELLA is not degraded, have a higher level of endogenous active gibberellin than the wild type. This is one criterion for deciding whether a dwarf mutation is a "biosynthesis defect" or a "signaling defect".

### 14.2.3. Sites of Synthesis, Environmental Regulation and Transport

The most active sites of gibberellin synthesis are young buds and leaves, elongating upper internodes, and developing seeds and fruit (the content of immature seeds is often several orders of magnitude higher than in vegetative organs); in germinating seeds, gibberellin synthesized by the embryo (scutellum) starts mobilization of the endosperm reserves (Section 14.3.5). Mesophyll cells of mature leaves cannot synthesize *ent*-kaurene but can carry out the third stage, converting imported precursors to active gibberellin. The tissue of synthesis and the tissue of action are often different: in germinating *Arabidopsis* seeds, *GA3ox* is expressed in the cortex of the hypocotyl, whereas gibberellin acts on the radicle and the plumule.

The environment regulates gibberellin levels through the genes of the biosynthetic enzymes:

- Light: in light-requiring seeds, red light induces *GA3ox* expression through phytochrome and represses *GA2ox*, raising active gibberellin ([7.13.20](/7.13.20)); in seedlings that have emerged into light, hypocotyl elongation is inhibited through stabilization of DELLA and a fall in gibberellin.
- Day length: when long-day rosette plants such as spinach are moved to long days, *GA20ox* expression rises, GA₂₀ and GA₁ increase, and the stem begins to elongate (bolting).
- Temperature: chilling treatment (vernalization or stratification) can raise the gibberellin level or sensitivity of some plants.

Gibberellin is not transported polarly and moves in both xylem and phloem. Grafting experiments show that the form that moves over long distances in the plant is mainly the precursor GA₁₂, and active gibberellin is generated locally in the target tissue; gibberellin levels in the root are controlled by active transport. This differs sharply from the cell-to-cell polar transport of auxin, which depends on dedicated carriers.

### 14.2.4. Growth Retardants

Synthetic compounds that inhibit gibberellin synthesis are called plant growth retardants: they shorten internodes and make plants short and sturdy with dark green leaves, but generally do not affect the activity of the apical meristem or flower development, and thus differ from growth inhibitors (such as triiodobenzoic acid and maleic hydrazide), which inhibit the apical meristem and destroy apical dominance. According to the enzyme on which they act they fall into three classes:

| Site of action | Agent | Main uses |
|---|---|---|
| CPS (plastid, stage 1) | Chlormequat (CCC, chlorocholine chloride), mepiquat (Pix) | Prevent lodging of wheat; control rank growth of cotton |
| KO (endoplasmic reticulum, P450) | Paclobutrazol, uniconazole (triazoles), ancymidol | Sturdy seedlings of rice and oilseed rape; shoot control in fruit trees; dwarfing of potted flowers |
| 2-ODD (cytosol, GA3ox and others) | Prohexadione-calcium, trinexapac-ethyl, daminozide (B9) | Prevent lodging of cereals and turf; shoot control in apple |

Prohexadione-calcium and related compounds resemble 2-oxoglutarate and competitively inhibit the dioxygenases, and therefore also affect enzymes of the same class in flavonoid synthesis. The integrated agricultural use of growth retardants is discussed in [7.13.15](/7.13.15).

## 14.3. Physiological Effects of Gibberellins

### 14.3.1. Stem Elongation and Dwarf Mutants

Gibberellin makes whole plants, especially genetically dwarf plants, markedly taller. It promotes mainly the elongation of existing internodes (both cell division and cell elongation increase) without increasing the number of nodes; after treatment, stems become thinner, leaves smaller and leaf color paler. Unlike auxin, gibberellin has little effect on the elongation of isolated stem segments but a marked effect on intact plants, and there is no inhibition above an optimum over a very wide range of concentrations.

Dwarf mutants fall into two classes whose responses to exogenous gibberellin are quite different:

| Type | Examples | Defect | Response to exogenous GA | Endogenous active GA |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Biosynthesis-deficient | Maize *d1*, pea *le* (the short-stem trait studied by Mendel), pea *na*, *Arabidopsis ga1* | *d1*, *le*: GA 3-oxidase; *na*: KAO; *ga1*: CPS | Normal height restored | Low |
| Signaling-deficient (dominant) | *Arabidopsis gai*, wheat *Rht-B1b*/*Rht-D1b* | Loss of the DELLA domain at the N terminus of DELLA protein, which cannot bind GID1 and is not degraded | Insensitive | High (loss of feedback) |
| Signaling-deficient (recessive) | Rice *gid1*, *gid2* (F-box) | Loss of receptor or E3 ligase | Insensitive | High |
| Constitutive response (slender) | Rice *slr1*, pea *la cry*, barley *sln1* | Loss of DELLA function | Already a "saturated" phenotype | Low |

**Definition 14.3.1 (DELLA Protein)** A protein of the GRAS family of transcriptional regulators that contains a conserved Asp–Glu–Leu–Leu–Ala motif at the N terminus and represses gibberellin responses in the nucleus is called a **DELLA protein**; after gibberellin binds the receptor GID1, the DELLA protein is recognized and degraded by an SCF ubiquitin ligase.

DELLA itself does not bind DNA; it binds and inhibits growth-promoting transcription factors such as PIFs (phytochrome-interacting factors) and BZR1, or acts as a coregulator of other transcription factors. DELLA is therefore a node where signals of gibberellin and of light, brassinosteroid, jasmonate and ethylene meet ([7.13.15](/7.13.15)).

A simplified model shows the origin of the dose-response. Let the fraction of receptors occupied by gibberellin be

$$
\theta = \frac{[\mathrm{GA}]^{\,n}}{K_{\mathrm{d}}^{\,n} + [\mathrm{GA}]^{\,n}},
\tag{14.1}
$$

DELLA is synthesized at rate $s$ and degraded with the basal rate constant $k_0$ and the GA-induced rate constant $k_1\theta$, so its steady-state content is

$$
D^* = \frac{s}{k_0 + k_1\theta},
\tag{14.2}
$$

and the growth rate declines as DELLA increases:

$$
G = \frac{G_{\max}}{1 + D^*/K_D}.
\tag{14.3}
$$

When $k_1 \gg k_0$, raising GA from zero to saturation lowers $D^*$ by a factor $k_1/k_0$, and growth rises correspondingly from the dwarf level to the maximum. In the middle of the range of $\theta$, $G$ is approximately linear in $\log[\mathrm{GA}]$, which is exactly the "log-linear" dose-response observed in the bioassays with dwarf maize and dwarf pea, and can be written as

$$
L = a + b\log_{10} m \qquad (m_{\min} < m < m_{\max}),
\tag{14.4}
$$

where $L$ is the length of the leaf sheath or internode and $m$ is the amount of GA applied per plant. The model also predicts the three kinds of mutants: a biosynthesis-deficient mutant simply sits at the low end of the curve; in the *gai* type DELLA is not degraded ($k_1 \to 0$) and the plant is dwarf at any GA concentration; in the *slr1* type there is no DELLA ($s \to 0$) and the plant is tall at any concentration.

![Dose-response to gibberellin and derepression of DELLA (numerical calculation of Eqs. (14.1)–(14.3); parameters are illustrative). A: As the GA concentration increases, the fraction of GID1 occupied rises, the steady-state DELLA content falls, and the growth rate increases approximately linearly with log[GA] over 2–3 orders of magnitude. B: Growth curves of three genotypes: wild type (or biosynthesis-deficient type, which rises along the same curve when exogenous GA is added); the gai or Rht type, in which DELLA is not degraded (degradation rate constant $k_1 = 0$), always dwarf and insensitive to GA; and the slr1 or la cry type, lacking DELLA ($s = 0$), always showing the "saturated" slender phenotype](./plots/ga-dose-della.py)

The cellular basis of the promotion of elongation by gibberellin is that it makes the cell wall more extensible and promotes cell division in the intercalary meristem. Deepwater rice is an extreme example: when submerged, ethylene accumulates in the internodes, lowering ABA and raising the content of and sensitivity to gibberellin, and internodes can elongate by more than 20 cm per day, keeping the leaves above the water ([7.13.27](/7.13.27)).

**Example 14.3.1** In a bioassay with dwarf maize *d5*, the length of the first leaf sheath depends on the amount of GA₃ per plant as follows: $0.001\ \mu\mathrm{g}$, $31\ \mathrm{mm}$; $0.01\ \mu\mathrm{g}$, $44\ \mathrm{mm}$; $0.1\ \mu\mathrm{g}$, $58\ \mathrm{mm}$; $1\ \mu\mathrm{g}$, $70\ \mathrm{mm}$ (hypothetical data). A plant extract (equivalent to 2 g fresh weight) gives a sheath length of $50\ \mathrm{mm}$; estimate the GA₃-equivalent content of the extract.

**Solution** Regress $L$ on $\log_{10}m$ according to Eq. (14.4): $\log_{10}m = -3, -2, -1, 0$ with mean $-1.5$; the mean of $L$ is $50.75$; the slope is $b = [(-1.5)(-19.75) + (-0.5)(-6.75) + (0.5)(7.25) + (1.5)(19.25)]/5 = 65.5/5 = 13.1\ \mathrm{mm}$ (per 10-fold dose) and the intercept is $a = 50.75 + 1.5\times13.1 = 70.4\ \mathrm{mm}$. For $L = 50$, $\log_{10}m = (50 - 70.4)/13.1 = -1.56$ and $m = 0.028\ \mu\mathrm{g}$, that is, about $14\ \mathrm{ng}$ GA₃ equivalent per gram fresh weight. A bioassay gives only an "activity equivalent": the extract contains several gibberellins with different activities, and accurate quantitation requires mass spectrometry.

### 14.3.2. The Green Revolution Genes

From the 1960s, semidwarf wheat and rice cultivars more than doubled grain yield in many countries within two or three decades; this was the **Green Revolution** ([7.13.28](/7.13.28)). Tall cultivars grow taller still when heavily fertilized with nitrogen and lodge easily; semidwarf cultivars have short, sturdy stems, tolerate fertilizer and resist lodging, and partition more assimilate to the grain, so that the harvest index (grain dry weight as a fraction of total aboveground dry weight) rose from about 0.3 in traditional tall cultivars to about 0.5.

The dwarfing genes of the two crops lie, respectively, in gibberellin synthesis and in signaling:

- Wheat *Rht-B1b* and *Rht-D1b* (from the Japanese cultivar 'Norin 10', used by Norman Borlaug in breeding in Mexico) are mutations of DELLA genes encoding N-terminally truncated DELLA proteins that cannot be degraded in response to gibberellin; they belong to the dominant signaling-deficient type in the table of Section 14.3.1 and were cloned in 1999.
- Rice *sd1* (from the Taiwanese landrace 'Dee-geo-woo-gen', the source of dwarfism in IR8 and other cultivars bred at the International Rice Research Institute in 1966) is a loss-of-function mutation of *OsGA20ox2* and was cloned in 2002. This gene is expressed mainly in vegetative organs, and the isozymes in reproductive organs are unaffected, so the plant is shorter but sets grain normally.

**Example 14.3.2** A tall rice cultivar has an aboveground dry matter of $12\ \mathrm{t\,ha^{-1}}$ and a harvest index of 0.30; a semidwarf cultivar under heavier fertilization has $14\ \mathrm{t\,ha^{-1}}$ and a harvest index of 0.48. What is the grain yield of each? How much of the yield increase comes from the increase in harvest index?

**Solution** Tall: $12\times0.30 = 3.6\ \mathrm{t\,ha^{-1}}$; semidwarf: $14\times0.48 = 6.7\ \mathrm{t\,ha^{-1}}$, an increase of $3.1\ \mathrm{t\,ha^{-1}}$. If the harvest index were unchanged and only biomass increased, the yield would be $14\times0.30 = 4.2$; if only the harvest index increased, it would be $12\times0.48 = 5.8$. Of the $3.1\ \mathrm{t\,ha^{-1}}$ increase, about $2.2$ (about 70%) comes from the change in assimilate partitioning, and the rest from the increase in biomass made possible by tolerance of fertilizer and resistance to lodging; the tall cultivar would lodge under the same fertilization and in practice could not reach 14 t. (The numbers are illustrative.)

### 14.3.3. Juvenile and Adult Phases, Flowering and Sex

Exogenous gibberellin can change the developmental phase of woody plants: GA₃ makes adult shoots of ivy (*Hedera helix*) again produce juvenile palmately lobed leaves and lose the ability to flower, whereas a mixture of GA₄ and GA₇ can make many juvenile conifers form cones early. The two effects run in opposite directions, showing that gibberellin acts differently on phase change in different groups (phase change is treated in [7.13.22](/7.13.22)).

Many long-day rosette plants (cabbage, spinach, radish) remain rosettes in short days, and long days induce stem elongation (bolting) and flowering; exogenous gibberellin can replace long days to make them bolt and flower, and for some biennials that require cold, gibberellin can also replace chilling. In short-day and day-neutral plants, gibberellin generally cannot induce flowering. Flowering of *Arabidopsis* in short days requires gibberellin: the *ga1* mutant does not flower in short days (the gibberellin pathway, [7.13.22](/7.13.22)).

Gibberellin also affects the sex of unisexual flowers: in dicots such as cucumber, gibberellin promotes the formation of male flowers and gibberellin synthesis inhibitors promote female flowers (the opposite of the action of ethylene); in maize, gibberellin represses stamen development, stamens appear in the ears of gibberellin-deficient dwarf maize, and exogenous gibberellin feminizes the tassel.

### 14.3.4. Fruit Growth

Gibberellin promotes fruit set and growth in some fruits and induces parthenocarpy. The most important application is seedless grape: in 'Thompson Seedless' the seeds abort early and the berries are naturally small; spraying GA₃ at flowering and the young-fruit stage enlarges the berries, elongates the pedicels, and loosens the clusters, making them less susceptible to disease. Gibberellin is also used to induce parthenocarpy in pear and tomato, to promote fruit set in apple, and to delay peel senescence in citrus. Developing seeds are an important source of gibberellin in fruit, and gibberellin and auxin from the seeds together drive the growth of the ovary ([7.13.23](/7.13.23)).

### 14.3.5. Seed Germination and the Aleurone

Germination of many seeds requires gibberellin. In light-requiring seeds (lettuce, tobacco) and seeds that require cold stratification (plum, apple and others), gibberellin can replace light or chilling in breaking dormancy; seeds of the gibberellin-deficient *Arabidopsis ga1* cannot germinate without gibberellin. Gibberellin has two kinds of action in germination: it promotes the growth potential of the embryo (enabling the radicle to break through the surrounding tissues), and it induces hydrolases and mobilizes reserves.

The **aleurone layer** of cereal seeds is the classic system for studying the action of gibberellin. When a barley grain germinates, gibberellin synthesized by the embryo (scutellum and embryonic axis) diffuses to the aleurone layer at the periphery of the endosperm and induces aleurone cells to synthesize and secrete hydrolases such as α-amylase, proteases and nucleases, which break down starch and protein in the endosperm; the products are taken up by the scutellum to supply the growth of the seedling.

- Half-grains without embryos cannot break down starch, but can once gibberellin is added, showing that the role of the embryo can be replaced by gibberellin.
- The α-amylase in the aleurone is newly synthesized: when induction takes place in medium containing $\ce{H2^{18}O}$, the density of the enzyme protein increases (newly synthesized protein incorporates heavy oxygen), showing that gibberellin starts gene expression and does not activate preexisting enzyme.
- Molecular mechanism: through GID1, gibberellin causes DELLA (SLN1 in barley) to be degraded, expression of the transcription factor GAMYB rises, and GAMYB binds the gibberellin response element (GARE) in the promoter of the α-amylase genes; ABA inhibits GAMYB expression and, through VP1, these genes, and so antagonizes gibberellin ([7.13.15](/7.13.15), [7.13.20](/7.13.20)).

The response of isolated barley aleurone layers to GA₃ increases approximately linearly with the logarithm of concentration between about $10^{-10}$ and $10^{-7}\ \mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$ and saturates near $10^{-6}\ \mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$; this sensitive and specific response was formerly used as a bioassay for gibberellin.

![Induction of hydrolase secretion by the aleurone layer by gibberellin during germination of cereal seeds (left), and dose-response of isolated aleurone layers to GA₃ (right, Hill equation, $\mathrm{EC}_{50}$ about $3\ \mathrm{nmol\,L^{-1}}$, schematic). Left: after imbibition the scutellum and embryonic axis of the embryo synthesize gibberellin (1), gibberellin diffuses to the aleurone layer (2), aleurone cells synthesize and secrete α-amylase, proteases and others (3), which break down starch and storage protein in the starchy endosperm (4), and sugars and amino acids are taken up by the scutellum to supply embryo growth (5). Right: secreted α-amylase activity rises approximately linearly with log[GA₃] between $10^{-10}$ and $10^{-7}\ \mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$; when ABA is added at the same time the curve shifts to the right and the maximum falls](./plots/aleurone.py)

This process is exploited in brewing: adding a small amount of gibberellin during germination of barley for malting makes the aleurone layer produce amylase faster and more uniformly, shortens the germination time, reduces the dry matter consumed by respiration during germination, and raises the diastatic power of the malt.

### 14.3.6. Breaking Dormancy

Gibberellin can break the dormancy of buds and tubers. Potato tubers have a dormant period after harvest, and soaking seed tubers in $0.5$–$1\ \mu\mathrm{g\,g^{-1}}$ gibberellin (by fresh weight of tuber) breaks dormancy and makes them sprout quickly, which is used for two crops per year. Some woody plants require winter chilling to release bud dormancy, and gibberellin can partly replace the chilling.

## 14.4. Discovery and Kinds of Cytokinins

### 14.4.1. Discovery

In the first half of the twentieth century, factors that make cells divide continuously were sought in plant tissue culture. Coconut milk (the liquid endosperm of coconut) and yeast extract promoted cell division in cultured tissues; Skoog and colleagues found that tobacco pith tissue on a medium containing only auxin enlarged its cells without dividing, and that adding adenine could partly promote division.

In 1955 Miller, Skoog and colleagues discovered by chance that herring sperm DNA that had been stored for many years strongly promoted cell division in tobacco pith tissue when added to the medium, whereas fresh DNA had no effect, and fresh DNA became active after autoclaving under acidic conditions. They isolated the active substance from the autoclaved DNA degradation products and identified it as 6-furfurylaminopurine ($N^6$-furfuryladenine, $\ce{C10H9N5O}$, relative molecular mass 215.2), which they named kinetin.

**Definition 14.4.1 (Kinetin)** The adenine derivative 6-furfurylaminopurine, produced by the degradation of DNA, is called **kinetin** (KT): in the presence of auxin it promotes cell division in cultured tissue, and it is the earliest cytokinin-type substance to have been discovered, but not a natural plant hormone.

In 1963 Letham isolated the first natural cytokinin from immature maize kernels and named it zeatin.

**Definition 14.4.2 (Zeatin)** 6-(4-Hydroxy-3-methyl-*trans*-2-butenylamino)purine ($\ce{C10H13N5O}$, relative molecular mass 219.2) is called **zeatin**; *trans*-zeatin is the most important natural cytokinin in higher plants.

In 1965 Skoog and colleagues proposed to call compounds whose physiological activity is similar to that of kinetin collectively cytokinins.

**Definition 14.4.3 (Cytokinin)** Compounds that promote plant cell division in the presence of auxin and have the same biological activity as *trans*-zeatin (natural ones are all $N^6$-substituted adenine derivatives) are collectively called **cytokinins**.

### 14.4.2. Kinds

Natural cytokinins are divided into two classes according to the $N^6$ side chain:

- Isoprenoid side chains: isopentenyladenine (iP), *trans*-zeatin (tZ), *cis*-zeatin (cZ) and dihydrozeatin (DHZ). tZ and iP are the most active; cZ is less active but more abundant in grasses such as rice and maize.
- Aromatic side chains: 6-benzyladenine (BA, naturally present in some plants and also synthesized in large quantities) and its hydroxylated derivatives (topolins).

Synthetic cytokinins also include phenylureas that are not purine derivatives, such as thidiazuron (TDZ) and forchlorfenuron (CPPU), the latter used to promote fruit enlargement in kiwifruit and grape.

Only the free bases are active. They also exist as nucleosides (ribose attached to N-9 of the purine, for example zeatin riboside, the main transport form), nucleotides (ribose carrying a phosphate, the direct products of synthesis) and glucosides: N-7 or N-9 glucosides are irreversibly inactive, whereas O-glucosides on the side-chain hydroxyl can be hydrolyzed by $\beta$-glucosidase and are storage forms.

Classic bioassays for cytokinins include growth of tobacco pith or soybean callus, expansion of isolated radish or cucumber cotyledons, retention of chlorophyll in detached leaves, and induction of betacyanin in amaranth seedlings.

## 14.5. Cytokinin Biosynthesis, Metabolism and Transport

### 14.5.1. The IPT and tRNA Pathways

De novo synthesis of cytokinin begins with the rate-limiting step catalyzed by isopentenyltransferase (IPT): plant IPT transfers the isopentenyl group of dimethylallyl diphosphate (DMAPP, a C5 unit from the MEP pathway, homologous with the gibberellin precursors) to the $N^6$ of the adenine of ATP or ADP, forming isopentenyladenine riboside triphosphate or diphosphate (iPRTP, iPRDP). The cytochrome P-450 monooxygenase CYP735A hydroxylates the side chain, converting these to *trans*-zeatin-type nucleotides. Finally, phosphoribohydrolases of the LONELY GUY (LOG) family remove ribose-5′-phosphate in a single step and directly produce the active free base; local expression of LOG, for example in the shoot apical meristem, lets active cytokinin be produced where it is needed.

Another pathway comes from tRNA: an adenine adjacent to the 3′ end of the anticodon of some tRNAs is isopentenylated by tRNA-IPT, and degradation of the tRNA releases isopentenyl-containing nucleosides, which are the main source of *cis*-zeatin. The amount produced by the tRNA pathway is not sufficient to meet the plant's need for *trans*-zeatin and iP, so de novo synthesis is the main route. The statement in Chinese textbooks that "cytokinin protects tRNA" lacks evidence; the modified bases in tRNA mainly affect the accuracy of translation.

The T-DNA of *Agrobacterium tumefaciens* also carries an *ipt* gene (*tmr*), which uses AMP and another side-chain donor to synthesize zeatin riboside nucleotide directly in the plastids of plant cells; together with the auxin synthesis genes on the T-DNA it makes transformed cells proliferate without control and form a tumor, the **crown gall** (for the **Ti plasmid** and crown gall see [7.11.17](/7.11.17)).

### 14.5.2. Conjugation and Degradation

The level of active cytokinin is set jointly by synthesis, activation by LOG, glycosylation and irreversible degradation (Eq. (13.1)). Irreversible degradation is catalyzed by cytokinin oxidase/dehydrogenase (CKX): this FAD-containing enzyme cleaves the unsaturated side chain on $N^6$, breaking iP or zeatin down to adenine and the corresponding aldehyde (iP yields 3-methyl-2-butenal). Dihydrozeatin has a saturated side chain and is not degraded by CKX. The activity of CKX determines the level of cytokinin in many organs: the quantitative trait locus for grain number in rice, *Gn1a*, encodes OsCKX2, and reduced expression of it lets cytokinin accumulate in the inflorescence meristem and increases the number of grains per panicle, an example of a yield trait.

### 14.5.3. Sites of Synthesis and Transport

The main site of cytokinin synthesis is the root tip, but *IPT* genes are expressed in many tissues: the shoot apex, leaf axils, developing seeds and fruit, and the phloem of vascular tissue can all synthesize it. Transport goes in two directions:

- Root → shoot: *trans*-zeatin-type cytokinins synthesized in the root (mainly zeatin riboside) are loaded into the xylem by the transporter ABCG14 and carried to the shoot with the transpiration stream. This signal reports the nutrient status of the root: nitrate induces expression of *IPT3* and *CYP735A2* in the root, so that the shoot "knows" that nitrogen supply is ample and promotes leaf growth.
- Shoot → root: iP-type cytokinins are carried to the root mainly through the phloem.

Entry into cells requires transporters: purine permeases (PUPs) transport free bases, and equilibrative nucleoside transporters (ENTs) transport nucleosides.

## 14.6. Physiological Effects of Cytokinins

### 14.6.1. Promotion of Cell Division

Cytokinin and auxin together control the **cell cycle**: auxin promotes synthesis of the cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs) needed for replication, and cytokinin induces D-type cyclins (such as CYCD3) and promotes the G2/M transition; without either one, cultured cells arrest in G1 or G2 (for the general mechanism of the cell cycle see [7.3.20](/7.3.20)). In intact plants, cytokinin maintains the size of the shoot apical meristem: KNOX transcription factors induce *IPT7* expression in the meristem while repressing gibberellin synthesis, and WUSCHEL represses A-type ARRs, the negative feedback regulators of cytokinin signaling (the WUS–CLV feedback is treated in [7.12.4](/7.12.4)). In the root the situation is the opposite: cytokinin promotes entry of cells in the meristematic zone into differentiation, limits the size of the root apical meristem, and inhibits the initiation of lateral roots.

Cytokinin also promotes cell enlargement in the cotyledons and leaves of dicots (enlargement of radish cotyledons is a classic bioassay); this enlargement is mainly lateral, unlike the longitudinal elongation caused by auxin.

### 14.6.2. The Skoog–Miller Ratio and Organogenesis

Skoog and Miller (1957) carried out an experiment with callus of tobacco pith that changed plant biology: at a fixed auxin concentration they varied the concentration of kinetin (or the reverse), and the direction of development of the isolated tissue depended on the ratio of the two:

| Auxin/cytokinin | Response of callus |
|---|---|
| High | Roots form |
| Intermediate (comparable) | Callus continues to proliferate without differentiating |
| Low | Shoots form (adventitious buds) |
| Both very low | Little growth |

This rule is the basis of organ regeneration in **plant tissue culture** ([7.12.24](/7.12.24)): a medium with high auxin induces rooting and a medium with high cytokinin induces shoot formation. The ratio itself is not a precise constant: what acts is the relative strength of the two signals in the tissue, and the uptake, metabolism and sensitivity of the tissue to exogenous hormones vary with species, explant and endogenous hormones, so the optimum ratio for each plant must be determined experimentally. At the molecular level, high cytokinin induces regulators of the shoot apical meristem such as *WUSCHEL* in callus, whereas high auxin starts the developmental program of root primordia; callus itself arises from pericycle-like cells by the program of "lateral root initiation".

### 14.6.3. Release of Apical Dominance

When cytokinin is applied directly to an axillary bud suppressed by the apex, the bud begins to grow even though the apical bud is still present: cytokinin is the hormone that releases **apical dominance**. In pea, after decapitation *IPT* genes are rapidly expressed at the node, and locally synthesized cytokinin enters the axillary bud; auxin from the apical bud represses expression of these genes. The interaction of auxin, cytokinin, strigolactone and sucrose in bud outgrowth is treated in [7.13.18](/7.13.18). Plants of *Arabidopsis* and tobacco overexpressing *ipt* have increased branching and a weakened root system; some pathogens (such as the phytoplasmas that cause "witches' broom" and *Rhodococcus fascians*) produce cytokinin and make the host grow many clustered branches.

### 14.6.4. Delay of Senescence and Mobilization of Nutrients

Detached leaves turn yellow quickly in darkness, whereas leaves treated with cytokinin stay green and degradation of protein and chlorophyll is slowed (Richmond and Lang, 1957, cocklebur). A classic experiment of Mothes and colleagues showed the relation between delay of senescence and the movement of nutrients: kinetin was applied to one region of a detached leaf and a radioactive amino acid (α-aminoisobutyric acid, which cannot be metabolized) to another region, and the radioactivity concentrated in the region treated with kinetin; when the amino acid was applied first to one region and kinetin then elsewhere, the amino acid also moved to the kinetin. Cytokinin makes the treated tissue a sink that attracts nutrients. This action is associated with induction of cell wall invertase: invertase splits sucrose into hexoses and maintains uptake of sucrose by the tissue (**sink strength**, [7.13.11](/7.13.11)).

In intact plants, leaf senescence is associated with a reduced supply of cytokinin from the root. When the *ipt* gene was placed under the senescence-specific *SAG12* promoter and introduced into tobacco (Gan and Amasino, 1995), the leaves produced cytokinin as soon as senescence began, senescence was delayed, and the photosynthetic period of the plant was prolonged, without the developmental abnormalities of constitutive overexpression; this is a self-regulating negative feedback design. The delay of senescence by cytokinin and the control of senescence by other hormones are treated in [7.13.24](/7.13.24).

### 14.6.5. Other Effects

- Chloroplast development: cytokinin promotes conversion of etioplasts to chloroplasts and chlorophyll synthesis in etiolated seedlings; callus cultured in light requires cytokinin to turn green.
- Stomata: cytokinin promotes stomatal opening in some plants (the opposite of ABA).
- Seed germination: in some light-requiring seeds (lettuce) it can partly replace light.
- Nutrients and symbiosis: cytokinin signaling takes part in the initiation of nodule primordia (legumes) and is one of the signals by which root and shoot communicate their nitrogen, phosphorus, iron and sulfur status.

## 14.7. Comparison of Gibberellin, Cytokinin and Auxin

The three growth-promoting hormones act synergistically in many processes and oppositely in others. In the table below, "+" means promotion, "−" inhibition and "0" little or no effect:

| Process | Auxin | Gibberellin | Cytokinin |
|---|---|---|---|
| Elongation of isolated stem segments | + | 0 | 0 |
| Elongation of intact plants (especially dwarf types) | 0 (already near optimum) | + | 0 |
| Cell division | + (synergistic with cytokinin) | + (intercalary meristem) | + |
| Direction of cell enlargement | Longitudinal elongation | Longitudinal elongation | Lateral enlargement (cotyledons, leaves) |
| Inhibition above the optimum | Marked (through ethylene) | Not marked | Present (callus, roots) |
| Polar transport | Yes | No | No |
| Apical dominance | Maintains | Little effect (enhances in some plants) | Releases |
| Root growth | Promotes at very low concentrations, inhibits at higher | Promotes | Inhibits elongation of the primary root and initiation of lateral roots |
| Organ regeneration (callus) | Induces roots | Generally inhibits regeneration | Induces shoots |
| Seed dormancy and germination | 0 or maintains dormancy | Breaks dormancy, promotes germination | Partly promotes |
| Bolting and flowering (long-day rosette plants) | 0 | + | 0 |
| Sex of unisexual flowers (cucurbits) | Increases female flowers (through ethylene) | Increases male flowers | Increases female flowers |
| Parthenocarpy | + | + (grape, pear) | + |
| Leaf senescence | Delays | Delays (in some plants) | Delays markedly |

The relation between auxin and cytokinin is opposite in shoot and root: at the shoot apex, auxin maintains apical dominance and cytokinin promotes outgrowth of axillary buds; in the root, auxin promotes the initiation of lateral roots and cytokinin inhibits it. Their ratio therefore determines not only organogenesis in culture but also the growth balance between root and shoot in the intact plant. Gibberellin and auxin act synergistically in stem elongation: auxin promotes gibberellin synthesis (in pea, auxin from the shoot tip maintains expression of *GA3ox* in the internodes; decapitation lowers GA₁, and applying IAA restores it), and gibberellin in turn affects auxin transport.

## Questions

1. The pea *le* mutation (reduced GA 3-oxidase activity) makes plants dwarf. (a) Which of GA₂₀ and GA₁ applied to *le* plants restores height? (b) The *na* mutation blocks KAO; what are the effects of applying GA₁₂ and GA₂₀? (c) A new dwarf mutant is insensitive to GA₁ and has five times the wild-type content of endogenous GA₁; where is its defect most likely to lie?

   **Answer** (a) GA₁ restores height and GA₂₀ essentially does not: *le* lacks precisely the 3β-hydroxylation GA₂₀ → GA₁. (*le* is a leaky mutant, and large doses of GA₂₀ have a slight effect.) (b) KAO catalyzes *ent*-kaurenoic acid → GA₁₂; on *na* plants both GA₁₂ and GA₂₀ restore height, because both lie after the blocked step and can be converted to GA₁ by the downstream GA13ox, GA20ox and GA3ox. (c) In the signaling pathway: insensitivity to GA shows that the problem is not in synthesis, and the high endogenous GA₁ is due to the DELLA feedback (Section 14.2.2): when signaling is blocked, DELLA is not degraded and continues to induce *GA20ox* and *GA3ox*. It may be loss of the receptor GID1 or the F-box protein (recessive), or a dominant mutation deleting the DELLA domain (*gai* type); a cross to determine dominance or recessiveness can distinguish them.

2. According to Eqs. (14.1)–(14.3), let $n = 1$, $K_{\mathrm{d}} = 10\ \mathrm{nmol\,L^{-1}}$, $k_1/k_0 = 20$, and take $s/k_0 = 10K_D$ (so that $D^* = 10K_D$ without GA). (a) Find the relative growth rate $G/G_{\max}$ at GA of 0, 10, 100 and $1000\ \mathrm{nmol\,L^{-1}}$. (b) Between 10 and 100 nmol/L, by how much does $G$ rise per 10-fold increase in concentration? (c) If the DELLA synthesis rate $s$ of a cultivar is halved, how does the curve change?

   **Answer** $D^*/K_D = 10/(1 + 20\theta)$ and $\theta = c/(10 + c)$. (a) $c = 0$: $\theta = 0$, $D^*/K_D = 10$, $G/G_{\max} = 1/11 = 0.09$; $c = 10$: $\theta = 0.5$, $D^*/K_D = 10/11 = 0.91$, $G = 0.52$; $c = 100$: $\theta = 0.909$, $D^*/K_D = 10/19.2 = 0.52$, $G = 0.66$; $c = 1000$: $\theta = 0.990$, $D^*/K_D = 0.48$, $G = 0.68$. (b) $G$ rises from 0.52 to 0.66, about 0.14 $G_{\max}$ per 10-fold concentration; from $c = 1$ ($\theta = 0.091$, $D^*/K_D = 3.55$, $G = 0.22$) to 10 the increase was 0.30, so with these parameters the log-linear segment lies at about 1–30 nmol/L. (c) Halving $s$ halves $D^*$ at all concentrations: without GA, $G = 1/6 = 0.17$, and at saturation $G = 1/1.24 = 0.81$; the whole curve shifts upward, dwarfing is less severe and maximum growth is higher. This shows that the amount of DELLA itself (and not only the amount of GA) determines plant height, and also explains why different alleles of *Rht* give different degrees of dwarfing.

3. The gibberellin activity of a sample is measured with isolated barley aleurone layers: standard GA₃ at $10^{-9}$, $10^{-8}$ and $10^{-7}\ \mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$ gives α-amylase release of $0.8$, $2.1$ and $3.4$ (units per layer). The sample, diluted 100-fold, gives $1.6$. (a) Estimate the GA₃-equivalent concentration in the sample. (b) If the same sample also contains ABA, will the result be too high or too low? How could this be tested?

   **Answer** (a) The three points are linear: each 10-fold increase in concentration adds 1.3 units, so $\log_{10}c = -9 + (1.6 - 0.8)/1.3 = -8.38$ and $c = 4.1\times10^{-9}\ \mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$; multiplying by the dilution factor, the sample is about $4\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$ GA₃ equivalent. (b) Too low: ABA antagonizes gibberellin-induced synthesis of α-amylase (shifting the dose-response curve to the right). Tests: internal standard addition, adding a known amount of GA₃ to the sample and checking whether recovery is normal; or measuring the sample at different dilutions, since if the calculated concentration increases with dilution, an inhibitor that is diluted away is present; the most reliable method is direct quantitation by mass spectrometry.

4. Explain why α-aminoisobutyric acid, and not an ordinary radioactive amino acid, is used in the Mothes experiment. According to this experiment, what might be the relation between the delay of senescence by cytokinin and its action as a "sink"?

   **Answer** α-Aminoisobutyric acid is taken up and transported by cells but is hardly metabolized, incorporated into protein or converted to other compounds, so the distribution of its radioactivity directly reflects the direction of transport and is not confounded by metabolism or incorporation into protein at some site. The experiment shows that cytokinin makes the treated site a sink for nutrients; one essence of senescence is that nutrients (nitrogen, phosphorus and others) are broken down and exported ([7.13.24](/7.13.24)), and cytokinin in turn maintains import of material and protein synthesis, so the two are two sides of the same process: cytokinin both inhibits breakdown (of chlorophyll and protein) and raises sink strength, for example by inducing cell wall invertase, so that material flows in. *SAG12::ipt* plants show that maintaining local cytokinin at the onset of senescence is enough to delay it.

5. (Open question) In a tissue culture experiment, an explant produces only callus and no shoots at IAA $2\ \mathrm{mg\,L^{-1}}$ with any concentration of BA. Based on Section 14.6.2, propose several possible causes and corresponding improvements.

   **Answer** Possible causes and remedies: (1) Auxin is too high and the ratio does not reach the "low auxin/high cytokinin" region: lower the IAA or use a weaker auxin (for example NAA down to 0.1 mg/L), or transfer the callus in the second stage to a medium containing only cytokinin. (2) IAA decomposes in light and in the medium, so the actual ratio is uncertain: use the stable NAA, or measure the residual in the medium. (3) The explant is insensitive to BA or quickly metabolizes it (high CKX activity, glycosylation): use the more active and less degradable TDZ or CPPU, or add a CKX inhibitor. (4) The endogenous hormones and developmental state of the explant are unsuitable: use young explants (such as cotyledons or hypocotyls) and first induce "callus competence" on a medium containing 2,4-D before transferring to the shoot-induction medium. (5) Other factors: light, temperature, carbon source, and accumulation of ethylene in the closed vessel (adding the ethylene antagonist silver nitrate often promotes shoot regeneration in grasses). A systematic approach is a factorial experiment on a two-dimensional grid of IAA–BA concentrations to find the region that produces shoots.
