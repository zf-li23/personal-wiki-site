Plants are autotrophs: given light, water, $\ce{CO2}$ and a dozen or so inorganic elements, they synthesize all of their own substance. Apart from carbon, hydrogen and oxygen, almost all of these elements are taken up by roots from the soil as inorganic ions and then carried to the organs where they are used; mineral elements enter the biosphere mainly through plant roots, so in this sense plants are the "miners" of the Earth's crust. The study of how plants acquire and use mineral elements is called mineral nutrition, and it underlies modern fertilizer practice. World consumption of nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium fertilizers rose from about 30 million tonnes in 1960 to about 180 million tonnes in recent years, yet crops usually take up less than half of the fertilizer applied to the soil; the rest is lost with water or to the atmosphere, causing eutrophication of water bodies and greenhouse gas emissions. This chapter first asks which elements are essential and how this is established. It then covers the functions of each element and its deficiency symptoms, and the quantitative use of tissue analysis to diagnose nutritional status. Next come the soil as a reservoir of nutrients, how nutrients reach the root surface, and how roots and mycorrhizae acquire them; the chapter ends with soilless culture and rational fertilization. How ions cross the plasma membrane into root cells and move through the root into the xylem is the subject of the next chapter, [7.13.5](/7.13.5); how nitrogen, sulfur, phosphorus and iron are assimilated into organic compounds once inside the cell is discussed in [7.13.6](/7.13.6). For root structure see [7.12.20](/7.12.20), and for the microbiology of mycorrhizal and nodule symbioses see [7.11.31](/7.11.31).

## 4.1. Elements in Plants and Essential Elements

### 4.1.1. Dry Matter, Ash and Mineral Elements

Between 70% and over 90% of fresh plant tissue is water. When tissue is dried at $100$–$105\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$, what remains is dry matter, about 5%–90% of fresh weight (seeds contain the least water). About 45% of the dry matter is carbon, 45% oxygen and 6% hydrogen; together these make up over 90% and come from $\ce{CO2}$ and water. If the dry matter is then fully burned at about $600\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$, carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen and part of the sulfur escape as gases ($\ce{CO2}$, $\ce{H2O}$, nitrogen oxides and $\ce{N2}$, $\ce{SO2}$), and the nonvolatile residue is the ash. The elements in the ash are called ash elements or mineral elements: all the metals, phosphorus, silicon and part of the sulfur, present in the ash as oxides, phosphates and sulfates. Nitrogen is volatilized on ashing and is therefore not an ash element, but because plants take it up from the soil mainly as nitrate and ammonium it is usually discussed together with the mineral elements.

Ash content varies with habitat and organ and roughly reflects how much salt the plant accumulates from the soil:

| Material | Ash (% of dry weight) |
|---|---|
| Aquatic plants | about 1% |
| Mesophytes (most land plants) | 5%–10% |
| Halophytes | up to 45% |
| Xylem (wood) | about 1% |
| Seeds | about 3% |
| Roots and stems of herbs | 4%–5% |
| Leaves | 10%–15% |

The presence of an element in the ash does not mean that the plant needs it: plants take up almost every ion in the soil solution, including aluminum, selenium, cadmium and radionuclides. For example, plants usually contain $0.1$–$500\,\mu\mathrm{g}$ of aluminum per gram of dry matter, and many plants such as *Astragalus* accumulate large amounts of selenium, but there is no evidence that they need these elements. Which elements are essential must be determined by specially designed experiments.

### 4.1.2. Criteria for Essential Elements and Solution Culture

**Definition 4.1.1 (Essential Element)** An **essential element** is an element that a plant cannot do without for normal growth and development. The three classical criteria for deciding whether an element is essential (Arnon and Stout, 1939) are:

1. Indispensability: in the complete absence of the element, growth and development are impaired and the plant cannot complete its life cycle;
2. Irreplaceability: its absence produces a specific deficiency symptom that can be prevented or corrected only by supplying that element, whose role cannot be taken over by another element;
3. Direct function: the element acts directly, not indirectly by improving the physical, chemical or microbial conditions of the soil (or culture medium).

Modern textbooks often use an equivalent but more operational statement: an essential element is an intrinsic component of the structure or metabolism of the plant, or its absence causes severe abnormalities in growth, development or reproduction, so that the plant cannot complete its life cycle.

To show that an element is essential, plants must be grown in an environment lacking only that element with everything else in ample supply, which is almost impossible in a soil of complex composition. In the nineteenth century de Saussure, Sachs, Boussingault, Knop and others grew plants with their roots immersed in solutions containing only inorganic salts, and showed that plants need neither soil nor organic matter: mineral elements, water, atmospheric $\ce{CO2}$ and sunlight suffice for normal growth.

**Definition 4.1.2 (Solution Culture)** Growing plants with their roots immersed in a solution that contains all or some of the nutrient elements is called **solution culture** (hydroponics). Fixing the plant in a nutrient-free medium such as washed quartz sand, vermiculite, perlite or rock wool and irrigating it with nutrient solution is called substrate culture (sand culture and the like); suspending the roots in air and supplying nutrient solution continuously as a mist is called aeroponics.

Solution culture demands rigorous technique. The volume of nutrient solution must be large or the solution replaced often, so that root uptake does not change concentrations and pH greatly; the solution must be well aerated (air bubbled through it) to support root respiration; and the chemicals and containers must be sufficiently pure. The last requirement is why several micronutrients were recognized as essential so late. Knop's solution contained only $\ce{KNO3}$, $\ce{Ca(NO3)2}$, $\ce{KH2PO4}$, $\ce{MgSO4}$ and an iron salt, and was thought to contain all the mineral elements plants need; in fact the trace amounts of boron, molybdenum and other elements that the plants required came from impurities in the reagents. As purification improved, manganese (1922), boron (1923), zinc (1926), copper (1931), molybdenum (1939), chlorine (1954) and nickel (1987) were successively confirmed as essential. The requirement for nickel is extremely low (about $0.1\,\mu\mathrm{g\,g^{-1}}$ dry weight), so low that the amount stored in the seed suffices for several generations; only barley grown over successive generations in nickel-free solution demonstrated that it is essential.

Various hydroponic techniques are used in research and production: standard hydroponics (the plant is held above a tank of nutrient solution, aerated by an air stone); the *nutrient film technique* (nutrient solution flows as a thin film along a sloping channel bottom over the roots); aeroponics; and ebb-and-flow culture (the nutrient solution rises and falls periodically so that roots alternate between immersion and exposure to moist air). Aeroponics allows control of the gaseous environment of the rhizosphere but needs higher nutrient concentrations than hydroponics, and it is still not widely used.

### 4.1.3. Macronutrients, Micronutrients and Beneficial Elements

Seventeen elements are currently accepted as essential for higher plants: carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, calcium, magnesium, sulfur, chlorine, iron, boron, manganese, zinc, copper, nickel and molybdenum. The first three come mainly from water and $\ce{CO2}$ and are not counted as mineral nutrients; the other 14 come from the soil and are called the essential mineral elements. They are divided into two classes according to their content in tissues.

**Definition 4.1.3 (Macronutrients and Micronutrients)** Essential elements present at relatively high concentrations in plant tissue (usually above $0.1\,\%$ of dry weight, that is, $1000\,\mathrm{mg\,kg^{-1}}$) are called **macronutrients**: carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen, potassium, calcium, magnesium, phosphorus and sulfur. Those present at very low concentrations (usually below $100\,\mathrm{mg\,kg^{-1}}$), needed in minute amounts and potentially toxic in slight excess, are called **trace elements** (micronutrients): chlorine, iron, boron, manganese, zinc, copper, nickel and molybdenum.

The table below gives the tissue contents of the elements that suffice for normal growth in most plants (Epstein's data, Taiz 6e, Table 5.1). Converted to amounts of substance, they show the range of plant requirements: for every molybdenum atom in a plant there are about a million nitrogen atoms.

The division into macro- and micronutrients is merely quantitative. The iron and manganese content of mesophyll is sometimes almost as high as that of sulfur and magnesium, and a single element often has several functions. Mengel and Kirkby therefore proposed classifying the mineral elements into four groups by biochemical function (Section 4.2.1).

Besides the essential elements, some elements are beneficial to certain plants or under certain conditions; these are called beneficial elements:

- Sodium: many C4 and CAM plants require $\ce{Na+}$, which is involved in the regeneration of phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP); without sodium these plants become chlorotic and necrotic, and may even fail to flower. Many C3 plants also grow better at low concentrations of $\ce{Na+}$, which can partly replace $\ce{K+}$ as an osmotic solute.
- Silicon: only the horsetails (Equisetaceae) cannot complete their life cycle without silicon, but grasses and many other plants accumulate large amounts. Silicon is deposited as hydrated amorphous silica ($\ce{SiO2}\cdot n\ce{H2O}$) in cell walls, intercellular spaces and the endoplasmic reticulum, increasing wall strength and elasticity, reducing lodging and fungal infection, and alleviating aluminum and manganese toxicity. Rice is a typical silicon-loving plant.
- Cobalt: cobalt is a component of cobalamin (vitamin $\mathrm{B_{12}}$) and is required by several enzymes of nitrogen-fixing microorganisms; without cobalt, nodule development and nitrogen fixation are impaired, but plants that do not rely on symbiotic fixation do not need it.
- Selenium: a few selenium-accumulating plants (such as *Astragalus*) grow better on high-selenium soils, but a specific plant requirement for selenium has not been demonstrated.

## 4.2. Physiological Functions of Essential Elements and Deficiency Symptoms

### 4.2.1. Four Classes of Function

The roles of the essential elements fall into four categories: components of cell structures and organic compounds (nitrogen, phosphorus, sulfur); components or activators of enzymes, taking part in enzymatic reactions (potassium, magnesium, manganese, zinc, copper and others); electrochemical roles, that is, regulating osmotic potential, maintaining charge balance and taking part in redox reactions (potassium, chlorine, iron); and signaling ($\ce{Ca^2+}$ is a **second messenger**). By biochemical function the mineral elements fall into four groups:

| Group | Elements | State in the plant | Main functions |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Constituents of carbon compounds | N, S | Form covalent bonds with carbon through oxidation–reduction | Amino acids, proteins, nucleic acids, coenzymes (N); cysteine, methionine, coenzyme A, lipoic acid, glutathione (S) |
| 2. Energy storage and structure | P, Si, B | Bound to organic molecules as phosphate, borate and silicate esters | Sugar phosphates, nucleotides, phospholipids, phytate (P); amorphous silica in cell walls (Si); cross-linking of wall polysaccharides (B) |
| 3. Elements kept in ionic form | K, Ca, Mg, Cl, Zn, Na | Free ions, or electrostatically bound to pectic acids and the like | Enzyme cofactors, osmotic regulation, membrane permeability; calcium is a second messenger and magnesium a component of chlorophyll |
| 4. Elements in redox reactions | Fe, Mn, Cu, Ni, Mo | Bound to cytochromes, proteins and other macromolecules | Electron transfer and energy conversion; reversible changes between oxidation states |

The grouping overlaps in places: manganese exists as an ion and also takes part in electron transfer.

### 4.2.2. Functions and Deficiency Symptoms of Each Element

**Definition 4.2.1 (Nutrient Deficiency Symptom)** The characteristic symptoms displayed by a plant whose metabolism is disturbed by the lack of an essential element are called **nutrient deficiency symptoms**. Common symptoms are: *chlorosis*, in which a leaf turns yellow or white because of reduced chlorophyll, and which may affect the whole leaf, the interveinal areas or the leaf margin; **necrosis**, death of tissue with brown or black necrotic spots; accumulation of anthocyanins (leaves, petioles and stems turn purplish red); stunted growth (small plants, shortened internodes, small leaves); and malformed organs.

Deficiency symptoms are the manifestation of the loss of the corresponding physiological function, so function and symptoms are treated together below. Symptoms differ among species, and even within one species they vary somewhat with developmental stage.

Nitrogen. Nitrogen is the mineral element required in the largest amounts. It is a component of proteins, nucleic acids, phospholipids, **chlorophyll**, coenzymes, certain hormones (auxin, cytokinins), vitamins and alkaloids, and Chinese textbooks call it the "element of life". In most natural and agricultural ecosystems nitrogen is the first element limiting productivity. Roots take up mainly $\ce{NO3-}$ and $\ce{NH4+}$, and can also absorb organic nitrogen such as urea, amino acids and oligopeptides. In nitrogen deficiency growth is rapidly inhibited and plants are small with few branches or tillers. The old leaves become uniformly chlorotic and yellow first, and in severe cases the whole leaf turns yellow (yellowish brown) and falls, while the upper leaves are still pale green, because nitrogen can be remobilized from old to young leaves. The root–shoot ratio increases. Carbohydrates that cannot be used to synthesize nitrogenous compounds accumulate, making stems thin and lignified, or are diverted into anthocyanin synthesis, which gives leaves, petioles and stems of tomato and some maize varieties a purple color. Grains are poorly filled and yield is low. Excess nitrogen gives dark green leaves and excessive vegetative growth with thin cell walls, so that plants lodge and are susceptible to pests and diseases, and maturity is delayed. Leafy vegetables, whose vegetative parts are harvested, need more nitrogen fertilizer.

Phosphorus. Plants take up $\ce{H2PO4-}$ and $\ce{HPO4^2-}$. After uptake phosphate is not reduced; it enters sugar phosphates (intermediates of photosynthesis and respiration), nucleotides (ATP, NAD, NADP, FAD), nucleic acids, phospholipids and phytate (the storage form of phosphorus in seeds) as phosphate. Phosphorus is unevenly distributed in the plant and concentrated in actively growing parts such as root tips, shoot tips, fruits and seeds. Phosphorus deficiency affects almost all of metabolism: protein synthesis and cell division are inhibited; plants grow slowly and are small, with small leaves, few branches or tillers and delayed maturity. Leaves are dark green, probably because cell growth is inhibited more severely than chlorophyll synthesis. In some plants, impaired sugar export causes sugars to accumulate in the leaf, which are then channeled into anthocyanin synthesis, giving purplish-red leaves. Unlike nitrogen deficiency, the purple of phosphorus deficiency is not accompanied by chlorosis: leaves are dark green tinged with purple. Symptoms appear first in old leaves, which die.

Potassium. Potassium is taken up as $\ce{K+}$ and stays in ionic form throughout the plant; it is the most abundant cation in cells. Its main roles are as follows. It regulates osmotic potential and turgor (cell elongation, stomatal movement and the nastic movements of leaves all depend on $\ce{K+}$ fluxes; see 7.13.10). It activates more than 40 enzymes, including pyruvate kinase and starch synthase, enzymes of respiration and photosynthesis. It maintains electroneutrality in the cell. And it promotes the transport of photosynthate to tubers, tuberous roots and seeds and the synthesis of cellulose and lignin, which makes stems tough and resistant to lodging; for this reason potassium is also called the "quality element". The first symptoms of potassium deficiency are mottled chlorosis or marginal chlorosis of old leaves, followed by necrosis of leaf tips, margins and interveinal areas ("marginal scorch"); in monocots necrosis spreads from the tip and margins toward the base. Leaves curl and crinkle, stems are weak with short internodes and lodge easily, and maize roots are more readily infected by root-rot fungi. Potassium can be transferred to young leaves, so symptoms appear first in the lower, older leaves.

Calcium. Calcium is taken up as $\ce{Ca^2+}$. It has two quite different roles. One is structural: $\ce{Ca^2+}$ binds to the acidic groups of phospholipids and sulfolipids, stabilizing membranes, and cross-links pectin, especially in the middle lamella between newly divided cells (calcium pectate). The other is signaling: as a second messenger, $\ce{Ca^2+}$ binds to **calmodulin** and regulates the activity of many proteins such as kinases and phosphatases (see [7.1.15](/7.1.15)). So that $\ce{Ca^2+}$ can act as a signal, the concentration of free $\ce{Ca^2+}$ in the cytosol is kept at about $0.1$–$0.2\,\mu\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$, roughly ten thousand times lower than in the apoplast (on the order of $\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$; see 7.13.5). Excess calcium is often deposited as calcium oxalate crystals (prismatic crystals, druses and raphides) in the vacuoles of idioblasts, reaching 90% of total calcium in some plants; this removes the toxicity of excess oxalate and is also a defense against herbivores. Calcium is almost immobile in the phloem and moves mainly with the transpiration stream in the xylem, so young organs and fruits with weak transpiration are most prone to calcium deficiency. In deficiency, cell wall formation is impaired and cell division affected; meristems (root tips, shoot tips, young leaves) die, young leaves hook downward and curl, and roots are short, much branched and brown. Blossom-end rot of tomato, tipburn of lettuce, internal browning (dry heart) of Chinese cabbage and bitter pit of apple are all local calcium deficiencies in fruits or inner leaves.

Magnesium. Magnesium is the central atom of the chlorophyll porphyrin ring and activates many enzymes involved in phosphate group transfer (kinases, ATPases) as well as enzymes of respiration, photosynthesis and DNA and RNA synthesis; ribosome assembly also needs $\ce{Mg^2+}$. Magnesium is very mobile, and the typical symptom of deficiency is interveinal chlorosis of old leaves: chlorophyll near the vascular bundles is retained longer than between the veins, so the leaf shows a network of green veins bordered by yellow bands. In severe cases the whole leaf turns yellow or white, sometimes purplish red with necrotic spots, and leaves senesce and fall early.

Sulfur. Sulfur is taken up as $\ce{SO4^2-}$ and after reduction enters cysteine, cystine and methionine, as well as coenzyme A, S-adenosylmethionine, biotin, thiamine, lipoic acid and glutathione (for the assimilation pathway see [7.13.6](/7.13.6)). The symptoms of sulfur deficiency resemble those of nitrogen deficiency (chlorosis, stunting, anthocyanin accumulation) because both elements are components of proteins. The difference is that sulfur is not readily exported from old leaves in most plants, so chlorosis usually appears first in young and mature leaves. In some species, however, the whole plant becomes chlorotic at once, or even the old leaves first. Sulfur is generally adequate in soils.

Iron. Iron is a component of electron-transfer proteins such as cytochromes and iron–sulfur proteins; in the respiratory chain, the photosynthetic electron transport chain and nitrogenase it transfers electrons through reversible changes between $\ce{Fe^2+}$ and $\ce{Fe^3+}$. Iron is also needed for the synthesis of some chlorophyll–protein complexes in the chloroplast, where most of the iron in the plant is found. The typical symptom of iron deficiency is again interveinal chlorosis, but unlike magnesium deficiency it appears first in young leaves, because iron precipitates in old leaves as insoluble oxides or phosphates and is difficult to load into the phloem; when deficiency is severe or prolonged the veins also become chlorotic and the whole leaf turns white. In calcareous soils the high pH makes iron precipitate as hydroxide and phosphate, so that its availability is extremely low; the "yellow-leaf disease" of fruit trees in northern China is iron-deficiency chlorosis of this kind. How roots acquire iron from such soils is described in [7.13.6](/7.13.6).

Manganese. $\ce{Mn^2+}$ activates many enzymes, especially the decarboxylases and dehydrogenases of the tricarboxylic acid cycle; its best-established function is as a component of the $\ce{Mn4CaO5}$ cluster of the oxygen-evolving complex of photosystem II, taking part in the photolysis of water (see [7.1.24](/7.1.24)). The main symptom of manganese deficiency is interveinal chlorosis accompanied by small necrotic spots, which appears in young or old leaves depending on species and growth rate.

Zinc. Many enzymes require $\ce{Zn^2+}$, for example alcohol dehydrogenase, glutamate dehydrogenase, carbonic anhydrase and Cu/Zn superoxide dismutase. Zinc deficiency is characterized by inhibited internode growth, rosetted plants, and small, deformed leaves with crinkled margins, the "little-leaf disease" of fruit trees. These symptoms may result from insufficient synthesis of auxin (indole-3-acetic acid), but the specific link between zinc and auxin synthesis is not clear. Chinese textbooks often state that zinc is a component of tryptophan synthase; current evidence does not support this, since tryptophan synthase is a pyridoxal phosphate enzyme and contains no zinc. In maize, sorghum and bean, old leaves may also show interveinal chlorosis and white necrotic spots in zinc deficiency (the "white bud" of maize), possibly related to a requirement for zinc in chlorophyll synthesis. Zinc deficiency is the most widespread micronutrient deficiency of crops in the world, and about half of agricultural soils are deficient in zinc. Zinc is also a commonly deficient element in the human diet: the World Health Organization estimated in 2002 that about 800 000 deaths per year were associated with zinc deficiency, so increasing the zinc content of cereal grain (biofortification) is an important breeding goal.

Copper. Like iron, copper takes part in redox reactions, changing reversibly between $\ce{Cu+}$ and $\ce{Cu^2+}$; it is a component of plastocyanin (photosynthetic electron transport), cytochrome c oxidase, ascorbate oxidase, polyphenol oxidase, laccase and others. The initial symptom of copper deficiency is dark green leaves, with necrotic spots at the tips of young leaves that spread along the margin toward the base, and twisted, malformed leaves; in cereals, leaves become chlorotic and necrotic with curled tips; in extreme deficiency leaves drop early and flowers are sterile.

Boron. Boron is taken up as uncharged boric acid $\ce{B(OH)3}$ (Chinese textbooks often write $\ce{H2BO3-}$, but at soil pH boron is almost entirely present as the boric acid molecule), entering cells through aquaporin-type channels. The functions of boron are still not fully understood. What is established is that it cross-links rhamnogalacturonan II (a pectic polysaccharide) in the cell wall through borate diesters, and that it is involved in cell elongation, nucleic acid synthesis, hormone responses and membrane function. Boron is especially important for reproduction: in deficiency, anthers and filaments shrivel, pollen development is poor and pollen tube growth is blocked, so "flowers without seeds" in rapeseed and "buds without bolls" in cotton are manifestations of boron deficiency. Vegetative symptoms are black necrosis of young leaves and terminal buds (often starting at the leaf base), brittle, hard stems, loss of apical dominance with increased branching, though the branch tips also die quickly; internal tissues of fleshy roots, tubers and fruits become necrotic (heart rot of sugar beet).

Molybdenum. Molybdenum ($\ce{Mo^4+}$ to $\ce{Mo^6+}$) is a component of **nitrate reductase**, **nitrogenase**, xanthine dehydrogenase, aldehyde oxidase and sulfite oxidase. The initial sign of molybdenum deficiency is interveinal chlorosis and necrosis of old leaves; in cauliflower and broccoli the leaves do not become necrotic but are twisted, crinkled and eventually die (whiptail); flower formation is impaired or flowers drop early. Because molybdenum takes part in nitrate assimilation and symbiotic nitrogen fixation, plants that depend on nitrate as the main nitrogen source or on nodule nitrogen fixation show nitrogen-deficiency symptoms when molybdenum is lacking. Plants need very little molybdenum, but some soils (such as the acidic soils of Australia) supply too little, and applying a few tens of grams of molybdenum per hectare greatly increases yield at negligible cost.

Chlorine. Chlorine occurs as $\ce{Cl-}$ and is required for the oxygen-evolving reaction of photosystem II; cell division in leaves and roots may also need it, and $\ce{Cl-}$ is an important osmotic solute (it enters and leaves guard cells together with $\ce{K+}$ in stomatal movement). In deficiency leaf tips wilt, then the whole leaf becomes chlorotic and necrotic and may turn bronze, and roots are short and thick. $\ce{Cl-}$ is highly soluble; salt from the sea is carried into the atmosphere by wind and returns to the soil in rain, so chlorine deficiency is extremely rare under natural conditions and plants usually take up far more chlorine than they need.

Nickel. Urease is the only nickel-containing enzyme known in higher plants; it hydrolyzes urea to $\ce{NH3}$ and $\ce{CO2}$. The uptake hydrogenase of nitrogen-fixing microorganisms, which recycles the $\ce{H2}$ produced during fixation, also needs nickel (see 7.13.6). Nickel-deficient plants accumulate urea in their leaves and have necrotic leaf tips, and seeds of nickel-deficient barley fail to germinate. Nickel deficiency in the field has so far been found only in pecan in the southeastern United States ("mouse-ear"), because plants need so little nickel.

### 4.2.3. Element Mobility and the Site of Deficiency

An important clue to which element is deficient is whether symptoms appear first on old or on young leaves. This depends on whether the element can be redistributed from old to young leaves (reutilization), which in turn depends mainly on whether it can enter the phloem (see Section 5.6 of 7.13.5 and [7.13.11](/7.13.11)).

| Mobility | Elements (in order of abundance in plants) | Site where deficiency symptoms first appear |
|---|---|---|
| Mobile (reutilized) | N, K, Mg, P, Cl, Na, Zn, Mo | Old leaves (lower leaves) |
| Immobile (not reutilized) | Ca, S, Fe, B, Cu | Young leaves, terminal buds and root tips |

The classification in the table follows Taiz 6e; the mobility of zinc, molybdenum, sulfur, copper and manganese varies with species and nutritional status. For example, manganese symptoms may appear first on young or old leaves depending on the species. Mobile elements are mostly found in the metabolically active parts of the plant, and are transferred in large amounts to seeds and storage organs when the plant flowers and sets seed and before leaf fall in deciduous plants; immobile elements, once deposited in old leaves, are "fixed", and their content rises with organ age. Hormones such as cytokinins take part in regulating nutrient remobilization, but the mechanism is unclear.

### 4.2.4. Diagnosing Deficiencies

Deficient elements are commonly identified from symptoms with a dichotomous key. The following simplified key is arranged by the site where symptoms appear (choose one of each pair of options):

1. Symptoms appear first in older tissues (elements that are readily reutilized)
   - Growth of the whole plant inhibited: older leaves uniformly chlorotic: nitrogen; leaves dark green or reddish purple, not chlorotic: phosphorus
   - Chlorotic spots, streaks or necrosis: interveinal chlorosis: magnesium; marginal chlorosis progressing to scorch and necrosis: potassium; small leaves, short internodes, rosetting, interveinal chlorosis with necrotic spots: zinc
2. Symptoms appear first in young tissues (elements that are not readily reutilized)
   - Terminal bud or growing point dies: necrosis at the base of young leaves, brittle stems, much branching: boron; young leaves hooked and curled, root tips necrotic: calcium
   - Growing point does not die: interveinal chlorosis with small necrotic spots: manganese; interveinal chlorosis, whole leaf yellowish white in severe cases: iron; whole leaf pale green to yellow, thin stems: sulfur; leaf tips whitened, leaves narrow and twisted, wilting easily: copper

The key has limitations. Plants growing in soil may lack several elements at once, so that symptoms overlap; deficiency or excess of one element can induce deficiency or accumulation of another (for example, excess phosphorus induces zinc deficiency); the symptoms of some virus diseases and other stresses resemble nutrient deficiencies; and by the time symptoms appear, yield has often already been affected. Visual diagnosis therefore needs to be confirmed by chemical analysis, namely soil analysis and plant tissue analysis (Section 4.3), and by "fertilizer diagnosis", in which the suspected element is applied to see whether the symptoms disappear.

## 4.3. Tissue Analysis and Critical Concentration

### 4.3.1. Yield–Tissue Concentration Curves

Soil analysis measures the amount of a nutrient in the root-zone soil and reflects the quantity that may be available to roots, but it cannot tell us how much the plant actually needs or can absorb; that requires plant tissue analysis. Interpreting tissue analysis requires knowing the relation between plant growth (or yield) and nutrient concentration in the tissue. To measure this relation, plants are grown with all other nutrients in ample supply while the supply of one nutrient is increased stepwise; the concentration of that nutrient is measured in a specific tissue (for example the most recently fully expanded leaf, which is the most metabolically active, represents the nutritional status of the whole plant and is easy to sample) and plotted against growth or yield. Note that tissue concentration depends on the balance between uptake and growth dilution: if uptake stays constant while growth accelerates, concentration falls.

The curve obtained has three zones (Panel A below). In the deficient zone tissue concentration is low and growth is inhibited, and increasing nutrient supply and uptake increases yield directly. As supply continues to rise, beyond a certain point yield no longer increases and only tissue concentration continues to rise; this part is the adequate zone, in which the extra nutrient taken up is "luxury consumption". When tissue concentration exceeds the adequate zone, yield falls because of toxicity; this is the toxic zone.

**Definition 4.3.1 (Critical Tissue Concentration)** The tissue nutrient concentration at the transition between the deficient and adequate zones is called the *critical tissue concentration*, often shortened to critical concentration: it is the lowest tissue nutrient concentration corresponding to maximum growth or yield. In practice the tissue concentration at 90% (or 95%) of maximum yield is often taken.

When tissue concentration is below the critical concentration, the nutrient should be applied before the deficiency affects yield. Because nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium are the elements most often lacking in agricultural soils, farmers must at least consider crop responses to these three; separate curves must be established for the same element in different tissues and at different leaf ages.

From low to high concentration the zones are deficient (red), adequate (green) and toxic (yellow); the tissue concentration at 90% of maximum growth is defined as the critical concentration. B: grain yield response to nitrogen rate described by the Mitscherlich equation ($A = 10\,\mathrm{t\,ha^{-1}}$, $c = 0.02\,\mathrm{ha\,kg^{-1}}$, $b = 20\,\mathrm{kg\,ha^{-1}}$): 90% of maximum yield requires $95\,\mathrm{kg\,ha^{-1}}$ of nitrogen; when 1 kg of nitrogen costs as much as 10 kg of grain, the economically optimal nitrogen rate, at which the marginal yield equals the price ratio (red tangent), is $130\,\mathrm{kg\,ha^{-1}}$. ### 4.3.2. The Law of the Minimum and the Mitscherlich Equation

There are two classical quantitative descriptions of the response of yield to fertilization.

The *law of the minimum*: yield is determined by the nutrient whose supply is smallest relative to need, and no amount of the other nutrients can compensate (proposed by Sprengel in 1828 and Liebig in the 1840s, often illustrated by the image of a barrel: the amount of water it holds is set by the shortest stave). In mathematical form, yield $y = A\min(f_1, f_2, \dots, f_n)$, where $f_i \in [0, 1]$ is the relative yield determined by nutrient $i$ alone. It explains why extra nitrogen fertilizer is ineffective on a phosphorus-deficient soil (the plateau of the solid lines in Panel C of the figure above).

The *Mitscherlich equation*, proposed in 1909, assumes that for each added unit of nutrient the increase in yield is proportional to the distance of yield from its maximum, $\mathrm{d}y/\mathrm{d}x = c(A - y)$; integration gives

$$
y = A\left(1 - \mathrm{e}^{-c(x + b)}\right), \tag{4.1}
$$

where $x$ is the fertilizer rate, $A$ is the maximum attainable yield under the conditions, $c$ is the effect coefficient, and $b$ is the nutrient originally available in the soil (converted to an equivalent amount of fertilizer). The equation gives diminishing returns: each additional unit of fertilizer produces a smaller increase in yield. If the effects of several nutrients multiply (the Mitscherlich–Baule form $y = A\prod_i(1 - \mathrm{e}^{-c_i(x_i + b_i)})$), an increase in any one nutrient always raises yield at least somewhat, unlike the plateau of the law of the minimum (dashed lines in Panel C above). Actual crop responses lie between the two, and field trial data are often fitted with linear-plateau or quadratic models; whichever model is used, the parameters must be estimated from field trials.

**Example 4.3.1** Finding fertilizer rates with Eq. (4.1). For a wheat crop at a given site, let $A = 10\,\mathrm{t\,ha^{-1}}$, $c = 0.02\,\mathrm{ha\,kg^{-1}}$ and $b = 20\,\mathrm{kg\,ha^{-1}}$.

(a) Nitrogen rate for 90% of maximum yield: $1 - \mathrm{e}^{-c(x+b)} = 0.9$, so $x_{90} = \ln 10 / c - b = 2.303/0.02 - 20 = 95\,\mathrm{kg\,ha^{-1}}$.

(b) Economically optimal nitrogen rate: profit is greatest when the marginal yield $\mathrm{d}y/\mathrm{d}x = Ac\,\mathrm{e}^{-c(x+b)}$ equals the price ratio $r$ of fertilizer to grain. If 1 kg of nitrogen costs as much as 10 kg of grain, $r = 0.010\,\mathrm{t\,kg^{-1}}$, so $x^* = \ln(Ac/r)/c - b = \ln 20 / 0.02 - 20 = 130\,\mathrm{kg\,ha^{-1}}$, and the yield is $y = 10(1 - \mathrm{e}^{-3.0}) = 9.50\,\mathrm{t\,ha^{-1}}$.

Going from 95 to $130\,\mathrm{kg\,ha^{-1}}$ increases yield by only $0.5\,\mathrm{t\,ha^{-1}}$. The cheaper the fertilizer (the smaller $r$), the closer the economically optimal rate lies to the level that saturates yield, and the lower the fertilizer use efficiency of the crop and the more nutrient is lost to the environment.

## 4.4. Soil: The Reservoir of Nutrients

### 4.4.1. Soil Particles and Cation Exchange

Soil is a heterogeneous system of solid, liquid and gas phases. The inorganic particles of the solid phase are a reservoir of potassium, phosphorus, calcium, magnesium and iron, and organic matter contains nitrogen, phosphorus and sulfur. The liquid phase, the soil solution, contains dissolved mineral ions and is the medium through which ions move to the root surface; roots exchange gases with the soil mainly through the air-filled pores between particles. By diameter, soil particles are classified as gravel (larger than $2\,\mathrm{mm}$), coarse sand ($0.2$–$2\,\mathrm{mm}$), fine sand ($0.02$–$0.2\,\mathrm{mm}$), silt ($0.002$–$0.02\,\mathrm{mm}$) and clay (smaller than $0.002\,\mathrm{mm}$, that is, $2\,\mu\mathrm{m}$).

The surfaces of soil particles are mostly negatively charged. Clay minerals are layered lattices of silica tetrahedra and alumina octahedra; when $\ce{Si^4+}$ and $\ce{Al^3+}$ in the lattice are replaced by cations of lower charge ($\ce{Al^3+}$ for $\ce{Si^4+}$, $\ce{Mg^2+}$ for $\ce{Al^3+}$), the lattice acquires negative charge. The negative charge of organic particles (humus) arises from the dissociation of carboxyl and phenolic hydroxyl groups. Cations such as $\ce{K+}$, $\ce{NH4+}$, $\ce{Ca^2+}$ and $\ce{Mg^2+}$ are adsorbed on these negative charges, are not easily leached by water, and form a nutrient reserve available to roots; they can in turn be displaced by other cations in solution and pass into the soil solution.

**Definition 4.4.1 (Cation Exchange Capacity)** The total amount of cations that a soil adsorbs and can exchange with the solution is called its **cation exchange capacity** (CEC). It is usually expressed as the amount of substance of cation charge adsorbed per kilogram of dry soil, in $\mathrm{cmol(+)\,kg^{-1}}$, numerically equal to the older unit milliequivalents per 100 grams ($\mathrm{meq}/100\,\mathrm{g}$).

CEC depends on the type and content of clay and on organic matter content:

| Property | Montmorillonite | Illite | Kaolinite |
|---|---|---|---|
| Particle size / $\mu\mathrm{m}$ | 0.01–1.0 | 0.1–2.0 | 0.1–5.0 |
| Shape | Irregular thin plates | Irregular thin plates | Hexagonal crystals |
| Cohesion, swelling on wetting | High | Medium | Low |
| Cation exchange capacity / ($\mathrm{cmol(+)\,kg^{-1}}$) | 80–100 | 15–40 | 3–15 |
| Common in | Weakly weathered soils | Weakly weathered soils | Strongly weathered soils (tropics) |

The CEC of humus is higher still, above $200\,\mathrm{cmol(+)\,kg^{-1}}$, so although organic matter makes up only a few percent of soil it often contributes a considerable part of the exchange capacity. Soils with a high CEC retain fertilizer well. Exchange follows equivalence of charge (one $\ce{Ca^2+}$ displaces two $\ce{K+}$), and exchange power varies with ionic valence and hydrated radius, roughly $\ce{Al^3+} > \ce{Ca^2+} > \ce{Mg^2+} > \ce{K+} \approx \ce{NH4+} > \ce{Na+}$.

**Example 4.4.1** Estimating exchangeable potassium in the plow layer of a field. Let the soil contain 25% illite-type clay (CEC taken as $25\,\mathrm{cmol(+)\,kg^{-1}}$) and 3% organic matter (taken as $200\,\mathrm{cmol(+)\,kg^{-1}}$); exchangeable $\ce{K+}$ is 3% of the CEC, the plow layer is $0.2\,\mathrm{m}$ deep, and the bulk density is $1.3\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$.

The CEC of the soil is about $0.25 \times 25 + 0.03 \times 200 = 12.3\,\mathrm{cmol(+)\,kg^{-1}}$, and exchangeable potassium is $0.03 \times 12.3 = 0.37\,\mathrm{cmol\,kg^{-1}} = 3.7\,\mathrm{mmol\,kg^{-1}}$, that is, $3.7 \times 39.1 = 144\,\mathrm{mg\,K\,kg^{-1}}$. The mass of the plow layer per hectare is $0.2\,\mathrm{m} \times 10^4\,\mathrm{m^2} \times 1300\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}} = 2.6 \times 10^6\,\mathrm{kg}$, so exchangeable potassium is about $375\,\mathrm{kg\,ha^{-1}}$, roughly twice the potassium uptake of a high-yielding crop. Only a small fraction of it is dissolved in the soil solution at any moment, however, and how much the roots actually obtain also depends on the rate of diffusion of potassium to the root surface (Section 4.5.1).

Anions behave in the opposite way: $\ce{NO3-}$ and $\ce{Cl-}$ are repelled by the negatively charged particles and remain in the soil solution, and in most agricultural soils the anion exchange capacity is much smaller than the cation exchange capacity, so nitrate is highly mobile in soil and easily leached. Phosphate is a special case. It exchanges with hydroxyl groups on the surfaces of iron- and aluminum-bearing particles and is held tightly, and it forms poorly soluble compounds with $\ce{Ca^2+}$, $\ce{Fe^3+}$ and $\ce{Al^3+}$, being fixed at both low and high pH, so its mobility and availability are very low; this is why phosphorus so often limits plant growth. The roots of white lupin (*Lupinus albus*) and of Proteaceae secrete large amounts of organic anions and protons that release phosphorus from iron, aluminum and calcium phosphates. Sulfate forms sparingly soluble gypsum ($\ce{CaSO4}$) in calcium-containing soils, which releases enough sulfate to meet plant needs without being easily leached.

### 4.4.2. Soil pH and Nutrient Availability

Soil pH affects the growth of roots and soil microorganisms and also determines the solubility of nutrients (figure below). Roots generally grow best in slightly acidic soils of pH $5.5$–$6.5$; fungi predominate in acidic soils and bacteria are more common in alkaline soils. The suitable pH for most crops is 6–7; for potato it is $4.8$–$5.4$, for sweet potato, peanut and tobacco $5.0$–$6.0$, for sugarcane $7.0$–$7.3$ and for sugar beet $7.0$–$7.5$.

The main processes that acidify soil are these: decomposition of organic matter produces $\ce{CO2}$, which forms carbonic acid with soil water ($\ce{CO2 + H2O <=> H+ + HCO3-}$); $\ce{NH4+}$ and $\ce{H2S}$ released by decomposition are oxidized to nitric and sulfuric acid (nitrification and sulfur oxidation); plants and microorganisms release $\ce{H+}$ when they take up and assimilate $\ce{NH4+}$ (see 7.13.5); and heavy rain leaches $\ce{K+}$, $\ce{Mg^2+}$, $\ce{Ca^2+}$ and $\ce{Mn^2+}$ displaced by $\ce{H+}$. In arid regions the cations released by rock weathering are not leached and the soil remains alkaline.

Acidity promotes rock weathering and increases the solubility of carbonates, sulfates and some phosphates, bringing $\ce{K+}$, $\ce{Mg^2+}$, $\ce{Ca^2+}$ and $\ce{Mn^2+}$ into solution. Under strong acidity, however, these ions are also more easily leached, and below pH about 5 the solubility of $\ce{Al^3+}$ and $\ce{Mn^2+}$ rises to toxic levels, phosphorus is fixed by iron and aluminum, and the survival and nodulation of rhizobia are inhibited. Above pH 6–7 many elements form insoluble compounds: the availability of iron, manganese, zinc, copper and boron decreases, and phosphorus forms insoluble calcium phosphate; molybdenum is the reverse, its availability increasing under alkaline conditions. Liming (a mixture of $\ce{CaO}$, $\ce{CaCO3}$ and $\ce{Ca(OH)2}$) raises the pH of acidic soils and reduces leaching but may lower the availability of iron and other elements; applying sulfur lowers the pH of alkaline soils, because microorganisms oxidize sulfur to sulfuric acid.

The green shading is pH 5.5–6.5, at which most nutrients are highly available. On the acid side the availability of iron, manganese, zinc, copper and boron is high while that of phosphorus, calcium, magnesium and molybdenum is low, and below pH about 5 $\ce{Al^3+}$ reaches toxic concentrations; on the alkaline side the availability of iron, manganese, zinc, copper, boron and phosphorus falls and that of molybdenum rises.](./plots/soil-ph-availability.py)

### 4.4.3. Saline Soils and Heavy Metals

An excess of mineral ions in the soil is called salinization. The most common salts in saline soils are $\ce{NaCl}$ and $\ce{Na2SO4}$. Salts lower the **water potential** of the soil solution, making it hard for roots to take up water, and excess $\ce{Na+}$ and $\ce{Cl-}$ can also cause ionic toxicity (the physiology of salt stress is treated in [7.13.27](/7.13.27)). In arid and semiarid regions rainfall is insufficient to leach salts below the root zone, and irrigation that does not supply enough water for leaching also causes secondary salinization of soil: irrigation water contains $100$–$1000\,\mathrm{g\,m^{-3}}$ of mineral ions, and one crop season needs about $10\,000\,\mathrm{m^3\,ha^{-1}}$ of water, so $1$–$10\,\mathrm{t\,ha^{-1}}$ of salt is brought in each season, which accumulates considerably over the years. Many plants are damaged at fairly low salt concentrations; salt-tolerant plants can survive on saline soil, and **halophytes** even grow better at high salinity. Some exclude excess ions at the root, some take them up and excrete them through salt glands on the leaves, and many compartmentalize ions in the vacuole.

Heavy metals such as zinc, copper, cobalt, nickel, mercury, lead, cadmium, silver and chromium accumulate in soil and are toxic to plants and humans. A few plants can accumulate large amounts of a particular heavy metal in the body without damage (hyperaccumulators), and can be used to remove metals from contaminated soil, which is phytoremediation: the plants are grown, the shoots harvested and properly disposed of.

## 4.5. Roots and Nutrient Acquisition

### 4.5.1. Nutrient Movement to the Root Surface: Interception, Mass Flow and Diffusion

Roots can absorb only the nutrients that reach the root surface. Nutrients in the soil reach the root surface in three ways: root interception (the root meets the nutrient directly as it grows, accounting for only a small share), *mass flow* (transpiration draws the soil solution, with its dissolved nutrients, toward the root) and diffusion (root uptake lowers the concentration at the root surface and the nutrient diffuses toward the root down its concentration gradient; see **Fick's law**, [2.5.1](/2.5.1)).

The nutrient flux delivered by mass flow equals the water flux times the concentration in the soil solution. It is important for nutrients of high solubility and high concentration (such as nitrate) and has almost no effect for nutrients present at very low concentrations (phosphate, zinc).

**Example 4.5.1** How much of the requirement can mass flow meet? Let a crop transpire $400\,\mathrm{mm}$ of water in a season, that is, $4 \times 10^6\,\mathrm{L\,ha^{-1}}$, and let the soil solution contain $2\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$ of $\ce{NO3-}$, $0.5\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$ of $\ce{K+}$ and $2\,\mu\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$ of phosphate. The nutrients delivered to the root surface by mass flow are:

- nitrogen: $4 \times 10^6 \times 2 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{mol} \times 14\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}} = 112\,\mathrm{kg\,ha^{-1}}$;
- potassium: $4 \times 10^6 \times 0.5 \times 10^{-3} \times 39.1 = 78\,\mathrm{kg\,ha^{-1}}$;
- phosphorus: $4 \times 10^6 \times 2 \times 10^{-6} \times 31.0 = 0.25\,\mathrm{kg\,ha^{-1}}$.

A high-yielding maize crop takes up about $200\,\mathrm{kg\,ha^{-1}}$ each of nitrogen and potassium and about $35\,\mathrm{kg\,ha^{-1}}$ of phosphorus in a season. Mass flow can thus supply a considerable part of the nitrogen, a small part of the potassium and less than 1% of the phosphorus: phosphorus arrives almost entirely by diffusion, together with mycorrhizal hyphae and continued root growth.

When the rate of root uptake exceeds the rate of replenishment by mass flow and diffusion, the concentration near the root surface falls, forming a nutrient depletion zone. The width of the depletion zone is roughly the distance moved by diffusion in a given time, $L \approx \sqrt{2D_{\mathrm{e}}t}$, where $D_{\mathrm{e}}$ is the effective diffusion coefficient of the nutrient in soil. It is much smaller than in water (diffusion occurs tortuously, only in water-filled pores), and for ions strongly adsorbed by soil it must also be divided by the buffer capacity $b$ (the total exchangeable amount per unit amount in solution). Nitrate is hardly adsorbed ($b \approx 1$), and its $D_{\mathrm{e}}$ is about $10^{-11}$–$10^{-10}\,\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$, so it moves a few millimeters per day; phosphate is strongly adsorbed ($b$ of several hundred), $D_{\mathrm{e}}/b$ is about $10^{-15}\,\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$, and it moves only about ten to a few tens of micrometers per day. Depletion zones generally extend about $0.2$–$2\,\mathrm{mm}$ from the root surface and matter especially for phosphorus. This illustrates a basic fact about how roots acquire nutrients: the efficiency of a root depends not only on the rate at which it removes nutrients from the soil solution but also on its continuing growth into new, undepleted soil; without continued growth a root soon exhausts the nutrients near its surface.

### 4.5.2. Root Morphology and Responses to Nutrients

The ability of a plant to acquire water and nutrients is closely related to the size of its root system. In the 1930s Dittmer measured a single 16-week-old winter rye plant: the main and lateral roots numbered about 13 million, with a total length of over 500 km and a surface area of about $200\,\mathrm{m^2}$, and more than $10^{10}$ **root hairs** provided a further surface area of about $300\,\mathrm{m^2}$. The roots of desert mesquite (*Prosopis*) can grow down more than 50 m to reach groundwater; roots of annual crops are generally $0.1$–$2.0\,\mathrm{m}$ deep and extend $0.3$–$1.0\,\mathrm{m}$ horizontally. In natural ecosystems annual root production often exceeds that of the shoots. Monocots form fibrous root systems and dicots taproot systems (see [7.12.20](/7.12.20)).

The most active region for ion uptake is the root hair zone, but the picture differs among ions and species. Uptake of $\ce{Ca^2+}$ by barley roots appears to be confined to the root tip zone; iron is taken up in the root tip zone in barley but over the whole root surface in maize; $\ce{K+}$, $\ce{NO3-}$, $\ce{NH4+}$ and phosphate can be absorbed anywhere on the root surface, though in maize the elongation zone has the highest rates of $\ce{K+}$ accumulation and $\ce{NO3-}$ uptake; and in maize, rice and wetland plants the root tip takes up $\ce{NH4+}$ faster than the elongation zone. Uptake is vigorous in the root tip zone partly because the tissues there need large amounts of nutrients (cell elongation requires accumulation of solutes such as $\ce{K+}$, $\ce{Cl-}$ and $\ce{NO3-}$ to raise osmotic pressure) and partly because the tip is growing into soil that has not yet been depleted. Meristems are often short of carbohydrate, and assimilating ammonium costs less energy than assimilating nitrate ([7.13.6](/7.13.6)), so ammonium is the preferred nitrogen source to support cell division in the root tip.

Plants are sessile and cannot leave an unfavorable environment; they can only look for better conditions within reach. The concentrations of $\ce{CO2}$ and $\ce{O2}$ above ground are relatively uniform, whereas water and nutrients in soil are very uneven in space and time: the concentration of inorganic nitrogen can differ a thousandfold within a few centimeters or a few hours. Roots are guided by gravitropism, thigmotropism, chemotropism and hydrotropism (see [7.13.19](/7.13.19)) and adjust root architecture to the distribution of nutrients (figure below):

- When nutrients are scarce roots grow slowly; as soil nutrients increase, roots proliferate; beyond the optimum concentration, root growth becomes carbohydrate-limited and slows.
- A local nutrient-rich patch (nitrate, phosphate or ammonium) causes lateral roots that grow into it to elongate and branch greatly, while lateral roots elsewhere remain short, a phenomenon first described in barley by Drew (1975).
- In phosphorus deficiency the main root grows more slowly, and lateral roots and root hairs increase and concentrate in the surface soil with higher phosphorus content; white lupin and Proteaceae also form cluster roots (proteoid roots) that secrete organic acids to dissolve phosphorus.
- When fertilization and irrigation make nutrients and water plentiful, a smaller root system suffices for the whole plant, and the plant allocates more resources to shoots and reproductive organs, lowering the **root–shoot ratio**. For example, spring wheat needs only 3.5% of its root system, and lettuce 12%, to supply all its nutrients; this shift in resource allocation is one mechanism by which fertilization raises yield. Nitrogen or phosphorus deficiency raises the root–shoot ratio.

### 4.5.3. Mycorrhizae

Nutrient uptake by roots is usually modified by **mycorrhizae**: a fungus associates with the root, the plant supplies carbohydrate to the fungus, and the fungus supplies mineral nutrients to the plant, and may also increase the host's tolerance of drought and disease. About 90% of land plant species, including most major crops, form mycorrhizae. About four-fifths of these are of the **arbuscular mycorrhiza** type (symbioses of Glomeromycota fungi with a wide range of angiosperms, gymnosperms, ferns and liverworts) and most of the rest are **ectomycorrhizae**; for the types and structure of mycorrhizae see [7.11.31](/7.11.31#sec-31.3.1) and [7.12.20](/7.12.20#sec-20.6.2). Only their significance for mineral nutrition is covered here:

- Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi form a highly branched hyphal network outside the root. The hyphae are only $2$–$10\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ in diameter, can enter fine pores that roots cannot, and extend beyond the depletion zone, taking up phosphorus from soil several centimeters away from the root. Hyphae grow fast, absorb efficiently and translocate rapidly, so with fewer resources they explore the soil more effectively than roots. They are especially important for the immobile elements phosphorus and zinc, while their contribution to nitrogen uptake has not been determined. Some plant phosphate transporters are expressed only in the plant membrane surrounding the arbuscules, where they receive phosphorus from the fungus. When soil available phosphorus is high, the growth promotion by mycorrhiza weakens and sometimes even becomes negative (the fungus consumes more carbon than the benefit of the phosphorus it supplies).
- Ectomycorrhizae occur mainly in trees such as Pinaceae, Fagaceae, Salicaceae, Betulaceae and Myrtaceae. The fungus forms a mantle around the root and a Hartig net between cortical cells, and all nutrients from the soil must first pass through the mantle. Ectomycorrhizal fungi convert insoluble nitrogen and phosphorus in organic matter into soluble forms for the plant, allowing the host to use organic nutrients and grow in boreal forest soils with very little inorganic nutrient.
- Brassicaceae (including *Arabidopsis*), Chenopodiaceae and Proteaceae do not form mycorrhizae. Flooding (paddy rice does not form mycorrhizae, upland rice does), frequent tillage, heavy fertilization, soil fumigation and some fungicides reduce or eliminate mycorrhizae and may reduce the yield of crops that depend heavily on them, such as maize. For most plants forming mycorrhizae is the normal state, and the nonmycorrhizal state is largely the result of agricultural practice.

## 4.6. Soilless Culture and Rational Fertilization

### 4.6.1. Soilless Culture and Nutrient Solutions

Applying solution culture to production gives soilless culture: many greenhouse crops such as tomato, cucumber and lettuce are produced by hydroponics (nutrient film, deep flow, aeroponics, ebb and flow) or by substrate culture (sand, gravel, vermiculite, rock wool, peat, sawdust). Soilless culture is independent of land conditions and season, saves water and fertilizer, makes it easy to control the environment and to industrialize production, and gives cleaner products; but the capital investment is large and the management of the nutrient solution is demanding. The main points of nutrient solution management are:

- The solution contains all the essential macronutrients and micronutrients, with concentrations and proportions set according to the physiological balance of the plant and chemical balance (to avoid precipitation); the modified Hoagland solution is the formulation most used in research (table below).
- The pH is generally held at $5.5$–$6.5$: above pH 7 the availability of phosphorus, calcium, magnesium, iron, manganese, boron and zinc decreases, and below 5 calcium uptake is reduced. Supplying nitrogen as both $\ce{NH4+}$ and $\ce{NO3-}$ slows the rapid rise in pH that occurs when only $\ce{NO3-}$ is supplied (see physiologically alkaline salts in 7.13.5), and most plants grow better when both nitrogen sources are present.
- Iron is supplied as a chelate: inorganic iron salts such as $\ce{FeSO4}$ precipitate as iron hydroxide under alkaline conditions, and as iron phosphate when phosphate is present, becoming unavailable. A **chelate** (a soluble complex of $\ce{Fe^3+}$ with EDTA, DTPA or o,o-EDDHA) keeps iron in solution; iron is released from the chelate after being reduced to $\ce{Fe^2+}$ at the root surface, and the chelating agent diffuses back into the solution to bind new $\ce{Fe^3+}$.
- The solution is aerated (stirring, air bubbling) to supply root respiration, and the root-zone temperature is controlled.

The concentrations in this formulation are the highest values that cause neither toxicity nor salt stress, and can exceed those of the rhizosphere soil solution by several orders of magnitude: for example, phosphorus in soil solution is usually below $2\,\mu\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$ but is $2\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$ in the nutrient solution. The high concentrations let plants grow for long periods without replenishment but may injure seedlings, so in research the solution is often diluted several-fold and replaced frequently.

**Example 4.6.1** Accounting for nitrogen in the nutrient solution. $\ce{KNO3}$ at 6 mM supplies 6 mM nitrogen, $\ce{Ca(NO3)2}$ at 4 mM supplies 8 mM, and $\ce{NH4H2PO4}$ at 2 mM supplies 2 mM, a total of $16\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$, of which ammonium nitrogen is $2/16 = 12.5\,\%$; by mass this is $16 \times 14.0 = 224\,\mathrm{mg\,L^{-1}}$. Potassium is $6\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}} \times 39.1 = 235\,\mathrm{mg\,L^{-1}}$ (plus the negligible $0.05\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$ from $\ce{KCl}$).

### 4.6.2. Fertilizers and Rational Fertilization

In traditional agriculture, crops take up nutrients from the soil, people and animals eat the local crops, and crop residues and human and animal manure return to the soil, so mineral elements cycle within the farmland; the main loss is leaching, especially of nitrate with drainage water. In industrialized high-yield agriculture, large amounts of biomass leave the field and nutrients are removed in one direction, and must be replaced with fertilizer.

- Chemical fertilizers are mostly inorganic salts of nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium. Those containing only one major nutrient are called straight fertilizers (superphosphate, ammonium nitrate, potassium chloride); those containing two or more are called compound or mixed fertilizers, and numbers such as "15-15-15" on the package give the mass percentages of N, $\ce{P2O5}$ and $\ce{K2O}$ in that order (phosphorus and potassium are expressed as oxides, a convention of fertilizer labeling). After long cultivation, micronutrients are also depleted by crops to the point where they must be replaced; many acidic sandy soils in humid regions are deficient in boron, copper, zinc, manganese, molybdenum or iron.
- Organic fertilizers come from animal and plant residues and from natural minerals permitted in organic farming (Chile saltpeter, rock phosphate). The nutrients in animal and plant residues are present in organic form and must be broken down by soil microorganisms (mineralization) before crops can take them up; the rate of mineralization depends on temperature, moisture, oxygen, pH and the kind and number of microorganisms, and varies greatly, with nutrients being released over days to years. Farms that use only organic fertilizer therefore often need to apply much more nitrogen and phosphorus, and losses may be larger; but organic residues improve soil structure and increase water retention in drought and drainage in heavy rain.
- Crops need different amounts of fertilizer at different growth stages: during seed germination they rely on the nutrients stored in the seed; uptake rises gradually as the seedling grows, is greatest during flowering and seed set, then declines as growth weakens, almost ceases at maturity and may even be reversed by efflux of mineral elements during senescence. Applying fertilizer in stages (basal and top dressing) according to the pattern of demand, and ensuring supply during the period of greatest demand and greatest sensitivity to deficiency (the nutritional critical period), is the basic principle of rational fertilization.
- Indicators for top dressing: morphological indicators (plant type and leaf color, which is a sensitive indicator of nitrogen nutrition) and physiological indicators (nutrient content of functional leaves compared with the critical concentration; chlorophyll content; nitrate content and nitrate reductase activity; starch content in rice leaf sheaths, and so on).
- Measures that improve fertilizer efficiency: appropriate irrigation (nutrients must be dissolved in water to be absorbed, and water also promotes root growth), appropriate deep plowing and soil improvement (better aeration, promoting root development), improved light (photosynthate provides the energy for nutrient uptake and assimilation), deep placement and foliar application (to reduce volatilization and fixation), and nitrification inhibitors to reduce nitrate leaching and denitrification losses.

Most plants can also absorb mineral nutrients sprayed on the leaves (foliar nutrition), which is particularly useful late in the crop's life when root uptake capacity declines, or when an element is easily fixed by the soil; how leaves absorb nutrients is described in Section 5.5 of [7.13.5](/7.13.5).

## Questions

1. Why is it much harder to prove by solution culture that nickel is essential than that potassium is essential? What must be attended to in the experimental design?

   **Answer** Plants need very little nickel (about $0.1\,\mathrm{mg\,kg^{-1}}$ of dry matter, an amount of substance only about $10^{-5}$ that of potassium), so trace nickel in reagents, containers, water or even air is enough to meet the need, and the nickel stored in the seed suffices for one generation. One must therefore use highly purified reagents and water and nickel-free containers, and grow plants in nickel-free solution for several generations until the nickel in the seed is diluted away, before deficiency symptoms (necrotic leaf tips, urea accumulation, seeds that fail to germinate) can be observed; one must then show that supplying nickel corrects the symptoms and that no other element can substitute. The requirement for potassium is large, and symptoms appear quickly if potassium is omitted.

2. One farmer finds that the lower leaves of his tomato plants are yellow between the veins while the veins remain green. Another finds that the young leaves on new citrus shoots are yellow between the veins, on calcareous soil, and nitrogen fertilizer brings no improvement. Which element is most likely deficient in each case, and how can this be confirmed?

   **Answer** Tomato: interveinal chlorosis in old leaves points to magnesium, which is mobile and tied to chlorophyll (potassium deficiency shows mainly as marginal scorch). Citrus: interveinal chlorosis in young leaves points to the immobile iron (or manganese, whose deficiency is often accompanied by small necrotic spots); the high pH of calcareous soil precipitates iron and makes its availability very low, consistent with iron deficiency, and the failure of nitrogen to help also rules out nitrogen deficiency. To confirm: measure magnesium or iron (and manganese) in functional leaves and compare with the critical concentration; or spray magnesium sulfate (tomato) or chelated iron (citrus) on the leaves and see whether newly formed leaves recover their green color. Note that total iron in leaves is sometimes not low (iron precipitates in the apoplast), so the spray test is more reliable for diagnosing iron deficiency.

3. (Calculation) In a maize field the soil solution contains $0.3\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$ of $\ce{K+}$, the crop transpires $450\,\mathrm{mm}$ in a season and takes up $180\,\mathrm{kg\,ha^{-1}}$ of potassium. How much potassium can mass flow supply? What supplies the rest? If the effective diffusion coefficient of potassium in the soil (buffering already included) is $2 \times 10^{-12}\,\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$, about how far does potassium diffuse in one day?

   **Answer** The transpired water is $0.45\,\mathrm{m} \times 10^4\,\mathrm{m^2} = 4.5 \times 10^3\,\mathrm{m^3} = 4.5 \times 10^6\,\mathrm{L\,ha^{-1}}$; the potassium delivered by mass flow is $4.5 \times 10^6 \times 0.3 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{mol} \times 39.1\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}} = 52.8\,\mathrm{kg\,ha^{-1}}$, about 29% of the requirement. The remaining $127\,\mathrm{kg\,ha^{-1}}$ or so comes by diffusion (and root interception and continued root growth). The diffusion distance is $L \approx \sqrt{2 \times 2 \times 10^{-12} \times 86\,400} = \sqrt{3.5 \times 10^{-7}}\,\mathrm{m} \approx 0.6\,\mathrm{mm}$, so each day only potassium within about half a millimeter of the root surface can diffuse to the root, and roots must be densely distributed and keep growing into new soil.

4. (Calculation) A sandy loam has a cation exchange capacity of $8\,\mathrm{cmol(+)\,kg^{-1}}$, of which 60% is exchangeable $\ce{Ca^2+}$. The plow layer is $0.2\,\mathrm{m}$ deep with bulk density $1.4\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$. How many kilograms of exchangeable calcium are there per hectare of plow layer? If applied $\ce{K+}$ displaces 5% of it, how many kilograms of potassium are adsorbed?

   **Answer** The charge of exchangeable calcium is $0.6 \times 8 = 4.8\,\mathrm{cmol(+)\,kg^{-1}}$, corresponding to an amount of $\ce{Ca^2+}$ of $2.4\,\mathrm{cmol\,kg^{-1}} = 24\,\mathrm{mmol\,kg^{-1}}$, that is, $24 \times 40.1 = 962\,\mathrm{mg\,kg^{-1}}$. The plow layer weighs $0.2 \times 10^4 \times 1400 = 2.8 \times 10^6\,\mathrm{kg\,ha^{-1}}$, so exchangeable calcium is $962 \times 2.8 \times 10^6\,\mathrm{mg} = 2.7 \times 10^3\,\mathrm{kg\,ha^{-1}}$. Displacing 5% of the calcium means $0.05 \times 4.8 = 0.24\,\mathrm{cmol(+)\,kg^{-1}}$ of charge, taken up by an equal charge of $\ce{K+}$: $2.4\,\mathrm{mmol\,K\,kg^{-1}} \times 39.1 = 94\,\mathrm{mg\,kg^{-1}}$, or about $263\,\mathrm{kg}$ of potassium adsorbed per hectare.

5. Why is it said that "plants that do not form mycorrhizae are a minority in nature but very common in modern farmland"? What does this suggest for fertilization?

   **Answer** About 90% of land plants form mycorrhizae under natural conditions, so mycorrhiza is the norm. But flooding, frequent tillage that destroys the hyphal network, heavy application of phosphorus and nitrogen fertilizer (the plant no longer needs the fungus), soil fumigation and fungicides all suppress mycorrhizae, so crops in intensive farmland are often nonmycorrhizal or weakly mycorrhizal, whereas only a few groups such as Brassicaceae never form mycorrhizae. Implication: under high phosphorus inputs, the dependence of crops on phosphorus fertilizer is partly caused by agricultural practice itself; reducing tillage, sensibly lowering phosphorus rates and rotating with mycorrhizal host crops can restore mycorrhizae, improve the use of the immobile phosphorus and zinc, and reduce fertilizer inputs and losses. The effect of mycorrhizae varies with fungus, crop and soil, however, and there may be no benefit when soil available phosphorus is high.
