The previous chapter discussed how plants recognize and resist pathogenic microbes. The biotic environment of plants extends far beyond pathogens. About half of the nearly one million species of insects feed on plants; pollinators, seed dispersers and the natural enemies that prey on herbivores ally with plants; rhizobia and mycorrhizal fungi exchange nutrients for photosynthate; parasitic plants feed directly from the vascular system of other plants; and neighboring plants compete for light, water and nutrients, and some release chemicals into the soil that inhibit other species. Plants' responses have two features. First, they are mainly chemical: the more than one hundred thousand secondary metabolites that plants synthesize are mostly involved in defense, attraction or competition, and they come from a small number of biosynthetic pathways. Second, many responses are induced: plants synthesize defense compounds in large amounts only after being attacked, the signal is initiated by wounding and by the saliva of herbivores, spreads through the whole plant by electrical signals and **jasmonic acid**, and is even passed to neighbors in the form of volatiles. This chapter first treats the physical and chemical defenses against herbivores, then the signals and ecological functions of induced defense, then mutualism, parasitic plants and allelopathy, and finally how climate change alters these relationships. The theory of plant–herbivore coevolution is in [7.10.17](/7.10.17#sec-17.2.3); the morphology of defensive structures and pollination syndromes are in [7.12.32](/7.12.32); the microbiology of root nodule and mycorrhizal symbioses is in [7.11.31](/7.11.31); the biochemistry of terpenoids and aromatic amino acids is in [7.1.26](/7.1.26) and [7.1.27](/7.1.27); and the synthesis and signaling of jasmonic acid are in [7.13.15](/7.13.15#sec-15.6.2).

## 26.1. Plants and Herbivorous Arthropods

### 26.1.1. Modes of Herbivory

Arthropods, including insects, arachnids (spiders and mites), myriapods and crustaceans, are the most species-rich group of animals. Of the roughly one million described insect species, about half feed on plants; insects and plants have coevolved for more than 350 million years. Most herbivorous insects are specialists: about 90% of species feed on plants of only one family or a few related species, and only about 10% are generalists. On the other hand, the great majority of plants are resistant to the great majority of insects. Even so, herbivorous arthropods still reduce crop yields by roughly 10%–30% each year.

Depending on the mode of feeding, the damage herbivores do to plants and the plant response differ greatly (Table 26.1).

| Feeding mode | Examples | Tissue damage | Main signal induced |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chewing | Lepidopteran larvae (caterpillars), locusts, beetles | Large, tears tissue | **Jasmonic acid** |
| Cell-content feeding | Spider mites, thrips | Moderate, pierces epidermal and mesophyll cells | Jasmonic acid |
| Phloem feeding | Aphids, whiteflies, planthoppers | Small, stylet passes between cells to the sieve tube | **Salicylic acid** (similar to the response to pathogens) |
| Leaf mining | Larvae of leaf-mining flies and moths | Feed on mesophyll between the upper and lower epidermis | Jasmonic acid |
| Root piercing | Root-knot nematodes, cyst nematodes | Pierce the root vascular cylinder | Similar to pathogens ([7.13.25](/7.13.25)) |

Phloem-feeding insects do little direct damage, but they are often vectors of viruses: aphids and whiteflies inject viruses directly into sieve tubes, causing losses far greater than the feeding itself.

### 26.1.2. Physical Defenses

The first line of defense of a plant is mechanical barriers. Thorns have several origins: stem thorns are modified branches (citrus, honey locust), leaf spines are modified leaves (cacti), and prickles are formed from epidermis and cortex and break off easily (rose); they act mainly against large mammals and have little effect on small insects (morphology and origins in [7.12.32](/7.12.32#sec-32.5.3)). More effective against insects are **trichomes**: dense hairs hinder small insects from walking, feeding and laying eggs; **glandular trichomes** store terpenoids and phenolics in small sacs between the cell wall and the cuticle and release them when broken, repelling insects by smell and bitterness, or secrete sticky substances that trap small insects. Tomato glandular trichomes produce compounds that repel whiteflies; those of wild potato (*Solanum berthaultii*) release (*E*)-β-farnesene, the alarm pheromone of aphids, causing aphids to leave. The stinging hair of nettle (*Urtica dioica*) is a needle-like tube with a multicellular base and a single-celled tip, the cell wall reinforced with silica and the tip covered with a tiny glassy bead that breaks off at a touch, leaving a sharp tip that pierces the skin, while the spongy tissue at the base acts like a syringe plunger and injects a mixture of histamine, serotonin, formic acid, oxalic acid and tartaric acid into the skin.

Mineral crystals are another kind of barrier. Silica deposited in the epidermal cell walls of grasses (phytoliths) makes leaves tough, hard to chew and abrasive to insect mandibles; horsetail stems contain so much silica that they were used to scour pots. Calcium oxalate crystals occur in plants of more than two hundred families, often concentrated in idioblasts; bundles of needle-shaped raphides (as in agave, taro and dumb cane) have sharp tips that can wound the mucous membranes of the mouth and esophagus of feeders and open a path for other toxins into the tissue. When touched, the compound leaves of *Mimosa pudica* fold their leaflets within seconds, and when the stimulus is strong the whole leaf droops; this seismonastic movement, driven by turgor changes in the pulvinus ([7.13.19](/7.13.19)), may also frighten off feeding insects.

## 26.2. Secondary Metabolites

### 26.2.1. Primary and Secondary Metabolism

The compounds present in all plants and directly involved in growth and development, such as sugars, amino acids, fatty acids, nucleotides, and the polysaccharides, proteins, membranes and nucleic acids built from them, are called primary metabolites. Another class of compounds is highly diverse, has strongly taxon-specific distribution and is mostly not required for growth but acts in the interaction between the plant and its environment; these are the **secondary metabolites** (also called specialized metabolites, [7.12.3](/7.12.3#sec-3.4.6)). More than one hundred thousand plant secondary metabolites are known, and most natural products known to humans come from plants.

Some plant hormones (**gibberellins**, **abscisic acid**, **brassinosteroids**, **cytokinins** and **strigolactones**) are synthesized by secondary metabolic pathways but are counted as primary metabolites because all plants need them. Conversely, the roles of many secondary metabolites in plants were at first unclear. In 1959 G. Fraenkel proposed that the reason for the existence of secondary metabolites is "to repel and attract insects", which started the study of chemical ecology. We now know that their functions include defense against herbivores and pathogens, attraction of pollinators and seed dispersers, signaling to symbiotic microbes, inhibition of neighboring plants, and protection against ultraviolet radiation and oxidative stress. By biosynthetic origin, secondary metabolites fall mainly into three classes: terpenoids, phenolics and nitrogen-containing compounds (figure below).

### 26.2.2. Terpenoids

**Terpenoids** (terpenes, **isoprenoids**, [7.1.12](/7.1.12)) are the largest class of plant secondary metabolites, with tens of thousands known. They are built from five-carbon isoprene units, so the number of carbon atoms is generally a multiple of 5:

$$
n_{\mathrm{C}} = 5k, \qquad k = 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, \dots, \tag{26.1}
$$

This empirical rule is called the *isoprene rule* (Wallach 1887; reformulated by Ruzicka in 1953 as the "biogenetic isoprene rule"). The classes by $k$ are as follows.

| Class | Carbons | Precursor | Site of synthesis | Examples and functions |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Monoterpenes | C10 | Geranyl diphosphate (GPP) | Plastid | Menthol, limonene, α- and β-pinene (resin, volatiles); pyrethrins (from pyrethrum daisy, acting on insect voltage-gated sodium channels) |
| Sesquiterpenes | C15 | Farnesyl diphosphate (FPP) | Cytosol | Artemisinin, gossypol, capsidiol and rishitin (phytoalexins, [7.13.25](/7.13.25)); abscisic acid arises by carotenoid cleavage (C15, but not via FPP) |
| Diterpenes | C20 | Geranylgeranyl diphosphate (GGPP) | Plastid | Gibberellins, taxol, resin acids such as abietic acid, the phytol side chain of chlorophyll |
| Triterpenes | C30 | 2 FPP → squalene | Cytosol, ER | Sterols, brassinosteroids, saponins, cardiac glycosides (digitoxin), limonoids of Meliaceae (azadirachtin) |
| Tetraterpenes | C40 | 2 GGPP → phytoene | Plastid | Carotenoids ([7.13.7](/7.13.7)) |
| Polyterpenes | $> \mathrm{C}_{40}$ | Many IPP | Cytosol | Natural rubber: cis-1,4-polyisoprene, molecular weight up to $10^6$ |

The precursors of all terpenoids are isopentenyl diphosphate (IPP) and its isomer dimethylallyl diphosphate (DMAPP). Plants have two pathways for the synthesis of IPP, in two compartments:

- The mevalonate (MVA) pathway is in the cytosol and ER and starts from acetyl-CoA: 3 molecules of acetyl-CoA form **mevalonate** via acetoacetyl-CoA and HMG-CoA (HMG-CoA reductase consumes 2 NADPH and is the rate-limiting step), and two phosphorylations and one decarboxylation (3 ATP consumed in all, 1 $\ce{CO2}$ released) then give IPP. It mainly supplies sesquiterpenes, triterpenes and sterols.
- The methylerythritol phosphate (MEP) pathway is in plastids and starts from pyruvate and glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate, directly using the products and reducing power of photosynthesis. It mainly supplies monoterpenes, diterpenes, carotenoids, the chlorophyll side chain, gibberellins and abscisic acid.

There is a small exchange of IPP between the two compartments. IPP and DMAPP are condensed "head to tail" by prenyltransferases in succession to GPP, FPP and GGPP; two molecules of FPP or GGPP condense "tail to tail" to give the skeletons of triterpenes and tetraterpenes. Terpene synthases then cyclize these linear precursors to a great variety of skeletons, and one terpene synthase often produces several products, which is one source of terpenoid diversity.

**Example 26.2.1 (The Cost of Making Terpenoids)** By the MVA pathway, how much acetyl-CoA, ATP and NADPH are needed to synthesize 1 molecule of a monoterpene (C10)? How many glucose molecules does this correspond to?

**Solution** One IPP requires 3 acetyl-CoA, 3 ATP and 2 NADPH and releases 1 $\ce{CO2}$. A monoterpene is made of 2 C5 units (IPP + DMAPP) and needs 6 acetyl-CoA, 6 ATP and 4 NADPH. One molecule of glucose gives 2 molecules of acetyl-CoA by glycolysis and pyruvate dehydrogenase, so the carbon skeleton requires 3 molecules of glucose (18 carbons), of which 10 carbons enter the product, a carbon efficiency of about 56%; in addition 6 ATP and 4 NADPH must be paid, coming from other respiration or photosynthesis. Per unit mass terpenoids are much more expensive than carbohydrates of equal carbon content, which is a biochemical basis of the growth–defense trade-off discussed in Section 26.3.6.

The defensive roles of terpenoids are varied: monoterpenes and sesquiterpenes are the main components of volatiles and resins and are toxic or repellent; pyrethrins (and their synthetic analogs, the pyrethroids) are important insecticides; azadirachtin is a strong antifeedant; cardiac glycosides inhibit the animal $\ce{Na+}/\ce{K+}$-ATPase (the **sodium pump**) and can cause cardiac arrest, and are also drugs for heart failure (digitalis); saponins bind sterols in animal cell membranes and destroy membrane integrity.

### 26.2.3. Phenolics

**Definition 26.2.1 (Phenolics)** Secondary metabolites that carry a hydroxyl group (or a functional group derived from it) on an aromatic ring are collectively called **phenolics**; most plant phenolics are synthesized via the shikimate pathway and phenylpropanoid metabolism, and a few via the acetate–malonate pathway.

The **shikimate pathway** ([7.1.27](/7.1.27)) converts phosphoenolpyruvate and erythrose-4-phosphate to the aromatic amino acids (phenylalanine, tyrosine, tryptophan); animals lack this pathway, so the aromatic amino acids are essential amino acids for animals, and glyphosate, which inhibits EPSP synthase in this pathway, has very low direct toxicity to animals. Phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL) removes the amino group of phenylalanine to give *trans*-cinnamic acid, the branch point between primary metabolism and phenylpropanoid metabolism, and PAL activity is strongly induced by light, wounding and pathogen infection. The main phenolics produced are:

- Simple phenolics and coumarins: C6–C1 salicylic acid and C6–C3 caffeic and ferulic acids; among coumarins, the linear furanocoumarins (psoralens and others) are themselves nontoxic but, activated by UV-A, intercalate into DNA and cross-link it, and are phototoxic to both insects and mammals (celery and parsnip of the Apiaceae).
- Lignin: a complex polymer formed by radical polymerization of three monolignols (*p*-coumaryl, coniferyl and sinapyl alcohols), giving H, G and S units respectively; it makes the cell wall rigid and impermeable and makes tissue hard to digest.
- Flavonoids: C6–C3–C6 skeleton, formed by chalcone synthase from 1 molecule of *p*-coumaroyl-CoA and 3 molecules of malonyl-CoA. Anthocyanins color flowers and fruits and attract pollinators and dispersers; flavones and flavonols absorb ultraviolet light and protect leaves from UV-B damage ([7.13.16](/7.13.16)); isoflavonoids are the phytoalexins of legumes and also have estrogen-like effects; flavonoids are also signals secreted by legume roots that induce the *nod* genes of rhizobia ([7.11.31](/7.11.31)).
- Tannins: condensed tannins (proanthocyanidins, polymers of flavonoids) and hydrolyzable tannins (esters of gallic acid with sugars). They bind proteins and precipitate them, reducing the digestion and use of dietary protein by herbivores and giving unripe fruit an astringent taste.

### 26.2.4. Nitrogen-Containing Compounds

**Alkaloids** are nitrogen-containing heterocyclic compounds, mostly synthesized from amino acids (lysine, tyrosine, tryptophan, ornithine, arginine); more than ten thousand are known, found in about 20% of vascular plants and more frequently in Fabaceae, Papaveraceae, Solanaceae, Apocynaceae, Ranunculaceae, Menispermaceae, Rutaceae, Rubiaceae and others. Most alkaloids are protonated at the pH of the cell sap, are weakly basic and are stored in the vacuole as water-soluble salts. Many alkaloids act on the animal nervous system: nicotine is synthesized from ornithine in tobacco roots and transported through the xylem to the leaves, where it mimics acetylcholine and activates nicotinic receptors; morphine and codeine (from tyrosine) are components of opium poppy latex and bind opioid receptors; caffeine is a purine alkaloid; cocaine, quinine and vinblastine are all important drugs.

Cyanogenic glycosides are themselves nontoxic; when tissue is broken they are hydrolyzed by β-glucosidases stored separately, producing a cyanohydrin that releases hydrogen cyanide (HCN) spontaneously or under the action of hydroxynitrile lyase:

$$
\ce{R1R2C(OGlc)CN + H2O ->[\beta\text{-glucosidase}] R1R2C(OH)CN + Glc ->[\text{hydroxynitrile lyase}] R1R2C=O + HCN}. \tag{26.2}
$$

HCN binds the heme iron of mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase and blocks the respiratory chain, so it is highly toxic to almost all aerobic organisms. Dhurrin of sorghum comes from tyrosine, linamarin and lotaustralin of cassava from valine and isoleucine, and amygdalin in the seeds of almond, apricot, peach and cherry from phenylalanine.

**Example 26.2.2 (Cyanide in Cassava)** The cyanogenic glycoside of cassava roots is mainly linamarin (molecular weight 247; 1 molecule releases 1 molecule of HCN, molecular weight 27). In roots of bitter cultivars the HCN that can be released is as high as several hundred milligrams per kilogram fresh weight. Suppose a root releases HCN at $300\,\mathrm{mg\,kg^{-1}}$ and the oral lethal dose of HCN for humans is about $1\,\mathrm{mg}$ per kilogram body weight; how much raw root could reach the lethal dose for a 60 kg person? How much linamarin does this correspond to?

**Solution** The lethal dose is about $60\,\mathrm{mg}$ HCN, equivalent to $60/300 = 0.2\,\mathrm{kg}$ of raw root. The corresponding linamarin is $60 \times 247/27 \approx 550\,\mathrm{mg}$. This is why bitter cassava must be processed by soaking, fermenting, sun-drying or boiling: breaking the tissue brings β-glucosidase into contact with the glycoside, and the HCN released (boiling point 26 °C) volatilizes. Long-term consumption of insufficiently processed cassava causes nerve damage (spastic paralysis) even when each dose is far below the lethal dose.

**Glucosinolates** (thioglucosides) are the characteristic defense compounds of the Brassicales (Brassicaceae, Cleomaceae, Caricaceae and others) and consist of glucose, an amino acid (chain-elongated derivatives of methionine, tryptophan, phenylalanine and others) and sulfur. They are stored in different cells from the myrosinase (a thioglucosidase) that hydrolyzes them: "S cells" rich in glucosinolates lie next to idioblasts containing myrosinase without mixing. When tissue is chewed, the two mix,

$$
\ce{R-C(=NOSO3^-)-S-Glc + H2O ->[\text{myrosinase}] R-C(=NOSO3^-)-SH + Glc}, \tag{26.3}
$$

and the unstable aglycone rearranges at once into an isothiocyanate ($\ce{R-N=C=S}$), nitrile or thiocyanate, releasing sulfate. This two-component system, called the "mustard oil bomb", is very effective against most generalist insects; the pungency of mustard, wasabi, radish and Brussels sprouts comes from isothiocyanates, and the main glucosinolate of black mustard is sinigrin (R = allyl).

Grasses (including cereal crops) use another kind of two-component defense: benzoxazinoids (DIBOA, DIMBOA), which are synthesized from indole, an intermediate of the tryptophan pathway, stored in the vacuole as glucosides, and hydrolyzed by glycosidases after tissue disruption to toxic aglycones; benzoxazinoids released by maize roots into the soil can also inhibit neighboring plants.

Besides small molecules, many nitrogen-containing defense compounds of plants are nonprotein amino acids and proteins: canavanine is a structural analog of arginine and is incorporated into proteins by insects that cannot distinguish it, making the proteins nonfunctional; defense proteins (protease inhibitors, α-amylase inhibitors, **lectins**, and cysteine proteases that destroy the insect peritrophic matrix) are discussed in Section 26.3.3.

### 26.2.5. Storage of Constitutive Defenses

**Definition 26.2.2 (Constitutive and Induced Defense)** Defensive structures or substances that are continuously present whether or not the plant is attacked are called *constitutive defense*; defenses that are produced or markedly strengthened only after attack by herbivores or pathogens are called *induced defense*.

Constitutive toxins are also a risk to the plant itself, so they must be stored in leak-proof compartments, separated from vulnerable tissues, and released at the wound only when an attack occurs. There are several modes of storage:

- Resin ducts: the resin ducts in the cortex and phloem of conifers contain a mixture of monoterpenes (α- and β-pinene, limonene), sesquiterpenes and resin acids, which flows out on wounding; the volatiles are toxic to insects, while resin acids seal the wound and glue insect mouthparts, sometimes embedding the whole insect (insects in amber). After attack by bark beetles, methyl jasmonate also induces the formation of new traumatic resin ducts, so that constitutive and induced defenses are combined here.
- **Laticifers**: composed of a chain of connected cells (articulated laticifers, as in mulberry) or a single multinucleate syncytial cell (non-articulated laticifers, as in oleander); latex is an emulsion of complex composition that coagulates on exposure to air. The latex of the rubber tree (*Hevea brasiliensis*) is the source of natural rubber; opium poppy latex contains morphine and codeine; the latex of milkweed (*Asclepias*) and oleander is rich in cardiac glycosides.
- Glandular trichomes and secretory cavities (Section 26.1.2).
- Storage in the vacuole as glycosides, which are water-soluble and nontoxic and spatially separated from the hydrolytic enzymes that activate them, such as cyanogenic glycosides, glucosinolates, benzoxazinoids and cardiac glycosides (Section 26.2.4).

Constitutive defense is unevenly distributed within the plant: the concentration of secondary metabolites is generally higher in young leaves than in mature leaves, and the response to induction is also stronger. This agrees with the prediction of the *optimal defense hypothesis*: defense resources are limited and should be concentrated in the parts of greatest value to fitness and most vulnerable to attack; young leaves are long-lived, have large photosynthetic potential and, being nutrient-rich, attract more insects, and so should receive more protection. Similar patterns have been observed in underground parts. Our everyday spices (basil, sage, thyme, rosemary, oregano) are plants rich in terpenoids and other defense compounds: their "fragrance" originally served to deter insects and fight disease.

## 26.3. Induced Defense

### 26.3.1. Recognizing Herbivores

Constitutive defense has two drawbacks: its cost must be paid whether or not there is an attack, and herbivores can gradually adapt to a defense that is always present. Induced defense is turned on only when needed. For this, the plant must be able to distinguish real feeding from mechanical damage such as wind and hail. The response of most plants to herbivores has two parts: the response to wounding itself, and the recognition of specific compounds in the saliva, regurgitant or oviposition secretions of the herbivore. These compounds are called elicitors, and elicitors from insects are also called *herbivore-associated molecular patterns* (HAMPs), corresponding to the PAMPs of pathogens ([7.13.25](/7.13.25)).

- Fatty acid–amino acid conjugates: volicitin, first isolated from the oral secretions of larvae of the beet armyworm (*Spodoptera exigua*), is *N*-(17-hydroxylinolenoyl)-L-glutamine. Its fatty acid part (linolenic acid 18:3 or linoleic acid 18:2) comes from the plant that was eaten, the glutamine comes from the insect, and they are conjugated in the insect gut. Treating wounds of maize seedlings with very low concentrations of volicitin induces the release of large amounts of terpenoid volatiles, which wounding alone cannot do. There are sites that bind volicitin on the plasma membrane of maize leaves, but the receptor has not been identified.
- Caeliferins: sulfated fatty acids isolated from the regurgitant of the American bird grasshopper (*Schistocerca americana*), with chain lengths of 15–19 carbons and trans double bonds, not of plant origin; their activity is highly species-specific (*Arabidopsis* responds to them, legumes and Solanaceae do not).
- Inceptin: an 11-residue peptide fragment produced when larvae of the fall armyworm digest the γ subunit of chloroplast ATP synthase of cowpea; cowpea recognizes it with an LRR receptor, so the plant "sees" its own digested protein.
- Damage-associated molecular patterns: oligogalacturonides of the plant cell wall, tomato **systemin**, peptides such as maize ZmPep3 ([7.13.25](/7.13.25#sec-25.2.1)).

### 26.3.2. Early Signals and Long-Distance Signals

Within seconds to minutes of elicitor recognition, the cytosolic $\ce{Ca^2+}$ concentration rises rapidly from the resting level of about $100\,\mathrm{nM}$, with $\ce{Ca^2+}$ coming from the cell wall, vacuole, ER and mitochondria; $\ce{Ca^2+}$ binds **calmodulin** and calcium-dependent protein kinases and activates downstream phosphorylation and transcription. In *Arabidopsis*, the calmodulin-binding transcriptional regulator IQD1 activates glucosinolate synthesis genes; in tobacco, silencing the MAPK genes *WIPK* (wound-induced protein kinase) or *SIPK* (salicylic acid-induced protein kinase) weakens different stages of jasmonate synthesis. $\ce{Ca^2+}$ signaling also takes part in negative feedback: in wild tobacco (*Nicotiana attenuata*), silencing two CPK genes makes feeding-induced jasmonate accumulation last longer, the plants produce more defense compounds, and larvae of the specialist tobacco hornworm (*Manduca sexta*) grow more slowly.

Wound signals can reach distant leaves within minutes. Plants have no nervous system, yet they do have electrical signals: when the cotton leafworm (*Spodoptera littoralis*) feeds on bean leaves, a wave of plasma membrane depolarization spreads from the wound into the uninjured region. In *Arabidopsis*, the change of surface potential caused by feeding travels to neighboring leaves at speeds of up to about $9\,\mathrm{cm\,min^{-1}}$, best received by leaves directly connected to the wounded leaf by vasculature; wherever the electrical signal arrives, jasmonate-responsive genes are activated (Mousavi et al. 2013). This propagation requires the glutamate receptor-like channels GLR3.3 and GLR3.6: in the *glr3.3 glr3.6* double mutant the electrical signal no longer propagates after wounding, and the jasmonate response of distant leaves is weakened. Later work found that glutamate released from the wound activates GLRs, causing a wave of rising cytosolic $\ce{Ca^2+}$ that propagates along the vascular system.

### 26.3.3. Jasmonate and Defense Proteins

Most of the defense of plants against herbivorous insects is mediated by **jasmonic acid** (synthesis, receptor and signaling in [7.13.15](/7.13.15#sec-15.6.2) and [7.1.15](/7.1.15#sec-15.5.6)). Within minutes after wounding or feeding, jasmonate accumulates rapidly in local and distant leaves: lipases in the chloroplast release linolenic acid from membrane lipids, 13-lipoxygenase, allene oxide synthase and allene oxide cyclase convert it to 12-oxo-phytodienoic acid (OPDA), which is reduced in peroxisomes and passes through three rounds of β-oxidation to give jasmonic acid; JAR1 conjugates it with isoleucine to the active form JA-Ile. JA-Ile makes the F-box protein COI1 bind the repressor JAZ, JAZ is ubiquitinated by SCF<sup>COI1</sup> and degraded by the 26S proteasome, and the released transcription factor MYC2 activates defense genes and at the same time represses growth-related genes. Jasmonate-deficient mutants of *Arabidopsis*, tomato and maize are readily killed by pests that normal plants resist, and exogenous jasmonate restores resistance almost to wild-type level.

In addition to secondary metabolites, jasmonate-induced defenses include a class of defense proteins that interfere with herbivore digestion:

- Protease inhibitors: proteins synthesized by plants such as legumes and Solanaceae that bind tightly and specifically to the active centers of trypsin, chymotrypsin and other enzymes, so that insects cannot digest protein; insects that feed on plants containing protease inhibitors grow and develop more slowly, and supplementing the diet with amino acids can offset this effect. Transgenic tobacco that highly expresses protease inhibitors suffers less insect damage. Some insects adapt by synthesizing proteases insensitive to the inhibitors.
- α-Amylase inhibitors (legumes) inhibit starch digestion; **lectins** bind glycoproteins of insect gut epithelial cells and interfere with nutrient absorption; a cysteine protease of maize destroys the peritrophic matrix that protects the insect gut epithelium.
- Enzymes that degrade essential amino acids (tomato arginase and threonine deaminase remain active in the insect gut) and polyphenol oxidases (which oxidize phenolics to quinones, which cross-link proteins and lower their nutritional value).

The systemic defense of tomato is the earliest-studied example (Green and Ryan 1972): a few hours after a leaf is wounded, protease inhibitors also accumulate in distant unwounded leaves. The sequence of events is: phloem parenchyma cells of the wounded leaf synthesize the 200-amino-acid prosystemin, prosystemin is cleaved to the 18-residue systemin, systemin is released into the apoplast and binds a receptor on the plasma membrane of neighboring cells, activating MAPK and phospholipase A2, which releases linolenic acid and initiates jasmonate synthesis; jasmonate (or its conjugate) is transported through the phloem to distant leaves, where it activates the protease inhibitor genes. Grafting experiments show that distant leaves need the ability to perceive jasmonate but not necessarily systemin, so the long-distance mobile signal is mainly jasmonate, and systemin acts as an amplifier at the wound.

Panel C of the figure below uses a simple cascade model to illustrate the time scales of induced defense: the wound signal decays within minutes, jasmonate peaks in about half an hour, defense gene mRNA peaks within hours, and defense protein reaches its maximum in about a day and persists for days. Let the wound signal be $S(t) = \mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau_S}$; jasmonate $J$, defense gene mRNA $M$ and protein $P$ satisfy

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}J}{\mathrm{d}t} = k_J S - \frac{J}{\tau_J}, \qquad
\frac{\mathrm{d}M}{\mathrm{d}t} = k_M\frac{J^2}{K^2 + J^2} - \frac{M}{\tau_M}, \qquad
\frac{\mathrm{d}P}{\mathrm{d}t} = k_P M - \frac{P}{\tau_P}, \tag{26.4}
$$

The lifetimes $\tau$ of the stages go from short to long (minutes, hours, days), and each stage integrates the signal of the previous one, so a stimulus of a few minutes is converted into a defense lasting days. This also illustrates a weakness of induced defense: during the day or two before defense proteins have accumulated the plant is still exposed to feeding.

### 26.3.4. Indirect Defense: Volatiles and Natural Enemies

**Definition 26.3.1 (Indirect Defense)** A defense in which the plant does not harm the herbivore directly but reduces feeding by attracting and sustaining the herbivore's natural enemies (predators and parasitoids) is called **indirect defense**.

Herbivore-induced plant volatiles are the main medium of indirect defense. The composition of the volatiles is often specific to the herbivore species and includes members of all three major classes of secondary metabolites: terpenoids (monoterpenes, sesquiterpenes, and the homoterpenes DMNT and TMTT), alkaloids (indole) and phenylpropanoids (methyl salicylate); in addition, all plants release green-leaf volatiles after mechanical damage: six-carbon aldehydes, alcohols and esters generated from 13-hydroperoxylinolenic acid by hydroperoxide lyase (HPL), such as (*Z*)-3-hexenal, (*Z*)-3-hexenol and (*Z*)-3-hexenyl acetate, the smell of freshly cut grass.

- Attracting natural enemies: maize seedlings painted with very low concentrations of volicitin release large amounts of terpenoids, which attract small parasitoid wasps that parasitize caterpillars; the parasitoid lays eggs inside the caterpillar, and the hatched larvae eat the host from within. When maize roots are fed on by larvae of the rootworm *Diabrotica*, they release the sesquiterpene (*E*)-β-caryophyllene, which attracts entomopathogenic nematodes that kill the rootworm larvae (Rasmann et al. 2005).
- Distinguishing attackers: wild tobacco (*Nicotiana attenuata*) of the Great Basin desert of the United States usually greatly increases nicotine when fed upon; but when fed on by larvae of the tobacco hornworm, which tolerates nicotine, it hardly increases nicotine and instead releases terpenoid volatiles that attract bugs that prey on hornworm eggs and larvae.
- Deterring oviposition: volatiles released by the plant when moths lay eggs repel other females and reduce further oviposition.
- Warning between plants and within a plant: maize exposed to green-leaf volatiles quickly induces the expression of jasmonate and related genes; more importantly, it enters a "primed" state and responds faster and more strongly when actually fed upon later. This priming has been observed in lima bean, sagebrush (*Artemisia tridentata*), *Arabidopsis*, European aspen and blueberry. The vascular system of sagebrush and other desert plants is "sectored", with no vascular connection between branches; when one branch is wounded, volatiles travel through the air to other branches of the same plant and induce resistance, and isolating air movement prevents induction. From an evolutionary point of view, this may have been the original function of volatile signals. Green-leaf volatiles released by lima bean immediately after leaf-beetle feeding make other leaves of the same plant secrete more extrafloral nectar, which attracts predatory arthropods.
- Sustaining "bodyguards": some Central American acacias (*Acacia*) house ants in hollow thorns and feed them with extrafloral nectaries and the protein- and lipid-rich Beltian bodies at the leaf tips, and the ants attack herbivores and clear away other plants that twine on the acacia ([7.12.32](/7.12.32#sec-32.4.6)).

Defense is also synchronized with the circadian clock. The jasmonate response of *Arabidopsis* and the feeding of larvae of the generalist cabbage looper (*Trichoplusia ni*) both peak during the day; when the phase of the plant's clock is artificially shifted by 12 h so that the defense peak is out of step with the feeding peak, after 72 h the out-of-phase plants are damaged markedly more, and larvae feeding on them gain about three times as much weight as on in-phase plants (Goodspeed et al. 2012).

### 26.3.5. Counter-Adaptations of Herbivores

Through reciprocal evolutionary change with plants, herbivores have developed mechanisms to bypass or overcome plant defenses ([7.10.17](/7.10.17#sec-17.2.3)). Constitutive adaptations are more common in specialists, and induced adaptations (such as induction of detoxifying enzymes) in generalists.

- Detoxification and excretion: cytochrome P450 monooxygenases, glutathione transferases and others rapidly degrade toxins or conjugate and excrete them. Specialists that feed on Brassicaceae each have their own way of dealing with the "mustard oil bomb": the diamondback moth has a glucosinolate sulfatase in the gut that removes the sulfate group before myrosinase can act, so that the glucosinolate cannot be hydrolyzed; a gut protein of the cabbage white caterpillar redirects hydrolysis to produce far less toxic nitriles.
- Insensitive targets: in some insects that feed on plants containing cardiac glycosides, amino acid substitutions have occurred at the cardiac glycoside-binding site of the $\ce{Na+}/\ce{K+}$-ATPase, which is no longer inhibited.
- Sequestration and use for self-defense: larvae of the monarch butterfly (*Danaus plexippus*) feed on milkweed and store cardiac glycosides in the body into the adult stage; insect-eating birds quickly learn to avoid the conspicuous monarch larvae and adults, a classic example of **aposematic coloration**. The large milkweed bug (*Oncopeltus fasciatus*) and the oleander aphid (*Aphis nerii*) likewise sequester cardiac glycosides and have conspicuous colors. A tachinid fly (*Zenillia adamsoni*) that parasitizes monarch larvae in turn obtains cardiac glycosides "second-hand" from the host and retains them to the adult.
- Behavior: some beetles and caterpillars first cut the leaf vein or chew a trench across the leaf to drain the latex from the laticifers and then feed safely downstream.
- Suppressing plant signals: effector proteins in aphid saliva (such as Mp10 and Mp42 of the green peach aphid) resemble pathogen effectors; glucose oxidase in the saliva of the corn earworm inhibits induced nicotine synthesis in tobacco; phloem-feeding insects induce the salicylic acid pathway, possibly using SA–JA antagonism to suppress the jasmonate defense that is more effective against them ([7.13.25](/7.13.25#sec-25.5.5)).

Plants for their part have also evolved resistance genes against insects: tomato *Mi-1.2* against aphids, whiteflies and root-knot nematodes, rice *Bph14* against the brown planthopper, melon *Vat* against the cotton aphid; they all encode NLRs that recognize effector proteins in insect saliva. In most natural environments, the interaction between plants and herbivorous insects has reached a stalemate: both sides can survive and reproduce under suboptimal conditions.

### 26.3.6. The Trade-Off between Growth and Defense

The synthesis of defense compounds requires large amounts of carbon, nitrogen and energy (Example 26.2.1), and these resources can no longer be used for growth and reproduction. In crops this trade-off is most evident: part of the reason that breeding has raised yield is precisely that it has lowered the plant's capacity for self-defense. Jasmonate inhibits growth while activating defense: JAZ proteins interact with DELLA proteins, and when jasmonate signaling strengthens, the repression of gibberellin signaling by DELLA increases and leaf expansion and stem elongation slow; *Arabidopsis* with five *JAZ* genes removed (*jazQ*) is highly insect-resistant but dwarfed. Several hypotheses in plant ecology try to predict investment in defense: the growth–differentiation balance hypothesis (Herms and Mattson 1992) holds that when growth is limited but photosynthesis is not (for example under nutrient deficiency), the surplus carbon is diverted to secondary metabolism; the resource availability hypothesis (Coley, Bryant and Chapin 1985) holds that slow-growing plants in resource-poor environments should invest more in defense than fast-growing plants, because the loss of a leaf is harder for them to replace.

A simple allocation model can quantify these ideas. Suppose a plant invests a fraction $f$ of its carbon in defense, the maximum relative growth rate without defense is $r_0$, the loss rate caused by herbivory without defense is $h_0$, and defense reduces the loss by a factor $\mathrm{e}^{-af}$ ($a$ expresses the efficiency of defense); the net relative growth rate is

$$
g(f) = r_0(1 - f) - h_0\,\mathrm{e}^{-af}. \tag{26.5}
$$

Setting $g'(f) = -r_0 + a h_0\mathrm{e}^{-af} = 0$ gives the optimal investment

$$
f^* = \frac{1}{a}\ln\frac{a h_0}{r_0}\quad(a h_0 > r_0), \qquad f^* = 0\quad(a h_0 \le r_0). \tag{26.6}
$$

The model gives three predictions: the greater the herbivory pressure $h_0$, the higher the optimal defense; the more effective the defense (large $a$), the more worthwhile the investment, but $f^*$ first rises and then falls with $a$; and for a given $h_0$, $f^*$ increases as $r_0$ decreases, that is, slow-growing plants should defend more, which is precisely the quantitative form of the resource availability hypothesis (panels A and B of the figure below).

**Example 26.3.1** Let $r_0 = 0.20\,\mathrm{d^{-1}}$ and $a = 5$. (a) For $h_0 = 0.20\,\mathrm{d^{-1}}$, what are the optimal defense investment and net growth rate? How do they compare with no defense? (b) For a slow-growing plant with $r_0 = 0.05\,\mathrm{d^{-1}}$, what is the optimal investment at the same $h_0$?

**Solution** (a) $a h_0/r_0 = 5$, $f^* = \ln 5/5 = 0.32$; $g(f^*) = 0.20 \times 0.68 - 0.20\,\mathrm{e}^{-1.61} = 0.136 - 0.040 = 0.096\,\mathrm{d^{-1}}$. Without defense $g(0) = 0.20 - 0.20 = 0$, and the plant cannot grow at all; investing about one third of the carbon in defense restores net growth to about half of the maximum. (b) $a h_0/r_0 = 20$, $f^* = \ln 20/5 = 0.60$, and the slow-growing plant should invest 60% of its carbon in defense.

Induced defense can be seen as a strategy for lowering the cost of the trade-off: it pays the cost only when attacked, but must bear the losses before the defense is established. Constitutive defense is therefore more advantageous in environments where attacks are frequent and predictable, and induced defense when attacks are few and unpredictable (Question 3).

## 26.4. Mutualism

### 26.4.1. Pollinators

Flowers and pollinators are physiologically matched to each other, as C. K. Sprengel described systematically as early as 1793. Pollination syndromes and floral morphology are in [7.12.32](/7.12.32#sec-32.5.1) and [7.12.29](/7.12.29); only the physiological basis is added here.

- Visual signals: the photoreceptors of bees are sensitive to ultraviolet, blue and green light and insensitive to red; the pigments of many flowers form patterns in the ultraviolet invisible to the human eye (nectar guides) that guide bees to the nectar. The spatial resolution of bees is also lower than that of humans, so floral patterns are often large and simple.
- Rewards: nectar was an early innovation of angiosperms and contributed greatly to their success; insects with mouthparts for lapping nectar already occur in fossils about 150 million years old. The sugar concentration of nectar is generally 20%–50%, and the ratio of sucrose, glucose and fructose correlates with the type of pollinator. Nectar is secreted by **nectaries**, and its sugar comes from the phloem and is secreted through unloading of sucrose and SWEET transporters ([7.13.11](/7.13.11)). Pollen rewards pollinators with protein and lipid.
- Morphological matching: some flowers secrete nectar at the end of a long spur, and the length of the spur correlates with the proboscis length of the pollinator. From the spur of the Madagascar star orchid (*Angraecum sesquipedale*), about 30 cm long, Darwin predicted a hawk moth with an equally long proboscis, which was discovered only after his death; in the genus *Aquilegia* the spur length increases progressively as pollinators shift from bees to hummingbirds to hawk moths (Whittall and Hodges 2007).
- Scent and heat production: floral scent consists mainly of terpenoid and phenylpropanoid volatiles; in some plants such as the Araceae, flowering is accompanied by vigorous heat production through the alternative oxidase pathway, which disperses volatiles and provides warmth to pollinators ([7.13.12](/7.13.12)).
- Deception: some orchids imitate female insects in flower shape and scent, luring males into "pseudocopulation" and thereby pollination, without offering a reward.

Pollination is mutually beneficial: the flower provides nectar or pollen and the pollinator carries pollen for the flower. About 75% of the leading food crop species worldwide benefit to some degree from animal pollination, mainly by insects, especially bees (Klein et al. 2007). The number of honeybee colonies in the United States fell from about 6 million in the 1940s to about 2.5 million in the 2000s, owing to parasitic mites (the *Varroa* mite arrived in the United States in 1987), viruses, insecticides and habitat loss.

### 26.4.2. The Physiology of Root Nodule Symbiosis

Legumes form **root nodules** with **rhizobia**, exchanging photosynthate for fixed nitrogen; the development of nodules and the microbiology of nitrogen fixation are in [7.11.31](/7.11.31#sec-31.2.1), and nitrogen assimilation in [7.13.6](/7.13.6). At the level of signaling, when rhizobia approach the root surface they secrete **Nod factors** (lipochitooligosaccharides), which are recognized by LysM receptor kinases in the plasma membrane of legumes: in *Lotus japonicus* these are NFR1 and NFR5, each with three extracellular LysM domains, which recognize Nod factors in a species-specific manner and act as a heterodimer (only NFR1 has kinase activity). Recognition starts two processes: signals that promote infection, and expression of genes that induce nodule organogenesis. The symbiosis receptor-like kinase SYMRK (extracellular LRR) takes part in both processes; it is also required for actinorhizal nodules and arbuscular mycorrhiza and belongs to the "common symbiosis pathway" shared by the three symbioses: downstream is calcium oscillation (calcium spiking) around the nuclei of epidermal cells, generated by ion channels in the nuclear envelope and decoded by the calcium- and calmodulin-dependent kinase CCaMK, which activates core symbiosis genes; nodule organogenesis finally requires **cytokinin** signaling ([7.13.14](/7.13.14)).

The common symbiosis pathway reflects evolutionary history. **Arbuscular mycorrhiza** is extremely ancient and appeared 400 million years ago, and its fungal partners (Glomeromycota) have lost the ability to complete their life cycle outside plants; the symbiosis of rhizobia with legumes appeared about 60 million years ago and evolved from the older mycorrhizal signaling pathway. Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi also secrete lipochitooligosaccharides (Myc factors) to promote mycorrhiza formation. The receptors for Nod factors are homologous to the LysM receptors of *Arabidopsis* and rice that recognize fungal chitin and mediate defense, indicating that a defense receptor was "co-opted" in evolution as a symbiosis receptor ([7.13.25](/7.13.25#sec-25.2.1)).

Symbiosis has a cost for the plant: for each molecule of $\ce{N2}$ fixed, nitrogenase consumes 16 ATP ([7.11.31](/7.11.31#sec-31.2.4)), and the carbon the plant supplies to the nodule is about 6–7 g per gram of nitrogen fixed; legumes therefore suppress nodulation when there is plenty of nitrate in the soil and regulate the number of nodules themselves through long-distance root–shoot–root signals (CLE peptides and their receptors). Industrial nitrogen fixation (the Haber–Bosch process) supplies nitrogen fertilizer for crops but is enormously energy-intensive; today the amount of nitrogen it fixes already equals that fixed biologically on land. In addition, the symbiosis of *Azolla* with *Anabaena* is used as green manure in rice fields, and the actinorhizal nodules of alder with *Frankia* allow it to be the first to colonize the poor land left by retreating glaciers.

### 26.4.3. Mycorrhiza

About 70%–80% of land plants form arbuscular mycorrhiza ([7.11.31](/7.11.31#sec-31.3.1)). The physiological significance of mycorrhiza lies mainly in phosphorus: phosphate diffuses extremely slowly in soil, and the root quickly forms a depletion zone around itself, whereas fungal hyphae are much finer and longer and can reach beyond the depletion zone to take up phosphorus and hand it over to the plant in the arbuscules through specific phosphate transporters ([7.13.5](/7.13.5)); the plant rewards the fungus with photosynthate, including sugars and fatty acids, estimated at about 4%–20% of photosynthate. Under phosphorus deficiency the root secretes more **strigolactones**, which promote spore germination and hyphal branching of mycorrhizal fungi (Akiyama et al. 2005), so that the plant establishes the symbiosis faster (Section 26.5).

Mycorrhiza is not always mutualistic: when nutrients are plentiful or light is insufficient, the cost of feeding the fungus may exceed the benefit, and mycorrhizal plants lie at different positions on a continuum from parasitism to mutualism. Mycorrhiza can also increase the resistance of plants to pathogens and herbivores (mycorrhiza-induced resistance), which resembles ISR and appears as priming of defense ([7.13.25](/7.13.25#sec-25.5.3)).

### 26.4.4. Beneficial Microbes of the Rhizosphere

The **rhizosphere** is the narrow layer of soil influenced by the root system. The sugars, organic acids, amino acids and secondary metabolites secreted by roots, together with sloughed cells, can account for a considerable fraction of photosynthetically fixed carbon (some estimates are as high as 40%), making the bacterial density of the rhizosphere up to 100 times higher than in the surrounding soil, and 15% of the root surface may be covered by bacterial microcolonies. Among them, a group of bacteria called *plant growth-promoting rhizobacteria* (PGPR) benefit plants in several ways:

- Increasing nutrient availability: volatiles released by *Bacillus subtilis* make iron-deficient *Arabidopsis* roots secrete protons and increase iron uptake, and the chlorophyll content, photosynthetic efficiency and size of the plants increase accordingly; **siderophores** secreted by bacteria also affect iron availability.
- Changing root architecture: the same volatiles change root length and lateral root density.
- Suppressing pathogens: a *Pseudomonas* that produces the antifungal compound 2,4-diacetylphloroglucinol suppresses the take-all fungus of wheat (*Gaeumannomyces graminis*), one reason why take-all disease actually declines after several years of continuous wheat (take-all decline); PGPR can also induce systemic resistance (ISR, [7.13.25](/7.13.25#sec-25.5.3)).

The amounts of antibiotics and siderophores secreted by bacteria are controlled by **quorum sensing**, and root exudates can modulate the quorum sensing signals of bacteria, through which the plant influences the rhizosphere community. Rhizobia can also affect the relationship between plants and herbivores: lima bean colonized by rhizobia releases more indole volatiles upon jasmonate induction, and the specialist Mexican bean beetle (*Epilachna varivestis*) prefers to feed on plants without nodules.

## 26.5. Parasitic Plants and Strigolactones

About 1% of angiosperms (about 4500 species) are **parasitic plants** ([7.12.32](/7.12.32#sec-32.4.1)). By degree of dependence on the host, those that retain some photosynthetic capacity and obtain mainly water and minerals from the host xylem are called hemiparasites, such as *Viscum* (mistletoe) and *Striga* (witchweed); those that have lost photosynthetic capacity completely and obtain organic compounds from the host phloem are called holoparasites, such as *Cuscuta* (dodder) and *Orobanche* and *Phelipanche* (broomrapes). Parasitic plants all invade the host with a **haustorium**: the haustorium is a modified root that penetrates the host epidermis or bark and grows through the parenchyma into the vascular tissue, connecting with host vessels or sieve tubes. Dodder and its host even exchange large amounts of mRNA and protein.

A parasitic plant must first find its host. A dodder seedling can survive only a few days on seed reserves, and it senses trace volatiles released by the host and grows toward it (attraction to tomato volatiles, Runyon et al. 2006). Root parasitic plants rely on the exudates of host roots, of which the most important signal is strigolactone.

Witchweed (*Striga hermonthica*, *S. asiatica*) and broomrape are among the most serious parasitic weeds of food crops (sorghum, maize, millet, upland rice, legumes, sunflower) in Africa, Asia and the Mediterranean, infesting tens of millions of hectares of farmland in sub-Saharan Africa. One witchweed plant can produce tens of thousands to hundreds of thousands of seeds about 0.2 mm in diameter, which survive in the soil for more than ten years. The seeds are tiny and can sustain growth for only a few days after germination, so germination is useful only within a few millimeters of a host root: they germinate only when they sense germination stimulants secreted by host roots. The first such stimulant, isolated in 1966 from the exudates of cotton roots, was named strigol, and this class of compounds is collectively called **strigolactones**. Witchweed senses them with a greatly expanded family of α/β hydrolase receptors (KAI2d/HTL, homologous to the receptors that sense karrikins in smoke and strigolactones), with picomolar sensitivity. After germination the radicle grows toward the host root, and quinones produced by oxidation of phenolics in the host cell wall (such as 2,6-dimethoxy-*p*-benzoquinone, DMBQ) induce the formation of the haustorium at the tip of the radicle.

This raises a question: why does a plant secrete a signal that is exploited by parasites? The answer is that the original function of strigolactones is to attract arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (Section 26.4.3): they are secreted in large amounts under phosphorus deficiency and promote spore germination and hyphal branching of mycorrhizal fungi. Root parasitic plants "eavesdrop" on this symbiotic signal. Later (2008) strigolactones were also found to be plant hormones that inhibit shoot branching ([7.13.15](/7.13.15#sec-15.6.4), [7.13.18](/7.13.18)).

![Strigolactones: two "readers" of the same signal (schematic). The host root (central vascular cylinder in blue) secretes more strigolactones (red dots) under phosphorus deficiency, forming a concentration gradient around the root. Left: after sensing strigolactones, arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi germinate and branch and penetrate the root to form arbuscules. Control of witchweed makes use of these physiological features:

- Breeding cultivars that secrete few strigolactones, or strigolactones of a different kind: the sorghum *lgs1* (low germination stimulant 1) mutant changes its main secreted strigolactone from 5-deoxystrigol to orobanchol, which stimulates witchweed far less, giving resistance to witchweed without clearly affecting mycorrhiza.
- "Suicidal germination": applying strigolactone analogs to the soil in the absence of a host induces the seeds to germinate, and the germinated seedlings die for lack of a host, depleting the soil seed bank.
- Planting "trap crops" that stimulate germination but are not parasitized (certain cultivars of cotton and soybean), and intercropping with the legume *Desmodium* (whose root exudates inhibit haustorium development of witchweed).

The dynamics of the soil seed bank can be described by first-order kinetics: let $m$ be the fraction lost each year to natural death and $q$ the fraction lost to germination (germination in the absence of a host is fatal); the number of seeds after $t$ years is then

$$
N(t) = N_0\,[(1 - m)(1 - q)]^t. \tag{26.7}
$$

**Example 26.5.1** The witchweed seed bank of a field is $10^4$ seeds per square meter, and the annual natural mortality of seeds is $m = 0.3$. (a) With no measures (and no new seeds entering), how many years does it take for the seed bank to fall to 100 seeds per square meter? (b) If strigolactone analogs are applied every year so that 60% of the remaining seeds germinate suicidally, how many years are needed?

**Solution** (a) $0.7^t = 10^{-2}$, $t = \ln 100/(-\ln 0.7) = 4.605/0.357 = 12.9$ years. (b) $(0.7 \times 0.4)^t = 0.28^t = 10^{-2}$, $t = 4.605/1.273 = 3.6$ years. But if witchweed is allowed to set seed on the crop in even one year, a yield of tens of thousands of seeds per plant restores the seed bank, so control must be continuous and must prevent seed set.

## 26.6. Between Plants: Competition and Allelopathy

Plants first compete for light, water and nutrients. Trees in a forest can grow to more than 100 m and shade the plants below; early perception of competitors relies on the ratio of red to far-red light under the canopy and causes the **shade-avoidance response** ([7.13.16](/7.13.16)). Root systems likewise compete for nutrients and water.

**Definition 26.6.1 (Allelopathy)** The phenomenon in which a plant releases secondary metabolites into the environment that affect (usually inhibit) the germination, growth or development of neighboring plants is called **allelopathy**; such compounds are called allelochemicals.

Root exudates of plants have many original uses: changing the chemistry of the rhizosphere to improve nutrient uptake or reduce metal toxicity, and communicating with rhizobia and other microbes. Some exudates act directly on other plants. Classic examples include juglone (a naphthoquinone) released by the roots, leaves and fruit husks of black walnut (*Juglans nigra*), which inhibits the growth of many plants under its canopy; sorgoleone (a lipophilic benzoquinone) secreted by sorghum root hairs, which inhibits photosystem II; the diterpene momilactone secreted by rice roots; and the benzoxazinoids released by rye and maize (Section 26.2.4).

Allelopathy has attracted attention because of invasive alien plants. Spotted knapweed (*Centaurea maculosa*) is native to Europe, where it is not dominant, but after introduction to northwestern North America it became one of the most serious invasive weeds, occupying more than 1.8 million hectares of pasture and grassland in Montana alone and forming dense monodominant stands. A 2003 study proposed that its roots secrete racemic (±)-catechin, which triggers a wave of reactive oxygen species in the root meristem of sensitive plants (such as *Arabidopsis*), followed by $\ce{Ca^2+}$ signaling and genome-wide changes of expression (the expression of about 1000 genes doubled within 1 h of treatment), finally causing death of root cells; native North American prairie plants differ greatly in their sensitivity to catechin, while European plants coexisting with it for a long time may have adapted to this substance. This is called the "novel weapons hypothesis" (Callaway and Ridenour 2004). However, catechin concentrations measured in field soils by several laboratories later were far lower than first reported, catechin is also quickly degraded or adsorbed in soil, and its role in invasion is still disputed. This also illustrates the methodological difficulty of allelopathy research: to demonstrate allelopathy, resource competition and the effects of microbes must be excluded, for example by adsorbing organic compounds in the soil with activated carbon and observing whether the inhibition disappears; it must also be shown that the allelochemical reaches an effective concentration in field soil.

The dose–response relationship of allelochemicals is often nonmonotonic: promotion at low concentration and inhibition at high concentration, a phenomenon called *hormesis*. A commonly used model describing it is the Brain–Cousens model, which is the log-logistic dose–response model ([7.7.11](/7.7.11)) with an added linear promotion term at low dose:

$$
y(x) = \frac{d + \phi x}{1 + (x/e)^b}, \tag{26.8}
$$

where $d$ is the response of the control (for example 100% root elongation), $\phi$ describes the promotion at low dose, and $e$ and $b$ determine the position and steepness of the inhibition; for $\phi = 0$ it reduces to the ordinary log-logistic model, in which case $e$ is the half-maximal inhibitory concentration IC50. With the promotion term, IC50 must be found numerically by solving $y(x) = d/2$.

## 26.7. Biotic Interactions under Climate Change

Human activity is changing the conditions of the interactions of plants with other organisms. The atmospheric $\ce{CO2}$ concentration has risen from about $280\,\mu\mathrm{mol\,mol^{-1}}$ before the Industrial Revolution to about $423\,\mu\mathrm{mol\,mol^{-1}}$ in 2024, higher than at any time in the past 800 000 years recorded in ice cores; the global mean surface temperature is about 1.1–1.3 °C higher than in 1850–1900. These changes affect biotic interactions through several routes:

- Herbivory: under high $\ce{CO2}$ the carbon-to-nitrogen ratio of leaves rises and protein content falls, and many herbivorous insects compensate by eating more; in soybean, high $\ce{CO2}$ also weakens jasmonate-dependent defense, making leaves more susceptible to beetle feeding. Warming raises the metabolic and feeding rates of insects and allows more generations to be completed in one growing season; it is estimated that for each 1 °C rise in global mean temperature, yield losses of wheat, rice and maize caused by insects increase by about 10%–25% (Deutsch et al. 2018).
- Disease: the distribution of many pests and pathogens is expanding toward higher latitudes. Temperature also directly affects plant immunity: in *Arabidopsis* above about 28–30 °C, part of NLR-mediated resistance and salicylic acid synthesis are suppressed, and the plants are more susceptible to pathogens.
- Phenological mismatch: the flowering time of plants is determined jointly by temperature and photoperiod ([7.13.21](/7.13.21)), while the emergence of pollinating insects is determined mainly by temperature; when the two respond to warming out of step, the flowering period and the active period of pollinators may become misaligned.
- Symbiosis and competition: high temperature and drought may weaken nodule and mycorrhizal symbioses; invasive plants become more competitive under the new climate.
- Habitat: agricultural clearing and deforestation have deprived many plants of their habitat, and many medicinal plants are threatened or extinct (*Global Biodiversity Outlook* 3, 2010).

The relationships of plants with other organisms tie the parts of this chapter together: the same class of secondary metabolites (such as terpenoid volatiles) can repel herbivores, attract natural enemies, attract pollinators and warn neighbors; the same signal (strigolactone) is read by both symbionts and parasites; and the same hormone (jasmonate) allocates resources between defense and growth. Keeping these relationships in balance in a changing climate is a problem for crop protection and ecosystem management; abiotic stress itself is treated in the next chapter, [7.13.27](/7.13.27).

## Questions

1. The glucosinolates and myrosinase of Brassicaceae and the cyanogenic glycosides and β-glucosidases of cassava are each stored separately. Why is this "two-component" design advantageous to the plant? What means do specialist insects have to deal with it?

   **Answer** The toxic aglycones (isothiocyanates, HCN) are also toxic to the plant's own cells, and if they were continuously present the plant would constantly pay the cost of detoxification and repair; stored as nontoxic, water-soluble glycosides in the vacuole or in particular cells, spatially separated from the hydrolytic enzymes, large amounts of toxin are produced instantly at the wound only when the tissue is chewed and the two mix, which is both safe and maximally effective at the time and place needed. The countermeasures of specialists include modifying the substrate before hydrolysis (the glucosinolate sulfatase of the diamondback moth removes the sulfate group so that it cannot be hydrolyzed by myrosinase); altering the products of hydrolysis (a gut protein of the cabbage white caterpillar changes the product to a less toxic nitrile); feeding quickly so that less tissue is disrupted; and using glucosinolates as signals for finding hosts and stimulating feeding and oviposition, turning the plant's defense into a cue for their own specialized feeding.

2. (Calculation) By the MVA pathway, how much acetyl-CoA, ATP and NADPH are needed to synthesize 1 molecule of a sesquiterpene (C15) and 1 molecule of the triterpene skeleton squalene (C30)? If FPP is synthesized in the plastid by the MEP pathway, how do the main sources of carbon and energy differ?

   **Answer** Each C5 unit requires 3 acetyl-CoA, 3 ATP and 2 NADPH. A sesquiterpene is made of 3 C5 units: 9 acetyl-CoA, 9 ATP, 6 NADPH. Squalene is formed by tail-to-tail condensation of 2 molecules of FPP (6 C5 units), and squalene synthase also consumes 1 NADPH in the condensation: 18 acetyl-CoA, 18 ATP and 13 NADPH in all. Acetyl-CoA comes from glycolysis of glucose (2 molecules per glucose), so the carbon skeleton of squalene requires 9 molecules of glucose (54 carbons, with 30 carbons in the product). The MEP pathway starts from pyruvate and glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate, direct products of photosynthesis, and uses in the plastid the ATP and reducing power supplied by the light reactions (reduced ferredoxin and NADPH); each IPP needs about 1 ATP, 1 CTP and reducing power equivalent to 3 NADPH, so when monoterpenes, diterpenes and carotenoids are synthesized in chloroplasts in the light, carbon and energy come directly from photosynthesis without passing through the breakdown of sugars and respiration.

3. (Calculation) Suppose a plant is attacked by a given herbivore with probability $p$ in each growing season. Constitutive defense costs $c_{\mathrm{c}} = 10\%$ of biomass per season and completely prevents feeding; induced defense pays a cost $c_{\mathrm{i}} = 8\%$ only when attacked, but the plant still loses $\delta = 6\%$ during the two days before the defense is established. For what $p$ is induced defense more advantageous? How does the conclusion change if priming (for example warning by volatiles of a neighboring plant) reduces $\delta$ to 2%?

   **Answer** The expected loss of constitutive defense is $c_{\mathrm{c}} = 10\%$; the expected loss of induced defense is $p(c_{\mathrm{i}} + \delta) = p \times 14\%$. Induced defense is more advantageous when $p \times 14\% < 10\%$, that is, $p < 0.71$. If $\delta = 2\%$, the condition becomes $p \times 10\% < 10\%$, that is, $p < 1$: unless the plant is certain to be attacked every season, induced defense is always more advantageous. Induced defense therefore wins when attacks are rare or unpredictable; constitutive defense wins when attacks are frequent and predictable, or when the loss before the defense is established is large (for example when the herbivore multiplies fast); and warning and priming extend the range over which induced defense is advantageous by reducing $\delta$.

4. Use Eq. (26.6) to explain why, under the same herbivory pressure, leaves of slow-growing tree species on poor soil (such as shade-tolerant species of tropical rain forest) are often rich in tannins and lignin, while leaves of pioneer species have fewer defense compounds and are eaten more. Under what conditions might this prediction fail?

   **Answer** By Eq. (26.6), $f^* = a^{-1}\ln(ah_0/r_0)$ increases as $r_0$ decreases. For a slow-growing species $r_0$ is small, and replacing a lost leaf takes a long time, so it pays to invest more resources in defense; pioneer species grow fast and have short-lived leaves, eaten leaves are quickly regrown, the marginal return on investment in defense is low, and they "substitute growth for defense". In addition, the leaves of slow-growing species on poor soil are long-lived, and long-lived leaves need durable defenses that are hard to decompose (the "quantitative" defenses such as tannins and lignin), consistent with the optimal defense hypothesis. Cases where the prediction may fail include: the efficiency of defense $a$ differs greatly between the two kinds of plants; the herbivory pressure $h_0$ itself varies with habitat (herbivores may be more or less numerous in the gaps where pioneers grow); and the cost of defense compounds is not only carbon (nitrogen-containing alkaloids are especially costly in nitrogen-poor environments, and by the growth–differentiation balance hypothesis surplus carbon under nitrogen deficiency is more likely to be diverted to nitrogen-free phenolics and terpenoids than to alkaloids).

5. Someone finds in a greenhouse that a root extract of an invasive plant at 1 mM reduces root elongation of a native grass by 50%, and concludes that the plant invades by allelopathy. What evidence is missing from this inference? Design experiments to test it.

   **Answer** It still needs to be shown that: (1) the actual concentration of the compound in field soil can reach the effective concentration and persist long enough (taking adsorption and microbial degradation into account); (2) the inhibition is not caused by competition for nutrients or water, by changes in soil microbes (such as inhibition of mycorrhizal fungi), or by other substances released in the extraction (osmotic pressure, pH); (3) plants of the native range tolerate the compound while native species of the invaded range are sensitive (the prediction of the novel weapons hypothesis). Experimental design: measure the concentration of the compound and its change over time in field and greenhouse soil by HPLC or mass spectrometry; in co-growth experiments add activated carbon to adsorb organic compounds, or use mutants or related species that do not produce the compound as controls, so that if the inhibition disappears under activated carbon while the resource levels are the same, allelopathy is supported; sterilize and inoculate the soil to separate the effect of microbes; perform dose–response experiments (Eq. 26.8) with plants from both regions and compare IC50; and finally perform removal or addition experiments in the field to test its actual effect on native populations.
