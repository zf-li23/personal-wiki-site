The photosynthetic assimilates made in leaves are the source of all plant biomass, but only mature leaves make them in quantity, whereas growth, storage and reproduction take place in root tips, shoot tips, young leaves, tubers, fruits and seeds. It is the **phloem** that carries sugar from the "place of production" to the "place of use." This pathway determines how much of the photosynthate enters harvested organs and in what proportion, and therefore to a large extent the yield and quality of crops: at the same photosynthetic rate, the more assimilate is partitioned to the grain, the higher the harvest index. The phloem also carries signals between organs: sucrose itself, hormones, proteins and RNA all move with the sap, and the flowering signal **florigen** is one example.

This chapter first treats the transport pathway and the facts about phloem sap (Sections 11.1–11.4), then source and sink (Section 11.5), and then discusses the mechanism of transport, the pressure-flow hypothesis and its quantitative tests (Section 11.6), and the two ends that drive it: loading at the source (Section 11.7) and unloading at the sink (Section 11.8). It ends with how assimilates are allocated and partitioned among organs (Section 11.9) and the role of the phloem as a signaling pathway (Section 11.10). The anatomy of sieve tubes and companion cells is described in [7.12.5](/7.12.5#sec-5.2), the mechanisms of sugar transport across membranes in [7.13.5](/7.13.5), and the synthesis of sucrose and starch in [7.1.25](/7.1.25) and [7.13.8](/7.13.8).

## 11.1. Evidence for the Transport Pathway

### 11.1.1. Girdling

Removing a ring of bark from a tree trunk (girdling) removes the phloem and leaves the xylem. After girdling, the bark above the cut swells because sugar accumulates there, and is often purple from accumulated anthocyanin; the bark below and the roots receive no sugar and eventually starve, while the leaves do not wilt for a considerable time, showing that the upward movement of water in the xylem is unaffected. This experiment, begun by Malpighi in the seventeenth century, showed that sugar is transported in the bark (phloem). Ringing (or girdling) in fruit culture uses exactly this: a ring of phloem is cut on a branch or trunk to block the downward movement of assimilates, so that the flower buds and fruits above receive more sugar, which promotes flowering and fruit set, enlarges fruit, hastens ripening and raises sugar content.

### 11.1.2. Radioactive Tracers

After the advent of radioisotopes, a source leaf was fed $\ce{^{14}CO2}$ and the fate of $\ce{^{14}C}$ followed by **autoradiography** or counting; this showed that the sugars produced by photosynthesis are transported in the sieve elements of the phloem, and allowed direct measurement of the direction and velocity of transport (Section 11.4). A classic experiment on sugar beet showed that the $\ce{^{14}C}$ exported by a source leaf goes mainly to the young leaf directly above it in the same vertical row (orthostichy) rather than being distributed evenly to all sinks (Section 11.5).

### 11.1.3. Aphid Stylets

The difficulty in studying phloem sap is that the turgor inside sieve elements is very high, so when one is cut the sap rushes toward the cut, P-protein and callose rapidly plug the sieve pores (Section 11.2), and the exuded liquid is contaminated by the contents of surrounding damaged cells and diluted by water from the xylem. An ingenious approach uses aphids: an aphid inserts its mouthparts, made of four tubular stylets, into a single sieve element to feed; after the aphid is anesthetized with $\ce{CO2}$, a laser is used to sever the stylets from the aphid's body, and the turgor of the sieve element forces sap out of the stylets left in the plant, which can be collected for several hours (substances in aphid saliva prevent the plant's sealing response). The quantities obtained are small, but the sap is considered to be fairly pure sieve element–companion cell sap. A micropressure probe sealed onto an exuding stylet can also measure the turgor of a single sieve element directly (Section 11.6.3).

## 11.2. The Sieve Element–Companion Cell Complex

The transporting cells in the phloem are sieve tube elements in angiosperms and sieve cells in gymnosperms, together called sieve elements; their structure is described in [7.12.5](/7.12.5#sec-5.2). Only the points relevant to transport physiology are stressed here.

- Mature sieve tube elements are living cells that retain a plasma membrane, modified mitochondria, plastids and smooth endoplasmic reticulum, but lack a nucleus, tonoplast, Golgi bodies and ribosomes; the remaining organelles are pressed against the wall, giving the lumen low resistance to bulk flow. The end walls between adjacent sieve tube elements form **sieve plates** whose pores are open channels (diameter from less than 1 µm to about 15 µm), so a file of sieve tube elements forms a continuous conduit, the **sieve tube**. The **sieve areas** of gymnosperms are all similar, with no sieve plates, and their pores are filled with membranes continuous with the smooth endoplasmic reticulum, which seems unfavorable for bulk flow (Section 11.6.3).
- When a sieve tube element is damaged, sap surges toward the wound and P-protein is carried onto the sieve plate and plugs the pores, a short-term seal; the long-term seal depends on callose synthase in the plasma membrane depositing **callose** (β-1,3-glucan) around the pores, which blocks them completely about 20 min after wounding. Dormant sieve tubes are also sealed by callose, which is dissolved by callose hydrolases when activity resumes. Legume sieve tubes contain a special P-protein (forisome) that, on wounding, disperses rapidly and reversibly under the action of $\ce{Ca^2+}$ and plugs the tube. Sealing is also important against piercing–sucking pests: rice resistant to the brown planthopper deposits callose when probed, whereas susceptible varieties are induced at the same time to express callose hydrolases, the sieve pores are reopened, and the pest can feed continuously.
- Each sieve tube element and one or several **companion cells** arise by division of the same mother cell, and between them there are abundant branched plasmodesmata through which fluorescent dyes are rapidly exchanged. The companion cell supplies the sieve tube element with proteins and ATP and is also responsible for loading sugar into it, so the two are treated as a functional unit, the sieve element–companion cell complex (SE–CC complex). There are three classes of companion cells in the minor veins of source leaves: ordinary companion cells (with well-developed chloroplasts and a variable number of plasmodesmata to surrounding cells), **transfer cells** (whose walls grow finger-like projections inward that greatly increase plasma membrane area, with almost no plasmodesmata except those to their own sieve elements), and intermediary cells (with abundant plasmodesmata to the bundle sheath cells, many small vacuoles and poorly developed chloroplasts). They correspond to different modes of loading (Section 11.7).

By their role in transport, the sieve elements that collect sugar in the source are called collection phloem, those along the way transport phloem, and those releasing sugar in the sink release phloem.

## 11.3. Composition of Phloem Sap

The main component of phloem sap is water, in which the dissolved substances are mostly carbohydrates, with amino acids, organic acids, inorganic ions, hormones, proteins and RNA. The composition of sap exuded from cut castor bean (Hall and Baker, 1972):

| Component | Concentration / $\mathrm{mg\,mL^{-1}}$ |
|---|---|
| Sugars | 80.0–106.0 |
| Amino acids | 5.2 |
| Organic acids | 2.0–3.2 |
| Proteins | 1.45–2.20 |
| $\ce{K+}$ | 2.3–4.4 |
| $\ce{Cl-}$ | 0.355–0.675 |
| Phosphate | 0.350–0.550 |
| $\ce{Mg^2+}$ | 0.109–0.122 |

Sugars. The sucrose concentration in sieve element sap can reach 0.3–0.9 M. The sugars transported are all nonreducing sugars: the most common is sucrose; many plants also transport the raffinose family oligosaccharides, in which sucrose is linked to one, two or three galactose units (raffinose, stachyose and verbascose), and sugar alcohols (sorbitol in the Rosaceae, mannitol in the Oleaceae, celery and others). Reducing sugars such as glucose and fructose have a free aldehyde or ketone group and are chemically reactive; in animal blood the glucose concentration is low and can be tolerated, whereas the high sugar concentration in the phloem would make the reactivity of hexoses a problem. In cells, hexoses are sequestered in the vacuole and have no route into the phloem.

Nitrogenous compounds. Nitrogen in the phloem is transported mainly as amino acids, especially glutamate, aspartate and their amides glutamine and asparagine; nodulated legumes also transport nitrogen as ureides (allantoin and allantoic acid) and citrulline.

Ions. $\ce{K+}$, $\ce{Mg^2+}$, phosphate and $\ce{Cl-}$ are mobile in the phloem; $\ce{NO3-}$, $\ce{Ca^2+}$, sulfur and iron are relatively immobile. This determines where deficiency symptoms appear ([7.13.4](/7.13.4)): when an element that can be redistributed through the phloem (N, P, K, Mg) is deficient, symptoms appear first in older leaves; when an element that cannot be redistributed (Ca, Fe, S, B) is deficient, symptoms appear first in young leaves and at the apex.

Hormones, proteins and RNA. Almost all endogenous hormones have been detected in sieve elements; the proteins in the sap include structural P-proteins and many soluble proteins, the latter mostly related to stress and defense; the RNAs include mRNAs, small RNAs and pathogen RNAs (Section 11.10).

Sucrose, $\ce{K+}$ and amino acids are the main contributors to the solute potential of phloem sap.

## 11.4. Direction, Velocity and Specific Mass Transfer Rate

### 11.4.1. Direction

The direction of phloem transport is not determined by gravity but by the relative positions of source and sink: it runs from source to sink, whether upward or downward. In the same stem, different vascular bundles, or adjacent sieve tubes in the same bundle, can transport in opposite directions at the same time, for example in the petiole of a leaf that is changing from sink to source, where some sieve tubes import and others export. Transport is mainly longitudinal but can also occur laterally through plasmodesmata; when longitudinal transport is blocked, lateral transport increases.

### 11.4.2. Velocity and Specific Mass Transfer Rate

Two quantities describe how fast transport is:

**Definition 11.4.1 (Transport Velocity and Specific Mass Transfer Rate)** The distance a substance moves in the phloem per unit time is the transport velocity, in $\mathrm{m\,h^{-1}}$; the mass of dry matter passing per unit time through a unit cross-sectional area of phloem (or sieve tubes) is the **specific mass transfer rate** (SMTR), in $\mathrm{g\,h^{-1}\,cm^{-2}}$. The specific mass transfer rate based on sieve tube cross section is more meaningful, because only the sieve tubes transport.

Transport velocities measured by various methods average about $1\ \mathrm{m\,h^{-1}}$, with a range of $0.3$–$1.5\ \mathrm{m\,h^{-1}}$ ($0.08$–$0.42\ \mathrm{mm\,s^{-1}}$); the mean flow velocity in castor bean phloem measured by magnetic resonance imaging is $0.25\ \mathrm{mm\,s^{-1}}$, very close to the results of older methods. Velocities differ among plants: castor bean $0.8$–$1.5\ \mathrm{m\,h^{-1}}$, cotton $0.35$–$0.4\ \mathrm{m\,h^{-1}}$, willow $1\ \mathrm{m\,h^{-1}}$, sugarcane about $2.7\ \mathrm{m\,h^{-1}}$. The specific mass transfer rate based on sieve tube area is $1$–$15\ \mathrm{g\,h^{-1}\,cm^{-2}}$ ($2.8$–$41.7\ \mathrm{\mu g\,s^{-1}\,mm^{-2}}$). The value "up to $200\ \mathrm{g\,h^{-1}\,cm^{-2}}$" sometimes quoted has no basis; by the estimate below, it would require unrealistic flow velocities or concentrations.

Such velocities far exceed diffusion. The **diffusion coefficient** of sucrose in water is about $5\times10^{-10}\ \mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$, so diffusion over 1 m takes on average $t \approx x^2/(2D) = 10^{9}\ \mathrm{s}$, about 32 years.

**Example 11.4.1 (Specific Mass Transfer Rate in Relation to Velocity and Concentration)** The specific mass transfer rate equals the concentration of dry matter in the sap times the flow velocity. Suppose the sieve tube sap contains 0.5 M sucrose ($0.5\times342.3 = 171\ \mathrm{g\,L^{-1}} = 171\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$) and the flow velocity is $1\ \mathrm{m\,h^{-1}}$. Then

$$
\text{SMTR} = 171\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}\times1\ \mathrm{m\,h^{-1}} = 171\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-2}\,h^{-1}} = 17.1\ \mathrm{g\,h^{-1}\,cm^{-2}},
$$
right at the upper end of the measured range. To reach $200\ \mathrm{g\,h^{-1}\,cm^{-2}}$ at 0.5 M would require a velocity of about $12\ \mathrm{m\,h^{-1}}$, inconsistent with all measurements.

**Example 11.4.2 (Estimating the Specific Mass Transfer Rate from Sink Growth)** A squash fruit increased in dry weight by 250 g in 33 days; the phloem cross-sectional area in the fruit stalk is $0.20\ \mathrm{cm^2}$, of which sieve tubes make up about 1/5. If all the dry matter of the fruit entered through the stalk phloem and the respiratory consumption of the fruit amounts to a further 15% of the input, then the import rate is $250/(1 - 0.15)/(33\times24) = 0.37\ \mathrm{g\,h^{-1}}$; the specific mass transfer rate based on phloem cross section is $0.37/0.20 = 1.9\ \mathrm{g\,h^{-1}\,cm^{-2}}$ and that based on sieve tube cross section is $9.3\ \mathrm{g\,h^{-1}\,cm^{-2}}$. This method of estimation from sink growth and the cross section of the transport channel was the earliest way of determining the specific mass transfer rate (values illustrative).

## 11.5. Source, Sink and the Source–Sink Unit

### 11.5.1. Source and Sink

**Definition 11.5.1 (Source and Sink)** An organ or tissue that makes or mobilizes assimilates and exports them to other organs is a **source**, such as a mature leaf, a storage organ in its export phase (the storage root of a biennial beet in its second year), or the cotyledons or endosperm of a germinating seed. An organ or tissue that consumes or accumulates assimilates and needs to import them is a **sink**, including all nonphotosynthetic organs and photosynthetic organs whose photosynthate is insufficient for their own needs: roots, tubers, developing fruits and seeds, young leaves and so on.

Source and sink are functional concepts that change with development: the storage root of beet is a sink in the first year, accumulating sugar; in the second year its reserves are mobilized to grow a new flowering stem, and it becomes a source. A young leaf is a sink and becomes a source when mature (Section 11.5.3). Sinks can be classified by the use of the assimilates imported: utilization sinks (most of the imported assimilate is used for growth, such as root tips, shoot tips and young leaves) and storage sinks (most is stored, such as fruits, tubers, tuberous roots and seeds).

### 11.5.2. The Source–Sink Unit and Rules of Transport

A plant has several sources and several sinks at the same time, but not every source supplies every sink equally.

**Definition 11.5.2 (Source–Sink Unit)** A source and the sinks it supplies, which correspond to one another in the supply and demand of assimilates, together constitute a **source–sink unit**.

The formation of source–sink units follows several rules:

1. Proximity: the assimilates of a sink come mainly from nearby source leaves. For example, the assimilates for grain filling in cereals come mainly from the flag leaf and the ear itself, and lower leaves supply mainly the roots.
2. Priority of vascular connection (ipsilateral transport): assimilates are transported preferentially between source and sink that are directly connected by vascular bundles; assimilates made by leaves in the same position (the same orthostichy) go mainly to young leaves, inflorescences and roots on the same side. The $\ce{^{14}C}$ experiment on beet is an example. Anastomoses (lateral connections) among vascular bundles make this restriction not absolute; when bundles are cut by injury or pruning, transport is rerouted through the anastomoses.
3. Priority for growth centers: crops have different growth centers at different developmental stages, usually sites of vigorous metabolism and fast growth; in the vegetative stage these are new leaves and roots, and in the reproductive stage they shift to ears, fruits and seeds. Growth centers move with development, and the source–sink units adjust accordingly.

### 11.5.3. The Sink-to-Source Transition of a Leaf

The leaves of dicots (tomato, bean and others) are at first sinks; they begin to export when they have reached about 25% of final area and complete the transition at about 40%–50%. Export begins at the tip and gradually proceeds toward the base: during the transition the tip is already exporting while the base is still importing from other source leaves.

Cessation of import and onset of export are two independent events. Albino tobacco leaves cannot photosynthesize and therefore cannot export, but they stop importing at the same developmental stage as green leaves. In tobacco leaves, unloading and loading are carried out almost by different veins: in the young leaf, assimilates are imported through the larger major veins and unloaded from them into the mesophyll, while the finest minor veins are not yet mature; at maturity, sugar is loaded through the minor veins, and the major veins serve only for export and no longer unload. Cessation of import may be due to blockage of the unloading channels in the major veins; closure and reduced number of plasmodesmata have both been observed. The onset of export requires several conditions: the leaf's photosynthate has become surplus, and genes for sucrose synthesis begin to be expressed; the minor veins responsible for loading have matured; and the **symporter** (SUC2 in *Arabidopsis*) is in place in the plasma membrane of the SE–CC complex, with activation of its promoter in the companion cells also following the order from tip to base.

## 11.6. The Pressure-Flow Hypothesis

### 11.6.1. Content of the Hypothesis

**Definition 11.6.1 (Pressure-Flow Hypothesis)** The hypothesis that transport in the phloem is bulk flow of solution in the sieve tubes, driven by a turgor gradient produced by osmosis at the source and sink ends, is the **pressure-flow hypothesis**, proposed by Münch in 1930.

Its main points:

1. At the source end, sugar is loaded into the SE–CC complex (Section 11.7), the solute potential $\Psi_{\mathrm{s}}$ of the sieve elements falls, their **water potential** falls with it, water enters the sieve elements from the neighboring xylem down the water potential gradient, and the turgor $\Psi_{\mathrm{p}}$ rises.
2. At the sink end, sugar is unloaded (Section 11.8), the solute potential of the sieve elements rises, their water potential becomes higher than that of the xylem, water flows out, and turgor falls.
3. The turgor difference between source and sink ends drives bulk flow of sap along the sieve tube, and the solutes move with the water without crossing a membrane as they pass the sieve pores.
4. As long as the source end keeps loading and the sink end keeps unloading, water circulates continually between phloem and xylem and a pressure gradient is maintained between source and sink.

Because it is the pressure difference and not the water potential difference that drives bulk flow, flow can run from a source of lower water potential to a sink of higher water potential, which does not violate thermodynamics: osmosis (water flow across a membrane) is driven by the water potential gradient, whereas bulk flow is driven by the pressure gradient. A set of possible values given by Nobel:

| Location | $\Psi_{\mathrm{w}}$ / MPa | $\Psi_{\mathrm{p}}$ / MPa | $\Psi_{\mathrm{s}}$ / MPa |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sieve element at source end | −1.1 | 0.6 | −1.7 |
| Xylem at source end | −0.8 | −0.7 | −0.1 |
| Sieve element at sink end | −0.4 | 0.3 | −0.7 |
| Xylem at sink end | −0.6 | −0.5 | −0.1 |

At the source end, the water potential of the sieve element (−1.1 MPa) is lower than that of the xylem (−0.8 MPa) and water enters the sieve element; at the sink end, the water potential of the sieve element (−0.4 MPa) is higher than that of the xylem (−0.6 MPa) and water flows out. In the sieve tube from source to sink, turgor falls from 0.6 MPa to 0.3 MPa, which drives the flow of sap, while the water potential actually rises from −1.1 MPa to −0.4 MPa.

### 11.6.2. The One-Dimensional Münch Model

Treat the sieve tube as a circular tube of radius $a$ and the sap as having viscosity $\eta$. By the **Hagen–Poiseuille law**, the cross-sectional mean velocity is proportional to the pressure gradient; sieve plates increase the resistance by a factor $f$ ($f \approx 2$–$3$; sieve plates contribute half or more of the total resistance):

**Formula 11.6.1 (Bulk Flow in the Sieve Tube)**

$$
u = -\frac{a^2}{8\eta f}\frac{\mathrm{d}P}{\mathrm{d}x}. \tag{11.1}
$$

Along the sieve tube, water enters or leaves from the xylem (water potential $\Psi_{\mathrm{x}}$) across the membrane, and the change in volume flow per unit length is

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}(\pi a^2 u)}{\mathrm{d}x} = 2\pi a L_{\mathrm{p}}\bigl(\Psi_{\mathrm{x}} - P + cRT\bigr), \tag{11.2}
$$
where $L_{\mathrm{p}}$ is the hydraulic conductivity of the sieve element membrane and $c$ is the osmolarity of the sap; conservation of solute is

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}(\pi a^2 u\,c)}{\mathrm{d}x} = s(x), \tag{11.3}
$$
where $s(x)$ is positive in the source region (loading) and negative in the sink region (unloading). Equations (11.1)–(11.3) form a boundary-value problem with both ends closed ($u = 0$).

**Example 11.6.1 (Pressure Gradient Required)** Take $a = 10\ \mu\mathrm{m}$, $\eta = 1.7\ \mathrm{mPa\,s}$ (about a 0.5 M sucrose solution), $f = 2$; to obtain $u = 1\ \mathrm{m\,h^{-1}} = 2.78\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, Eq. (11.1) gives

$$
-\frac{\mathrm{d}P}{\mathrm{d}x} = \frac{8\eta f u}{a^2} = \frac{8\times1.7\times10^{-3}\times2\times2.78\times10^{-4}}{(10\times10^{-6})^2}\ \mathrm{Pa\,m^{-1}} \approx 0.076\ \mathrm{MPa\,m^{-1}}.
$$
Herbaceous plants have transport distances of less than 1 m, and the sieve element turgor of 0.7–1.5 MPa measured with aphid stylets is sufficient to drive flow; but if the tube diameter were constant, a 50 m tree would need a pressure difference of about 4 MPa, whereas the turgor measured or calculated in trees is similar to that of herbaceous plants (Section 11.6.3). This is the main quantitative problem facing the pressure-flow hypothesis in large plants.

### 11.6.3. Tests of the Hypothesis and Open Questions

The pressure-flow hypothesis makes several testable predictions:

| Prediction | Evidence |
|---|---|
| Simultaneous bidirectional transport cannot occur within one sieve element (the solution in one tube can flow in only one direction at a time) | With two different tracers fed to two source leaves, bidirectional transport can be detected in the stem between them, but only in different vascular bundles or in adjacent sieve tubes of the same bundle; simultaneous bidirectional transport within a single sieve element has never been demonstrated |
| Water and solutes move at the same velocity | Velocities measured with carbon-labeled solutes and with magnetic resonance (detecting water flow) are very close |
| The transport pathway itself needs no large amount of energy (energy is used only to maintain the structure of the sieve elements and to retrieve leaked sugar) | Cooling a short section of a beet source-leaf petiole to about 1 °C lowers respiration and ATP synthesis and consumption by about 90%, yet transport is inhibited only briefly and recovers within tens of minutes to hours (Geiger and Sovonick, 1975); treating the petiole with cyanide inhibits transport, but electron microscopy shows the sieve pores plugged with cell debris, so this does not show that transport itself requires energy |
| Sieve pores are open | Sieve pores appeared plugged in early electron micrographs, an artifact of release of turgor during sampling; rapid freeze fixation and in vivo confocal microscopy show that most pores in transporting sieve elements are open |
| Turgor at the source end is higher than at the sink end, and the pressure difference suffices to overcome the resistance of the pathway | Turgor measured with aphid stylets is 0.7–1.5 MPa; in herbaceous plants such as soybean the calculated turgor gradient suffices to drive bulk flow; in willow saplings calculated and measured turgor average 0.6 and 0.8 MPa, respectively, and in large ash trees the calculated value can reach 2.0 MPa |

Several questions have long persisted: the turgor of trees is not higher than that of herbaceous plants; in living sieve tubes of *Arabidopsis*, aggregates of the protein SEOR1 are observed, sometimes filling the whole lumen; and the sieve pores of gymnosperms are filled with membranes. Several alternative models have been proposed, for example the "high-pressure manifold model" (pressure is generated mainly at the source end, and the main resistance lies in the plasmodesmata between the SE–CC complex and the sink cells rather than in the sieve tube) and the "relay model" (the phloem consists of functional units in series, with solutes actively transported between units, requiring energy along the way).

A more direct test of Münch's hypothesis came from the study by Knoblauch et al. (2016) on the annual climber morning glory (*Ipomoea nil*): they measured sieve tube turgor and geometry along the same plant and found that the longer the plant, the wider the sieve tubes and the more permeable the sieve plates, so the hydraulic conductance of the sieve tubes increases with transport distance, and the measured source–sink turgor difference suffices to drive the observed flow velocity. This shows that plants adjust the structure of their sieve tubes so that pressure flow remains feasible over longer distances, and partly explains why large plants do not need proportionally higher turgor. Similar measurements on tall trees remain a technical challenge.

Before the pressure-flow hypothesis was established, active mechanisms requiring energy along the pathway were also proposed: the cytoplasmic streaming hypothesis (longitudinal cytoplasmic strands in the sieve tube move rhythmically and sugar moves with them, which could explain bidirectional transport) and the contractile protein hypothesis (P-protein has ATPase activity and propels the sap by contraction). The structures these hypotheses require (longitudinal cytoplasmic strands, a contractile P-protein network) have not been demonstrated in living, transporting sieve tubes, and they are inconsistent with the cooling experiments and with observations of bidirectional transport; they now have only historical interest.

## 11.7. Phloem Loading

### 11.7.1. Steps of Loading

**Definition 11.7.1 (Phloem Loading)** The process by which photosynthate passes from the chloroplasts of mesophyll cells of a mature leaf into the SE–CC complexes of the leaf's minor veins is **phloem loading**. Leaving the source leaf with bulk flow after entering the sieve tubes is called export, and transport through the vascular system from source to sink is called long-distance transport.

Loading comprises three steps:

1. By day, triose phosphates produced by the **Calvin cycle** are exported to the cytosol by the triose phosphate translocator of the chloroplast inner membrane (in counter-exchange with $\mathrm{P_i}$), where sucrose is synthesized by fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase, sucrose phosphate synthase and other enzymes; at night, starch stored in the chloroplast is degraded, leaves the chloroplast mainly as maltose, and is then converted to sucrose. In some plants other transport sugars are then synthesized from sucrose.
2. Sucrose moves from the mesophyll cells to the vicinity of the sieve elements of the minor veins, a distance of only two or three cell diameters, called short-distance transport. Most of this stretch is the symplastic pathway through plasmodesmata.
3. The sugar enters the SE–CC complex.

There are three mechanisms for the last step: apoplastic loading, polymer trapping in symplastic loading, and passive symplastic loading. A species often uses mainly one of them, but many species can use more than one at the same time.

### 11.7.2. Apoplastic Loading: SWEET and Sucrose–H⁺ Symport

In apoplastic loading, sucrose is first released into the cell wall near the SE–CC complex (probably at the plasma membrane of phloem parenchyma cells) and then actively taken up by transport proteins in the plasma membrane of the SE–CC complex. Efflux is mediated by a subfamily of the SWEET family (SWEET11 and SWEET12 in *Arabidopsis*, with homologs in rice), uniporters that transport down the concentration gradient; uptake is mediated by sucrose–H⁺ symporters (SUC2 in *Arabidopsis*, SUT1 in the Solanaceae and grasses), which carry out **secondary active transport**:

- the **plasma membrane H⁺-ATPase** of the companion cell pumps $\ce{H+}$ into the apoplast, giving an apoplastic pH of about 5–5.5, a cytosolic pH of about 7.5 and a membrane potential of about −120 mV;
- the symporter couples the entry of each $\ce{H+}$ down its electrochemical gradient to the entry of one sucrose molecule.

Apoplastic loading makes three predictions, all confirmed in some species: the sugar transported appears in the apoplast; exogenous sugar supplied to the apoplast accumulates in the SE–CC complex (when $\ce{^{14}C}$ sucrose is fed to beet leaves with the cuticle removed, the label accumulates against the concentration gradient in the sieve elements and companion cells of the minor veins); and inhibition of efflux or uptake inhibits export from the leaf (in plants with SWEET knocked out or SUT1 silenced, sugar and starch accumulate in source leaves and growth is retarded). The solute potential of the SE–CC complex in beet is about −3.0 MPa, markedly lower than that of the surrounding mesophyll, showing that sugar is concentrated against its chemical potential gradient; respiratory inhibitors lower ATP concentration and at the same time inhibit loading of exogenous sugar.

**Example 11.7.1 (Concentration Ratio Attainable by Symport)** Suppose the stoichiometry of $\ce{H+}$ to sucrose is 1:1, the pH difference across the membrane is $\Delta\mathrm{pH} = \mathrm{pH}_{\mathrm{in}} - \mathrm{pH}_{\mathrm{out}} = 2$, the membrane potential is $\Delta\psi = \psi_{\mathrm{in}} - \psi_{\mathrm{out}} = -120\ \mathrm{mV}$, and the temperature is 25 °C. At equilibrium the free energy change for sucrose entry plus that for the entry of one $\ce{H+}$ is zero:

$$
RT\ln\frac{[\text{suc}]_{\mathrm{in}}}{[\text{suc}]_{\mathrm{out}}} + RT\ln\frac{[\ce{H+}]_{\mathrm{in}}}{[\ce{H+}]_{\mathrm{out}}} + F\Delta\psi = 0,
$$
so

$$
\ln\frac{[\text{suc}]_{\mathrm{in}}}{[\text{suc}]_{\mathrm{out}}} = 2.303\,\Delta\mathrm{pH} - \frac{F\Delta\psi}{RT} = 4.61 + \frac{0.120}{0.0257} = 4.61 + 4.67 = 9.28,
$$
and the concentration ratio can reach $\mathrm{e}^{9.28} \approx 1.1\times10^{4}$. With apoplastic sucrose at about 1 mM, the SE–CC complex could in theory accumulate to concentrations far above the actual 0.5–1 M; actual loading is far from equilibrium and is limited by the number and kinetics of the transporters ([7.13.5](/7.13.5)) rather than by thermodynamics. Each sucrose molecule loaded consumes one $\ce{H+}$, and the plasma membrane H⁺-ATPase pumps one $\ce{H+}$ per ATP hydrolyzed, so the energy cost of loading is about one ATP per sucrose, small compared with the energy stored in synthesizing sucrose.

### 11.7.3. Symplastic Loading and Polymer Trapping

In the minor veins of other plants there are abundant plasmodesmata between the SE–CC complex and the surrounding cells, which seems inconsistent with apoplastic loading. The phloem of these plants, such as coleus, squash and melon, transports large amounts of raffinose and stachyose in addition to sucrose, and the minor veins contain intermediary cells. Two questions arise: how can diffusion through plasmodesmata be selective for sugars, and how can sugar accumulate against a concentration gradient?

**Definition 11.7.2 (Polymer Trapping)** Sucrose synthesized in the mesophyll diffuses through plasmodesmata into the intermediary cells, where it is combined with galactinol to synthesize the larger raffinose and stachyose; because of their large size they cannot diffuse back through the plasmodesmata into the bundle sheath cells, but can enter the sieve elements through the wider plasmodesmata, and so accumulate in the SE–CC complex. This mechanism of symplastic loading is **polymer trapping**.

Because sucrose is continually synthesized in the mesophyll and continually consumed in the intermediary cells, the concentration gradient for sucrose diffusion into the intermediary cells is maintained, and the total sugar concentration in the intermediary cells and sieve elements can reach the level of apoplastic loaders. The model makes three predictions: sucrose concentration should be higher in the mesophyll than in the intermediary cells; the enzymes that synthesize raffinose and stachyose should be concentrated in the intermediary cells; and the plasmodesmata between the bundle sheath cells and the intermediary cells should exclude molecules larger than sucrose, while those between the intermediary cells and the sieve elements should be wider. Several studies support this model. The differences in molecular size are not large: the molar masses of sucrose, raffinose and stachyose are 342, 504 and 666 $\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}$, respectively; modeling studies show that pore-size sieving alone can hardly both prevent back-flow of oligosaccharides and let enough sucrose through, and there may be unidentified factors.

### 11.7.4. Passive Loading

There is yet another class of plants in which the plasmodesmata between the SE–CC complex and the surrounding cells are also numerous, but which have no intermediary cells and do not transport raffinose family oligosaccharides, for example many trees such as willow and apple and the gymnosperm Scots pine (*Pinus sylvestris*). There is no concentrating step between their mesophyll and the SE–CC complex, and sugar diffuses down its concentration gradient through plasmodesmata into the sieve elements, which is called passive symplastic loading and is in fact the situation Münch originally envisaged. To maintain a sufficiently high solute concentration and turgor in the sieve elements, the sugar concentration in the source leaves of these plants is generally high. Since there is no active concentrating step, turgor in their sieve tubes also need not be particularly high, consistent with the observation that tree turgor is not high.

| Sugars transported | Sucrose | Sucrose plus raffinose and stachyose | Sucrose and sugar alcohols |

The relation between loading mode and phylogeny was once thought to be that symplastic loading is "primitive" and common in tropical woody plants, whereas apoplastic loading is "more advanced" and common in temperate herbs. From a phylogenetic comparison of plasmodesmatal frequency, passive loading is now thought possibly to be the ancestral state in angiosperms, with apoplastic loading and polymer trapping evolving later; however, the earliest angiosperms may already have had several loading capacities. A combination of several loading capacities allows plants to adjust to the environment; for example, polymer-trapping plants can still maintain loading at low temperature. Loading mode is also associated with traits such as sensitivity to low temperature and the spread of viruses within the plant.

## 11.8. Phloem Unloading and Sink Tissues

### 11.8.1. Steps and Pathways of Unloading

**Definition 11.8.1 (Phloem Unloading)** The process by which assimilates delivered to sink tissue leave the SE–CC complex, enter the sink cells and are stored or metabolized is **phloem unloading**.

Unloading comprises three steps: unloading from the sieve elements; short-distance transport to the sink cells (post-sieve element transport); and storage or metabolism in the sink cells. As in loading, unloading and short-distance transport can proceed entirely through the symplast or can pass through the apoplast at one step:

- Symplastic unloading: all steps pass through plasmodesmata. It is found in growing young leaves (beet, tobacco), in the meristematic and elongation zones of root tips, and in sinks that store starch or protein (such as potato tubers). Sucrose in the sink cells is continually metabolized or converted to polymers and kept at low concentration, so sugar enters the sink cells passively down the concentration gradient; this pathway does not cross membranes, but sink metabolism (respiration and biosynthesis) needs energy to maintain the gradient.
- Apoplastic unloading: one step passes through the apoplast. It can occur at the site of unloading (type 1, the SE–CC complex releases sugar directly into the apoplast) or farther from the sieve element (type 2, first through the symplast and then entering the apoplast somewhere). Storage organs that accumulate high sugar concentrations (sugarcane stem, beet root) and developing seeds need an apoplastic step; in seeds there is no symplastic connection between maternal tissue and embryo, and sugar must first be released from the seed coat into the apoplast (SWEET11, SWEET12 and SWEET15 in the *Arabidopsis* seed coat take part in this step) and then taken up by the embryo or endosperm. The apoplastic step, across two membranes, lets the mother plant control what enters the embryo.

In the apoplast, sucrose can be taken up unchanged by the sucrose transporters of sink cells, or hydrolyzed by cell wall invertase to glucose and fructose and taken up by monosaccharide transporters. Invertase hydrolyzes sucrose irreversibly and keeps apoplastic sucrose concentration low, thereby maintaining the gradient for unloading; its activity is often correlated with sink demand and is one control point at which the sink regulates transport. In the apoplastic pathway sugar must cross at least two membranes (the plasma membrane of the releasing cell and that of the sink cell), and entry into the vacuole requires crossing the tonoplast; these steps may consume energy: in some storage cells sucrose enters the vacuole for storage through a sucrose–H⁺ antiporter, driven by the high $\ce{H+}$ concentration in the vacuole.

### 11.8.2. Developmental Switches

The unloading pathway of one sink changes with development. One method of studying unloading pathways is tracing in living cells: carboxyfluorescein diacetate (CFDA) is nonfluorescent and nonpolar and can cross membranes into living cells, where nonspecific esterases hydrolyze it to fluorescent, charged carboxyfluorescein (CF) that cannot cross membranes. If CFDA is applied to a source leaf, CF can move only in the symplast: if it diffuses from the sink sieve tubes into the surrounding tissue, unloading is symplastic; if it is confined to the phloem, unloading has an apoplastic step. Fusion proteins of virus movement proteins with fluorescent proteins can also be used to trace plasmodesmatal connectivity.

By this method, grape berries were found to have a low sugar concentration early in development and to unload symplastically from the phloem, which favors unloading driven by the concentration gradient; after véraison (the berry begins to soften and color), sugar accumulates rapidly and unloading switches to the apoplastic pathway, allowing the flesh cells to accumulate sugar against a concentration gradient. This switch, together with the large respiratory consumption of malate at véraison ([7.13.12](/7.13.12)), explains how grapes become sweet within a few days. In apple fruit the phloem also unloads through the apoplastic pathway. Re-entry through the apoplast, across membranes and against the concentration gradient, is a rational strategy for fruits that accumulate high sugar concentrations: the sugar concentration in the sink cells is far higher than in the phloem and cannot be reached by symplastic diffusion.

## 11.9. Allocation and Partitioning of Assimilates

### 11.9.1. Allocation

The photosynthetic rate determines the total carbon a leaf fixes, but how much of it can be exported depends on subsequent metabolism.

**Definition 11.9.1 (Allocation and Partitioning of Assimilates)** The process by which a plant apportions photosynthetically fixed carbon among various metabolic pathways (storage, utilization, transport) is **allocation**; the pattern in which photosynthetic assimilates are distributed in an orderly manner among the various sink organs of the plant is **partitioning**. (In the literature the two words are sometimes used interchangeably.)

Carbon in a source leaf has three fates:

- Synthesis of storage compounds: most plants synthesize and store starch in the chloroplast by day and mobilize it for export at night, and are called "starch storers"; other plants store sucrose mainly in the vacuole, and temperate grasses store mainly fructans. Starch in leaves is degraded at night at a nearly constant rate and is almost used up by dawn, which allows a fairly steady export at night as well.
- Metabolic use: for respiration and biosynthesis of the leaf itself.
- Synthesis of transport compounds: sucrose and other transport sugars are synthesized and exported to the sinks; part of the transport sugar can also be temporarily stored in the vacuole.

The control point of allocation is the fate of triose phosphate: whether it stays in the chloroplast to regenerate Calvin-cycle intermediates or to synthesize starch, or is exported to the cytosol by the triose phosphate translocator to synthesize sucrose. The key enzyme of starch synthesis in the chloroplast is **ADP-glucose pyrophosphorylase**, and the key enzymes of sucrose synthesis in the cytosol are fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase and sucrose phosphate synthase; these are the regulatory points that coordinate starch and sucrose synthesis ([7.1.25](/7.1.25)). For example, when the demand for sucrose elsewhere in soybean is high, source leaves store less starch. But in species whose main storage form is starch, there is an upper limit to the carbon that can be diverted from starch synthesis: maintaining a fairly steady export over the 24 h of the day seems to be the priority of most plants.

### 11.9.2. Partitioning and Sink Strength

The assimilates exported from source leaves are competed for by the sinks.

**Definition 11.9.2 (Sink Strength)** The ability of a sink to draw assimilates to itself is its **sink strength**,

$$
\text{sink strength} = \text{sink size} \times \text{sink activity}, \tag{11.4}
$$
where sink size is the total amount (dry weight) of sink tissue and sink activity is the rate of assimilate uptake per unit of sink tissue.

Changing either sink size or sink activity changes the pattern of transport. For example, the ability of one pod of pea to import carbon depends on the fraction of the total pod dry weight that it represents. Sink activity includes several possible rate-limiting steps: unloading from the sieve elements, metabolism in the cell wall, uptake from the apoplast, and the metabolism that uses the assimilates for growth or storage. Two enzymes of sucrose breakdown, acid invertase and sucrose synthase, can each catalyze the first step of sucrose utilization, and their activities are often correlated with sink strength.

Competition among sinks has been observed in many experiments: removing one sink usually increases assimilate going to the others; enlarging a sink (for example by increasing fruit set) decreases assimilate going to the others, especially the roots. Conversely, when all other source leaves are shaded and only one is left, photosynthesis and export of this leaf are unchanged in the short term (about 8 h), but the roots receive less sugar and the young leaves relatively more: young leaves are better able to lower the sugar concentration in the sieve elements and thereby increase the pressure gradient toward themselves. Shading generally reduces partitioning to roots. Lowering the water potential of a sink can also enhance transport: treating pea root tips with mannitol solution raises sucrose import more than threefold in the short term, possibly a result of lowered turgor in the sink cells.

The resistance that assimilate must overcome to reach a sink falls roughly into three segments: loading at the source end, transport along the way, and unloading and utilization at the sink end. In most cases it is sink strength rather than the transport channel that determines partitioning.

### 11.9.3. Source–Sink Coordination and Sugar Signaling

Source and sink depend on and constrain each other:

- the source is the supplier of the sink, and source strength (high photosynthetic capacity, large leaf area) allows the sink to receive more assimilate;
- the sink feeds back on the source: when the sink is strong, sucrose export from source leaves speeds up and the photosynthetic rate rises; when the sink is weak, export is inhibited, assimilate accumulates in the source leaf, and the photosynthetic rate falls.

When the source–sink ratio is changed over the long term, the metabolism of the source leaf adjusts. When soybean plants were shaded for 8 days except for one source leaf, the starch content of this leaf decreased, and photosynthetic rate, Rubisco activity, sucrose content, export rate and orthophosphate concentration increased. Sucrose or hexose accumulated in source leaves when sink demand falls inhibits expression of photosynthetic genes. In general, carbohydrate deficiency promotes expression of genes related to photosynthesis, mobilization of reserves and export, while carbohydrate abundance promotes expression of genes related to storage and utilization. Besides being a glycolytic enzyme, hexokinase is a glucose sensor; trehalose-6-phosphate as a signal of sucrose level and the SnRK1 kinase as a sensor of energy deficiency also take part in the sugar signaling network. Expression of the sucrose–H⁺ symporter is itself regulated by sugar: feeding sucrose to beet source leaves lowers symporter mRNA and activity, forming a feedback loop: lower sink demand → higher sucrose in the vascular tissue → downregulation of the symporter in the source → less loading → further rise of sucrose in the source leaf → fall in photosynthetic rate.

The feedback of sink demand on photosynthesis has an important corollary: whether the photosynthetic gain from elevated atmospheric $\ce{CO2}$ can be sustained may depend on whether the plant has enough sinks to use the extra carbon ([7.13.9](/7.13.9)).

Source–sink imbalance is common in agriculture. When the sink is small and the source large, assimilate export is limited and photosynthesis is inhibited; when the sink is large and the source small, photosynthesis is promoted, but when sink demand far exceeds source supply, grains are empty and leaves senesce early. The "green-stay" (*zhengqing*) syndrome of soybean is an example of source–sink imbalance after the sink has been destroyed: when pods are damaged at the pod-setting stage (often associated with piercing–sucking pests such as the bean bug *Riptortus*), the seeds are few and small, but the plant senesces late because the assimilate has nowhere to go, and the leaves are still green at maturity.

Hormones influence partitioning between source and sink: they control the growth rate of sinks, the development and senescence of leaves, and the differentiation of vascular tissue. In some source tissues, exogenous auxin promotes sucrose loading while ABA inhibits it; in some sink tissues, ABA promotes and auxin inhibits sucrose uptake; local application of cytokinin to a leaf can make the site of application a sink, drawing nutrients to it. Jasmonate-mediated defense responses also change the partitioning of assimilates.

### 11.9.4. Remobilization and Harvest Index

Except for the materials that make up cell walls, the organic and inorganic substances already assimilated in a plant can be redistributed and reused, by way of the phloem. When a wheat grain has reached about 25% of its final size, the plant has completed about 90% of its uptake of nitrogen and phosphorus, and thereafter grain filling relies essentially on remobilization of the nitrogen and phosphorus already in the vegetative organs; during leaf senescence about 85% of the original nitrogen and 90% of the phosphorus are transferred to the ear. Nutrient remobilization during leaf senescence is described in [7.13.24](/7.13.24).

Crop breeding has raised yield largely by changing partitioning. The harvest index (the ratio of economic yield to total aboveground biomass) has risen over the years mainly through breeding; for example, the semidwarf varieties of the **Green Revolution** diverted more assimilate from the stem to the grain ([7.13.28](/7.13.28)). Further yield gains require improving partitioning without sacrificing other necessary processes, for example by reducing unnecessary respiratory consumption ([7.13.12](/7.13.12)) or reducing root exudation losses without harming beneficial rhizosphere microbes.

## 11.10. The Phloem as a Signaling Pathway

Besides transporting photosynthate, the phloem is a channel for long-distance signals. Signals can be physical (changes in turgor propagate rapidly along the interconnected sieve elements) or chemical.

Turgor and hormones. If unloading at the sink end speeds up, the turgor of sieve elements at the sink end falls, and this change is transmitted to the source end; if loading is partly controlled by sieve element turgor, loading speeds up. Turgor may also regulate the activity of the plasma membrane H⁺-ATPase and thereby the rate of membrane transport. Auxin produced in the stem can be transported rapidly to the roots in the phloem, cytokinin produced in the roots is transported to the shoot in the xylem, and gibberellin and ABA are also transported in the vascular system.

Proteins. In *Arabidopsis* and tobacco, green fluorescent protein (GFP) expressed in companion cells under the SUC2 promoter enters the sieve elements of source leaves through plasmodesmata, travels with the sap to sink leaves, and diffuses from the sieve elements of the sink leaf into the surrounding mesophyll; larger GFP fusion proteins can be transported in the phloem to the sink but cannot leave the phloem into the sink tissue of the root. The classic example is the FLOWERING LOCUS T (FT) protein: it is synthesized in the companion cells of source leaves, enters the sieve elements through plasmodesmata, is carried to the shoot apex to induce flowering, and is the main component of florigen ([7.13.21](/7.13.21)); entry of FT into the apical tissue is thought to occur by a selective pathway.

RNA. RNA in the phloem is mostly transported as RNA–protein complexes. A few specific mRNAs can be transported over long distances and produce visible effects in the sink: pumpkin phloem sap contains the mRNA of the gibberellin response regulator GAI; tomato plants expressing a mutant GAI are dwarf with dark green leaves, and when a wild-type scion is grafted onto such a rootstock the mutant mRNA crosses the graft union into the apex of the scion, and the newly formed parts of the scion also show the mutant phenotype. In potato, the mRNA of the transcription factor BEL5 synthesized in leaves is carried through the phloem to the tips of stolons and promotes tuber formation. Sequence motifs in the coding and untranslated regions of the mRNA determine whether it can move over long distances. Small RNAs also take part in long-distance signaling; for example, under phosphate deficiency miR399 produced in the shoot is carried through the phloem to the roots and regulates phosphate uptake ([7.13.4](/7.13.4)); small RNAs related to gene silencing can pass between grafted rootstock and scion.

Control of plasmodesmata. Trafficking through plasmodesmata can be passive (the molecule is smaller than the size exclusion limit of the plasmodesma, as for GFP) or selective and regulated (the molecule carries a transport signal or is targeted). Some developmental transcription factors and viral movement proteins are trafficked selectively: movement proteins act directly on plasmodesmata and increase their size exclusion limit, allowing the viral genome to move between cells; endogenous proteins are thought to act similarly on endogenous macromolecules such as FT. Viruses use this pathway to spread systemically in the plant.

For a macromolecule to be accepted as a phloem signal, it must satisfy the following: it is transported in the phloem from source to sink; it can leave the SE–CC complex in the sink, or trigger a second signal that can; and above all, it can change the function of specific cells in the sink.

## Questions

1. The turgor of a sieve element at the source end of a herbaceous plant, measured with an aphid stylet, is 1.2 MPa and at the sink end 0.8 MPa, the two ends being 0.6 m apart; the sieve tube radius is 8 µm, the sap viscosity 1.6 mPa s, and the sieve plates double the resistance. (a) Estimate the mean flow velocity from Eq. (11.1); (b) if the sap contains 0.6 M sucrose, what is the specific mass transfer rate?

   **Answer** (a) $-\mathrm{d}P/\mathrm{d}x = 0.4\ \mathrm{MPa}/0.6\ \mathrm{m} = 6.7\times10^{5}\ \mathrm{Pa\,m^{-1}}$; $u = a^2(-\mathrm{d}P/\mathrm{d}x)/(8\eta f) = (8\times10^{-6})^2\times6.7\times10^{5}/(8\times1.6\times10^{-3}\times2) = 1.67\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}} \approx 6\ \mathrm{m\,h^{-1}}$. This is several times larger than the measured 0.3–1.5 m/h, indicating that the real resistance is greater than that of an ideal tube with sieve plates (sieve pores partly occupied by P-protein, a smaller effective sieve tube radius), or that the real pressure gradient is smaller; this is one of the quantitative difficulties met in testing the pressure-flow hypothesis with measured parameters. (b) 0.6 M sucrose is $205\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$, so SMTR $= 205\times6.0 \approx 1.2\times10^{3}\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-2}\,h^{-1}} = 123\ \mathrm{g\,h^{-1}\,cm^{-2}}$, far above the measured range, which likewise shows that the velocity in (a) is overestimated.

2. Why are the sugars transported in the phloem almost all nonreducing? State the loading mode of plants that transport mainly raffinose family oligosaccharides, and name a group of plants that transport sorbitol.

   **Answer** Reducing sugars (glucose, fructose) have a free aldehyde or ketone group and are chemically reactive, able to undergo nonenzymatic glycation with the amino groups of proteins; with a sugar concentration in the phloem as high as 0.3–0.9 M, transporting hexoses would cause serious damage. The two anomeric carbons of sucrose are joined by a glycosidic bond, so it is nonreducing and chemically stable; the raffinose family oligosaccharides based on it and the sugar alcohols formed by reduction of the aldehyde group are also nonreducing. Plants that transport mainly raffinose and stachyose (coleus, squash, melon) use polymer trapping, a symplastic loading mode, with intermediary cells in the minor veins. Plants that transport sorbitol include apple, pear and peach of the Rosaceae.

3. In a plant, the apoplastic pH of a source leaf is 5.0, the companion cell cytosolic pH 7.4, the membrane potential −150 mV, and apoplastic sucrose 2 mM. If loading is carried out by a 1:1 sucrose–H⁺ symporter, what is the maximum equilibrium sucrose concentration in the companion cell (25 °C)? What if the stoichiometry is 2 H⁺:1 sucrose? What does it mean that the actual sucrose in the SE–CC complex is about 0.8 M?

   **Answer** $\ln$ of the concentration ratio $= n(2.303\Delta\mathrm{pH} - F\Delta\psi/RT)$, with $\Delta\mathrm{pH} = 2.4$ and $-F\Delta\psi/RT = 0.150/0.0257 = 5.84$. For $n = 1$, $\ln$ ratio $= 5.53 + 5.84 = 11.37$, ratio $= 8.7\times10^{4}$, and the maximum concentration is $2\ \mathrm{mM}\times8.7\times10^{4} \approx 174\ \mathrm{M}$ (far beyond solubility, only a thermodynamic upper bound); for $n = 2$ the ratio is squared, larger still. The actual 0.8 M is a tiny fraction of the theoretical upper bound, showing that loading is far from thermodynamic equilibrium and that actual accumulation is determined by the number and kinetics of transporters and by retrieval of leakage, not limited by the size of the proton-motive force; a 1:1 stoichiometry is more than sufficient.

4. Design an experiment to determine whether the phloem unloading pathway of a fruit changes between early development and maturity, and state the expected result.

   **Answer** Feed CFDA to the source leaves of the plant (after entering living cells it is hydrolyzed to fluorescent carboxyfluorescein, which cannot cross membranes and can move only in the symplast), and after a time section fruits of different developmental stages and observe them by fluorescence microscopy. If CF diffuses from the fruit vascular bundles into the surrounding flesh cells, there is a symplastic connection between the SE–CC complex and the flesh and unloading is symplastic; if the fluorescence is confined to the vascular bundles (phloem), unloading has an apoplastic step. The conclusion can be confirmed by counting plasmodesmatal density between the SE–CC complex and surrounding cells by electron microscopy, by examining expression of cell wall invertase and sugar transporters, and by measuring sugar in the apoplast with $\ce{^{14}C}$ sucrose. For grape, the expected result is that early on CF diffuses into the flesh (symplastic unloading) and after véraison it is confined to the phloem (apoplastic unloading).

5. After anthesis, half of the ears of wheat are cut off, or the flag leaf is cut off. Predict the effect on the grain weight of the remaining grains, on the photosynthetic rate of the flag leaf and on the root system, and explain why.

   **Answer** Cutting off half the ears (reducing the sink): the remaining grains receive more assimilate and grain weight generally increases slightly, but only to a limited extent (the sink size of a grain is determined by cell number and other early factors); assimilate export from the flag leaf is limited, sugar and starch accumulate in the source leaf, and the photosynthetic rate falls within several days (feedback inhibition of photosynthetic genes by sugar), and senescence may also come earlier; more assimilate is partitioned to the stem and roots. Cutting off the flag leaf (reducing the source): the main source for grain filling is removed and grain weight falls; the photosynthetic rate of the remaining leaves (the penultimate leaf and others) and of the ear itself rises (feedback promotion by sink demand), more carbohydrate stored in the stem is mobilized to the grain, and the roots receive less assimilate. Both show that source and sink depend on each other and that grain yield depends on their match.
