The three classes of hormones in the preceding two chapters mainly promote growth. The two classical hormones of this chapter are often classed as "inhibitory": abscisic acid closes stomata, induces seed dormancy and stops growth, and is called the stress hormone; ethylene ripens fruit, makes leaves and flowers senesce and abscise, and causes the short, thick, bent "triple response" of etiolated seedlings. This classification is only rough: abscisic acid is also required to maintain root growth during drought, and ethylene makes the internodes of flooded rice elongate rapidly. This chapter first treats the discovery, biosynthesis, metabolism, transport and physiological effects of abscisic acid (Sections 15.1–15.3) and ethylene (Sections 15.4–15.5), with emphasis on the parts that can be treated quantitatively: the dynamics of ABA content during drought and the effect of xylem pH on ABA partitioning, the response of stomatal conductance to xylem ABA, the stoichiometry of the Yang cycle, the conversion of ethylene concentration between gas and aqueous phases, and the dose-response of the triple response. It then introduces the later-recognized brassinosteroids, jasmonates, salicylic acid, strigolactones and peptide hormones (Section 15.6), discusses interactions among hormones (Section 15.7), and finally summarizes the agricultural use of growth regulators (Section 15.8).

General concepts of hormones and auxin are in [7.13.13](/7.13.13), gibberellins and cytokinins in [7.13.14](/7.13.14). The receptors and signaling pathways of ethylene, brassinosteroid, abscisic acid and jasmonate were treated in [7.1.15](/7.1.15#sec-15.5.3); the ABA signal that closes stomata was treated in [7.13.10](/7.13.10#sec-10.6) in the order of the guard cell and is not repeated here. The developmental processes related to this chapter are developed later: seed dormancy and germination ([7.13.20](/7.13.20)), fruit ripening ([7.13.23](/7.13.23)), senescence and abscission ([7.13.24](/7.13.24)), the roles of jasmonate and salicylic acid in defense against pathogens and insects ([7.13.25](/7.13.25), [7.13.26](/7.13.26)), and stress physiology ([7.13.27](/7.13.27)).

## 15.1. Discovery and Structure of Abscisic Acid

### 15.1.1. Discovery

Abscisic acid was discovered independently by two groups in studies of growth-inhibiting substances associated with dormancy and abscission. In 1963 Addicott and colleagues in the United States isolated from young cotton bolls a substance that promotes boll abscission, called abscisin II; at about the same time Wareing and colleagues in Britain obtained from birch leaves in autumn short days a substance that induces bud dormancy, called dormin. In 1965 the two were shown to be the same compound and its structure was determined, and in 1967 the Sixth International Conference on Plant Growth Substances gave it the common name abscisic acid (ABA).

The name is in fact not appropriate. Later work showed that ABA promotes senescence of organs (the process preceding abscission) and not abscission itself; in most plants it is ethylene that directly controls abscission (Section 15.5.3). The main functions of ABA are to regulate seed maturation and dormancy and the response of plants to stresses such as drought, salt and cold.

### 15.1.2. Structure and Activity

**Definition 15.1.1 (Abscisic Acid)** The sesquiterpenoid carboxylic acid (*S*)-5-(1-hydroxy-2,6,6-trimethyl-4-oxo-2-cyclohexen-1-yl)-3-methyl-2-*cis*-4-*trans*-pentadienoic acid, built from three isoprene units, is called **abscisic acid**; it is a hormone found throughout vascular plants that regulates seed maturation and dormancy, stomatal movement and stress responses.

ABA has one chiral carbon (C-1′), and the natural form is the dextrorotatory (*S*)-(+)-ABA; the double bond at C-2 of the side chain is *cis*. Synthetic ABA is a racemic mixture of the (*S*) and (*R*) enantiomers. (*R*)-ABA is also active in some long-term responses (such as inhibition of germination) but has very low activity in fast responses such as stomatal closure. In light the 2-*cis* side chain can isomerize to the inactive 2-*trans* form, so ABA solutions too must be protected from light.

ABA is a weak acid ($\mathrm{p}K_{\mathrm{a}} \approx 4.8$) and, like auxin, obeys the rule of "ion trapping" ([7.13.13](/7.13.13) Eq. (13.5)): undissociated ABAH can cross membranes, dissociated $\mathrm{ABA^-}$ cannot (it crosses only through transporters), so that within cells ABA is concentrated in compartments of high pH. In light the pH of the chloroplast stroma is about 8, and the ABA in a leaf therefore accumulates largely in chloroplasts; this partitioning has important quantitative consequences for the long-distance signaling of ABA (Section 15.2.3).

ABA occurs in vascular plants and has also been detected in mosses, some algae, pathogenic fungi and a variety of animals.

## 15.2. Abscisic Acid Biosynthesis, Metabolism and Transport

### 15.2.1. The Indirect Carotenoid Pathway

ABA is a C15 compound, but plants do not synthesize it directly from the C15 farnesyl diphosphate (some fungi, such as *Botrytis cinerea*, follow this "direct pathway"); it is derived by cleavage of C40 carotenoids, the indirect pathway. Almost all cells containing chloroplasts or other plastids (such as amyloplasts) can synthesize ABA; the main sites of synthesis are vascular tissues (phloem companion cells and vascular parenchyma cells), and guard cells also synthesize it autonomously.

1. In the plastid: C40 carotenoids are synthesized from IPP of the MEP pathway. Zeaxanthin is converted by zeaxanthin epoxidase (ZEP), through antheraxanthin, to all-*trans*-violaxanthin, and then to 9′-*cis*-neoxanthin or 9-*cis*-violaxanthin. (The interconversion of zeaxanthin and violaxanthin is also part of the **xanthophyll cycle** of photoprotection, [7.13.7](/7.13.7).)
2. 9-*cis*-Epoxycarotenoid dioxygenase (NCED) cleaves the 9-*cis* carotenoids into the C15 xanthoxin and a C25 by-product. This is the rate-limiting and most strongly regulated step of ABA synthesis: drought and salt stress raise transcription of *NCED3* in leaves greatly within tens of minutes, and in seed development NCED controls the peak of ABA. The first NCED gene was cloned from the vivipary mutant *vp14* of maize (1997).
3. In the cytosol: xanthoxin is oxidized by the short-chain dehydrogenase/reductase ABA2 to abscisic aldehyde, which is oxidized to ABA by abscisic aldehyde oxidase AAO3 (containing a molybdenum cofactor, whose sulfuration requires ABA3).

### 15.2.2. Inactivation and Concentration Dynamics

ABA is inactivated by two pathways:

- Oxidation: the ABA 8′-hydroxylase of the CYP707A family (a cytochrome P-450) hydroxylates ABA to 8′-hydroxy ABA, which cyclizes spontaneously to phaseic acid (PA) and is then reduced to dihydrophaseic acid (DPA), with activity lost in turn. On rewatering, *CYP707A* is rapidly induced and ABA returns to its basal level within hours.
- Conjugation: ABA forms ABA glucose ester (ABA-GE), which is inactive and is stored in the vacuole or transported in the apoplast; under stress, the β-glucosidase BG1 in the endoplasmic reticulum and BG2 in the vacuole hydrolyze ABA-GE and release free ABA rapidly, faster than de novo synthesis.

Both synthesis and inactivation are strongly regulated, so ABA concentration can change greatly in a very short time. After a leaf is subjected to water stress, its ABA content can increase about 50-fold within 4–8 h (50–100-fold in some reports), and falls back to the normal level in a similar time after rewatering. In developing seeds ABA can increase about 100-fold within a few days, reaching the micromolar range, and then falls to a very low level at maturity.

The increase of ABA in a leaf under water stress has four sources: increased synthesis in the leaf itself (the main source, induction of *NCED3* in vascular tissue), hydrolysis of ABA-GE, redistribution of intracellular stores (chloroplast), and ABA synthesized in the root and carried in the transpiration stream.

**Example 15.2.1** The basal ABA content of a leaf is $30\ \mathrm{ng\,g^{-1}}$ (fresh weight), and the half-life of clearance of free ABA is about 1 h. After water stress begins, the synthesis rate rises to 50 times its original value within 1 h and then stays constant. (a) What is the new steady-state content? (b) About what is the content 4 h and 8 h after the onset of stress? (c) What if the stress also halves the clearance rate constant?

**Solution** Let $\mathrm{d}c/\mathrm{d}t = s - kc$ ([7.13.13](/7.13.13) Eq. (13.1)), with $k = \ln 2/1\ \mathrm{h} = 0.693\ \mathrm{h^{-1}}$. (a) The steady state $c^* = s/k$ is proportional to $s$, so $c^* = 50\times30 = 1500\ \mathrm{ng\,g^{-1}}$. (b) Approximating the rise from $t = 0$ as $c(t) = c^* - (c^* - c_0)\mathrm{e}^{-kt}$: at 4 h, $1500 - 1470\times\mathrm{e}^{-2.77} = 1500 - 92 \approx 1400\ \mathrm{ng\,g^{-1}}$ (about 47-fold), and at 8 h the steady state has essentially been reached. (In reality the synthesis rate increases gradually, so the rise is somewhat slower.) (c) With $k$ halved, the steady state doubles to $3000\ \mathrm{ng\,g^{-1}}$ (100-fold), but the time to reach the steady state ($\sim 3/k$) is also doubled, to about 9 h. This shows that an "increase of 50–100-fold in 4–8 h" depends on both increased synthesis and decreased degradation, whereas the rapid fall after rewatering depends mainly on an increase in $k$ (induction of CYP707A). (The numbers are hypothetical, for illustration.)

### 15.2.3. Transport and Root-Sourced Signals

ABA is transported in both xylem and phloem and is not polar. ABA synthesized in leaves is carried mainly through the phloem to roots and developing seeds (girdling blocks this transport); ABA synthesized in roots is carried in the xylem with the transpiration stream to the shoot. Besides diffusion of ABAH, transmembrane transport of ABA involves dedicated transporters: ABCG25 exports ABA from vascular cells, and ABCG40 and NPF4.6 (AIT1) import it into guard cells and other cells.

When the soil dries, the root can send a signal before the leaf water potential has fallen appreciably, closing stomata and slowing leaf growth; this "root-sourced signal" lets the plant respond to soil water in an anticipatory manner. In the xylem sap of sunflower, ABA is about $1$–$15\ \mathrm{nmol\,L^{-1}}$ when the soil is wet and can reach about $3\ \mu\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$ when the soil is dry. Early work held that root-sourced ABA was the main signal; recent work shows that synthesis in the leaf itself is often the main source of ABA, and that the root signal may be conveyed more by hydraulic signals and other molecules, for example the small peptide CLE25 synthesized in *Arabidopsis* roots, which reaches the leaf through the xylem and induces expression of *NCED3* in vascular tissue ([7.13.10](/7.13.10#sec-10.6)).

Besides concentration, a change in the pH of xylem sap is also a signal. When the soil dries, the xylem sap of many plants (tomato, *Commelina*, sunflower and others) becomes alkaline by about 0.5–1 pH unit. In the leaf apoplast ABA has two fates: it is taken up and "trapped" by the (more alkaline) mesophyll cells, or it is carried by the transpiration stream to the guard cells. By the rule of ion trapping, the higher the apoplastic pH, the less undissociated ABAH and the less uptake by mesophyll cells, so that more ABA stays in the apoplast and reaches the guard cells.

**Example 15.2.2** The $\mathrm{p}K_{\mathrm{a}}$ of ABA is 4.8 and the cytosolic pH of mesophyll cells is 7.5. (a) What fraction of ABA is undissociated at an apoplastic pH of 6.3 and of 7.2? (b) By diffusion of ABAH alone, how many-fold do mesophyll cells concentrate ABA at equilibrium in each case?

**Solution** (a) By [7.13.13](/7.13.13) Eq. (13.4): at pH 6.3, $1/(1 + 10^{1.5}) = 1/32.6 = 3.1\%$; at pH 7.2, $1/(1 + 10^{2.4}) = 1/252 = 0.40\%$. (b) In the cytosol $1 + 10^{2.7} = 502$: at pH 6.3 the concentration factor is $502/32.6 \approx 15$, and at pH 7.2 only $502/252 \approx 2.0$. An alkalinization of the xylem sap by about 0.9 pH unit reduces the capacity of mesophyll cells as a "sink" for ABA to about 1/8, so that even with unchanged ABA concentration in the xylem, appreciably more ABA reaches the guard cells.

The response of stomatal conductance to xylem ABA also depends on leaf water potential: the same ABA concentration causes stronger closure at a lower leaf water potential. Tardieu and Davies (1993) integrated the two signals in a simple empirical model:

$$
g_{\mathrm{s}} = g_{\min} + (g_{\max} - g_{\min})\exp\!\left(-\beta[\mathrm{ABA}]_{\mathrm{xyl}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\delta\Psi_{\mathrm{leaf}}}\right),
\tag{15.1}
$$

where $[\mathrm{ABA}]_{\mathrm{xyl}}$ is the ABA concentration in xylem sap, $\beta > 0$ expresses the sensitivity of stomata to ABA, and the factor $\mathrm{e}^{-\delta\Psi_{\mathrm{leaf}}}$ ($\delta > 0$, $\Psi_{\mathrm{leaf}} < 0$) expresses the increase in sensitivity as leaf water potential falls, so that chemical and hydraulic signals amplify each other. This model has been used in crop models to simulate transpiration under drought (other models of stomatal conductance are in [7.13.10](/7.13.10#sec-10.8)).

## 15.3. Physiological Effects of Abscisic Acid

### 15.3.1. Seed Development, Maturation and Dormancy

Seed development falls roughly into two stages: an embryogenesis stage of cell division and tissue differentiation, followed by a maturation stage of accumulation of reserves, desiccation and acquisition of desiccation tolerance ([7.13.23](/7.13.23)). ABA is low in early embryogenesis, reaches a peak at the beginning of the maturation stage (mid-development), and then declines as the seed dries. The ABA at the peak has two parts, maternal (carried in the phloem) and synthesized by the embryo and endosperm themselves; reciprocal crosses show that ABA synthesized by the embryo itself is required to induce dormancy, whereas maternal ABA mainly affects early development.

Actions of ABA in the maturation stage:

- It induces the synthesis of late embryogenesis abundant (LEA) proteins and dehydrins. These are highly hydrophilic proteins that protect membranes and other proteins during seed desiccation and give the embryo tolerance of drying; LEA mRNA levels rise in parallel with ABA, and treating early embryos with ABA makes LEA synthesis begin earlier.
- It promotes accumulation of storage protein and lipid: in seeds of *Arabidopsis* double mutants deficient in ABA synthesis and signaling (*aba*, *abi3-1*), storage protein is very scarce, and its synthesis stops before the seed matures.
- It prevents precocious germination of the embryo: in the maturation stage the embryo is already able to germinate, and ABA prevents it from germinating early within the fruit.
- It induces seed dormancy: seeds of the ABA-deficient *Arabidopsis* mutant (*aba*) have no dormancy, and the ABA-insensitive *abi1* and *abi3* have weak dormancy; ABA-deficient mutants of tomato likewise.

The germination of seeds on the mother plant before shedding is called **preharvest sprouting** ([7.13.20](/7.13.20)). Among the series of viviparous (*viviparous*) mutants of maize, *vp5*, *vp14* and others are defective in the synthesis of ABA or of its carotenoid precursors and can be rescued by exogenous ABA to prevent sprouting; *vp1* is defective in a transcription factor required for ABA signaling (a homolog of *Arabidopsis* ABI3) and exogenous ABA is ineffective. Spike sprouting in wheat and rice during continuous rain before harvest is also associated with insufficient ABA content or sensitivity and is an important agronomic problem.

Whether a seed is dormant or germinates depends on the balance between ABA and gibberellin: ABA maintains dormancy and inhibits germination, and gibberellin breaks dormancy and promotes germination. The two antagonize each other in synthesis and signaling: ABA represses expression of the gibberellin biosynthetic enzyme *GA3ox* and promotes accumulation of DELLA, and gibberellin promotes expression of the ABA-inactivating enzyme *CYP707A*; in the aleurone layer, ABA inhibits gibberellin-induced synthesis of α-amylase ([7.13.14](/7.13.14#sec-14.3.5)). How the maintenance of dormancy, after-ripening and stratification change this balance is treated in [7.13.20](/7.13.20).

### 15.3.2. Stress Signaling

Stresses such as drought, salinity, cold and heat all cause ABA in the plant to increase rapidly, and exogenous ABA can also raise the resistance of plants to these stresses, so ABA is called the stress hormone. Through SnRK2 kinases and ABF/AREB transcription factors, ABA induces many stress-response genes: enzymes for synthesizing compatible solutes such as proline (**osmotic adjustment**), LEA proteins and dehydrins, antioxidant enzymes, regulation of aquaporins, and others. An increase in ABA induced by one stress also raises resistance to other stresses, which is one basis of **cross-tolerance** ([7.13.27](/7.13.27)).

Flooding is an exception: in the internodes of submerged deepwater rice ABA declines, favoring gibberellin-promoted internode elongation (Section 15.5.4).

### 15.3.3. Stomata

ABA is the main hormone that closes stomata under drought: within minutes it opens anion channels and outward $\ce{K+}$ channels of guard cells and inhibits the plasma membrane H⁺-ATPase and inward $\ce{K+}$ channels, so that guard cells lose solutes and water and the stomata close; it also inhibits light-induced stomatal opening. The signaling pathway (PYR/PYL–PP2C–SnRK2–SLAC1) and the roles of $\ce{Ca2+}$ and reactive oxygen species are in [7.13.10](/7.13.10#sec-10.6). Because of this action, ABA and its stable analogs can be used as metabolic antitranspirants.

### 15.3.4. Growth, Senescence and Other Effects

ABA usually inhibits the growth of the shoot, but it is not always a growth inhibitor. A moderate basal level of ABA is necessary for normal growth: plants of ABA-deficient tomato and *Arabidopsis* mutants are small with smaller leaves, and growth recovers when ABA is supplied, partly because ethylene production is excessive in the absence of ABA. At low water potential the primary root of maize continues to elongate while the shoot stops growing, and this difference depends on ABA accumulated in the root, which limits ethylene production and maintains the extensibility of the cell wall in the elongation zone; as a result the plant increases its **root–shoot ratio** during drought, which favors water uptake.

ABA promotes leaf senescence and promotes ethylene production, and thus indirectly promotes abscission. ABA is also involved in bud dormancy (autumn dormancy of woody plants, associated with short days), heterophylly (aerial leaves formed under the action of ABA in some aquatic plants) and responses to pathogens (in most cases it lowers resistance to pathogens, antagonizing salicylic acid).

## 15.4. Discovery, Biosynthesis and Metabolism of Ethylene

### 15.4.1. Discovery

In the nineteenth century it was noticed that trees near gas street lamps shed their leaves early and plants near leaking pipes grew abnormally. In 1901 the Russian graduate student Neljubov showed that the active component of illuminating gas is ethylene: it makes etiolated pea seedlings short and thick, with stems growing horizontally (later called the triple response). In 1910 Cousins reported that a gas given off by oranges made bananas stored with them ripen early; in 1934 Gane showed chemically that the gas given off by apples is ethylene, confirming ethylene as a natural plant product. Because the amounts of ethylene are so small, it was generally accepted as a plant hormone only after 1959, when gas chromatography made accurate measurement of trace ethylene possible.

**Definition 15.4.1 (Ethylene)** As a plant hormone, **ethylene** is the gaseous hydrocarbon $\ce{CH2=CH2}$, synthesized from methionine in almost all tissues of the plant, which regulates fruit ripening, organ senescence and abscission, seedling growth and stress responses as a gas at concentrations of the order of $0.01$–$1\ \mu\mathrm{L\,L^{-1}}$.

Ethylene is the simplest plant hormone in structure, is slightly lighter than air, has low solubility in water and dissolves readily in lipids, and passes freely through membranes.

### 15.4.2. The Yang Cycle

The biosynthetic pathway of ethylene was worked out by Shang Fa Yang and colleagues in the 1970s, and in 1979 Adams and Yang showed that 1-aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylic acid (ACC) is the immediate precursor of ethylene. Synthesis has three steps:

1. S-Adenosylmethionine synthetase combines methionine with ATP to form **S-adenosylmethionine** (SAM), releasing $\mathrm{PP_i}$ and $\mathrm{P_i}$.
2. ACC synthase (ACS) converts SAM to ACC and 5′-methylthioadenosine (MTA). This is the first specific step of ethylene synthesis and usually the rate-limiting one. ACS has **pyridoxal phosphate** as its cofactor and is therefore inhibited by aminoethoxyvinylglycine (AVG) and aminooxyacetic acid (AOA), inhibitors of pyridoxal phosphate enzymes.
3. ACC oxidase (ACO) oxidizes ACC to ethylene with the participation of $\ce{O2}$, producing $\ce{CO2}$ and HCN at the same time; this step requires $\ce{Fe2+}$ and ascorbate, is activated by $\ce{CO2}$, is inhibited by $\ce{Co2+}$, and stops in the absence of oxygen (ACC accumulates in anoxic tissue, and large amounts of ethylene are released on reoxygenation). HCN is detoxified by $\beta$-cyanoalanine synthase.

MTA is not wasted: through intermediates such as 5′-methylthioribose and 2-keto-4-methylthiobutyrate it is regenerated to methionine, and this cycle is called the Yang cycle (methionine cycle). The cycle conserves the methylthio group ($\ce{CH3S-}$), and the rest of the carbon skeleton of methionine comes from the ribose of ATP. The plant therefore needs only a small amount of methionine to sustain a high rate of ethylene synthesis, without being limited by the supply of sulfur.

**Example 15.4.1 (Stoichiometry of the Yang Cycle)** Write the net result of one turn of the Yang cycle that produces one molecule of ethylene from methionine and regenerates methionine. Why is it said that ethylene synthesis "consumes the ribose of ATP"?

**Solution** The steps: Met + ATP → SAM + $\mathrm{PP_i}$ + $\mathrm{P_i}$; SAM → ACC + MTA; ACC + $\frac12\ce{O2}$ + ascorbate → $\ce{C2H4}$ + $\ce{CO2}$ + HCN + dehydroascorbate + $\ce{H2O}$; MTA → adenine + MTR; MTR + ATP → MTR-1-P + ADP; MTR-1-P → … → KMB (releasing formate); KMB + amino acid → Met + 2-oxo acid (transamination). Net result: each molecule of ethylene produced consumes 2 molecules of ATP (one supplying the adenosyl and ribose, one for phosphorylating MTR), plus an amino donor and $\ce{O2}$; the methylthio group is conserved throughout, the two carbons of ethylene come from C-3 and C-4 of methionine, and the four-carbon skeleton of the regenerated methionine comes from the ribose of ATP (the other two carbons of ACC leave as $\ce{CO2}$ and HCN). Hence in the long run ethylene synthesis consumes ribose and energy, not sulfur-containing amino acids.

:::

ACS is encoded by a multigene family (eight *ACS* genes encoding active enzymes in *Arabidopsis*), and different members are induced by different signals such as development, auxin, wounding, pathogens, cold and flooding; this is the main point at which ethylene synthesis is regulated. The stability of the ACS protein is also regulated: type 2 ACS is recognized and degraded by the E3 ligase ETO1, and the ethylene-overproducing mutant *eto1* is precisely a defect in this degradation; cytokinin and MAP kinase phosphorylation stabilize ACS. ACC can be conjugated with malonate to N-malonyl ACC (MACC), or with glutathione or jasmonate, reducing the ACC available for ethylene synthesis.

Ethylene feeds back on its own synthesis in two opposite ways: in the ripening of **climacteric fruits** ethylene induces *ACS* and *ACO*, forming autocatalytic positive feedback (system 2); in vegetative tissues and fruit before ripening, ethylene inhibits its own synthesis (autoinhibition, system 1). The two systems and the **climacteric** (the rise in respiration at ripening) are treated in [7.13.23](/7.13.23).

### 15.4.3. Metabolism and Transport

Metabolism of ethylene in plants is slight: a small part is oxidized to ethylene oxide and ethylene glycol or converted to a glucoside of ethylene glycol, but this is of little importance in clearing ethylene. Once synthesis is blocked, ethylene disappears rapidly, mainly by diffusing out of the tissue.

Ethylene diffuses through the intercellular spaces within the tissue and can move only over short distances. Long-distance signaling is carried by its precursor ACC: in flooded tomato the root is anoxic and ACO cannot act, the accumulated ACC is carried through the xylem to the shoot and converted to ethylene in the aerobic petiole, causing epinasty of the leaves (Section 15.5.4).

Ethylene concentration is usually expressed as a volume fraction in the gas phase ($\mu\mathrm{L\,L^{-1}}$, that is ppm), whereas the receptor lies in the membrane and senses dissolved ethylene. By Henry's law, the concentration in solution is proportional to the partial pressure in the gas phase:

$$
c_{\mathrm{aq}} = k_{\mathrm{H}}\,p,
\tag{15.2}
$$

and at 25 °C the Henry's law constant of ethylene in water is $k_{\mathrm{H}} \approx 5.9\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}\,atm^{-1}}$.

**Example 15.4.2** (a) At 25 °C and 1 atm, what gas-phase molar concentration and what solution concentration correspond to $1\ \mu\mathrm{L\,L^{-1}}$ of ethylene in the gas phase? (b) A 20 kg box of apples (ethylene release rate $50\ \mu\mathrm{L\,kg^{-1}\,h^{-1}}$ at the climacteric peak) is placed in a closed cold-storage compartment of $0.10\ \mathrm{m^3}$; without ventilation, how long before ethylene reaches $1\ \mu\mathrm{L\,L^{-1}}$?

**Solution** (a) The partial pressure is $p = 10^{-6}\ \mathrm{atm}$. Gas phase: $n/V = p/RT = 1.013\times10^{5}\times10^{-6}/(8.314\times298) = 4.1\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{mol\,m^{-3}} = 41\ \mathrm{nmol\,L^{-1}}$. Solution: $c_{\mathrm{aq}} = 5.9\times10^{-3}\times10^{-6} = 5.9\times10^{-9}\ \mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$, about $6\ \mathrm{nmol\,L^{-1}}$. So a physiologically effective concentration of $0.1\ \mu\mathrm{L\,L^{-1}}$ corresponds to less than 1 nmol/L in solution; the affinity of the ethylene receptor is comparable to that of other hormone receptors. (b) The release rate is $20\times50 = 1000\ \mu\mathrm{L\,h^{-1}}$; the air in the box is about $100\ \mathrm{L}$ (neglecting the volume of the fruit), and reaching $1\ \mu\mathrm{L\,L^{-1}}$ requires $100\ \mu\mathrm{L}$, which takes only about 6 min. The speed with which ethylene accumulates shows why storage rooms must be ventilated, ethylene must be absorbed (potassium permanganate) or ethylene action inhibitors used (Section 15.8), and why "one rotten apple spoils the whole box".

## 15.5. Physiological Effects of Ethylene

### 15.5.1. The Triple Response

**Definition 15.5.1 (Triple Response)** The characteristic form of dark-grown dicot seedlings under the action of ethylene is called the **triple response**: in pea, inhibition of epicotyl elongation, radial thickening, and loss of negative gravitropism so that the epicotyl grows horizontally; in *Arabidopsis*, a short, thick hypocotyl and root and an exaggerated curvature of the apical hook.

The thickening and inhibited elongation of the stem in the triple response arise from a change in the direction of cell expansion: ethylene turns the cortical microtubules of cortical cells from transverse to longitudinal (or oblique), the newly deposited cellulose microfibrils change direction accordingly, and cells switch from longitudinal elongation to lateral swelling (cell walls and microfibrils are treated in [7.13.17](/7.13.17)). The adaptive significance of the triple response lies in seedling emergence: when an etiolated seedling meets an obstacle such as a stone in the soil, the compressed tissues produce ethylene, the stem becomes thicker and stronger, the apical hook protects the delicate shoot apical meristem, and horizontal growth lets it go around the obstacle.

The triple response is sensitive and specific, was formerly used as a bioassay for ethylene, and is the basis of genetic screens for ethylene mutants ([7.1.15](/7.1.15#sec-15.5.3)): seedlings that remain slender in ethylene are insensitive mutants (*etr1*, *ein2*), and those that show the triple response even without ethylene are constitutive response mutants (*ctr1*) or ethylene-overproducing mutants (*eto*). The dose-response of the triple response can be described by an inhibitory Hill equation:

$$
L(C) = L_{\min} + (L_0 - L_{\min})\frac{K^{\,n}}{K^{\,n} + C^{\,n}},
\tag{15.3}
$$

where $C$ is the ethylene concentration, $L_0$ and $L_{\min}$ are hypocotyl lengths without ethylene and with saturating ethylene, and $K$ is the half-inhibiting concentration. Hypocotyl elongation falls rapidly with the logarithm of concentration between about $0.01$ and $1\ \mu\mathrm{L\,L^{-1}}$, the half-inhibiting concentration is of the order of $0.1\ \mu\mathrm{L\,L^{-1}}$, and the response saturates at about $1$–$10\ \mu\mathrm{L\,L^{-1}}$.

**Remark 15.5.1** The statement often seen in Chinese sources that "ethylene above 0.1 pL/L causes the triple response" is a unit error: picoliters per liter ($10^{-12}$) are five or six orders of magnitude below the actual effective concentration, which is about $0.1\ \mu\mathrm{L\,L^{-1}}$. In addition, the "apical hook" refers to the apex of the hypocotyl in seedlings with epigeal germination such as *Arabidopsis*; in seedlings with hypogeal germination such as pea, the corresponding response is horizontal growth of the epicotyl, and the two should not be confused.

### 15.5.2. Fruit Ripening

Induction of ripening is the most conspicuous effect of ethylene. In **climacteric fruits** such as apple, banana, tomato, peach, pear and kiwifruit, the respiration rate rises suddenly at the onset of ripening (the **climacteric**) while ethylene production increases sharply; exogenous ethylene makes these fruits ripen early, and inhibitors of ethylene action delay ripening. Nonclimacteric fruits such as citrus, grape and strawberry show no ethylene peak at ripening, and exogenous ethylene has little effect on their ripening (except for degreening of citrus). Through the EIN3/EIL transcription factors and MADS-box transcription factors such as tomato RIN, ethylene induces cell wall hydrolases (fruit softening), carotenoid synthesis (lycopene in tomato), starch hydrolysis and synthesis of aroma compounds. In 1991, transgenic tomatoes in which the ACC synthase gene was suppressed by antisense produced almost no ethylene, their fruit did not ripen normally on the plant, and treatment with exogenous ethylene made them ripen and turn red quickly, directly proving that ethylene is a necessary signal for ripening. The climacteric, the molecular regulation of ripening and postharvest physiology are in [7.13.23](/7.13.23).

### 15.5.3. Senescence and Abscission

Ethylene promotes **plant senescence** of leaves and flowers; cytokinin delays it, and the two are the pair of hormones most closely related to leaf senescence. Cut flowers such as carnation produce large amounts of ethylene after pollination or on senescing, and the petals wilt; treatment with silver thiosulfate (STS, in which silver ions block the action of the ethylene receptor) markedly prolongs vase life.

Abscission of organs occurs in the **abscission zone** at the base of the petiole, fruit stalk or pedicel, in which the cells of the **abscission layer** secrete cellulase, polygalacturonase and other enzymes that dissolve the middle lamella of the cell walls (anatomy in [7.12.23](/7.12.23)). Ethylene induces the synthesis and secretion of these enzymes and is the main promoter of abscission. Auxin and ethylene act oppositely in the abscission zone: a stable flow of auxin in the leaf makes the abscission zone insensitive to ethylene; when the leaf senesces and auxin declines, the abscission zone becomes sensitive to ethylene and abscission occurs. Low concentrations of auxin therefore prevent abscission, while high concentrations of auxin (including high concentrations of 2,4-D) induce ethylene and promote abscission instead ([7.13.13](/7.13.13#sec-13.6)). The molecular mechanism of abscission (IDA peptide, HAE/HSL2 receptors) and environmental factors are in [7.13.24](/7.13.24). Ethephon is used to promote boll opening in cotton, fruit thinning, and defoliation before mechanical harvest.

### 15.5.4. Epinasty, Adventitious Roots and Aerenchyma

- Epinasty: cells on the upper side of the petiole grow faster than those on the lower side, making the leaf bend downward, a phenomenon called *epinasty*. Ethylene and high concentrations of auxin induce epinasty; in flooded tomato, ACC carried from the root is converted to ethylene in the petiole and causes marked epinasty (nastic movements are discussed in general in [7.13.19](/7.13.19)).
- Adventitious roots and root hairs: ethylene induces the formation of adventitious roots on stem segments, leaves, flower stalks and even roots (adventitious roots formed at the stem base during flooding favor aeration) and promotes the formation of root hairs; at higher concentrations (about $10\ \mu\mathrm{L\,L^{-1}}$) it makes *Arabidopsis* form ectopic root hairs at positions where they do not usually form.
- **Aerenchyma**: in hypoxia (flooding) ethylene accumulates in the cortex of maize roots and induces programmed cell death of some cortical cells, forming air-filled spaces that allow oxygen to diffuse from the shoot to the root (lysigenous aerenchyma, [7.12.20](/7.12.20), [7.13.27](/7.13.27)).
- Internode elongation: flooding makes ethylene accumulate in tissues (diffusion in water is about ten thousand times slower than in air); in deepwater rice ethylene lowers ABA and raises the action of gibberellin, so that internodes elongate rapidly and the leaves emerge above the water (the *SNORKEL* genes), whereas in tolerant lowland rice cultivars *SUB1A* is induced by ethylene to inhibit elongation and conserve energy while waiting for the water to recede (the "escape" and "quiescence" strategies, [7.13.27](/7.13.27)). This is an example of ethylene promoting or inhibiting growth in different cultivars of the same species.

### 15.5.5. Flowering, Sex and Other Effects

Ethylene inhibits flowering in many plants (such as mango), but induces and promotes flowering in the Bromeliaceae (pineapple) and makes flowering synchronous; in production, ethephon or calcium carbide (which produces acetylene, with a similar action) is used to induce flowering, and the action of auxin in inducing flowering of bromeliads is also through ethylene. Ethylene increases the number of female flowers in cucurbits such as cucumber and melon, the opposite of the action of gibberellin: the *F* and *M* loci that control sex expression in cucumber encode ACC synthases, and plants that produce more ethylene are feminized. Ethylene also promotes the secretion of secondary products: when ethephon is applied to the tapping cut of the rubber tree, latex flow lasts longer and yield increases. Ethylene breaks the dormancy of some seeds (peanut and others) and buds, and acts synergistically with jasmonate in defense against pathogens (especially necrotrophs) and wounding ([7.13.25](/7.13.25)).

## 15.6. Other Hormones

### 15.6.1. Brassinosteroids

In 1970 Mitchell and colleagues found that an extract of rape pollen strongly promoted internode elongation in bean and called it brassins; in 1979 Grove and colleagues isolated the most active component from large amounts of rape pollen and determined its structure, naming it brassinolide, the first plant steroid hormone to be identified.

**Definition 15.6.1 (Brassinosteroid)** The polyhydroxylated sterols and their lactones, synthesized from plant sterols such as campesterol and similar in structure to animal steroid hormones, are collectively called **brassinosteroids**; the most active is brassinolide, and its immediate precursor castasterone is also active.

Brassinosteroids are present at low levels in all organs and are more abundant in pollen and immature seeds. They are synthesized from campesterol by a series of cytochrome P-450 monooxygenases on the endoplasmic reticulum (C-22 hydroxylation catalyzed by DWF4 is usually the rate-limiting step, together with CPD and others) and the 5α-reductase DET2, and finally castasterone is oxidized to brassinolide; high concentrations of brassinolide repress synthetic genes and induce catabolic genes, forming negative feedback. Grafting experiments show that it is essentially not transported over long distances between root and shoot and acts mainly near its site of synthesis. The receptor is the receptor kinase BRI1 at the plasma membrane with its coreceptor BAK1, and the signal passes through inhibition of BIN2 to activate BZR1/BES1 ([7.1.15](/7.1.15#sec-15.5.6)).

Physiological effects:

- Promotion of cell elongation and division: brassinosteroids are highly active in promoting elongation (higher than auxin in many assays) and promote elongation of hypocotyls, coleoptiles and pollen tubes. *Arabidopsis* with synthesis defects (*det2*, *cpd*) and signaling defects (*bri1*) are severe dwarfs with dark green, wrinkled leaves.
- Inhibition of skotomorphogenesis: mutants such as *det2* show a de-etiolated form even in darkness (short hypocotyl, expanded cotyledons), showing that brassinosteroid is required to maintain etiolated growth ([7.13.16](/7.13.16)).
- Promotion of xylem differentiation, and maintenance of male fertility and pollen tube growth; in maize, male flowers are feminized in its absence.
- Enhancement of resistance to cold, drought, salt and pathogens.
- In agriculture: bending of the angle between leaf blade and leaf sheath in rice (elongation of the lamina joint) is a sensitive bioassay for brassinosteroid; moderately deficient rice mutants have erect leaves, which improves light interception by the canopy at high planting density and raises yield. Synthetic 24-epibrassinolide and others are used to improve stress resistance and fruit set of crops.

### 15.6.2. Jasmonates

**Definition 15.6.2 (Jasmonic Acid)** The cyclopentanone-type oxidized lipid (3-oxo-2-(2-pentenyl)cyclopentaneacetic acid) synthesized from $\alpha$-linolenic acid of chloroplast membrane lipids by oxidative cyclization and $\beta$-oxidation is called **jasmonic acid**; together with its methyl ester and its conjugate with isoleucine and others it forms the jasmonates, of which (+)-7-iso-jasmonoyl-L-isoleucine (JA-Ile) is the active form that binds the receptor.

Synthesis of jasmonic acid (the octadecanoid pathway, so called because the precursor is a C18 fatty acid) spans three compartments: in the chloroplast, a lipase releases $\alpha$-linolenic acid (18:3) from membrane lipids, 13-lipoxygenase (13-LOX) oxygenates it, and allene oxide synthase (AOS) and allene oxide cyclase (AOC) convert it to 12-oxophytodienoic acid (OPDA); in the peroxisome, OPDA reductase (OPR3) reduces it and three rounds of $\beta$-oxidation shorten the side chain to give jasmonic acid; in the cytosol, JAR1 links it to isoleucine to form JA-Ile. The volatile methyl jasmonate can transmit signals between plants. JA-Ile makes the F-box protein COI1 bind JAZ repressors, JAZ is degraded, and the transcription factor MYC2 is released ([7.1.15](/7.1.15#sec-15.5.6)).

Jasmonate is the main hormone by which plants respond to wounding, herbivorous insects and necrotrophic pathogens: after wounding, jasmonate rises rapidly in local and distant leaves and induces defense compounds such as protease inhibitors, polyphenol oxidase and terpenoid volatiles ([7.13.26](/7.13.26)). It is also required for stamen development and anther dehiscence (*coi1* and synthesis-deficient mutants of *Arabidopsis* are male sterile) and takes part in the formation of trichomes, tendril coiling, tuber formation and leaf senescence. Jasmonate generally inhibits growth (inhibition of root elongation, germination and photosynthesis), which is one molecular basis of the trade-off between "growth and defense": JAZ and DELLA bind each other, so that jasmonate and gibberellin signaling antagonize each other.

### 15.6.3. Salicylic Acid

**Definition 15.6.3 (Salicylic Acid)** *o*-Hydroxybenzoic acid, which acts in plants as a hormone mainly regulating resistance to biotrophic pathogens and systemic acquired resistance, is called **salicylic acid**.

Salicin from willow bark is the source of the earliest antipyretic and analgesic drug used by humans (aspirin is acetylsalicylic acid). Salicylic acid has two biosynthetic pathways in plants: in *Arabidopsis* the great majority comes from the isochorismate pathway in the plastid: isochorismate synthase (ICS1, also called SID2) converts chorismate from the shikimate pathway to isochorismate, which in the cytosol is conjugated with glutamate by PBS3 and then breaks down spontaneously or through EPS1 to salicylic acid; another pathway proceeds from phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL) through *trans*-cinnamic acid and benzoic acid (for the **shikimate pathway** see [7.13.26](/7.13.26)).

Physiological roles of salicylic acid:

- Disease resistance: after pathogen infection, salicylic acid accumulates at the site of infection and at distant sites and induces pathogenesis-related proteins; it is required for the **hypersensitive response** and **systemic acquired resistance**; the receptor NPR1 and its homologs pass the salicylic acid signal to transcription ([7.13.25](/7.13.25)). The signals of salicylic acid and jasmonate usually antagonize each other, so a response against biotrophic pathogens often lowers resistance to herbivores and necrotrophic pathogens.
- Thermogenesis: when the voodoo lily (*Sauromatum guttatum*, Araceae) flowers, salicylic acid in the appendix of the inflorescence increases about 100-fold in one day and induces large amounts of **alternative oxidase**; cyanide-resistant respiration raises the temperature of the appendix by more than 10 °C above the air temperature, and volatile amines and indoles of foul odor attract pollinating insects (Raskin and colleagues, 1987; quantitation of thermogenesis in [7.13.12](/7.13.12)).
- Other: it inhibits ACC oxidase and reduces ethylene formation (used in some formulations for preserving cut flowers); it induces flowering in some duckweeds.

### 15.6.4. Strigolactones

Strigolactones were first discovered as germination stimulants of parasitic plants: witchweed (*Striga*) and broomrape (*Orobanche*, *Phelipanche*) are root parasites that damage cereals, legumes and other crops, and their minute seeds germinate only when they sense a signal exuded by the host root. In 1966 Cook and colleagues isolated the first such substance, strigol, from root exudates of cotton. In 2005 Akiyama and colleagues found that it is also a rhizosphere signal that induces hyphal branching of arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi and promotes symbiosis, and in 2008 two groups showed that it is a hormone that inhibits shoot branching.

**Definition 15.6.4 (Strigolactone)** The terpenoid lactones produced by cleavage of $\beta$-carotene, containing a butenolide lactone ring (D ring) linked through an enol ether, are collectively called **strigolactones**; within the plant they inhibit branching, and exuded into the rhizosphere they promote the symbiosis of **arbuscular mycorrhiza** and stimulate germination of seeds of **parasitic plants**.

Strigolactones are widespread in land plants. Like ABA, they are derived from carotenoids (see the figure in Section 15.2.1): in the plastid, the isomerase D27 converts all-*trans* $\beta$-carotene to the 9-*cis* form, and the carotenoid cleavage dioxygenases CCD7 (MAX3 in *Arabidopsis*) and CCD8 (MAX4) cleave it in turn to give carlactone; in the cytosol, P450 enzymes such as MAX1 convert carlactone to the various strigolactones, and the differences in kinds among species lie mainly in this step. Strigolactones are synthesized mainly in the root and transported upward through the xylem; ABCG transporters exude them into the rhizosphere. The receptor is the $\alpha/\beta$ hydrolase D14, which binds and hydrolyzes strigolactone and, together with the F-box protein MAX2 (D3 in rice), causes the repressor D53 (SMXL6/7/8 in *Arabidopsis*) to be degraded.

The earliest mutants with increased branching obtained in various species, *Arabidopsis max*, pea *rms*, petunia *dad* and rice *d* (dwarf with high tillering), were all later shown to be defective in strigolactone synthesis or signaling; grafting experiments show that a synthesis-deficient shoot can be "rescued" by a wild-type root, indicating that it can move from root to shoot. The interactions of strigolactone with auxin and cytokinin in bud outgrowth are treated in [7.13.18](/7.13.18). Under phosphorus deficiency the synthesis and exudation of strigolactone in the root increase markedly, both inhibiting branching to save nutrients and recruiting mycorrhizal fungi to raise phosphorus uptake ([7.13.4](/7.13.4)). In agriculture, synthetic strigolactone analogs can be applied to fields before sowing to induce "suicidal germination" of parasitic plant seeds in the absence of a host, reducing the soil seed bank.

### 15.6.5. Peptide Hormones

Besides small molecules, plants use many short peptides to transmit signals between cells. Most are processed from longer precursor proteins, often carry posttranslational modifications (tyrosine sulfation, proline hydroxylation and arabinosylation), are perceived by leucine-rich repeat receptor kinases at the plasma membrane, and act over distances from adjacent cells to the whole plant.

**Definition 15.6.5 (Peptide Hormone)** Gene-encoded peptide signaling molecules, produced by processing and usually 5 to a little over 20 amino acids long, which regulate growth, development or defense at very low concentrations through receptor kinases, are called plant **peptide hormones**.

| Peptide | Length and modification | Receptor | Action |

| Phytosulfokine (PSK) | 5 amino acids, two sulfated tyrosines | PSKR1 | Promotes cell proliferation in low-density culture; balance of growth and immunity |
| CLAVATA3 (CLV3) and other CLE peptides | 12–13 amino acids, hydroxyproline (can be arabinosylated) | CLV1, CLV2–CRN and others | Limit the number of stem cells in the shoot apical meristem (WUS–CLV feedback, [7.12.4](/7.12.4)); CLE25 transmits the drought signal through the xylem |
| SCR/SP11 | Cysteine-rich | SRK of the stigma | Sporophytic self-incompatibility in *Brassica* ([7.13.23](/7.13.23)) |

**Definition 15.6.6 (Systemin)** The 18-amino-acid peptide produced after wounding by processing of the precursor protein prosystemin in tomato and other Solanaceae is called **systemin**; it is the first plant peptide hormone to have been discovered (Pearce and colleagues, 1991) and induces jasmonate synthesis and defense gene expression after wounding.

### 15.6.6. Polyamines and Other Signaling Molecules

Polyamines are aliphatic nitrogenous bases with several amino groups: putrescine (a diamine), spermidine (a triamine) and spermine (a tetraamine). They are present in cells at fairly high levels (micromolar to millimolar), bind nucleic acids and membrane phospholipids, and affect cell division, embryogenesis, flower development, senescence and stress tolerance. Synthesis of spermidine and spermine requires decarboxylated SAM to supply the aminopropyl group and competes with ethylene synthesis for the same precursor: when polyamine synthesis increases, ethylene decreases, and vice versa, which may be one reason why polyamines delay senescence. Because of their relatively high levels and nonspecific action, polyamines are usually not listed as hormones but called plant growth substances.

In addition, nitric oxide (NO) takes part in signaling of stomatal closure, germination and disease resistance; *karrikin* in smoke from burning plant material is a signal for germination after fire, and its receptor KAI2 is homologous to the strigolactone receptor D14, which suggests an endogenous ligand not yet identified ([7.13.20](/7.13.20)).

## 15.7. Interactions Among Hormones

### 15.7.1. Levels of Interaction

Almost every physiological process is regulated jointly by several hormones, and interactions among hormones can occur at several levels:

| Level | Mode | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Synthesis and inactivation | One hormone changes the synthesis or degradation of another | Auxin induces ACC synthase → ethylene; auxin maintains expression of *GA3ox* in pea internodes; ABA represses *GA3ox*, gibberellin induces *CYP707A*; auxin represses *IPT* at the node and induces strigolactone synthesis; ethylene lowers ABA in deepwater rice |
| Transport | Altering expression or localization of hormone transporters | Ethylene, cytokinin and gibberellin affect expression and localization of PIN; ethylene promotes auxin transport to the elongation zone in the root |
| Signal transduction | Direct binding between signaling components or shared targets | DELLA binds and inhibits PIF, BZR1, JAZ and ARF, so that signals of gibberellin, light, brassinosteroid and jasmonate interact; BIN2 phosphorylates ARF; SnRK2 with ABA and osmotic stress; salicylic acid and jasmonate antagonize through NPR1 and others |
| Sensitivity | Altering the ability of a tissue to respond to another hormone | Auxin makes the abscission zone insensitive to ethylene; vernalization and sensitivity to gibberellin |
| Ratio | What acts is the relative level of two hormones | Auxin/cytokinin and organogenesis; ABA/GA and dormancy; auxin/ethylene and abscission |

Taiz and colleagues group interactions into three levels: at the first level, two hormones jointly regulate the same target process (additive, synergistic or antagonistic); at the second, one hormone regulates the level of another; at the third, one hormone regulates the level of the receptor or signaling components of another. In analyzing a developmental process, all three levels are often present at once, and the effect of applying a single hormone is therefore often indirect: inhibition of root elongation and promotion of leaf epinasty by auxin are, in many cases, through ethylene.

## 15.8. Agricultural Use of Plant Growth Regulators

By action, plant growth regulators can be divided into growth promoters (auxins, gibberellins, cytokinins, brassinosteroids), growth inhibitors (inhibiting the apical meristem, such as triiodobenzoic acid and maleic hydrazide), growth retardants (inhibiting gibberellin synthesis, [7.13.14](/7.13.14#sec-14.2.4)), and ethylene-type compounds and inhibitors of ethylene action. The main regulators and their uses:

| Class | Agent | Principle | Main uses |
|---|---|---|---|
| Auxins | IBA, NAA, 2,4-D, 4-chlorophenoxyacetic acid | Activate auxin signaling | Rooting, fruit set, thinning, prevention of drop, weed control ([7.13.13](/7.13.13#sec-13.6)) |
| Gibberellins | GA₃, GA₄₊₇ | Degrade DELLA | Enlarging seedless grape, breaking dormancy of potato, brewing malt, promoting bolting and seed production |
| Cytokinins | 6-BA, KT, TDZ, CPPU | Activate cytokinin signaling | Tissue culture, fruit enlargement (kiwifruit), preservation of vegetables and cut flowers |
| Ethylene releasers | Ethephon (2-chloroethylphosphonic acid) | Decomposes in plant tissue at pH > 4 and releases ethylene | Ripening and coloring of fruit (banana, tomato, persimmon), flower induction in pineapple, boll opening in cotton, increasing rubber yield, more female flowers in cucurbits |
| Inhibitors of ethylene action | 1-Methylcyclopropene (1-MCP), silver thiosulfate (STS) | Irreversibly occupy or block the ethylene receptor | Storage preservation of climacteric fruits such as apple and pear; preservation of cut flowers |
| Inhibitors of ethylene synthesis | AVG, AOA | Inhibit ACC synthase | Prevent preharvest drop of apple, delay ripening |
| Growth retardants | Chlormequat (CCC), mepiquat, paclobutrazol, uniconazole, prohexadione-calcium | Inhibit gibberellin synthesis (CPS, KO, 2-ODD) | Prevent lodging (wheat, rice), control rank growth of cotton, sturdy seedlings, shoot control in fruit trees |
| Growth inhibitors | Maleic hydrazide, triiodobenzoic acid | Inhibit meristem activity, auxin transport | Prevent sprouting of onion and potato in storage; control axillary shoots |
| Abscisic acid types | (*S*)-ABA | Activates ABA signaling | Antitranspirant, improved stress resistance, promotion of grape coloring |
| Brassinosteroid types | 24-Epibrassinolide | Activates BR signaling | Improved stress resistance and fruit set |

Ethephon is stable in acidic solution and decomposes on entering plant tissue of higher pH:

$$
\ce{ClCH2CH2PO3H- + OH- -> CH2=CH2 + Cl- + H2PO4-}
\tag{15.4}
$$

Each mole of ethephon releases one mole of ethylene. 1-MCP is a derivative of cyclopropene; at very low concentrations (treatment at about $1\ \mu\mathrm{L\,L^{-1}}$ for ten-odd hours in storage) it binds the copper cofactor of the ethylene receptor almost irreversibly, so that the fruit no longer responds to ethylene for a considerable time, until new receptors are synthesized.

Three points need attention in using growth regulators: concentration (the same agent can have opposite effects at different concentrations, such as the prevention of drop and thinning by auxin), timing (the sensitive period of a crop to an agent is often very short, as for GA₃ on grape berries and chlormequat on wheat internodes), and residues and safety (observe the registered uses, doses and preharvest intervals).

## Questions

1. In a drought-stressed sunflower, xylem sap ABA rises from $10\ \mathrm{nmol\,L^{-1}}$ to $1\ \mu\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$ while leaf water potential falls from $-0.4$ to $-1.0\ \mathrm{MPa}$. Use Eq. (15.1) with $g_{\max} = 0.40$, $g_{\min} = 0.03\ \mathrm{mol\,m^{-2}\,s^{-1}}$, $\beta = 1.2\ \mathrm{L\,\mu mol^{-1}}$, $\delta = 1.0\ \mathrm{MPa^{-1}}$. (a) Calculate the stomatal conductance before and after stress. (b) What is the conductance if only ABA rises and leaf water potential is unchanged? And if only water potential falls? What does this show about the relation of the two signals?

   **Answer** (a) Before stress: $\beta[\mathrm{ABA}]\mathrm{e}^{-\delta\Psi} = 1.2\times0.01\times\mathrm{e}^{0.4} = 0.012\times1.49 = 0.018$ and $g_{\mathrm{s}} = 0.03 + 0.37\mathrm{e}^{-0.018} = 0.03 + 0.363 = 0.39$. After stress: $1.2\times1.0\times\mathrm{e}^{1.0} = 3.26$, $\mathrm{e}^{-3.26} = 0.038$, and $g_{\mathrm{s}} = 0.03 + 0.014 = 0.044\ \mathrm{mol\,m^{-2}\,s^{-1}}$. (b) With only ABA raised: $1.2\times1.49 = 1.79$, $\mathrm{e}^{-1.79} = 0.167$, $g_{\mathrm{s}} = 0.03 + 0.062 = 0.092$; with only water potential lowered: $1.2\times0.01\times2.72 = 0.033$, $g_{\mathrm{s}} = 0.03 + 0.358 = 0.39$, almost unchanged. In this model water potential does not by itself close the stomata but amplifies the action of ABA: ABA alone lowers conductance to about 1/4, and both acting together to about 1/9. This agrees with observation: at very low ABA, a fall in leaf water potential has little effect on stomata.

2. Using Eq. (15.3) with $K = 0.15\ \mu\mathrm{L\,L^{-1}}$, $n = 1.2$, $L_0 = 10\ \mathrm{mm}$ and $L_{\min} = 2\ \mathrm{mm}$, calculate the length of an etiolated *Arabidopsis* hypocotyl in $0.01$, $0.1$ and $1\ \mu\mathrm{L\,L^{-1}}$ ethylene. If a partial loss-of-function mutation of an ethylene receptor raises $K$ to $1.5\ \mu\mathrm{L\,L^{-1}}$, what is the length at $1\ \mu\mathrm{L\,L^{-1}}$? How would this mutant be classified in a screen for the triple response?

   **Answer** $K^n = 0.15^{1.2} = 0.103$. At $C = 0.01$: $C^n = 0.0040$, fraction $0.103/0.107 = 0.963$, $L = 2 + 8\times0.963 = 9.7\ \mathrm{mm}$; at $C = 0.1$: $C^n = 0.063$, fraction $0.103/0.166 = 0.62$, $L = 7.0\ \mathrm{mm}$; at $C = 1$: $C^n = 1$, fraction $0.103/1.103 = 0.093$, $L = 2.7\ \mathrm{mm}$. For the mutant $K^n = 1.5^{1.2} = 1.63$; at $C = 1$ the fraction is $1.63/2.63 = 0.62$ and $L = 7.0\ \mathrm{mm}$. At the commonly used screening concentration (about $10\ \mu\mathrm{L\,L^{-1}}$), its fraction is $1.63/(1.63 + 15.8) = 0.094$ and $L \approx 2.7\ \mathrm{mm}$, almost no different from the wild type, so it would be missed; only by screening at lower concentrations ($0.5$–$1\ \mu\mathrm{L\,L^{-1}}$) could it be identified as a "weakly insensitive" mutant. The dose chosen for screening itself determines which class of mutants can be found.

3. An orchard uses ethephon to ripen fruit. (a) In a closed ripening room of $1\ \mathrm{m^3}$, to reach $100\ \mu\mathrm{L\,L^{-1}}$ ethylene, how much ethephon must at least be decomposed (relative molecular mass 144.5, 25 °C, 1 atm)? (b) Why must ethephon solution be kept acidic, and why does it release ethylene rapidly in air only after base is added?

   **Answer** (a) The ethylene needed is $100\ \mu\mathrm{L\,L^{-1}}\times1000\ \mathrm{L} = 0.1\ \mathrm{L}$, with amount of substance $n = pV/RT = 1.013\times10^{5}\times10^{-4}/(8.314\times298) = 4.1\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{mol}$; by Eq. (15.4), the ethephon required is $4.1\times10^{-3}\times144.5 = 0.59\ \mathrm{g}$ (not counting leakage and uptake by the fruit). (b) Decomposition of ethephon is a $\beta$-elimination of the 2-chloroethylphosphonate anion, which requires dissociation of the phosphonate group (and is promoted by $\ce{OH-}$); in acidic solution at pH < 3.5 it exists mainly in the protonated form and is essentially stable, which is convenient for storage; on entering plant tissue of pH about 5–7, or on addition of base, the elimination proceeds rapidly and releases ethylene. Commercial ethephon is therefore an acidic aqueous formulation that releases ethylene in the plant after spraying.

4. Compare the synthesis of ABA and strigolactones, based on Sections 15.2.1 and 15.6.4. If maize seeds are treated with an inhibitor of carotenoid synthesis (such as fluridone, which inhibits phytoene desaturase), what phenotype do you expect? Why would these seedlings also be albino?

   **Answer** Both are derived by cleavage of C40 carotenoids in the plastid: ABA from 9-*cis*-epoxycarotenoids (violaxanthin, neoxanthin) cleaved by NCED, and strigolactones from 9-*cis* $\beta$-carotene cleaved by CCD7 and CCD8. Fluridone blocks carotenoid synthesis and so blocks both: insufficient ABA makes developing seeds germinate precociously on the ear (preharvest sprouting, as in mutants such as *vp5*; fluridone itself is a tool for studying vivipary), and insufficient strigolactone increases tillering. The seedlings are albino because carotenoids in the chloroplast protect chlorophyll from photooxidation (quenching triplet chlorophyll and singlet oxygen, [7.13.7](/7.13.7)), and in the absence of carotenoids chlorophyll is destroyed in light.

5. (Open question) In a field with water deficit, the leaf expansion of maize slows while root elongation is largely maintained and the root–shoot ratio increases. Design a set of experiments, taking ABA, ethylene and cell wall properties into account, to test the hypothesis that "ABA accumulated in the root is required to maintain root elongation".

   **Answer** Outline: (1) Under low water potential (for example vermiculite or PEG at $-1.6\ \mathrm{MPa}$) compare wild-type seedlings with ABA-deficient mutants (*vp14*, *vp5*) or fluridone-treated seedlings: if the hypothesis holds, roots deficient in ABA should elongate markedly more slowly at low water potential, with small differences under ample water; exogenous ABA should restore elongation. (2) Measure the spatial distribution of ABA content and elongation rate at different positions along the root tip to confirm that ABA accumulates in the elongation zone. (3) Test the mechanism: does ethylene production increase in ABA-deficient roots; if an ethylene synthesis inhibitor (AVG) or action inhibitor (1-MCP, STS) is applied at the same time, does elongation of the deficient roots partly recover; if so, one action of ABA is to limit ethylene. (4) Compare the properties of the cell wall of the elongation zone by extensometer measurement or wall extensibility assays, and expression of expansins; at the same time measure osmotic adjustment (solute accumulation, **osmotic adjustment**) in root tip cells, to distinguish between the two possibilities, "wall more extensible" and "higher turgor". Controls should include the shoot under the same treatment, to show that ABA acts oppositely in root and leaf.
