Most chapters of this course follow a signal through one system: a sensory pathway, a motor loop, a memory circuit. The highest functions of the brain, language, thought and conscious experience, are not located in one system but depend on the coordination of many areas. This chapter takes the view of the whole brain as a network. We first describe its anatomy as a graph and the relation between anatomy and the correlations of activity measured with fMRI, then two global dynamical properties: a hierarchy of time scales along the cortex, and the hypothesis that cortex operates near a critical point. The second half applies this view to language and to consciousness, where large-scale theories make quantitative, testable claims. The cellular basis of cortical areas and the classical anatomy of language and the EEG are in [7.14.46](/7.14.46); network theory is in [7.6.34](/7.6.34#sec-34.1); the dynamics of single networks (E–I balance, oscillations) in [7.16.15](/7.16.15); recurrent networks at the edge of chaos in [7.16.31](/7.16.31); working memory and its prefrontal basis in [7.16.45](/7.16.45).

## 49.1. The Connectome and Network Neuroscience

### 49.1.1. Structural and Functional Connectivity

The **connectome** of a brain can be described at three scales: synapses between neurons (the electron-microscopic connectomes of *C. elegans*, of the *Drosophila* brain with about $1.4 \times 10^5$ neurons and $5 \times 10^7$ synapses, and of a cubic millimeter of mouse cortex), projections between cell types (viral tracing in the mouse), and tracts between areas. At the last scale, human data come from diffusion MRI tractography, which infers the direction of fiber bundles from the anisotropy of water diffusion, and animal data from injections of tracers. In the macaque, quantitative tracing of 29 areas found that about two thirds of all possible area pairs are connected, much denser than older estimates, and that projection strengths span five orders of magnitude and fall off exponentially with the distance between areas (Markov and colleagues, 2014).

**Definition 49.1.1 (Structural and Functional Connectivity)** **Structural connectivity** is the matrix $W$ of anatomical connection strengths between brain regions (axonal projections or fiber tracts). **Functional connectivity** is the matrix of statistical dependence between the activity time series of regions, usually the Pearson correlation of their fMRI signals, measured at rest or during a task.

Biswal and colleagues (1995) found that the slow ($< 0.1\,\mathrm{Hz}$) fluctuations of the BOLD signal ([7.16.3](/7.16.3)) in the left and right motor cortex were correlated in subjects at rest; correlations of this kind organize the whole cortex into a handful of reproducible resting-state networks. Clustering the correlations of 1000 subjects gives seven networks (Yeo and colleagues, 2011): visual, somatomotor, dorsal attention, ventral attention (salience), limbic, frontoparietal control and default.

**Definition 49.1.2 (Default Mode Network)** The **default mode network** is a set of regions, the medial prefrontal cortex, the posterior cingulate cortex and precuneus, the inferior parietal lobule and parts of the medial and lateral temporal lobe, whose activity is higher at rest than during most externally directed tasks and whose fluctuations are mutually correlated (Raichle and colleagues, 2001).

The default mode network is active during mind wandering, recall of autobiographical memory, imagining the future and thinking about other people's mental states, all forms of internally generated cognition. In the principal gradient of cortical functional connectivity, it lies at the far end from the primary sensory and motor areas (Margulies and colleagues, 2016), at the top of a sensory-to-transmodal hierarchy whose other properties appear in 49.2.

### 49.1.2. Graph Properties

As graphs, brain networks share the features of other complex networks ([7.6.34](/7.6.34#sec-34.1)). They have a high **clustering coefficient** and short paths (they are **small-world networks**), a modular organization, with modules that correspond roughly to the resting-state networks, and a set of densely interconnected **hubs** (in humans the precuneus, superior frontal and superior parietal cortex), the "rich club" (van den Heuvel and Sporns, 2011). The arrangement is a compromise between wiring cost, which favors short local connections, and efficient integration, which requires a few long ones; the hubs, which carry much of the long-distance communication and have high metabolic rates, are where the lesions of several diseases concentrate (amyloid deposition in **Alzheimer's disease** begins in default mode hubs, [7.16.51](/7.16.51)).

### 49.1.3. From Structure to Function

Functional connectivity is not structural connectivity: regions without a direct connection are correlated through indirect paths, and correlations change with task and state while anatomy does not. The simplest model that links the two treats each region as a noisy unit near a stable fixed point.

**Formula 49.1.1 (Linear Model of Functional Connectivity)** Let $\boldsymbol{x}(t)$ be the activities of $n$ regions, each with local recurrent strength $w_k$, coupled through the structural matrix $W$ with global coupling $g$ and driven by independent noise:
$$
\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x} = A\boldsymbol{x}\,\mathrm{d}t + \mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\xi}, \qquad A = \frac{1}{\tau}\bigl(-I + \operatorname{diag}(\boldsymbol{w}) + gW\bigr), \tag{49.1}
$$
where $\boldsymbol{\xi}$ is a Wiener process with covariance $Q$ per unit time.
If all eigenvalues of $A$ have negative real part, the stationary covariance $\Sigma$ solves the Lyapunov equation $A\Sigma + \Sigma A^{\mathsf T} + Q = 0$, the functional connectivity is $\Sigma_{kl}/\sqrt{\Sigma_{kk}\Sigma_{ll}}$, and the autocovariance at lag $s \ge 0$ is $\mathrm{e}^{As}\Sigma$.

This multivariate **Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process** reproduces a correlation of about $0.5$ between empirical structural and functional connectivity across region pairs in humans (Honey and colleagues, 2009, with a nonlinear neural mass model; linear models do about as well). The correlation is higher for strongly connected pairs and depends on the global coupling $g$: models fit best when $g$ is just below the value at which the fixed point loses stability, one of several indications that the brain operates near an instability (49.3). Whole-brain models replace each linear unit by a neural mass model (a Wilson–Cowan population, [Definition 15.4.1](/7.16.15#def-15.4.1), or a model of the cortical column with its EEG rhythms) and add conduction delays proportional to fiber length; they are used to simulate the effects of lesions, stimulation and disease on network dynamics.

## 49.2. A Hierarchy of Time Scales

Sensory areas must follow rapidly changing input, while areas that integrate evidence, hold information in **working memory** or plan must keep it for seconds. This division appears in the spontaneous activity of single neurons.

**Definition 49.2.1 (Intrinsic Time Scale)** The **intrinsic time scale** of a neuron or region is the decay time of the autocorrelation of its ongoing activity (spike counts in successive bins, or the field potential or BOLD signal), usually obtained by fitting $C(s) = A\,\mathrm{e}^{-s/\tau_{\mathrm{int}}} + B$ to the autocorrelation at lags $s$ during a baseline period.

Murray and colleagues (2014) measured intrinsic time scales from the baseline activity of neurons recorded in different tasks in macaques and found that they increase along the anatomical hierarchy: from about $50$–$80\,\mathrm{ms}$ in early visual and somatosensory areas, through parietal areas, to about $200$–$350\,\mathrm{ms}$ in lateral and orbital prefrontal and anterior cingulate cortex. Human imaging shows the same ordering at longer scales: when subjects watch movies or listen to stories scrambled at different time scales, early auditory and visual areas respond reliably to segments of a fraction of a second, while the default mode network responds reliably only when paragraphs of tens of seconds are intact (temporal receptive windows; Hasson and colleagues, 2008).

The mechanism in the model of Formula 49.1.1 is local recurrence. An isolated region with recurrent strength $w < 1$ obeys $\tau\dot x = -(1 - w)x + \xi$, so its time constant is
$$
\tau_{\mathrm{int}} = \frac{\tau}{1 - w}, \tag{49.2}
$$
which grows without bound as $w \to 1$, the condition for a **line attractor** ([7.16.33](/7.16.33), [7.16.45](/7.16.45)). Pyramidal neurons in association areas have many more dendritic spines (more excitatory synapses) than those in primary sensory areas, and a large-scale model of macaque cortex with a gradient of local excitation along the hierarchy, constrained by tracer data, produced the hierarchy of time scales while long-range connections let a fast input reach slow areas (Chaudhuri, Knoblauch, Gariel, Kennedy and Wang, 2015). The price of slow time scales near $w = 1$ is fragility: the network must be stabilized by inhibition, as in the inhibition-stabilized regime of [7.16.15](/7.16.15#sec-15.4).

## 49.3. Criticality

### 49.3.1. Neuronal Avalanches

**Definition 49.3.1 (Neuronal Avalanche)** A **neuronal avalanche** is a cascade of activity bounded by periods of silence: in recordings binned at a resolution comparable to propagation times (a few milliseconds), it is a sequence of consecutive bins with at least one event (spike or negative field-potential peak) on any electrode, preceded and followed by an empty bin; its size $s$ is the number of events and its duration $T$ the number of bins.

Beggs and Plenz (2003) recorded local field potentials with 60-electrode arrays from organotypic cortical slices and found that avalanche sizes were distributed as a power law with exponent close to $-3/2$ and durations with exponent close to $-2$, over two orders of magnitude, and that the average ratio of the number of events in the second bin of an avalanche to the number in the first, the branching ratio, was close to 1. Power-law avalanches have since been reported in awake monkeys, in zebrafish whole-brain imaging and in human MEG, although a power law over a limited range is weak evidence, since several noncritical processes produce similar distributions.

### 49.3.2. The Branching Process

**Definition 49.3.2 (Branching Ratio and Criticality)** In a **branching process** model of activity, each active unit activates on average $\sigma$ units in the next time step, the **branching ratio**. The dynamics is subcritical for $\sigma < 1$ (activity dies out, avalanches are small), supercritical for $\sigma > 1$ (activity can grow without bound), and **critical** at $\sigma = 1$, the boundary between the two phases.

**Theorem 49.3.1 (Avalanche Statistics of the Critical Branching Process)** For a branching process started by one unit with $\mathrm{Poi}(\sigma)$ offspring, the expected avalanche size is $1/(1 - \sigma)$ for $\sigma < 1$, and at $\sigma = 1$ the size distribution is
$$
\mathbb{P}(S = s) = \frac{\mathrm{e}^{-s}s^{s - 1}}{s!} \sim \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\,s^{-3/2} \quad (s \to \infty), \tag{49.3}
$$
while the duration distribution decays as $T^{-2}$.

**Proof** Generation $k$ has expected size $\sigma^k$, so $\mathbb{E}S = \sum_k\sigma^k = 1/(1 - \sigma)$. The total progeny of a Galton–Watson process with offspring distribution $\mathrm{Poi}(\sigma)$ has the Borel distribution $\mathbb{P}(S = s) = \mathrm{e}^{-\sigma s}(\sigma s)^{s - 1}/s!$ (by the hitting-time theorem, $\mathbb{P}(S = s) = \frac1s\mathbb{P}(X_1 + \dots + X_s = s - 1)$ with $X_i$ the independent offspring counts, and $X_1 + \dots + X_s \sim \mathrm{Poi}(\sigma s)$). At $\sigma = 1$ Stirling's formula $s! \sim \sqrt{2\pi s}\,s^s\mathrm{e}^{-s}$ gives Eq. (49.3). The probability that a critical process survives to generation $T$ decays as $2/(\sigma_X^2 T)$ (Kolmogorov), with $\sigma_X^2 = 1$ the offspring variance, so the duration density is its negative derivative, $\propto T^{-2}$. $\square$

The exponents $3/2$ and $2$ are those of mean-field critical branching, the values measured by Beggs and Plenz; a further test is the scaling relation between them, which predicts that the mean size of avalanches of duration $T$ grows as $T^{(2 - 1)/(3/2 - 1)} = T^2$, and avalanche shapes that collapse onto one curve after rescaling (Friedman and colleagues, 2012).

### 49.3.3. Why Criticality?

Models predict several computational advantages at $\sigma = 1$: the dynamic range of the response to input, the range of stimulus intensities that the network distinguishes, is maximal (Kinouchi and Copelli, 2006), and so are the information transmitted through the network and the number of metastable patterns it can hold. Cortical cultures pharmacologically shifted toward excitation or inhibition lose the power law and have smaller dynamic range (Shew, Yang, Petermann, Roy and Plenz, 2009). Critical dynamics has a cost, however: the variance and the correlation time diverge at the critical point, and a network operating there is sensitive to perturbations. Estimates of the branching ratio in vivo that correct for subsampling (only a tiny fraction of neurons is recorded) give values slightly below 1, about $0.98$ in the awake rat, monkey and cat (Wilting and Priesemann, 2018): a reverberating, slightly subcritical regime that has long, but finite, intrinsic time scales, in which input is amplified and remembered for hundreds of milliseconds without runaway. The related idea of the "edge of chaos" for recurrent networks with continuous rates, where the largest Lyapunov exponent crosses zero, is treated in [7.16.31](/7.16.31). E–I balance ([Definition 15.7.1](/7.16.15#def-15.7.1)) and **homeostatic plasticity** ([7.16.14](/7.16.14#sec-14.6)) are proposed mechanisms by which the brain tunes itself toward this regime; in **epilepsy** the regulation fails ([7.16.52](/7.16.52)).

## 49.4. Language

### 49.4.1. The Language Network

The classical model of Broca and Wernicke, with an anterior production area, a posterior comprehension area and the arcuate fasciculus connecting them, and the aphasias that follow their lesions, is described in [7.14.46](/7.14.46#sec-46.2.3). Modern lesion mapping and imaging revise it in three ways. First, the regions are larger and more numerous: Broca's aphasia as a lasting syndrome requires damage beyond Broca's area, to the underlying white matter and insula. Second, speech processing runs in two streams, by analogy with vision ([7.16.18](/7.16.18#sec-18.5)).

**Definition 49.4.1 (Dual-Stream Model of Speech)** In the **dual-stream model** (Hickok and Poeppel, 2007), a ventral stream from the superior temporal gyrus along the middle and inferior temporal cortex maps speech sounds onto meaning (comprehension, largely bilateral), and a dorsal stream from posterior superior temporal cortex through the temporoparietal junction to premotor and inferior frontal cortex maps sounds onto articulation (repetition, production and phonological working memory, strongly left-lateralized).

Third, the core language network is selective. Fedorenko and colleagues identified, in each individual, left frontal and temporal regions that respond more to sentences than to lists of nonwords, and showed that these regions respond little to arithmetic, music, logic, computer code or reasoning about others' minds, which engage the frontoparietal multiple-demand network and the default mode network instead (Fedorenko, Piantadosi and Gibson, 2024). Patients with global aphasia can solve arithmetic and logic problems. This supports the view that the language network is a specialized system for mapping between word sequences and meanings, used for communication, and not the substrate of thought in general. Language is left-lateralized in about $95\%$ of right-handers and in most left-handers, and the lateralization is the same for sign languages, whose aphasias after left-hemisphere lesions parallel those of spoken language: the network serves language, not sound.

### 49.4.2. Acquisition and Critical Periods

Infants learn the sound structure of their language before they produce words (Kuhl, 2004). At 6 months they discriminate the phonetic contrasts of all languages; between 6 and 12 months discrimination of native contrasts improves and discrimination of nonnative contrasts declines (Japanese infants lose the English /r/–/l/ distinction). They extract statistical regularities: 8-month-old infants exposed for two minutes to a continuous stream of nonsense syllables distinguish "words", whose syllables follow each other with transitional probability 1, from "part-words" spanning word boundaries (Saffran, Aslin and Newport, 1996). Learning at this age requires a social context: 9-month-old American infants who had twelve sessions of live interaction with a Mandarin speaker kept Mandarin phonetic contrasts, while those exposed to the same speech by video or audio did not (Kuhl, Tsao and Liu, 2003).

The acquisition of grammar has a **critical period** in the sense of [7.16.21](/7.16.21): in immigrants who learned English as a second language, performance on grammaticality judgments was native-like for those who arrived before age 7 and declined steadily with age of arrival thereafter (Johnson and Newport, 1989), and children deprived of language input until adolescence never fully acquire syntax. Sensitive periods differ for phonetics (closing first), vocabulary (lifelong) and syntax.

### 49.4.3. Language Models and the Brain

Artificial language models ([Transformer](/4.7.6), [4.7.9](/4.7.9)) provide quantitative hypotheses about the representations of the language network. They are compared with brain recordings by encoding models.

**Formula 49.4.1 (Encoding Model)** Let $\boldsymbol{y}_v \in \mathbb{R}^{\mathsf T}$ be the response of a voxel or electrode $v$ to $T$ words or sentences and $X \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times p}$ the activations of one layer of a language model for the same stimuli (convolved with the hemodynamic response function for fMRI). The encoding model is the ridge regression
$$
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_v = (X^{\mathsf T}X + \alpha I)^{-1}X^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol{y}_v, \tag{49.4}
$$
and its quality is the correlation between predicted and measured responses on held-out stimuli, divided by the noise ceiling (the correlation expected between two repetitions of the measurement).

Schrimpf and colleagues (2021) compared 43 models in this way with fMRI and electrocorticography data recorded while subjects read sentences or listened to stories. Transformer models of the GPT family predicted responses of the language network close to the noise ceiling, and across models, the brain predictivity correlated with the models' performance on next-word prediction but not with their performance on other language tasks. Electrocorticography shows that the brain, like the models, predicts upcoming words: activity before word onset carries information about the next word, and responses after onset scale with surprise, the negative log probability of the word under the model (Goldstein and colleagues, 2022). The middle layers predict best, and adding representations of longer contexts improves predictions most in higher-level regions, consistent with the temporal hierarchy of 49.2. Sentences chosen by a model to maximize or minimize predicted activity in fact drove or suppressed the human language network (Tuckute and colleagues, 2024). These results support prediction as an objective of language processing, the same objective as in [7.16.48](/7.16.48#sec-48.5); they do not imply that the brain uses attention mechanisms or backpropagation, and models are trained on several orders of magnitude more words (about $10^{12}$) than a child hears (about $10^7$–$10^8$ by age 10).

## 49.5. Consciousness

### 49.5.1. Neural Correlates and the Contrastive Method

Two senses of "consciousness" must be separated: the *level* of consciousness (wakefulness, sleep, anesthesia, coma), regulated by the brainstem, thalamus and arousal systems ([7.16.35](/7.16.35)), and the *contents* of consciousness, what one is aware of at a given moment. Attention and consciousness are also distinct: subjects can attend to stimuli they do not see, and attention is not necessary for the gist of a scene to be perceived (Koch and Tsuchiya, 2007).

**Definition 49.5.1 (Neural Correlates of Consciousness)** The **neural correlates of consciousness** are the minimal neural mechanisms jointly sufficient for a specific conscious percept or experience (Crick and Koch, 1990; Koch and colleagues, 2016). They are sought by the contrastive method: comparing neural activity between conditions in which the physical stimulus is the same but the reported experience differs.

Paradigms that dissociate stimulus and experience include masking (a briefly flashed stimulus followed by a mask is seen on some trials and not on others), threshold stimulation, and binocular rivalry, in which different images presented to the two eyes alternate in perception every few seconds. In monkeys trained to report rivalry, the fraction of neurons whose firing follows the perceived rather than the presented image grows along the ventral stream, from a minority in V1 and V2 to about $90\%$ in inferotemporal cortex (Leopold and Logothetis, 1996; Sheinberg and Logothetis, 1997). Patients with lesions of V1 can point to and discriminate stimuli in the blind field that they deny seeing (blindsight; Weiskrantz), showing that visually guided behavior does not require visual awareness. A difficulty of the method is that the report itself and the processing before and after the experience also differ between conditions; "no-report" paradigms, which infer perception from reflexes such as eye movements, reduce the prefrontal activity found in report paradigms.

### 49.5.2. Two Theories

**Definition 49.5.2 (Global Neuronal Workspace Theory)** The **global neuronal workspace theory** (Baars, 1988; Dehaene and Changeux, 2011) holds that information becomes conscious when it is broadcast to many specialized processors by a network of long-range excitatory neurons in prefrontal, parietal and cingulate cortex. Broadcasting occurs by *ignition*, a sudden, nonlinear, self-sustained increase of activity in the workspace that follows sufficiently strong input after about $300\,\mathrm{ms}$, while weaker input produces only local, transient activity.

The theory accounts for the observation that in masking experiments the early sensory responses grow linearly with stimulus strength and are similar for seen and unseen trials, while from about $250$–$300\,\mathrm{ms}$ the seen trials show a large, widespread response (the P3b component of the EEG, long-distance gamma synchrony, and frontal and parietal activation), all-or-none across trials. A recurrent population with strong self-excitation, the bistable systems of [7.16.43](/7.16.43), produces this behavior (figure below).

**Definition 49.5.3 (Integrated Information Theory)** **Integrated information theory** (Tononi, 2004; Tononi and colleagues, 2016) starts from properties claimed to be essential to every experience (it exists intrinsically, is structured, specific, unified and definite) and postulates that a physical system is conscious to the degree that it has *integrated information* $\Phi$: the extent to which the cause–effect structure of the system as a whole is irreducible to that of its parts, measured by the information lost under the partition that least disrupts it. The theory locates the substrate of experience in the posterior cortex (a temporo-parietal-occipital "hot zone"), whose grid-like recurrent connectivity is predicted to have high $\Phi$, rather than in prefrontal cortex.

$\Phi$ cannot be computed for real brains (the number of partitions grows super-exponentially with the number of elements), and the theory implies that some simple but highly integrated physical systems are conscious while a feedforward network with identical input–output behavior is not, consequences that critics regard as reasons to doubt it. The two theories make different predictions, which an adversarial collaboration tested in intracranial EEG, MEG and fMRI with stimuli shown for different durations (Cogitate Consortium, 2025). The results challenged both: information about the content of experience was found mainly in posterior cortex, with limited representation in prefrontal cortex and no ignition at stimulus offset, contrary to the workspace theory; but the sustained synchronization within posterior cortex predicted by integrated information theory was not found either.

### 49.5.3. Measuring the Level of Consciousness

A practical problem is to determine whether an unresponsive patient is conscious. A measure inspired by the integration–differentiation idea, but computable, perturbs the cortex and measures the complexity of its response.

**Definition 49.5.4 (Perturbational Complexity Index)** The **perturbational complexity index** (PCI; Casali and colleagues, 2013) is obtained by stimulating the cortex with a TMS pulse, recording the EEG response with high-density electrodes, reconstructing the significant cortical sources as a binary matrix of space × time, and computing its algorithmic complexity (the Lempel–Ziv compressibility) normalized by its entropy; a response that is both widespread (integrated) and differentiated in space and time has a high PCI.

In wakefulness and REM sleep the TMS response is a complex, long-lasting pattern of activations; in non-REM sleep, general anesthesia with propofol or midazolam, and the vegetative state, it is either local or a stereotyped slow wave, and PCI is low. In a benchmark population of 102 healthy subjects and 48 brain-injured patients able to communicate, an empirical cutoff $\mathrm{PCI}^* = 0.31$ separated conscious from unconscious conditions without error, and among patients in a vegetative state about a fifth had values above it, suggesting covert consciousness (Casarotto and colleagues, 2016). The bistability of cortical neurons in non-REM sleep, which switch between up and down states ([7.16.35](/7.16.35)), is thought to prevent complex interactions and thus to reduce PCI.

## 49.6. Automatic and Controlled Processing

Psychology distinguishes fast, automatic, effortless processing, which runs in parallel and does not require **working memory**, from slow, controlled, effortful processing, which is serial, limited by working memory capacity and flexible (dual-process theories, popularized as "System 1" and "System 2" by Kahneman, 2011). The Stroop task is the standard example: naming the ink color of the word RED printed in green is slowed because reading is automatic and must be overridden. The neural evidence favors a graded distinction rather than two separate systems. Controlled processing engages the frontoparietal *multiple-demand* network, active across diverse demanding tasks and correlated with fluid intelligence (Duncan, 2010), and the anterior cingulate cortex, which signals conflict between competing responses and recruits control (Botvinick, Braver, Barch, Carter and Cohen, 2001). With practice, behavior becomes automatic and activity shifts from prefrontal to sensorimotor and striatal circuits, the transition from goal-directed actions to **habits** of [7.16.40](/7.16.40). Prefrontal neurons can represent abstract rules: in monkeys switching between a "same" and a "different" rule for matching pictures, many neurons in prefrontal cortex encoded the current rule regardless of the pictures (Wallis, Anderson and Miller, 2001), and the learning of such rules from trial and error can be modeled as **meta-reinforcement learning** in a prefrontal recurrent network ([7.16.41](/7.16.41)). Controlled processing in this sense is a function of the slow, densely interconnected top of the cortical hierarchy described in 49.1–49.2.

## Summary

- Structural connectivity (anatomy) and functional connectivity (correlations of activity) are distinct (Definition 49.1.1); brain graphs are modular small worlds with a rich club of hubs, and resting-state correlations define a few networks, including the default mode network (Definition 49.1.2) at the top of a sensory–transmodal gradient.
- A linear stochastic network (Formula 49.1.1) relates the two through the Lyapunov equation; correlations arise from direct and indirect paths and fit best near instability.
- Intrinsic time scales (Definition 49.2.1) increase from about $50$–$80\,\mathrm{ms}$ in sensory to $200$–$350\,\mathrm{ms}$ in prefrontal cortex; local recurrence $w$ lengthens them as $\tau/(1 - w)$ (Eq. (49.2)).
- Neuronal avalanches (Definition 49.3.1) with size and duration exponents $3/2$ and $2$ match the critical branching process (Definition 49.3.2, Theorem 49.3.1); criticality maximizes dynamic range, and in vivo the cortex appears slightly subcritical ($\sigma \approx 0.98$).

| Topic | Key quantity | Main finding |
|---|---|---|
| Networks | SC, FC, graph measures | FC follows SC with $r \approx 0.5$; hubs and modules |
| Time scales | $\tau_{\mathrm{int}}$ | Hierarchy from sensory to prefrontal cortex |
| Criticality | Branching ratio $\sigma$ | $P(s) \sim s^{-3/2}$ at $\sigma = 1$; in vivo $\sigma \approx 0.98$ |
| Language | Encoding-model predictivity | Selective left frontotemporal network; next-word prediction models fit it |
| Consciousness | Ignition, $\Phi$, PCI | Contrastive NCC; adversarial tests of GNW and IIT; $\mathrm{PCI}^* = 0.31$ |

- Language depends on a selective left frontotemporal network with ventral (meaning) and dorsal (articulation) streams (Definition 49.4.1); infants learn phonetics statistically and socially, and syntax has a critical period. Encoding models (Formula 49.4.1) show that language models trained on next-word prediction predict the language network's responses.
- Conscious contents are studied by the contrastive method (Definition 49.5.1). The global workspace theory (Definition 49.5.2) explains all-or-none late ignition; integrated information theory (Definition 49.5.3) locates experience in posterior cortex; neither survived adversarial testing intact. The PCI (Definition 49.5.4) measures the level of consciousness at the bedside.

In Formula 49.1.1 take two regions with $w_1 = w_2 = 0$, no direct connection to each other, but both coupled with strength $g$ to a third region $x_3$ (also $w_3 = 0$), and unit noise in each region. Show that $x_1$ and $x_2$ are positively correlated, and explain why this limits the interpretation of functional connectivity.

   **Answer** With $W$ the adjacency matrix of the star ($W_{13} = W_{31} = W_{23} = W_{32} = 1$), $A = (-I + gW)/\tau$. The common input from $x_3$ makes $x_1$ and $x_2$ covary: to first order in $g$, $x_k \approx \xi_k * h + g\,(x_3 * h)$ for $k = 1, 2$ with $h$ the exponential kernel of the unit, so $\operatorname{Cov}(x_1, x_2) \approx g^2\operatorname{Var}(x_3 * h) > 0$; solving the Lyapunov equation exactly gives a positive $\Sigma_{12}$ for all $0 < g < 1/\sqrt2$ (the stability limit, where the largest eigenvalue $\sqrt2 g$ of $W$ reaches 1). Functional connectivity therefore does not imply a direct connection; it reflects common inputs and indirect paths, and its changes may reflect changes of a third region's activity rather than of the coupling between the two. Partial correlations or the inverse covariance (precision) matrix are closer to direct coupling: in this linear model with symmetric $W$ and isotropic noise, $\Sigma^{-1} \propto -A$, whose entry $(1, 2)$ is zero.

2. Use Theorem 49.3.1 to compute the probability that a critical avalanche in the Poisson branching process has size 1, 2 and 3, and the extinction probability for $\sigma = 1.2$.

   **Answer** From the Borel distribution at $\sigma = 1$, $\mathbb{P}(S = 1) = \mathrm{e}^{-1} = 0.368$ (the first unit has no offspring), $\mathbb{P}(S = 2) = \mathrm{e}^{-2}\cdot 2/2 = 0.135$, $\mathbb{P}(S = 3) = \mathrm{e}^{-3}\cdot 9/6 = 0.075$. Half of all avalanches have size 1 or 2, while the mean size is infinite at criticality (the tail $s^{-3/2}$ is not summable with weight $s$). For $\sigma > 1$ the extinction probability $q$ is the smallest root of $q = G(q) = \mathrm{e}^{\sigma(q - 1)}$, the generating function of $\mathrm{Poi}(\sigma)$; iterating from $q = 0$ for $\sigma = 1.2$ gives $q \approx 0.686$, so about $31\%$ of avalanches run away, as in panel A of the figure in 49.3.2.

3. In a masking experiment, the EEG response at $100\,\mathrm{ms}$ grows linearly with target duration while the P3b at $300$–$500\,\mathrm{ms}$ is absent below a threshold and full-sized above it, and the subjective visibility ratings follow the P3b. Explain this pattern with the model of the ignition figure, and state one observation that would count against the workspace interpretation.

   **Answer** The early response reflects the sensory population, which is linear in its input. The late response reflects the workspace population, which is bistable because of strong recurrent excitation: input below a threshold decays back to rest, input above it pushes the state across the unstable fixed point into the self-sustained high state, which then decays only through adaptation. Noise makes the crossing probabilistic near threshold, so trial averages show a steep sigmoid and single trials are bimodal, as visibility reports are. Against the workspace interpretation would be finding that the late ignition depends on the requirement to report rather than on seeing (in no-report paradigms the P3b is greatly reduced while the stimulus is still perceived), or that the contents of a conscious percept are not decodable from prefrontal and parietal cortex while they are decodable from posterior cortex, as the 2025 adversarial study partly found.

4. Why do encoding models such as Eq. (49.4) need regularization and a held-out test set, and why is the predictivity divided by a noise ceiling? What would a model that predicts the language network perfectly still fail to explain?

   **Answer** Language-model layers have hundreds to thousands of features ($p$), comparable to or larger than the number of stimuli ($T$), so ordinary least squares would fit noise perfectly and generalize poorly; ridge regularization shrinks the coefficients, and only the correlation on held-out stimuli measures genuine prediction. Because the measured responses contain noise that no model can predict, even the true model would not reach a correlation of 1; dividing by the correlation between repeated measurements (the noise ceiling) expresses performance as a fraction of the explainable variance. A perfect encoding model would establish that the model's features are linearly related to the brain's, but not how the brain computes them (with which circuits, learning rules and data), nor which features are causally used for comprehension; that requires perturbation, lesion data and models constrained by biology.
