Neuroscience asks how a nervous system produces perception, action, memory and thought, and how it comes to be wired the way it is. Its objects span more than ten orders of magnitude in space and time, from a channel that opens in a millisecond to a memory that lasts a lifetime, from a synapse of a fraction of a micrometer to networks that cover the whole brain. Computational neuroscience is the part of the field that describes these objects in mathematical terms, and in this course the term is meant broadly: the aim is not a separate theory beside biology but the whole framework of neurobiology, written wherever possible in quantitative language. A channel is a conductance with first-order gates, and the **action potential** follows from the **Hodgkin–Huxley model**; whether a neuron starts firing at low rates or abruptly is decided by the **bifurcation** through which it leaves rest; transmitter release is a binomial process; a stimulus is encoded in **spike trains** whose statistics determine how well it can be decoded; learning is a change of synaptic weights under a rule, and memory is an **attractor** of the network dynamics that the rule creates; and the dopamine signal that drives conditioning is the prediction error of **reinforcement learning**. Throughout, two sources of evidence are treated together: the experiments that perturb and record from nervous systems, and the models that make a proposed mechanism precise enough to be calculated and tested.

The course has nine parts, and within them chapters of two kinds alternate, as in [developmental biology](/7.5). The main-line chapters follow the framework of neurobiology, each with its key experiments and its numbers; the model chapters develop the mathematics that the next main-line chapters need and follow the biology on which they depend. After an introduction to the questions, levels of explanation and methods of the field, and to the organization of the nervous system into cell types, circuit motifs and brain regions, the course builds the nervous system from its parts. Ion channels, the passive membrane and the action potential are followed by the geometry of **excitability**, bifurcations and bursting, and simplified spiking neurons; transmitter release, receptors and neuromodulation by models of synapses and short-term plasticity, dendritic integration and long-term **synaptic plasticity**. A chapter on firing-rate models and network dynamics then supplies the language of the rest of the course. The sensory part pairs each system with the theory it motivates: neural encoding and **receptive fields**, the retina and visual cortex with hierarchical models of vision and efficient coding, the activity-dependent wiring of the brain with Hebbian self-organization, the chemical senses, hearing and somatosensation, and then decoding, Bayesian perception and the analysis of population activity on **neural manifolds**. The motor part goes from the principles of sensorimotor control through spinal circuits and recurrent network dynamics to motor cortex, brain–machine interfaces and the cerebellum. A part on regulation and motivation covers the hypothalamus, circadian rhythms and sleep, social behavior, emotion and reward, nondeclarative memory and the basal ganglia, interleaved with two chapters on reinforcement learning. The part on memory and cognition begins with the hippocampus and continues through discrete and continuous attractor networks, working memory, cognitive maps, decision-making, generative models, large-scale brain networks with language and consciousness, and the learning algorithms the brain might use to solve the **credit assignment problem**. Neurodegenerative, psychiatric and neurodevelopmental disorders, the evolution of nervous systems and a course summary close the course.

The physiology on which these chapters build, membrane potentials, the senses, reflexes and the gross anatomy of the motor and regulatory systems, is treated in [animal physiology](/7.14), and the physics of ion transport, the cable equation and the simulation of the action potential in [biophysics](/7.6.30); the neural tube, neurogenesis and the molecular cues of axon guidance come from [developmental biology](/7.5). The mathematics comes from several courses: phase planes, stability and bifurcations from [ordinary differential equations](/2.4), diffusion and the cable from [partial differential equations](/2.5), Poisson processes and the statistics of spike counts from [probability](/3.1), principal components from [multivariate analysis](/3.4) and inference from [Bayesian statistics](/3.7). The relation with [machine learning](/4.6) and [artificial neural networks](/4.7) runs in both directions: the perceptron, the convolutional network and temporal-difference learning were modeled on neurons, the visual cortex and conditioning, and deep networks are now used in turn as models of the brain and as tools to analyze its data; here we use their results and do not repeat their theory. Feedback loops and oscillators are shared with [systems biology](/7.9), and the comparative and evolutionary view with [evolutionary biology](/7.10).

In olympiad training I treated the nervous system as one more branch of physiology, and the branch I understood least: a long list of fragmented facts, with models that I knew only qualitatively. Reading Luo's *Principles of Neurobiology* changed this picture. It showed me how large a subject neurobiology is, and why so many of my classmates at university want to become neuroscientists. It is also, I think, the field in which artificial intelligence and science now advance each other most actively. Neuroscience gave AI its first ideas, from the artificial neuron to convolution and reward prediction error; AI now returns models of the brain that can be compared with recordings, brain-inspired and **neuromorphic computing**, and the tools with which connectomes are reconstructed and behavior is tracked. Never before have we had such powerful means to study the nervous system: thousands of neurons recorded at once, cell types switched on and off with light, circuits reconstructed synapse by synapse. Even the questions that sound qualitative, which cells carry a memory or what a brain area does, are now answered by measurement, and the answer is a statistical one: how much information a population carries, which variable it encodes, which model explains its dynamics. For this reason almost every problem of modern neuroscience needs quantitative means to be posed or answered.

These pages are therefore more than their title suggests. Although the course is called computational neuroscience, I wanted it to cover the framework of neurobiology completely, following Luo and Kandel's *Principles of Neural Science*, the most classic textbook of neuroscience I know, while the three courses together with Izhikevich and Dayan and Abbott supply the computational side, which I believe they cover thoroughly. The arrangement follows the one we used for developmental biology: the main line and the models depend on each other, a model is introduced right after the biology it describes and before the chapters that use it, and every part of the main line is itself described as quantitatively as possible. Read in this way, a model is no longer an appendix to the facts but the way in which the facts become one subject.
