The previous chapter treated the body as a plant to be controlled. Here we descend to the last stages of the motor hierarchy, where commands become force. Every movement is produced by motor neurons in the spinal cord and brainstem, the final common path of [7.14.44](/7.14.44#sec-44.1.1), and the spinal circuits that drive them are not passive relays: they grade force by recruiting motor units in a fixed order, amplify and prolong their inputs with intrinsic currents, and generate the rhythms of walking and swimming by themselves. The physiology of the motor unit, of the muscle and of the spinal reflexes is described in [7.14.8](/7.14.8), [7.14.9](/7.14.9) and [7.14.44](/7.14.44), and the rate model of the half-center oscillator in [7.14.6](/7.14.6#sec-6.4). This chapter adds the quantitative models of recruitment and rhythm generation and the genetic dissection of the locomotor network that has identified, over the last two decades, which classes of spinal interneurons implement which aspect of the rhythm.

## 30.1. Motor Units and the Size Principle

### 30.1.1. The Motor Unit as the Quantum of Force

A **motor unit** ([Definition 8.1.1](/7.14.8#def-8.1.1)) is a motor neuron and the muscle fibers it innervates; because the neuromuscular junction has a large safety factor, each motor neuron spike makes all its fibers contract, and the motor unit is the smallest increment of force the nervous system can command. The motor neurons that innervate one muscle form a motor pool, a cluster of dozens to a few thousand cells at a fixed position in the ventral horn, and the fibers of one unit are scattered across the muscle so that each unit pulls evenly. The innervation ratio varies over three orders of magnitude: about 10 fibers per unit in extraocular muscles, about 100 in the small muscles of the hand and up to 2000 in the gastrocnemius. Within a pool, units differ in size and type: small, slow, fatigue-resistant (type S) units, and larger fast units that are fatigue-resistant (FR) or fatigable (FF), whose twitch forces span a range of up to a hundredfold (Burke and colleagues, 1973).

The nervous system grades force in two ways: by recruiting more units and by raising the firing rate of the active ones, which fuses their twitches into a tetanus ([7.14.9](/7.14.9#sec-9.8.1)). Recruitment follows the **size principle** ([Definition 9.8.2](/7.14.9#def-9.8.2)): Henneman and colleagues (1957, 1965) found that as the stretch of a muscle increased, motor axons were recruited in order of increasing spike amplitude, that is, of increasing axon diameter and unit size, and derecruited in the reverse order. Most of the order follows from Ohm's law. Smaller motor neurons have a smaller membrane area and therefore a higher **input resistance** $R_{\mathrm{in}}$, so a common synaptic current depolarizes them more; their rheobase, the current needed to reach threshold, scales with their input conductance, and the order is preserved whether the input comes from the stretch reflex, from descending pathways or from direct current injection.

### 30.1.2. A Quantitative Recruitment Model

**Formula 30.1.1 (Recruitment and Rate Coding in a Motor Pool)** Let a pool of $N$ units receive a common excitatory drive $E$. Unit $i$ has recruitment threshold $E_i$ and peak twitch force $P_i$, both increasing with $i$, typically exponentially:
$$
E_i = E_1\,\rho_E^{(i-1)/(N-1)}, \qquad P_i = P_1\,\rho_P^{(i-1)/(N-1)}, \tag{30.1}
$$
with ranges $\rho_E \approx 30$ and $\rho_P \approx 100$. Above threshold the unit fires at $f_i = f_{\min} + g_{\mathrm{e}}(E - E_i)$, up to $f_{\max}$, and produces the force $F_i = P_i\,\phi(f_i)$, where $\phi$ rises from about $0.1$ (an unfused twitch train) to 1 (a fused tetanus). The force of the muscle is $F = \sum_iF_i$ (Fuglevand, Winter and Patla, 1993).

The exponential distribution means that each unit adds a roughly constant fraction of the force already present: a fine increment where the force is small and a coarse one where it is large. The precision of force is therefore roughly constant in relative terms, a motor analogue of the **Weber–Fechner law**. The same structure generates noise. Each spike adds a twitch of size about $P_i$, so the variance contributed by unit $i$ grows as $f_iP_i^2$; because larger forces are produced by recruiting larger units, the standard deviation of the force grows approximately in proportion to its mean (figure below, C). This is the signal-dependent noise ([Definition 29.5.1](/7.16.29#def-29.5.1)) that shapes the optimal planning of movements, and Jones, Hamilton and Wolpert (2002) showed that a recruitment model of this kind accounts for the measured coefficient of variation of isometric force, a few percent over most of the range. A pool of equal units would instead produce a standard deviation that grows only as the square root of the force, with poor relative precision at low forces.

![Recruitment and force in a pool of $120$ motor units (Formula 30.1.1; thresholds spread exponentially between $1.7$ and $50\,\%$ of the maximal drive, twitch forces over a $100$-fold range, firing rates from $8\,\mathrm{Hz}$ at threshold rising by $1\,\mathrm{Hz}$ per $\%$ of drive to $35\,\mathrm{Hz}$). A: firing rate of every tenth unit against the drive: units are recruited in order of size (dark to light), and each then increases its rate. B: total force (blue) and number of active units (orange, dashed) against the drive: recruitment is complete at $50\,\%$ of the drive, and force increases further by rate coding. C: coefficient of variation of the force (shot-noise model, each spike adding a twitch) against mean force: for the size-ordered pool it is nearly constant over most of the range, whereas for a pool of equal units it decreases steadily, so that the absolute noise grows only as the square root of the force.](./plots/size-principle.py)

The size principle is a rule of thumb, not a law. During some fast ballistic movements and in some tasks for which muscles have several mechanical actions, the order can be altered, and in the cat paw-shaking response fast units are active with little activity of slow ones. These exceptions show that recruitment order can be overridden by the distribution of synaptic input, but in the great majority of movements the intrinsic order holds, which reduces the control of a muscle with thousands of motor neurons to the control of a single drive.

## 30.2. Inputs to Motor Neurons

### 30.2.1. Convergence onto the Final Common Path

A spinal motor neuron has a dendritic tree that spreads several millimeters through the ventral horn and receives tens of thousands of synapses. Only a small fraction come directly from primary afferents (the Ia afferents of the **stretch reflex**) and from descending pathways; most come from spinal interneurons, the premotor neurons. Retrograde trans-synaptic tracing with modified rabies virus, injected into a single muscle, labels these premotor neurons and shows that each motor pool receives a distinct combination of excitatory and inhibitory interneurons spread over many segments and both sides of the cord (Stepien, Tripodi and Arber, 2010). Motor neurons also inhibit themselves through recurrent collaterals onto Renshaw cells, inhibitory interneurons that feed back onto the same and synergist motor neurons, a negative feedback that limits and stabilizes their firing (Renshaw, 1941; [7.14.6](/7.14.6#sec-6.1.3)).

### 30.2.2. Persistent Inward Currents

Motor neurons are not passive integrators of this input. Their dendrites contain L-type $\ce{Ca^2+}$ channels ($\mathrm{Ca_V1.3}$) and persistent $\ce{Na+}$ channels that activate near threshold and do not inactivate.

**Definition 30.2.1 (Persistent Inward Current)** A **persistent inward current** (PIC) is a slowly or non-inactivating depolarizing current, carried in motor neurons mainly by dendritic L-type $\ce{Ca^2+}$ channels and by persistent $\ce{Na+}$ channels, that amplifies synaptic input and can sustain a depolarized plateau after the input has ended. Its amplitude depends strongly on the neuromodulators serotonin and norepinephrine released by descending axons from the brainstem.

The effect is a bistability of the kind analyzed for the persistent sodium model in [7.16.6](/7.16.6#sec-6.1). The steady-state current of the membrane, $I_{\mathrm{ss}}(V) = g_{\mathrm{L}}(V - E_{\mathrm{L}}) + \bar g_{\mathrm{PIC}}\,m_\infty(V)(V - E_{\mathrm{Ca}})$, is monotonic when $\bar g_{\mathrm{PIC}}$ is small, but when the inward current is large it has a region of negative slope, and a range of injected currents then has two stable membrane potentials, one at rest and one on a plateau. A brief excitatory input switches the neuron to the plateau and a brief inhibitory input switches it back; the firing rate during a slow ramp of current is higher on the descending than on the ascending limb (hysteresis), and synaptic input is amplified two- to fivefold (Hounsgaard and Kiehn, 1989; Heckman and colleagues, 2008).

With **serotonin** and **norepinephrine** acting as gain controls, the brainstem can set the excitability of the whole motor output according to the behavioral state: the raphe neurons that release serotonin fire most during vigorous motor activity and fall silent in REM sleep, when the PICs are reduced and, together with active glycinergic and GABAergic inhibition of motor neurons, the muscles become atonic. After spinal cord injury, the loss of descending monoamines first abolishes the PICs (spinal shock); over weeks, serotonin receptors of the 5-$\mathrm{HT_{2C}}$ type become constitutively active and the PICs return without their regulation, producing the long-lasting muscle spasms of spasticity (Murray and colleagues, 2010).

## 30.3. Central Pattern Generators

### 30.3.1. Intrinsic and Network Mechanisms

Walking, swimming, flying, breathing, chewing and scratching are rhythmic and are generated by **central pattern generators** ([Definition 6.4.2](/7.14.6#def-6.4.2)) that produce the rhythm without rhythmic input. The classical evidence (the deafferented cat of Graham Brown, fictive locomotion in isolated spinal cords, the mesencephalic locomotor region) is reviewed in [7.14.44](/7.14.44#sec-44.4). A rhythm can arise in two ways. In a pacemaker-driven circuit some neurons oscillate by themselves through their intrinsic currents, for example by the slow alternation of a persistent $\ce{Na+}$ current and a $\ce{Ca^2+}$-activated $\ce{K+}$ current (the bursting of [7.16.7](/7.16.7#sec-7.4)), and the network distributes their rhythm. In a network oscillator no single neuron oscillates, and the rhythm emerges from the connections, the classic example being the **half-center oscillator** ([Definition 6.4.3](/7.14.6#def-6.4.3)): two groups that inhibit each other alternate if each has a slow process that ends its own activity.

In a half-center, the switch from one side to the other occurs by one of two mechanisms (Wang and Rinzel, 1992). In *release*, the active neuron weakens, by adaptation or synaptic depression, until its inhibition can no longer suppress the partner, which is released. In *escape*, the inhibited neuron gradually depolarizes despite the inhibition (for example through a hyperpolarization-activated current) until it fires and takes over. In both cases the period is set by the slow process, not by the time constants of membrane and synapse, and a descending drive or a neuromodulator that changes the slow process changes the rhythm (figure below).

### 30.3.2. Degeneracy and Neuromodulation

The best-understood pattern generators are in invertebrates, whose neurons can be identified individually. The pyloric circuit of the crustacean stomatogastric ganglion, about a dozen neurons, produces a triphasic rhythm driven by a pacemaker kernel (the AB neuron electrically coupled to two PD neurons), with the phases of the follower neurons set by inhibitory synapses and by their intrinsic currents, such as the A-type current that delays rebound from inhibition. Two findings from this circuit have changed how neuroscientists think about circuits in general. First, neuromodulators reconfigure the circuit: each of more than twenty amines and peptides changes the intrinsic currents and synaptic strengths of particular neurons, so that one anatomical network produces many functional circuits (Marder and Bucher, 2007). Second, the same output can be produced by very different parameters.

**Definition 30.3.1 (Degeneracy of Neural Circuits)** A circuit is **degenerate** with respect to an output when many different combinations of its parameters (maximal conductances of the intrinsic currents, synaptic strengths) produce the same functional output, so that individual animals with the same behavior can differ several-fold in the values of individual parameters.

Prinz, Bucher and Marder (2004) simulated more than 20 million versions of a three-cell pyloric model and found that the rhythm was reproduced by widely different combinations of conductances; in real animals, the conductances and the mRNA levels of channel genes vary two- to sixfold between individuals, with correlations between some pairs of channels that maintain function. Degeneracy makes circuits robust to perturbations and to the turnover of proteins, but it also means that the mechanism of a behavior cannot be read from one set of parameters, and that a drug or a mutation can have different effects in different individuals.

## 30.4. The Molecular–Genetic Anatomy of the Locomotor Network

### 30.4.1. Cardinal Classes of Spinal Interneurons

In the developing spinal cord, gradients of the morphogens Sonic hedgehog (ventral) and BMP and Wnt (dorsal) divide the progenitors into domains, each defined by a combination of transcription factors ([7.5.18](/7.5.18)). The ventral domains p0, p1, p2 and p3 produce four cardinal classes of interneurons, V0 to V3, and pMN produces motor neurons; the dorsal domain dI6 contributes further locomotor interneurons. Each class can be labeled, ablated or silenced in mice by targeting the gene that marks it, and the resulting deficits have revealed which class contributes what (Goulding, 2009; Kiehn, 2016).

| Class | Marker | Transmitter, projection | Role in locomotion (loss-of-function phenotype) |
|---|---|---|---|
| V0 | *Dbx1* (V0D: inhibitory; V0V: excitatory, *Evx1*) | Commissural | Left–right alternation: without V0, mice hop at all speeds; without V0D, hopping at low speeds; without V0V, at high speeds (Lanuza and colleagues, 2004; Talpalar and colleagues, 2013) |
| V1 | *En1* (includes Renshaw cells and Ia inhibitory interneurons) | Inhibitory, ipsilateral | Speed of stepping: ablation slows the rhythm; with V2b, flexor–extensor alternation (Gosgnach and colleagues, 2006; Zhang and colleagues, 2014) |
| V2a | *Chx10* (*Vsx2*) | Excitatory, ipsilateral | Drive to commissural V0V neurons: ablation uncouples left and right at high speeds (Crone and colleagues, 2008) |
| V2b | *Gata3* | Inhibitory, ipsilateral | Flexor–extensor alternation together with V1 |
| V3 | *Sim1* | Excitatory, mainly commissural | Robustness and left–right balance of the rhythm |
| dI6 | *Dmrt3* | Inhibitory, commissural | Coordination of limbs; a *DMRT3* mutation enables the lateral gait ("pace") of Icelandic and harness racing horses (Andersson and colleagues, 2012) |

Ablating all of V0, V1, V2 and V3 together leaves a rhythm in which the flexor and extensor motor neurons fire synchronously on each side: the rhythm generator itself is spared and is thought to lie in excitatory neurons (including a subset of the Shox2-expressing V2 neurons and the Hb9 interneurons), whereas the cardinal classes organize the pattern. The left–right alternation also requires the axons of commissural neurons to cross the midline correctly; mice that lack the repulsive receptor EphA4 or the attractive guidance cue netrin-1 hop because of miswired crossing ([7.5.21](/7.5.21)).

### 30.4.2. A Four-Center Model

The roles of the commissural classes can be captured by a model of four half-centers, the flexor and extensor centers of the left and right sides. Each is a Matsuoka unit, a rate unit with self-adaptation.

**Formula 30.4.1 (Matsuoka Oscillator)** The activity $x_i$ of center $i$, its adaptation $a_i$ and its output $y_i = [x_i]_+$ obey
$$
\tau\dot x_i = -x_i - b\,a_i - \sum_j w_{ij}y_j + s, \qquad \tau_a\dot a_i = -a_i + y_i, \tag{30.2}
$$
where $w_{ij} > 0$ is an inhibitory and $w_{ij} < 0$ an excitatory connection from $j$ to $i$, $s$ the tonic drive from the brainstem and $b$ the strength of adaptation (Matsuoka, 1985). Two units with mutual inhibition and $b > 0$ oscillate as a half-center when the inhibition is strong enough; the period grows with $\tau_a$.

In the model, flexor and extensor of each side inhibit each other (V1/V2b), and homologous centers of the two sides are connected by crossed inhibition (through V0) and crossed excitation (V3-like). When inhibition dominates, the two sides alternate; when it is removed, the remaining crossed excitation synchronizes them, the hopping of the V0 knockout (figure below). The transition is sharp: the phase difference jumps from synchrony to alternation when the crossed inhibition exceeds the crossed excitation, which is why partial loss of commissural classes produces hopping only in part of the speed range. Danner, Rybak and colleagues (2017) extended such models with speed-dependent recruitment of the V0D and V0V pathways and reproduced the transitions between walk, trot, gallop and bound of mice as the brainstem drive increases.

## 30.5. Speed and Gait

### 30.5.1. Speed-Dependent Recruitment of Interneurons

Moving faster requires not only a faster rhythm but a different set of neurons. In larval zebrafish, motor neurons and V2a interneurons are recruited in a fixed order as the swimming frequency increases, the most excitable neurons at low frequencies and progressively less excitable ones at high frequencies, while some neurons active during slow swimming are silenced during fast swimming (McLean and colleagues, 2008). In adult zebrafish, separate modules of V2a interneurons and motor neurons are dedicated to slow, intermediate and fast swimming, each with its own intrinsic properties and connectivity (Ampatzis and colleagues, 2014). Recruitment by speed thus resembles the size principle on a larger scale: modules are engaged in order of excitability, and speed is controlled by a single descending drive.

### 30.5.2. Gaits and Their Control

Quadrupeds switch between gaits with different interlimb phases as speed increases, at speeds that minimize metabolic cost and mechanical load ([7.14.44](/7.14.44#sec-44.6.2)). In mice, the walk and trot are alternating gaits, and the gallop and bound are synchronous ones; mice lacking V0V neurons lose the trot and switch from walking directly to bounding, and mice lacking all V0 neurons only bound (Bellardita and Kiehn, 2015). The gait is selected from the brainstem. Glutamatergic neurons of the cuneiform nucleus in the mesencephalic locomotor region can drive the whole range of speeds and gaits up to the gallop and are needed for fast escape, whereas those of the pedunculopontine nucleus support only slow, exploratory locomotion (Caggiano and colleagues, 2018; Josset and colleagues, 2018). The signal descends through reticulospinal neurons in the medulla, and a distinct population of V2a neurons in the gigantocellular reticular nucleus stops locomotion (Bouvier and colleagues, 2015). Brainstem commands for start, speed, gait and stop are therefore carried by different cell types.

## 30.6. Brainstem Control, Posture and Motor Modules

### 30.6.1. Standing as Control of an Inverted Pendulum

The brainstem nuclei, the reticular formation and the vestibular nuclei, control posture through the medial descending pathways, and their reflexes are described in [7.14.44](/7.14.44#sec-44.5). Quantitatively, quiet standing is the control of an inverted pendulum: the body pivots about the ankles with its center of mass at height $h$ above them, and gravity produces a toppling torque that grows with the angle of lean.

**Example 30.6.1** Linearized for a small angle $\theta$, the body of mass $m$ and moment of inertia $J$ about the ankle obeys $J\ddot\theta = mgh\,\theta - K\theta + M_{\mathrm{act}}$, where $K$ is the stiffness of the ankle muscles and tendons and $M_{\mathrm{act}}$ the actively controlled torque. Passive stability requires $K > mgh$. For $m = 70\,\mathrm{kg}$ and $h = 0.9\,\mathrm{m}$, $mgh = 618\,\mathrm{N\,m\,rad^{-1}}$. The measured intrinsic stiffness of the human ankle is about $90\,\%$ of this value (Loram and Lakie, 2002), so the body is passively unstable, with a time constant of instability $\sqrt{J/(mgh - K)} \approx 1\,\mathrm{s}$ for $J = mh^2$. Active control by the nervous system is needed, but because the instability is slow it can be stabilized by intermittent corrections based on predicted states, despite feedback delays of $100$–$200\,\mathrm{ms}$.

:::

The same problem arises before voluntary movements. Raising an arm quickly shifts the center of mass and produces reaction forces; leg and trunk muscles are activated tens of milliseconds before the arm muscles, in a pattern that depends on the expected perturbation (anticipatory postural adjustments; Massion, 1992). Like the anticipatory grip force of [7.16.29](/7.16.29#sec-29.3.1), these adjustments are feedforward commands computed from an internal model.

### 30.6.2. Motor Modules

How the spinal cord simplifies the control of many muscles is suggested by experiments on spinalized frogs. Microstimulation of a site in the lumbar cord produces a pattern of forces at the ankle that, measured at many limb positions, forms a convergent force field with an equilibrium point; stimulating two sites together produces the vector sum of the two fields (Bizzi, Mussa-Ivaldi and Giszter, 1991; Mussa-Ivaldi, Giszter and Bizzi, 1994). A small set of such fields, combined linearly, could produce a wide range of movements. The corresponding analysis of muscle activity decomposes the electromyograms of many muscles, recorded during natural behaviors, by non-negative matrix factorization into a few time-varying synergies, $\boldsymbol{m}(t) \approx \sum_kc_k\,\boldsymbol{w}_k(t - t_k)$; in frogs, cats and humans a handful of synergies account for most of the variance across behaviors (d'Avella, Saltiel and Bizzi, 2003). Whether such modules are an organizational principle of the spinal cord or a description of the statistics of behavior remains debated, because low-dimensional muscle activity also follows from task constraints and from the optimal control of [7.16.29](/7.16.29#sec-29.5); the identified interneuron classes of 30.4 are the first candidates for a cellular basis.

## Summary

- The motor unit is the quantum of force; units are recruited in order of size (size principle) and modulate their firing rate (Formula 30.1.1). Exponentially distributed thresholds and twitch forces give constant relative precision and make force noise proportional to force, the signal-dependent noise of Definition 29.5.1.
- Motor neurons integrate convergent premotor, afferent and descending input; Renshaw cells provide recurrent inhibition, and persistent inward currents (Definition 30.2.1), set by serotonin and norepinephrine, amplify input and produce plateaus and hysteresis; their dysregulation causes spasticity.
- Central pattern generators use pacemaker neurons or network mechanisms such as the half-center, which switches sides by release or escape with a period set by slow processes; circuits are degenerate (Definition 30.3.1) and reconfigured by neuromodulators.
- The cardinal spinal interneuron classes have distinct roles (table in 30.4.1): V0 left–right alternation (V0D at low, V0V at high speed), V1 and V2b flexor–extensor alternation and speed, V2a drive to commissural neurons, V3 balance, dI6 limb coordination. A four-center Matsuoka model (Formula 30.4.1) reproduces alternation and its loss.
- Speed recruits interneuron modules in order of excitability; gaits are selected by brainstem command neurons (cuneiform vs pedunculopontine nucleus), and stopping by reticulospinal V2a neurons.
- Standing is control of a passively unstable inverted pendulum (Example 30.6.1); posture is adjusted in anticipation of movement; force fields and muscle synergies suggest modular spinal output.

## Questions

1. In Formula 30.1.1, why does an exponential distribution of twitch forces give nearly constant relative precision of force? Estimate the relative force increment produced by recruiting the next unit in a pool of 120 units with a 100-fold range of twitch forces.

   **Answer** With $P_i = P_1\rho_P^{(i-1)/(N-1)}$, successive units differ by the constant factor $\rho_P^{1/(N-1)} = 100^{1/119} \approx 1.039$. When $i$ units are active, the total force is dominated by the largest ones, $F \approx \sum_{k\le i}P_k \approx P_i/(1 - 1/1.039) \approx 27P_i$, so the next unit adds roughly $P_{i+1}/F \approx 1.039/27 \approx 4\,\%$ of the existing force, independent of $i$. Each recruitment step is therefore a constant fraction of the force, which is the condition for Weber-like precision. (Rate coding fills in between recruitment steps.)

2. A motor neuron has a bistable plateau produced by a persistent inward current. Describe its firing during a slow triangular ramp of injected current and explain why the firing rate is higher on the descending limb. What does this imply for postural muscles?

   **Answer** On the ascending ramp the neuron starts to fire at its recruitment threshold; near this point the PIC activates and adds an inward current, which raises the rate abruptly (often with a rate jump). On the descending ramp the PIC remains active, because it is slow to deactivate and is self-sustaining, so the same injected current produces a higher rate and the neuron continues to fire below its recruitment current; it stops at a lower current than it started (hysteresis: the derecruitment current is lower than the recruitment current). Postural motor neurons can therefore maintain tonic firing after a brief excitatory input with little sustained synaptic drive, which saves descending activity; brief inhibition is needed to stop them.

3. In the four-center model (Formula 30.4.1), predict the effect of deleting only the V3-like crossed excitation, and of deleting the flexor–extensor inhibition on each side. Relate the predictions to experiments.

   **Answer** Without crossed excitation, crossed inhibition alone still produces left–right alternation, so gait is preserved, but the two sides are less tightly balanced; in mice, silencing V3 neurons leaves alternation but makes the rhythm less robust and the bursts of the two sides unequal. Without ipsilateral flexor–extensor inhibition, flexor and extensor centers on each side are no longer forced into antiphase; each side behaves as one unit and flexors and extensors fire together, as after combined ablation of V1 and V2b neurons (Zhang and colleagues, 2014), while the left–right relation is still set by the commissural connections. (Whether each center still oscillates depends on its adaptation; in the model a single adapting unit with tonic drive relaxes to a steady state unless inhibited, so rhythm generation then requires another source.)

4. Using Example 30.6.1, compute the time constant of the toppling instability of a standing child of mass $25\,\mathrm{kg}$ with center of mass at $h = 0.6\,\mathrm{m}$, assuming the same relative ankle stiffness ($K = 0.9\,mgh$). Is balance easier or harder to control for the child?

   **Answer** With $J = mh^2$, $\sqrt{J/(mgh - K)} = \sqrt{mh^2/(0.1\,mgh)} = \sqrt{10h/g}$, independent of mass: for $h = 0.6\,\mathrm{m}$ it is $\sqrt{6/9.8} = 0.78\,\mathrm{s}$, compared with $\sqrt{9/9.8} = 0.96\,\mathrm{s}$ for $h = 0.9\,\mathrm{m}$. The shorter body falls faster, so the child's feedback loop, whose delays are not much shorter, has less time to correct: balance is harder, one reason why short animals rely more on wide stances and reflex stiffness.

5. (Open question) Degeneracy (Definition 30.3.1) implies that individuals with the same rhythm can have very different conductances. How could a circuit maintain its output despite this variability, and what experiment would test the mechanism?

   **Answer** One proposal is homeostatic regulation of intrinsic conductances: each neuron senses its activity, for example through its mean intracellular $\ce{Ca^2+}$, and adjusts the expression of channel genes until the activity matches a target (O'Leary, Marder and colleagues). Such rules produce correlated conductances and many different solutions with the same output. Tests: block a current pharmacologically or remove neuromodulatory input and follow the activity and the mRNA of channel genes over hours to days, expecting recovery of the rhythm with compensatory changes in other conductances; perturb the temperature or $\ce{Ca^2+}$ sensing to change the set point; and compare the variability of conductances with the predictions of homeostatic models fitted to the same circuit.
