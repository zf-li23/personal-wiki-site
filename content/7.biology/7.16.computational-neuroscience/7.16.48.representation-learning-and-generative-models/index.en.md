The sensory cortex receives a high-dimensional input $\boldsymbol{x}$, an image on the retina or a sound in the cochlea, and must represent its causes: objects, their positions, their illumination. Earlier chapters described what the representations look like (receptive fields in [7.16.18](/7.16.18), hierarchies in [7.16.19](/7.16.19)) and derived some of them from principles of efficient coding ([7.16.20](/7.16.20)) and from Hebbian learning ([7.16.22](/7.16.22)). This chapter asks how a brain could *learn* a good representation from unlabeled input. The answer developed here, going back to Helmholtz's "unconscious inference", is that the brain learns a generative model of its input, a model of how causes produce sensations, and that perception inverts it; the Bayesian observer of [7.16.27](/7.16.27) is the same idea with a known model. We treat the classical models (the Helmholtz machine, factor analysis and mixtures), the variational autoencoder and its relation to disentangled codes in face patches, predictive coding as a circuit implementation, self-supervised objectives, and diffusion models as a way of sampling. The machine-learning methods themselves (network training, Transformers, pre-training) are in [4.7](/4.7); only their bearing on the brain is discussed here. Attractor networks that store and complete memories ([7.16.43](/7.16.43)) are a related but distinct form of unsupervised learning, and how the brain could compute the gradients that these models need is the subject of [7.16.50](/7.16.50).

We write the input as $\boldsymbol{x}$ and the latent causes as $\boldsymbol{z}$, as in the machine-learning literature (in the neural interpretation $\boldsymbol{x}$ is the activity of the input layer and $\boldsymbol{z}$ that of a higher area).

## 48.1. Inference and Generation

### 48.1.1. Recognition and Generative Models

**Definition 48.1.1 (Generative Model and Recognition Model)** A **generative model** of data $\boldsymbol{x}$ with latent variables $\boldsymbol{z}$ is a joint distribution $p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x}, \boldsymbol{z}) = p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{z})\,p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x} \mid \boldsymbol{z})$, the product of a prior over causes and a likelihood that describes how causes produce data; its marginal $p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x}) = \int p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x}, \boldsymbol{z})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{z}$ is the model's density of the data. A **recognition model** is a distribution $q_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\boldsymbol{z} \mid \boldsymbol{x})$ that maps an input to (a distribution over) its causes and approximates the posterior $p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{z} \mid \boldsymbol{x})$.

The two directions have different difficulty. Generation is easy: sample $\boldsymbol{z}$, then $\boldsymbol{x}$. Recognition requires Bayes' rule, $p(\boldsymbol{z} \mid \boldsymbol{x}) = p(\boldsymbol{x} \mid \boldsymbol{z})p(\boldsymbol{z})/p(\boldsymbol{x})$, whose normalizer is an integral over all causes and is intractable for interesting models. A discriminative classifier learns $p(\text{label} \mid \boldsymbol{x})$ directly from labeled examples; a generative model learns the structure of $\boldsymbol{x}$ itself and can be fitted without labels, by maximizing the likelihood $\sum_i \log p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x}_i)$ of the observed data. The latent space is useful when it is low-dimensional, $\dim\boldsymbol{z} \ll \dim\boldsymbol{x}$, and when its coordinates correspond to independent causes of the world: a good representation makes the downstream tasks (classification, control, memory) simple, for example linearly decodable.

The neural motivation is threefold. First, labels are rare: an infant receives few names for the objects it sees. Second, the cortex is full of feedback: in the visual system, connections from higher to lower areas are as numerous as those in the forward direction ([7.16.18](/7.16.18#sec-18.4)), which is natural if the higher areas generate predictions of the activity in lower ones. Third, perception is shaped by expectation, as the priors and illusions of [7.16.27](/7.16.27#sec-27.3) show, and imagery and dreams show that the brain can generate sensory-like activity without input.

### 48.1.2. The Helmholtz Machine and the Wake–Sleep Algorithm

**Definition 48.1.2 (Helmholtz Machine)** A **Helmholtz machine** (Dayan, Hinton, Neal and Zemel, 1995) is a layered network of stochastic binary units with two sets of connections: bottom-up recognition weights $\boldsymbol{\phi}$, which compute $q_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\boldsymbol{z} \mid \boldsymbol{x})$ layer by layer, and top-down generative weights $\boldsymbol{\theta}$, which define $p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x}, \boldsymbol{z})$ layer by layer from a prior on the top layer.

**Algorithm 48.1.1 (Wake–Sleep Algorithm)** The wake–sleep algorithm (Hinton, Dayan, Frey and Neal, 1995) trains the two sets of weights alternately, each with a local delta rule.

1. *Wake phase.* Present a real input $\boldsymbol{x}$, sample causes $\boldsymbol{z} \sim q_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\boldsymbol{z} \mid \boldsymbol{x})$ with the recognition weights, and change the generative weights to increase $\log p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x}, \boldsymbol{z})$: each unit learns to predict the activity of the layer below from the layer above.
2. *Sleep phase.* Generate a "fantasy" $(\boldsymbol{z}, \boldsymbol{x}) \sim p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$ from the top down, and change the recognition weights to increase $\log q_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\boldsymbol{z} \mid \boldsymbol{x})$: the recognition network learns to recover causes that are known because they were generated.

For a sigmoid unit $j$ with input $\boldsymbol{y}$ from the adjacent layer, both updates have the form $\Delta w_{jk} = \eta\,(s_j - p_j)\,y_k$, where $s_j$ is the sampled state of unit $j$ and $p_j$ its probability under the other pathway: a product of a presynaptic activity and a postsynaptic prediction error, which is local. The wake phase maximizes a lower bound on $\log p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x})$ (the quantity introduced in 48.3); the sleep phase minimizes the reverse divergence $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{z} \mid \boldsymbol{x})\,\Vert\,q_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\boldsymbol{z} \mid \boldsymbol{x}))$ on fantasies rather than the right one, so the algorithm does not follow the gradient of a single objective, but it works for simple models. Its interest for neuroscience is the division of labor between a phase driven by the world and an offline phase driven by the model itself, which invites a comparison with sleep, whose replay and spontaneous activity are discussed in [7.16.35](/7.16.35) and [7.16.42](/7.16.42); the comparison remains a hypothesis.

## 48.2. Density Estimation with Latent Variables

The simplest generative models are the latent-variable models of statistics, which the brain-inspired models generalize.

- In **factor analysis** ([Definition 28.3.2](/7.16.28#def-28.3.2)), $\boldsymbol{z} \sim \mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0}, I)$ and $\boldsymbol{x} \mid \boldsymbol{z} \sim \mathcal{N}(W\boldsymbol{z} + \boldsymbol{\mu}, \Psi)$ with diagonal $\Psi$; with $\Psi = \sigma^2 I$ it is probabilistic PCA (Tipping and Bishop, 1999), whose maximum-likelihood solution spans the leading principal components ([3.4.5](/3.4.5)). Recognition is linear: $\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{z} \mid \boldsymbol{x}] = (W^{\mathsf T}\Psi^{-1}W + I)^{-1}W^{\mathsf T}\Psi^{-1}(\boldsymbol{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu})$.
- In a **Gaussian mixture model**, $z \in \{1, \dots, K\}$ is a category with prior probabilities $\pi_k$ and $\boldsymbol{x} \mid z = k \sim \mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_k, \Sigma_k)$; recognition gives the responsibilities $p(z = k \mid \boldsymbol{x}) \propto \pi_k\,\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{x}; \boldsymbol{\mu}_k, \Sigma_k)$, a soft competition between categories that a network implements with divisive normalization of exponentiated inputs.
- In **sparse coding** ([Definition 20.5.1](/7.16.20#def-20.5.1)) the prior on $\boldsymbol{z}$ is heavy-tailed and the inference is nonlinear; the learned dictionary resembles V1 receptive fields.

All three are fitted by the **EM algorithm** ([3.5.6](/3.5.6)), which alternates inference and learning: the E step computes the posterior over $\boldsymbol{z}$ for each data point under the current parameters, the M step re-estimates the parameters from the expected complete-data log likelihood. In neural terms, the E step is the fast settling of activity in response to an input, the M step a slow change of synapses that uses the settled activity, a separation of time scales that recurs in all the models below. The EM algorithm requires the exact posterior, which is available only for these simple models; the methods of 48.3 replace it by an approximate recognition model, which is learned together with the generative model.

## 48.3. Autoencoders and Variational Autoencoders

### 48.3.1. Autoencoders

**Definition 48.3.1 (Autoencoder)** An **autoencoder** is a network that maps an input $\boldsymbol{x}$ through an encoder $\boldsymbol{z} = f_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\boldsymbol{x})$ to a code of lower dimension (a bottleneck) and back through a decoder $\hat{\boldsymbol{x}} = g_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{z})$, and is trained without labels to minimize the reconstruction error $\mathbb{E}\Vert\boldsymbol{x} - \hat{\boldsymbol{x}}\Vert^2$.

A linear autoencoder with a $k$-dimensional bottleneck learns the subspace of the first $k$ principal components (Baldi and Hornik, 1989), so the nonlinear autoencoder is a nonlinear PCA. Its code is useful for compression and denoising (a denoising autoencoder reconstructs $\boldsymbol{x}$ from a corrupted copy), but it is not a generative model: nothing constrains the codes of the training data to fill the latent space, so decoding a point between two codes, or a random point, produces garbage, and nearby codes need not mean similar inputs.

### 48.3.2. The Variational Autoencoder

The variational autoencoder (Kingma and Welling, 2014; Rezende, Mohamed and Wierstra, 2014) makes the autoencoder a generative model by giving the code a prior and the encoder a probabilistic output.

**Theorem 48.3.1 (Evidence Decomposition)** For any distribution $q(\boldsymbol{z} \mid \boldsymbol{x})$,
$$
\log p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x}) = \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q}\bigl[\log p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x} \mid \boldsymbol{z})\bigr] - D_{\mathrm{KL}}\bigl(q(\boldsymbol{z} \mid \boldsymbol{x})\,\Vert\,p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{z})\bigr)}_{\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}, q;\,\boldsymbol{x})} + D_{\mathrm{KL}}\bigl(q(\boldsymbol{z} \mid \boldsymbol{x})\,\Vert\,p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{z} \mid \boldsymbol{x})\bigr). \tag{48.1}
$$
Since the last term is nonnegative, $\mathcal{L} \le \log p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x})$, with equality exactly when $q$ is the true posterior.

**Proof** Write $\log p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x}) = \mathbb{E}_q[\log p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x}, \boldsymbol{z}) - \log p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{z} \mid \boldsymbol{x})]$, which holds for every $\boldsymbol{z}$ and hence in expectation. Adding and subtracting $\mathbb{E}_q\log q(\boldsymbol{z} \mid \boldsymbol{x})$ gives $\mathbb{E}_q[\log p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x}, \boldsymbol{z}) - \log q] + \mathbb{E}_q[\log q - \log p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{z} \mid \boldsymbol{x})]$; the second term is the posterior divergence, and the first splits as $\mathbb{E}_q\log p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x} \mid \boldsymbol{z}) + \mathbb{E}_q[\log p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{z}) - \log q]$. The nonnegativity of the **Kullback–Leibler divergence** gives the bound. $\square$

**Definition 48.3.2 (Variational Autoencoder and Evidence Lower Bound)** The quantity $\mathcal{L}$ of Eq. (48.1) is the **evidence lower bound** (ELBO). A **variational autoencoder** (VAE) is a generative model $p(\boldsymbol{z}) = \mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0}, I)$, $p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x} \mid \boldsymbol{z})$ given by a decoder network, together with an encoder network that outputs a Gaussian $q_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\boldsymbol{z} \mid \boldsymbol{x}) = \mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\boldsymbol{x}), \operatorname{diag}\boldsymbol{\sigma}^2_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\boldsymbol{x}))$; both are trained by maximizing the average ELBO over the data.

The two terms of the ELBO are the two parts of the familiar loss: the expected log likelihood is a reconstruction error (for a Gaussian decoder with variance $\sigma^2$ it is $-\mathbb{E}_q\Vert\boldsymbol{x} - g_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{z})\Vert^2/(2\sigma^2)$ up to a constant), and the divergence from the prior is a regularizer that pulls every code toward $\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0}, I)$, so that the codes fill the latent space and random samples decode to plausible inputs. Maximizing the ELBO over $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ raises a bound on the likelihood (learning); maximizing it over $\boldsymbol{\phi}$ makes the recognition model a better approximation of the posterior (inference). The gradient with respect to $\boldsymbol{\phi}$ passes through the sampling step by the *reparameterization* $\boldsymbol{z} = \boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\boldsymbol{x}) + \boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\boldsymbol{x}) \odot \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}$, $\boldsymbol{\varepsilon} \sim \mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0}, I)$, and the networks are trained by **backpropagation** ([4.7.2](/4.7.2#sec-2.4)). The wake phase of Algorithm 48.1.1 maximizes the same bound; the VAE differs by training the recognition model on the ELBO as well, instead of on fantasies.

### 48.3.3. β-VAE and Disentangling

**Definition 48.3.3 (β-VAE and Disentangled Representation)** A **β-VAE** (Higgins and colleagues, 2017) maximizes $\mathbb{E}_q\log p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x} \mid \boldsymbol{z}) - \beta\,D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\boldsymbol{z} \mid \boldsymbol{x})\,\Vert\,p(\boldsymbol{z}))$ with $\beta > 1$. A representation is **disentangled** when each latent coordinate varies with one generative factor of the data (position, size, identity, lighting) and is nearly invariant to the others.

With $\beta > 1$ the KL term acts as a capacity limit: each latent carries information about $\boldsymbol{x}$ only if the gain in reconstruction pays for it, and the diagonal posterior favors codes in which the coordinates are independent. On images of objects whose factors vary independently, β-VAEs learn latents that correspond to single factors, which an ordinary autoencoder does not. Disentangling without supervision is not guaranteed in general (Locatello and colleagues, 2019 proved that it is impossible without inductive biases on model and data), but the bias toward independent, economical latents is the same as that of the **efficient coding hypothesis**. The linear case can be solved exactly and shows what the capacity limit does.

**Proposition 48.3.1 (Linear β-VAE)** Let the data have covariance with eigenvalues $\lambda_1 \ge \dots \ge \lambda_d$ and eigenvectors $\boldsymbol{e}_i$, the decoder be $p(\boldsymbol{x} \mid \boldsymbol{z}) = \mathcal{N}(W\boldsymbol{z}, \sigma^2 I)$ with fixed $\sigma^2$, and the encoder be linear with diagonal Gaussian posterior. Then the β-VAE optimum is probabilistic PCA with noise variance $s^2 = \beta\sigma^2$: up to a sign or permutation of latents, the $i$th decoder column is $\sqrt{\max(\lambda_i - s^2, 0)}\,\boldsymbol{e}_i$, latent $i$ carries
$$
D_{\mathrm{KL}}^{(i)} = \tfrac12\log^+\frac{\lambda_i}{\beta\sigma^2}\ \text{nats}, \tag{48.2}
$$
and the expected reconstruction error along $\boldsymbol{e}_i$ is $\min(\lambda_i, \beta\sigma^2)$.

**Proof** The negative β-objective is $\mathbb{E}_q\Vert\boldsymbol{x} - W\boldsymbol{z}\Vert^2/(2\sigma^2) + \beta D_{\mathrm{KL}} + \text{const}$; divided by $\beta$, it is the negative ELBO of the same model with noise variance $\beta\sigma^2$, up to a constant. So the optimum is that of the ordinary linear VAE at $s^2 = \beta\sigma^2$, which is the maximum-likelihood solution of probabilistic PCA (its posterior is Gaussian, and with the columns along the orthogonal $\boldsymbol{e}_i$ it is diagonal, so the bound is tight). Along $\boldsymbol{e}_i$ with $w_i^2 = \lambda_i - s^2 > 0$, the posterior has variance $s^2/\lambda_i$ and its mean $w_ix_i/\lambda_i$ has variance $w_i^2/\lambda_i$ over the data, so $D_{\mathrm{KL}}^{(i)} = \frac12[s^2/\lambda_i + (\lambda_i - s^2)/\lambda_i - 1 - \log(s^2/\lambda_i)] = \frac12\log(\lambda_i/s^2)$; the reconstruction error is $\lambda_i(s^2/\lambda_i)^2 + w_i^2s^2/\lambda_i = s^2$. For $\lambda_i \le s^2$ the latent is unused and the error is $\lambda_i$. $\square$

The result has two lessons. First, $\beta$ sets a water level $\beta\sigma^2$: directions of the data whose variance is below it are not encoded at all, those above it are encoded with a fixed error, the solution of rate–distortion theory for Gaussian sources (Cover and Thomas). A capacity-limited encoder of this kind is the efficient code of [7.16.20](/7.16.20) with a cost per bit, and $\beta$ is the price of information. Second, the diagonal posterior picks out the principal axes, while the ordinary likelihood of probabilistic PCA is invariant to any rotation of the latent space: it is the form of the recognition model, not the likelihood, that breaks the symmetry and aligns latents with axes, a mechanism that also operates in nonlinear VAEs (Rolinek, Zietlow and Martius, 2019).

### 48.3.4. Disentangled Latents and Face Cells

Face patches of the macaque inferotemporal cortex ([Definition 18.6.1](/7.16.18#def-18.6.1)) contain neurons whose responses are linear in a few axes of a face space (the axis model, [Formula 18.6.1](/7.16.18#fml-18.6.1)). Higgins and colleagues (2021) trained a β-VAE without labels on face images and compared its latents with recordings from 159 neurons in the face patches ML/MF and AM, in response to 2100 face images. A small number of β-VAE latents explained the responses of single neurons, often in a one-to-one way (a single latent predicted a single neuron), more closely than the units of supervised face-recognition networks or of ordinary autoencoders, and the latents were readable as factors such as hair, age or skin tone. The disentangled latents also allowed decoding of novel faces from a dozen neurons. The study suggests that the axes of IT neurons are those of a factorized generative model learned under an information constraint, but the match is one of several and does not show that IT computes an ELBO; the same data are compatible with other models whose units are independent.

## 48.4. Predictive Coding

### 48.4.1. The Rao–Ballard Model

A generative model can be inverted by a recurrent network in which each level predicts the activity of the level below and only the prediction errors are passed upward. The idea, proposed for the retina by Srinivasan, Laughlin and Dubs (1982) as removal of predictable signal ([7.16.20](/7.16.20#sec-20.3)) and for the cortical hierarchy by Mumford (1992), was formulated as a learning model by Rao and Ballard (1999).

**Definition 48.4.1 (Predictive Coding)** **Predictive coding** is a hierarchical scheme for inference in a generative model in which each level $\ell$ holds a representation $\boldsymbol{r}_\ell$, sends a top-down prediction $U_\ell\boldsymbol{r}_\ell$ of the representation $\boldsymbol{r}_{\ell - 1}$ of the level below (with $\boldsymbol{r}_0 = \boldsymbol{x}$), and receives from it the prediction error $\boldsymbol{e}_{\ell - 1} = \boldsymbol{r}_{\ell - 1} - U_\ell\boldsymbol{r}_\ell$, carried by separate error units; representations change to reduce the weighted errors.

**Formula 48.4.1 (Rao–Ballard Dynamics and Learning)** For Gaussian levels with variances $\sigma_\ell^2$, gradient descent on the negative log posterior $\sum_\ell\Vert\boldsymbol{e}_\ell\Vert^2/(2\sigma_\ell^2)$ plus a prior term gives
$$
\tau\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{r}_\ell}{\mathrm{d}t} = \frac{U_\ell^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol{e}_{\ell - 1}}{\sigma_{\ell - 1}^2} - \frac{\boldsymbol{e}_\ell}{\sigma_\ell^2}, \qquad \Delta U_\ell = \eta\,\frac{\boldsymbol{e}_{\ell - 1}\boldsymbol{r}_\ell^{\mathsf T}}{\sigma_{\ell - 1}^2}. \tag{48.3}
$$
Each representation is driven up by the error below it (through the transpose of its prediction weights) and pulled toward the prediction from above; the weights learn by a Hebbian product of the error below and the representation above.

At equilibrium the representations are the MAP estimates of the causes, and the learning rule is the same as the dictionary update of sparse coding (Definition 20.5.1), which is a one-level case of Eq. (48.3) with a sparse prior. Rao and Ballard trained a three-level network on natural images. Level-1 units learned oriented, localized receptive fields, and level-1 *error* units showed extra-classical effects: their response to an oriented bar was suppressed when the bar extended beyond the classical receptive field. The explanation is end-stopping by explanation: a long bar, the typical statistics of edges in natural images, is predicted by the level above, so little error is left, while a short bar is unexpected (figure below). Surround suppression, which the normalization model explains as divisive gain control ([7.16.18](/7.16.18)), is here a consequence of prediction.

### 48.4.2. Evidence and Open Questions

Predictive coding makes specific predictions: neurons that signal errors should respond to unexpected input more than to expected input, the response should be suppressed by top-down predictions, and errors and representations should be carried by different cells or layers. Several observations agree with them.

- *Repetition suppression and mismatch.* The response of sensory cortex to a repeated or predictable stimulus declines, and a deviant stimulus in a sequence evokes a larger response (the mismatch negativity of the EEG); in the mouse, part of the deviance response remains after controlling for stimulus-specific adaptation.
- *Sensorimotor mismatch.* In mice running in a virtual corridor whose visual flow is coupled to locomotion, a subset of layer 2/3 neurons in V1 responds to brief halts of the visual flow while the animal runs, a mismatch between predicted and actual input (Keller, Bonhoeffer and Hübener, 2012); the prediction arrives from motor-related areas, such as anterior cingulate cortex, through top-down axons.
- *Laminar organization.* Superficial layers, which project forward, and deep layers, which project backward, are proposed to carry errors and predictions respectively (Bastos and colleagues, 2012), and in many studies forward signals are carried in the gamma band and feedback in the alpha/beta band ([7.16.15](/7.16.15#sec-15.5)).

The evidence for separate error neurons is mixed: most neurons combine responses to input and to its violation, and many expectation effects can be explained by adaptation or attention ([7.16.18](/7.16.18#sec-18.7)) without an explicit generative model. Predictive coding is best regarded as a family of circuit hypotheses for implementing inference, whose details (which cells compute errors, whether errors are signed, how the precisions $\sigma_\ell^{-2}$ are set by neuromodulators) are being tested with cell-type-specific recordings.

### 48.4.3. The Free-Energy Principle

Friston (2005, 2010) generalized predictive coding into the *free-energy principle*: the negative ELBO, called *variational free energy* by analogy with statistical physics, $F = -\mathcal{L} \ge -\log p(\boldsymbol{x})$, bounds the surprise of sensory input, and the principle proposes that perception, learning and action all minimize it. Perception minimizes $F$ over the recognition density (inference), learning over the parameters of the generative model, and action over the input itself, by changing the world so that it matches predictions ("active inference"). Precision (inverse variance) weighting of errors is identified with attention and with neuromodulation. The principle unifies many models, but as stated it is very general, and specific predictions come only from specific generative models; it is therefore a framework, not a testable theory by itself.

## 48.5. Self-Supervised Learning as an Objective for Cortex

Generative models reconstruct every detail of the input, including the details that no behavior requires (the texture of noise, the exact pixels). **Self-supervised learning** ([4.7.7](/4.7.7#sec-7.1.3)) trains representations on a pretext task defined by the data itself. Two families relate to theories of the brain.

**Definition 48.5.1 (Contrastive Learning)** **Contrastive learning** trains an encoder $f$ so that the representations of two views $\boldsymbol{x}, \boldsymbol{x}^+$ of the same thing (two augmentations of an image, two moments of a video, an image and its caption) are similar and those of unrelated inputs $\boldsymbol{x}^-_1, \dots, \boldsymbol{x}^-_K$ are dissimilar, typically by minimizing the InfoNCE loss
$$
\ell = -\log\frac{\exp\bigl(\operatorname{sim}(f(\boldsymbol{x}), f(\boldsymbol{x}^+))/T\bigr)}{\exp\bigl(\operatorname{sim}(f(\boldsymbol{x}), f(\boldsymbol{x}^+))/T\bigr) + \sum_{k=1}^K\exp\bigl(\operatorname{sim}(f(\boldsymbol{x}), f(\boldsymbol{x}^-_k))/T\bigr)}, \tag{48.4}
$$
where $\operatorname{sim}$ is the cosine similarity and $T$ a temperature; $\log(K + 1) - \ell$ is a lower bound on the mutual information between the two views' representations (van den Oord, Li and Vinyals, 2018).

**Definition 48.5.2 (Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture)** A **joint-embedding predictive architecture** (JEPA) encodes a context $\boldsymbol{x}$ and a target $\boldsymbol{y}$ (a masked part of the image, the next frame) and trains a predictor to predict the representation of $\boldsymbol{y}$ from that of $\boldsymbol{x}$, with a loss in representation space rather than in input space; collapse to a constant representation is prevented by regularizers on the variance and covariance of the representations (VICReg, Barlow Twins) or by an asymmetric target encoder.

Examples are SimCLR and CLIP (contrastive, the latter across images and text), Barlow Twins, which decorrelates the components of the representation in the spirit of Barlow's redundancy reduction ([7.16.20](/7.16.20#sec-20.3)), and I-JEPA (LeCun, 2022; Assran and colleagues, 2023). A JEPA predicts what can be predicted and ignores the rest, which is what a sensory system needs; it is also a "world model" in the sense of a model that predicts the consequences of time and action in an abstract space.

Three lines of evidence connect these objectives with cortex. First, as discussed in [7.16.19](/7.16.19#sec-19.5), contrastive networks trained on unlabeled images, including videos from a head camera worn by a child, predict the responses of V4 and IT as well as supervised networks. Second, the brain has a natural source of positive pairs: objects persist over time while the retinal image changes. Learning that maps temporally adjacent views to similar representations, the *slowness* principle (Földiák, 1991; slow feature analysis of Wiskott and Sejnowski, 2002), is a temporal form of contrastive learning, and IT invariance depends on it: when Li and DiCarlo (2008) swapped the identity of an object during the monkey's saccades, so that one object at one retinal position was consistently replaced by another, IT neurons began within an hour of exposure to respond similarly to the two objects at that position. Third, prediction of the next input is a ubiquitous training signal; the mismatch responses of 48.4.2 are prediction errors of this kind. What the brain does not have is the huge negative batch of Eq. (48.4) or a stored copy of past representations; biologically plausible variants use local, layer-wise contrastive losses (Illing, Ventura, Bellec and Gerstner, 2021) or the contrast between wake and offline (sleep) phases, which links back to Algorithm 48.1.1.

## 48.6. Diffusion Models and the Sampling Hypothesis

A diffusion model (Sohl-Dickstein and colleagues, 2015; Ho, Jain and Abbeel, 2020) generates data by reversing a process that gradually adds noise. The forward process is a fixed Ornstein–Uhlenbeck diffusion $\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x} = -\tfrac12 b\,\boldsymbol{x}\,\mathrm{d}t + \sqrt{b}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{W}$, which turns any data distribution $p_0$ into $\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0}, I)$. Its time reversal (Anderson, 1982) is again a diffusion,
$$
\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x} = \bigl[-\tfrac12 b\,\boldsymbol{x} - b\,\nabla_{\boldsymbol{x}}\log p_t(\boldsymbol{x})\bigr]\mathrm{d}t + \sqrt{b}\,\mathrm{d}\bar{\boldsymbol{W}}, \tag{48.5}
$$
run backward from noise, which needs only the score $\nabla\log p_t$ of the noised distributions. A network trained to denoise noisy samples learns this score (Song and colleagues, 2021), and integrating Eq. (48.5) turns noise into samples of the data distribution (figure below). The training objective is again a variational bound, as for the VAE, with a fixed encoder (the noising process) and a learned decoder. Diffusion models in latent spaces of autoencoders, conditioned on text representations from contrastive encoders, are the basis of current image generators; their methods are not discussed further here.

For neuroscience the interest of diffusion models lies less in their architecture than in the idea that the dynamics of a noisy recurrent network can implement sampling. A stochastic system that follows the gradient of a log density plus noise, the **Langevin equation** of [7.6.5](/7.6.5), has that density as its stationary distribution. The *sampling hypothesis* (Hoyer and Hyvärinen, 2003; Fiser, Berkes, Orbán and Lengyel, 2010) proposes that neural variability is not merely noise but represents uncertainty: the activity of a sensory population at successive moments is a sequence of samples from the posterior over causes, so that the variance of responses reports the width of the posterior, in contrast to the probabilistic population codes of [Definition 27.5.1](/7.16.27#def-27.5.1), which represent the posterior in the mean responses. One prediction is that spontaneous activity, in the absence of input, samples the prior: Berkes, Orbán, Lengyel and Fiser (2011) found that the distribution of multi-unit activity patterns in ferret V1 during spontaneous activity became increasingly similar, over development, to the average distribution evoked by natural movies, but not to that evoked by artificial gratings. Imagery, which activates early visual areas, and dreaming are natural cases of generation from an internal model, but whether the brain uses noise-driven denoising dynamics of the diffusion type is unknown.

## Summary

- Perception can be cast as inverting a generative model $p(\boldsymbol{z})p(\boldsymbol{x} \mid \boldsymbol{z})$ by a recognition model $q(\boldsymbol{z} \mid \boldsymbol{x})$ (Definition 48.1.1); learning the model requires no labels. The Helmholtz machine trains both with the local wake–sleep rule (Definition 48.1.2, Algorithm 48.1.1).
- Classical latent-variable models (factor analysis, mixtures, sparse coding) are fitted by EM, whose fast inference and slow learning steps map onto activity and synaptic change (48.2).

| Model | Recognition | Objective | Neural counterpart (hypothesis) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Helmholtz machine | Bottom-up network, trained in sleep | Approximate likelihood | Wake and offline phases |
| VAE, β-VAE | Encoder network, Gaussian posterior | ELBO, Eq. (48.1), with capacity $\beta$ | Disentangled axes of face cells |
| Predictive coding | Recurrent settling on errors | Posterior of Gaussian hierarchy, Eq. (48.3) | Error units, mismatch responses, feedback |
| Contrastive / JEPA | Encoder only | Agreement of views, Eq. (48.4) | Slowness, invariance learning in IT |
| Diffusion / sampling | Denoising dynamics | Score matching, Eq. (48.5) | Variability as samples, spontaneous activity as prior |

- The ELBO equals the log evidence minus the posterior divergence (Theorem 48.3.1). The VAE (Definition 48.3.2) learns a generative model and an amortized recognition model jointly; the β-VAE (Definition 48.3.3) favors disentangled latents. In the linear case the optimum is probabilistic PCA with noise $\beta\sigma^2$, and $\beta$ acts as a water level for rate and distortion (Proposition 48.3.1).
- Predictive coding (Definition 48.4.1, Formula 48.4.1) implements inference by passing prediction errors forward and predictions backward; it explains end-stopping and mismatch responses, but the existence of dedicated error neurons is debated. The free-energy principle is a general framework built on the same bound.
- Self-supervised objectives (Definitions 48.5.1 and 48.5.2) learn representations that predict ventral-stream responses; temporal slowness is a biological source of positive pairs.

## Questions

1. Show that the wake-phase update $\Delta w_{jk} = \eta(s_j - p_j)y_k$ is the gradient of $\log p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x}, \boldsymbol{z})$ for a sigmoid unit, and explain why the sleep phase does not maximize the ELBO with respect to the recognition weights.

   **Answer** In the generative pathway unit $j$ is on with probability $p_j = \sigma(\sum_k w_{jk}y_k)$, where $y_k$ are the states of the layer above; its contribution to $\log p$ is $s_j\log p_j + (1 - s_j)\log(1 - p_j)$, whose derivative with respect to $w_{jk}$ is $(s_j - p_j)y_k$ because $\mathrm{d}\sigma(a)/\mathrm{d}a = \sigma(1 - \sigma)$. Averaged over $\boldsymbol{z} \sim q(\boldsymbol{z} \mid \boldsymbol{x})$, this is the gradient of $\mathbb{E}_q\log p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{x}, \boldsymbol{z})$, which is the part of the ELBO that depends on $\boldsymbol{\theta}$. The ELBO's dependence on $\boldsymbol{\phi}$ is through $-D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\,\Vert\,p(\boldsymbol{z} \mid \boldsymbol{x}))$, an expectation over $q$ that is hard to differentiate for binary units. The sleep phase instead minimizes $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p(\boldsymbol{z} \mid \boldsymbol{x})\,\Vert\,q)$ averaged over fantasies $\boldsymbol{x} \sim p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$, the divergence with the arguments reversed and weighted by the model's data rather than the real data. The two coincide only when $q$ can match the posterior exactly; otherwise the reversed divergence makes $q$ cover all modes of the posterior instead of concentrating on one.

2. In Proposition 48.3.1, take data variances $\lambda = (6, 3, 1.5, 0.6, 0.2)$ and $\sigma^2 = 0.4$. How many latents are active for $\beta = 1$ and $\beta = 4$, and what are the total rate and distortion?

   **Answer** The water level is $\beta\sigma^2$. For $\beta = 1$ it is $0.4$: four eigenvalues exceed it, the rate is $\frac12[\ln 15 + \ln 7.5 + \ln 3.75 + \ln 1.5] = \frac12\ln 632.8 \approx 3.23$ nats and the distortion is $4 \times 0.4 + 0.2 = 1.8$. For $\beta = 4$ the level is $1.6$: two eigenvalues exceed it, the rate is $\frac12[\ln 3.75 + \ln 1.875] \approx 0.98$ nats, and the distortion is $2 \times 1.6 + 1.5 + 0.6 + 0.2 = 5.5$. These are the marked points of panel C in the figure of 48.3.3.

3. In the predictive coding model of Formula 48.4.1, what is the steady state of a single level with Gaussian prior $\boldsymbol{r} \sim \mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0}, \sigma_{\mathrm{p}}^2I)$, and how does it compare with factor analysis? What would a lesion of the top-down connections do to the error units?

   **Answer** At steady state $U^{\mathsf T}(\boldsymbol{x} - U\boldsymbol{r})/\sigma^2 = \boldsymbol{r}/\sigma_{\mathrm{p}}^2$, so $\boldsymbol{r} = (U^{\mathsf T}U + (\sigma^2/\sigma_{\mathrm{p}}^2)I)^{-1}U^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol{x}$, the posterior mean of the linear Gaussian model, which is the factor-analysis (probabilistic PCA) recognition formula of 48.2 with $W = \sigma_{\mathrm{p}}U$ and isotropic noise. The network computes the same quantity by recurrent relaxation instead of a matrix inverse. If top-down predictions are removed, the error units of the lower level equal the representation itself ($\boldsymbol{e}_{\ell - 1} = \boldsymbol{r}_{\ell - 1}$), so expected and unexpected inputs evoke the same response: end-stopping, surround suppression and mismatch responses should disappear, and activity in the lower area should increase. Experiments that silence feedback from higher visual areas indeed reduce surround suppression in V1 in some studies, a prediction shared with other models of feedback.

4. A critic argues that contrastive learning cannot happen in the brain because it needs negative examples. Give two ways in which a biological system could obtain the "push apart" term, or avoid it.

   **Answer** (a) Negatives are implicit in competition: lateral inhibition and divisive normalization within a population, together with homeostatic plasticity that keeps each neuron's average activity and the variance of its responses fixed, prevent all inputs from mapping to the same representation; this is the variance–covariance regularization of VICReg and Barlow Twins rather than explicit negatives. (b) Temporal structure provides contrasts: inputs separated by long intervals or by saccades and scene changes are on average unrelated, so a rule that strengthens agreement between successive representations (slowness) and weakens it between representations far apart in time is contrastive with negatives drawn from memory traces; a two-phase scheme, in which plasticity has the opposite sign during an offline phase driven by the network's own spontaneous activity (as in wake–sleep and in contrastive Hebbian learning), supplies negatives from the model itself.
