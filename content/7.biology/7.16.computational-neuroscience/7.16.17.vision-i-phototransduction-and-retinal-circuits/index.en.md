The retina is the part of the brain that can be studied in a dish: a sheet about $200\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ thick in which five classes of neurons, arranged in three cellular and two synaptic layers, transform a pattern of photons into the spike trains of about forty kinds of ganglion cells. The physiology of the eye, the steps of the phototransduction cascade and the basic retinal circuitry are covered in animal physiology ([7.14.41](/7.14.41)); the physics of photon counting, including the Hecht–Shlaer–Pirenne experiment and dark noise, in biophysics ([7.6.18](/7.6.18#sec-18.6)). This chapter builds on both and asks how the circuits compute: how a single photon is amplified into a reproducible current, how gain is adjusted over ten orders of magnitude of light intensity, how center–surround receptive fields and direction selectivity arise, and why the retina sends the brain dozens of parallel representations of the image. The encoding tools of [7.16.16](/7.16.16) (receptive fields, linear–nonlinear models) are used throughout; the cortical stages follow in [7.16.18](/7.16.18), and the interpretation of retinal filtering as efficient coding in [7.16.20](/7.16.20).

## 17.1. The Psychophysical Starting Point

Modern visual neuroscience began with a quantitative question: how many photons are needed to see? Hecht, Shlaer and Pirenne (1942) presented dim, brief flashes to the dark-adapted peripheral retina and found that the frequency-of-seeing curve has the shape predicted if a flash is seen whenever at least $5$–$7$ photons are absorbed, the absorptions being Poisson distributed ([7.6.18](/7.6.18#sec-18.6.3)). Since the flash covered about $500$ rods, almost no rod absorbed two photons: each rod must signal the absorption of a single photon, and perception requires a coincidence of several such events, a criterion set by the thermal noise of rhodopsin ([7.6.18](/7.6.18#sec-18.6.4)).

The prediction was confirmed in 1979 when Baylor, Lamb and Yau drew the outer segment of a single toad rod into a suction electrode and recorded discrete current steps of about $1\,\mathrm{pA}$ in dim light, which is equivalent to blocking the entry of about $10^7$ cations. The single-photon response defines the problems of this section: how one molecule changes so much current, how the response ends reliably, and how the same cell avoids saturating in daylight, when it absorbs millions of photons per second.

## 17.2. Amplification and Recovery of Phototransduction

### 17.2.1. Gain of the Cascade

The steps of the cascade, rhodopsin ($\mathrm{R} \to \mathrm{R}^*$), **transducin**, phosphodiesterase PDE6, the fall of cGMP and the closure of cyclic nucleotide-gated (CNG) channels, are listed in [7.14.41](/7.14.41#sec-41.2.4). The gain multiplies along the chain:

| Stage | Gain per upstream molecule | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| $\mathrm{R}^* \to$ transducin | Tens to hundreds of $\mathrm{T}_\alpha$-GTP during the $\sim 0.1\,\mathrm{s}$ lifetime of $\mathrm{R}^*$ | $\mathrm{R}^*$ is a catalyst that diffuses in the disc membrane and activates transducins one after another |
| Transducin $\to$ PDE6 | About $1$ (each $\mathrm{T}_\alpha$-GTP removes one inhibitory $\gamma$ subunit) | No amplification |
| PDE6 $\to$ cGMP | Hundreds to thousands of cGMP hydrolyzed per second | One of the fastest enzymes known, close to the diffusion limit |
| cGMP $\to$ current | Cooperative channel opening, Hill coefficient $\approx 3$ | A fractional change of cGMP gives a three times larger fractional change of current |

Two features of the design deserve attention. The amplification is placed at the first two stages, where a single molecule controls many, so that the downstream signal is carried by many molecules and is no longer limited by molecular noise. And the cascade is confined to the disc membrane and the small cytoplasmic volume of the outer segment: a local fall of cGMP closes channels in a short stretch of the outer segment, and the response is spatially restricted.

### 17.2.2. Kinetics: a Cascade of Stages

The shape of the dim-flash response is well described by a chain of linear first-order stages.

**Formula 17.2.1 (Independent-Activation Model)** If the response to a dim flash is generated by $n$ identical first-order stages with time constant $\tau$, the single-photon response is proportional to the impulse response of the chain,

$$
h(t) = \frac{1}{(n-1)!\,\tau}\Bigl(\frac{t}{\tau}\Bigr)^{n-1}\mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau}, \tag{17.1}
$$
which peaks at $t_{\mathrm{peak}} = (n-1)\tau$ (Baylor, Hodgkin and Lamb 1974). Fits to rod and cone responses require $n = 4$–$6$.

The impulse response of each stage is $\mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau}/\tau$, and the cascade is their $n$-fold convolution, whose Laplace transform is $(1 + s\tau)^{-n}$: the gamma-shaped curve of Eq. (17.1). The early part of the response is captured by a biochemical argument that does not assume linear stages.

**Formula 17.2.2 (Lamb–Pugh Activation Model)** Suppose that a flash produces $\Phi$ photoisomerizations, that each $\mathrm{R}^*$ activates PDE6 at a constant rate during the rising phase, and that cGMP is hydrolyzed at a rate proportional to the activated PDE. Then the fraction of the dark current suppressed rises initially as

$$
F(t) = 1 - \exp\Bigl(-\tfrac12\Phi A\,(t - t_{\mathrm{eff}})^2\Bigr) \approx \tfrac12\Phi A\,(t - t_{\mathrm{eff}})^2, \tag{17.2}
$$
where $t_{\mathrm{eff}}$ is a short delay of a few milliseconds and $A$ is the amplification constant (Lamb and Pugh 1992).

**Proof** The activated PDE grows linearly, $E^*(t) = \nu\Phi t$. cGMP obeys $\mathrm{d}G/\mathrm{d}t = -\beta_{\mathrm{sub}}E^*(t)\,G$ when synthesis is neglected on this short time scale, so $\ln(G/G_{\mathrm{dark}}) = -\frac12\beta_{\mathrm{sub}}\nu\Phi t^2$. The current is proportional to $G^{n_{\mathrm{H}}}$ with $n_{\mathrm{H}} \approx 3$, so $J/J_{\mathrm{dark}} = \exp(-\frac12 n_{\mathrm{H}}\beta_{\mathrm{sub}}\nu\Phi t^2)$, which is Eq. (17.2) with $A = n_{\mathrm{H}}\beta_{\mathrm{sub}}\nu$ and $F = 1 - J/J_{\mathrm{dark}}$. $\square$

The amplification constant is a product of the three gains of the table; it is several times larger in mammalian rods than in amphibian rods, mainly because of the higher temperature, and much smaller in cones.

### 17.2.3. Saturation and Recovery

The exponential in Eq. (17.2) also explains saturation: a bright flash closes all channels, and the response stays at its maximum until enough of the cascade has shut off for some channels to reopen. In the flash family of a rod, responses to brighter flashes therefore reach the same ceiling sooner and remain there longer; the duration of the saturated plateau grows with the logarithm of flash strength, with a slope equal to the time constant of the slowest shutoff step.

Recovery requires the shutoff of every active intermediate:

- $\mathrm{R}^*$ is phosphorylated at several sites by rhodopsin kinase (GRK1) and then capped by **arrestin**; in mice lacking GRK1 or arrestin, single-photon responses last many seconds instead of a fraction of a second.
- Transducin hydrolyzes its GTP, which removes it from PDE6. Alone this reaction is slow; the GTPase-activating complex RGS9–Gβ5–R9AP accelerates it, and in mouse rods it is the rate-limiting step of recovery (time constant about $200\,\mathrm{ms}$): rods overexpressing RGS9 recover faster (Krispel et al. 2006).
- Guanylate cyclase restores cGMP, accelerated by the fall of $\ce{Ca^2+}$ through GCAPs (Section 17.3).

The multistep shutoff of $\mathrm{R}^*$ also makes the single-photon response reproducible: a sequence of $m$ steps of comparable rate has a much less variable duration than a single exponential step ([7.6.18](/7.6.18#sec-18.6.2)).

## 17.3. Adaptation and Weber's Law

Natural light intensities span about ten orders of magnitude between starlight and sunlight, while a ganglion cell can fire at rates between zero and a few hundred hertz. The retina solves this by adapting: the background intensity $I_{\mathrm{B}}$ sets the gain, and the signal transmitted is the deviation from the background.

**Formula 17.3.1 (Weber Behavior of Photoreceptors)** In steady background light $I_{\mathrm{B}}$ the flash sensitivity $S_{\mathrm{F}}$ (peak response per photon, in the linear range) of a rod or cone falls as

$$
\frac{S_{\mathrm{F}}(I_{\mathrm{B}})}{S_{\mathrm{F}}^{\mathrm{dark}}} = \frac{1}{1 + I_{\mathrm{B}}/I_0}, \tag{17.3}
$$
where $I_0$ is the background that halves the sensitivity. For $I_{\mathrm{B}} \gg I_0$ the sensitivity is inversely proportional to the background, so the response to an increment $\Delta I$ depends only on the contrast $\Delta I/I_{\mathrm{B}}$: this is the **Weber–Fechner law** at the level of the receptor.

Three mechanisms produce this behavior in photoreceptors. The fall of intracellular $\ce{Ca^2+}$ when channels close accelerates cGMP synthesis through GCAPs, shortens the lifetime of $\mathrm{R}^*$ through recoverin and raises the cGMP sensitivity of the CNG channel; a minimal model of this feedback, with the resulting shift of the operating range and the Weber law, is in [7.14.41](/7.14.41#sec-41.2.4). In bright light, bleaching of the pigment reduces the number of photons caught, which makes cones effectively unsaturable. And at the circuit level, gain is set again in bipolar and ganglion cells by the mean and variance of their inputs: ganglion cells adapt to contrast (the variance of intensity) within seconds, reducing their gain in high-contrast scenes (Smirnakis et al. 1997), a form of divisive normalization ([7.16.23](/7.16.23)).

The functional consequence is the constancy of perception: a sheet of white paper reflects about $90\,\%$ and black ink about $5\,\%$ of the incident light, indoors or in sunlight, and a retina that reports contrast reports these ratios regardless of the illumination. Rod vision obeys Weber's law over about four orders of magnitude of background, before the rods saturate; cones take over at higher intensities and never saturate.

## 17.4. Rods, Cones and Color

The two classes of photoreceptors divide the range of intensities and trade sensitivity for speed and acuity:

| Property | Rods | Cones |
|---|---|---|
| Number in a human retina | About $9\times10^7$ | About $4.5\times10^6$ (S cones about $5$–$10\,\%$) |
| Distribution | Absent from the central fovea, densest at about $20^\circ$ | Peak density about $1.5$–$2\times10^5\,\mathrm{mm^{-2}}$ in the fovea |
| Single-photon response | About $1\,\mathrm{pA}$, reliably detected | About $30$–$100$ times smaller, buried in noise |
| Time to peak of dim-flash response | About $100$–$200\,\mathrm{ms}$ | About $30$–$50\,\mathrm{ms}$ |
| Saturation | At moderate daylight | Practically never (bleaching adaptation) |
| Pigments | Rhodopsin, peak $498\,\mathrm{nm}$ | S, M, L opsins, peaks near $420$, $530$, $560\,\mathrm{nm}$ |
| Convergence onto ganglion cells | High (sensitivity) | One-to-one in the fovea (acuity) |

Color vision begins with the comparison of cones whose pigments have different spectra (**trichromacy**, and the principle of univariance in [7.6.18](/7.6.18#def-18.6.2)). The genes behind it were identified by Jeremy Nathans and colleagues (1986), who used the bovine rhodopsin gene as a probe to clone the human rhodopsin gene and three cone opsin genes. The S opsin gene lies on chromosome 7, the rhodopsin gene on chromosome 3, and the L and M opsin genes in a tandem array on the X chromosome. Their sequences are $96\,\%$ identical, so unequal crossing over produces the deleted and hybrid genes found in men with red–green color vision deficiencies; the variants and their frequencies are in [7.14.41](/7.14.41#sec-41.2.6).

The X-linked arrangement also explains the evolution of primate color vision. Most mammals are dichromats with an S and an M/L cone; Old World primates acquired a third cone by a duplication of the X-linked gene about 30–40 million years ago, whereas in many New World monkeys a single X-linked locus with several alleles makes heterozygous females trichromats and all males dichromats. The plasticity of the circuits that read the cones was demonstrated by gene therapy: adult dichromatic male squirrel monkeys that received a human L-opsin gene in a subset of their M cones became able to discriminate the colors that separate red from green (Mancuso et al. 2009). The comparison of cone signals is done by retinal circuits: the midget ganglion cells of the fovea, whose center receives a single L or M cone and whose surround a mixture, are red–green opponent, and the small bistratified ganglion cells combine S-ON with (L + M)-OFF signals.

## 17.5. Center–Surround Receptive Fields

### 17.5.1. The Circuit

Photoreceptor signals reach ganglion cells through bipolar cells, which split them into **ON bipolar cells** and **OFF bipolar cells** by their glutamate receptors ([7.14.41](/7.14.41#sec-41.3.2)); ON and OFF bipolar cells terminate in different sublaminae of the inner plexiform layer, where they contact ON and OFF ganglion cells. The center of a ganglion cell's receptive field is this direct vertical path; the surround is generated laterally.

Horizontal cells receive input from many photoreceptors, are electrically coupled to one another through gap junctions so that their signals spread over hundreds of micrometers, and feed back onto the photoreceptor terminals: when light in the surround hyperpolarizes horizontal cells, the feedback depolarizes the terminal of a central photoreceptor, increasing its transmitter release, which opposes the effect of light in the center. Several mechanisms contribute to this feedback (proton release by horizontal cells that changes the voltage dependence of presynaptic $\ce{Ca^2+}$ channels, ephaptic currents in the narrow synaptic cleft). Amacrine cells add a second surround in the inner retina. Because the surround is a subtraction of a spatially averaged signal from a local one, it is a form of lateral inhibition, the circuit motif of [7.16.2](/7.16.2).

### 17.5.2. The Difference-of-Gaussians Model

The receptive field of a ganglion cell, mapped with small spots or by reverse correlation ([7.16.16](/7.16.16#sec-16.5)), is well described by a *difference of Gaussians*: a narrow excitatory center Gaussian of radius $\sigma_{\mathrm{c}}$ and weight $A_{\mathrm{c}}$ minus a broad surround Gaussian of radius $\sigma_{\mathrm{s}} > \sigma_{\mathrm{c}}$ and weight $A_{\mathrm{s}}$ (Rodieck 1965; the formula is [Eq. (41.9)](/7.14.41#eq-41.9) of animal physiology). Two experimental tests follow directly from it.

**Formula 17.5.1 (Area Summation and Spatial-Frequency Tuning of a DoG Receptive Field)** For a difference-of-Gaussians receptive field with center and surround weights $A_{\mathrm{c}}, A_{\mathrm{s}}$ and radii $\sigma_{\mathrm{c}} < \sigma_{\mathrm{s}}$:

1. the linear response to a centered spot of radius $\rho$ is
   $$
   R(\rho) = A_{\mathrm{c}}\bigl(1 - \mathrm{e}^{-\rho^2/2\sigma_{\mathrm{c}}^2}\bigr) - A_{\mathrm{s}}\bigl(1 - \mathrm{e}^{-\rho^2/2\sigma_{\mathrm{s}}^2}\bigr);
   $$
2. the response to a sinusoidal grating of spatial frequency $k$ (cycles per unit length) and unit contrast has amplitude
   $$
   \tilde W(k) = A_{\mathrm{c}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-2\pi^2\sigma_{\mathrm{c}}^2k^2} - A_{\mathrm{s}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-2\pi^2\sigma_{\mathrm{s}}^2k^2}. \tag{17.4}
   $$

**Proof** For (1), integrate each normalized Gaussian over a disc in polar coordinates: $\int_0^\rho \frac{1}{2\pi\sigma^2}\mathrm{e}^{-r^2/2\sigma^2}\,2\pi r\,\mathrm{d}r = 1 - \mathrm{e}^{-\rho^2/2\sigma^2}$. For (2), the linear response to a grating is the **Fourier transform** of the receptive field at the grating's frequency, and the two-dimensional Fourier transform of a normalized Gaussian of standard deviation $\sigma$ is $\mathrm{e}^{-2\pi^2\sigma^2k^2}$; linearity gives the difference. $\square$

The response to a growing spot first rises, as the spot fills the center, and then falls, as it invades the surround: the optimal spot just covers the center, and large uniform spots give a response proportional to $A_{\mathrm{c}} - A_{\mathrm{s}}$, small when center and surround are balanced. In the frequency domain the same receptive field is a band-pass filter: it passes intermediate spatial frequencies and attenuates the low frequencies that carry most of the power of natural images, so that neighboring ganglion cells, which would otherwise report highly correlated signals, report mostly the differences. Barlow's proposal that the retina reduces redundancy in this way, and the derivation of the optimal filter (which becomes low-pass when light is dim and noise dominates), are the subject of [7.16.20](/7.16.20).

![The difference-of-Gaussians receptive field of an ON-center ganglion cell (center radius $\sigma_{\mathrm{c}} = 0.1^\circ$, surround radius $\sigma_{\mathrm{s}} = 0.3^\circ$). A: the radial profile (black) and its two components; the surround weight is 0.85 of the center weight. B: area summation (Formula 17.5.1, part 1) for three surround weights. Without a surround the response saturates as the spot grows; with a surround it peaks for a spot of about $0.25^\circ$ radius and then declines toward $A_{\mathrm{c}} - A_{\mathrm{s}}$. C: spatial-frequency tuning, Eq. (17.4); the surround turns a low-pass filter into a band-pass filter peaking near 1 cycle per degree](./plots/dog-receptive-field.py)

The DoG is a linear model. Real ganglion cells add the nonlinearities of [7.16.16](/7.16.16#sec-16.6): rectification of bipolar cell inputs within the center makes some cells (the Y cells of the cat, the parasol cells of primates) respond to fine gratings whose bright and dark bars cancel in a linear sum, and nonlinear subunits make object motion detectors respond to local motion that differs from the global motion caused by eye movements.

## 17.6. Direction Selectivity

### 17.6.1. Direction-Selective Ganglion Cells

**Definition 17.6.1 (Direction Selectivity)** A neuron is direction selective if it responds strongly to a stimulus moving in one direction (its preferred direction) and weakly or not at all to the same stimulus moving in the opposite (null) direction. The strength is measured by the direction selectivity index $\mathrm{DSI} = (r_{\mathrm{pref}} - r_{\mathrm{null}})/(r_{\mathrm{pref}} + r_{\mathrm{null}})$.

Direction selectivity cannot be a property of a linear, time-invariant receptive field that is separable in space and time; it requires either a receptive field that is tilted in space–time (the input from one location reaches the cell later than from a neighboring location) or a nonlinear interaction between inputs from different locations. Barlow and Levick (1965) found direction-selective ganglion cells in the rabbit retina and, by presenting two flashed slits in sequence, showed that the selectivity arises within small subregions of the receptive field and depends on inhibition in the null direction: a stimulus moving in the null direction is vetoed by inhibition that arrives, delayed, from the side the stimulus has just crossed. In the mouse, ON–OFF direction-selective ganglion cells come in four types, each preferring one of four directions aligned with the axes defined by the eye muscles, and ON direction-selective cells project to the accessory optic system, which drives the optokinetic reflex that stabilizes the image on the retina.

### 17.6.2. Starburst Amacrine Cells

**Definition 17.6.2 (Starburst Amacrine Cell)** **Starburst amacrine cells** are amacrine cells with symmetric radial dendrites that release both acetylcholine and GABA from varicosities in the distal third of their dendrites; ON and OFF starburst cells stratify in the same sublaminae as the dendrites of direction-selective ganglion cells.

The asymmetry needed for direction selectivity arises at two levels.

1. Each dendrite of a starburst cell is itself direction selective: calcium in its distal varicosities rises when a stimulus moves from the soma toward the tip (centrifugal motion) and not in the reverse direction (Euler, Detwiler and Denk 2002). Contributing mechanisms are the electrotonic structure of the thin dendrite, in which input from the proximal part summates with the local input as the stimulus moves outward, the kinetics of bipolar inputs along the dendrite, and mutual inhibition between neighboring starburst cells.
2. The wiring is asymmetric: reconstruction of a piece of retina by serial-section electron microscopy combined with calcium imaging showed that a direction-selective ganglion cell receives GABAergic synapses mainly from those starburst dendrites that point in its null direction (Briggman, Helmstaedter and Denk 2011). Motion in the null direction is centrifugal for these dendrites and inhibits the ganglion cell strongly; motion in the preferred direction is centripetal for them and inhibits weakly.

Genetic ablation of starburst cells or deletion of the vesicular GABA transporter in them abolishes direction selectivity of the ganglion cells. The circuit is the anatomical realization of Barlow and Levick's veto: inhibition offset toward the null side and delayed relative to excitation.

### 17.6.3. The Reichardt Detector

Long before the retinal circuit was known, Hassenstein and Reichardt (1956) derived a model of motion detection from the optomotor turning responses of the beetle *Chlorophanus* walking on a spherical treadmill inside a rotating patterned drum.

**Definition 17.6.3 (Hassenstein–Reichardt Detector)** A **Reichardt detector** receives the luminance signals $s_1(t)$, $s_2(t)$ of two neighboring points separated by $\Delta x$. Each of two mirror-symmetric half-detectors multiplies the delayed (low-pass filtered) signal of one input by the undelayed signal of the other, and the output is their difference:

$$
R(t) = \tilde s_1(t)\,s_2(t) - \tilde s_2(t)\,s_1(t), \qquad \tilde s_i = L * s_i, \tag{17.5}
$$
where $L$ is a low-pass filter, here first order with time constant $\tau$.

**Theorem 17.6.1 (Response to a Drifting Grating)** For a sinusoidal grating of contrast $c$, spatial wavelength $\lambda$ and temporal frequency $f$ ($\omega = 2\pi f$) moving from input 1 toward input 2, with the mean luminance removed, the time-averaged output of the detector of Eq. (17.5) is

$$
\bar R = c^2\sin\Bigl(\frac{2\pi\Delta x}{\lambda}\Bigr)\frac{\omega\tau}{1 + (\omega\tau)^2}, \tag{17.6}
$$
and motion in the opposite direction gives $-\bar R$.

**Proof** Write $s_1 = c\cos\omega t$ and $s_2 = c\cos(\omega t - \varphi)$ with $\varphi = 2\pi\Delta x/\lambda$. The filter multiplies a sinusoid by $\lvert H\rvert = (1 + \omega^2\tau^2)^{-1/2}$ and delays its phase by $\theta = \arctan\omega\tau$. Averaging products of sinusoids, $\overline{\tilde s_1 s_2} = \frac{c^2}{2}\lvert H\rvert\cos(\varphi - \theta)$ and $\overline{\tilde s_2 s_1} = \frac{c^2}{2}\lvert H\rvert\cos(\varphi + \theta)$. Their difference is $c^2\lvert H\rvert\sin\varphi\sin\theta$, and $\lvert H\rvert\sin\theta = \omega\tau/(1 + \omega^2\tau^2)$. Reversing the direction changes the sign of $\varphi$. $\square$

The theorem makes three predictions that distinguish a correlation detector from a velocity meter, and all three hold for insect optomotor behavior and for the fly's motion-sensitive neurons:

- The response depends on the temporal frequency $f = v/\lambda$, not on the velocity $v$: it peaks at $f = 1/(2\pi\tau)$ for every wavelength.
- The response grows with the square of contrast, because it is a product of two signals.
- For wavelengths shorter than $2\Delta x$ the sine is negative and the detector signals the wrong direction (spatial aliasing); flies indeed turn against the motion of very fine gratings.

In *Drosophila* the detector has been located: T4 cells compute the direction of moving ON edges and T5 cells that of OFF edges, each in four subtypes for the four cardinal directions, and they project to four layers of the lobula plate, where wide-field neurons integrate them. Their inputs come from neurons with different temporal filters at neighboring positions in the visual field (Mi1 and Tm3 for T4, for example), and T4 and T5 combine a Reichardt-like enhancement in the preferred direction with a Barlow–Levick-like suppression in the null direction (Haag, Arenz and Borst 2016). The correlation detector is also equivalent, for its average output, to the motion energy model of mammalian cortex ([7.16.18](/7.16.18)): different implementations of the same computation.

## 17.7. Parallel Channels

### 17.7.1. The Diversity of Retinal Cell Types

The retina is a set of parallel circuits. Large-scale recording with calcium imaging of the mouse retina distinguished at least $32$ functional types of ganglion cells by their responses to a standard set of stimuli (Baden et al. 2016), and single-cell transcriptomics identified about $45$ molecular types in the mouse, together with about $15$ types of bipolar cells and more than $60$ types of amacrine cells (Shekhar et al. 2016; Tran et al. 2019; Yan et al. 2020). The criteria agree largely: a type defined by gene expression has a characteristic morphology, stratification depth in the inner plexiform layer, and light response. Each type tiles the retina in a regular mosaic, its dendritic fields covering the retina with little overlap or gap, so that every point of the visual field is sampled once by every type. Mosaic spacing is set during development partly by homotypic repulsion between cells of the same type ([7.16.21](/7.16.21#sec-21.8)).

| Ganglion cell type (examples) | Feature computed | Main target |
|---|---|---|
| Midget (primate P) and parasol (primate M) | Fine spatial detail and color; luminance contrast and motion | Parvocellular and magnocellular layers of the LGN |
| ON–OFF direction selective (4 types) | Local motion direction | Superior colliculus, LGN shell |
| ON direction selective (3 types) | Slow global motion | Accessory optic system (optokinetic reflex) |
| Object motion sensitive (W3, local edge detector) | Motion of small objects against a still background | Superior colliculus |
| Alpha cells | Fast, large-field contrast | LGN, superior colliculus |
| ipRGCs (M1–M6) | Absolute irradiance (and contrast for some types) | Suprachiasmatic nucleus, olivary pretectal nucleus, LGN |

The same neurons can be reused for different computations under different conditions. In dim light, rod signals reach ganglion cells through rod bipolar cells and the AII amacrine cell, which passes them by gap junctions to ON cone bipolar cells and by glycinergic synapses to OFF cone bipolar cells; in daylight the AII cell, driven by cones, instead mediates crossover inhibition between the ON and OFF pathways. Light adaptation can even change the receptive fields of ganglion cells, for example by weakening their surround in dim light, where averaging is more valuable than redundancy reduction ([7.16.20](/7.16.20)).

### 17.7.2. Intrinsically Photosensitive Ganglion Cells

Mice lacking all rods and cones are blind to images but still constrict their pupils and entrain their circadian clock to light. The photoreceptor responsible is a small population of ganglion cells that express the opsin melanopsin and respond to light directly, the **intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells** (Berson, Dunn and Takao 2002; Hattar et al. 2002).

Melanopsin is related to invertebrate opsins and signals through $\mathrm{G}_{\mathrm{q}}$ and TRP channels, like a fly photoreceptor; it is insensitive (it catches about $10^{-4}$ as many photons as a rod outer segment) but its responses are sustained for as long as the light lasts, so ipRGCs report the absolute irradiance that rods and cones discard by adaptation. The M1 type projects to the suprachiasmatic nucleus, the circadian pacemaker ([7.16.35](/7.16.35)), and to the olivary pretectal nucleus, which controls the pupil; other types also receive rod and cone input through the retinal circuit and contribute to image-forming vision. The example shows the general principle of this section: the retina does not transmit one image but many, each tuned to the needs of a particular downstream circuit.

## Questions

1. A rod's dim-flash response is well fitted by Eq. (17.1) with $n = 4$ and a time to peak of $180\,\mathrm{ms}$. What is $\tau$? A mutant rod has the same peak time but its response decays much more slowly. Which parameter of the model, and which biochemical step, is most likely changed?

   **Answer** $t_{\mathrm{peak}} = (n-1)\tau$, so $\tau = 60\,\mathrm{ms}$. A model with identical stages cannot change the decay without changing the peak time; a slower decay with an unchanged rising phase means that one stage (the slowest shutoff step) has acquired a longer time constant. Candidates are the inactivation of $\mathrm{R}^*$ (GRK1 or arrestin deficiency, which produces very long responses) and the GTPase of transducin (RGS9 deficiency); in mouse rods the latter is normally rate limiting, and the dominant time constant of recovery from saturating flashes measures it.

2. Using Eq. (17.3), a rod has $I_0 = 100$ photoisomerizations per second. By what factor is its flash sensitivity reduced in a background of $10^4$ photoisomerizations per second? Show that for $I_{\mathrm{B}} \gg I_0$ two increments produce equal responses when they have equal contrast $\Delta I/I_{\mathrm{B}}$.

   **Answer** $S_{\mathrm{F}}/S_{\mathrm{F}}^{\mathrm{dark}} = 1/(1 + 10^4/100) = 1/101$, about a hundredfold reduction. In the linear range the response to an increment is $\Delta R = S_{\mathrm{F}}(I_{\mathrm{B}})\,\Delta I = S_{\mathrm{F}}^{\mathrm{dark}}\,\Delta I/(1 + I_{\mathrm{B}}/I_0) \approx S_{\mathrm{F}}^{\mathrm{dark}}I_0\,\Delta I/I_{\mathrm{B}}$ for $I_{\mathrm{B}} \gg I_0$. The response depends on $\Delta I$ and $I_{\mathrm{B}}$ only through their ratio: the photoreceptor transmits contrast, and the threshold increment is proportional to the background (Weber's law).

3. Show from Eq. (17.6) that a Reichardt detector cannot report velocity independently of the pattern. How could a downstream circuit estimate velocity from a population of detectors?

   **Answer** At fixed velocity $v$ the temporal frequency is $f = v/\lambda$, so the same velocity gives different responses for different wavelengths (through both $\sin(2\pi\Delta x/\lambda)$ and $\omega\tau/(1+\omega^2\tau^2)$), and the output also scales with $c^2$. A single detector therefore confounds velocity, spatial structure and contrast. Velocity can be estimated by comparing detectors with different input separations $\Delta x$ or different time constants $\tau$: the ratio of responses of two detectors with time constants $\tau_1$, $\tau_2$ depends only on $\omega$, and combining it with an estimate of the dominant spatial frequency gives $v = f\lambda$. Natural images contain many spatial frequencies, and averaging over them makes the response of a detector array vary more smoothly with velocity than with a single grating.

4. (Open question) Why might it be advantageous for the retina to send about forty distinct channels to the brain rather than a single high-resolution image?

   **Answer** Points to discuss: (a) the optic nerve is a bottleneck (about $10^6$ fibers for $10^8$ photoreceptors), so computing features locally where the full-resolution signal is available, before compression, preserves information that the brain could not recover; (b) different downstream targets need different features (the clock needs irradiance, the optokinetic system needs global slip, the colliculus needs small moving objects), and dedicated channels deliver them with minimal latency; (c) each channel can be adapted to its own statistics, and decorrelating the channels reduces redundancy ([7.16.20](/7.16.20)); (d) the cost is wiring and cell types, which the retina pays by tiling each type at the density its feature requires (dense midget cells, sparse direction-selective cells).
