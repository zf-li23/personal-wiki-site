All the processing of the preceding chapters serves one purpose: the only way the brain acts on the world is by contracting muscles. This chapter opens the part of the course on movement by asking what computations any motor system must perform, independently of where in the nervous system they are carried out. Sensory signals arrive late and are noisy, the body has far more degrees of freedom than a task needs, the muscles add noise that grows with the force they produce, and the dynamics of the limbs change with growth, fatigue and every tool we pick up. Control theory and Bayesian statistics give a compact set of answers: predict the consequences of one's own commands with internal models, estimate the state of the body by combining these predictions with sensory evidence, choose commands that are optimal given the noise, and keep recalibrating the models by learning. The reflex circuits on which all this rests, the stretch reflex and the hierarchy of motor control, are described in [7.14.44](/7.14.44); the Bayesian combination of cues is the subject of [7.16.27](/7.16.27). The following chapters locate the principles in the spinal cord ([7.16.30](/7.16.30)), motor cortex ([7.16.32](/7.16.32)), cerebellum ([7.16.33](/7.16.33)) and basal ganglia ([7.16.40](/7.16.40)).

## 29.1. The Problems of Motor Control

### 29.1.1. Redundancy, Delay, Noise and Change

Four properties of the body make movement a hard computational problem.

| Problem | Example | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Redundancy | About 600 muscles and more than 200 joints; a point in space can be reached with infinitely many arm postures, trajectories and muscle activations | The brain must choose one solution (Bernstein's degrees-of-freedom problem) |
| Delay | Spinal reflex $25$–$50\,\mathrm{ms}$, long-latency (transcortical) response $50$–$100\,\mathrm{ms}$, visual feedback $100$–$200\,\mathrm{ms}$ | Feedback alone cannot control fast movements |
| Noise | Sensory noise, variability of motor neurons, noise in force that grows with the force | Repeated movements scatter; the scatter depends on the plan |
| Nonlinearity and change | Interaction torques between joints, muscle force depending on length and velocity ([7.14.9](/7.14.9)), growth, fatigue, tools | The mapping from commands to movements must be learned and relearned |

Redundancy is a problem and an opportunity: because many solutions exist, the brain can choose the one that is least affected by noise or costs least effort (29.5). Delay is the most fundamental constraint, because it limits what feedback can achieve and forces the brain to predict.

### 29.1.2. A Hierarchy of Sensorimotor Loops

Motor responses form a hierarchy in which speed is traded for sophistication. The fastest are the spinal reflexes, with fixed connectivity but gains that are set by descending pathways ([7.14.44](/7.14.44#sec-44.1)). Rhythmic movements such as walking, breathing and chewing are generated by **central pattern generators** in the spinal cord and brainstem and started, stopped and modulated from above ([7.16.30](/7.16.30)). Voluntary movements are planned and initiated by cortical networks that receive the richest sensory information but respond slowest. Even the long-latency response to a mechanical perturbation of the arm, at $50$–$100\,\mathrm{ms}$, is already flexible: it depends on the task and on the goal of the movement (Pruszynski and Scott, 2012), which is a prediction of optimal feedback control (29.5.2).

## 29.2. Feedforward and Feedback Control

**Definition 29.2.1 (Feedforward and Feedback Control)** In *feedforward* (open-loop) control the motor command is computed in advance from the desired movement and a model of the body, without using sensory information about the movement as it proceeds. In *feedback* (closed-loop) control the command is computed continuously from the difference between the desired and the sensed state.

Feedforward control is fast but blind: it fails if the model is wrong or if something perturbs the movement. Feedback control corrects errors whatever their cause, but only after they have been sensed. The **vestibulo-ocular reflex** is nearly pure feedforward control, because the retinal image would be stabilized too late by visual feedback (the eye begins to counter-rotate about $10\,\mathrm{ms}$ after the head moves; [7.16.33](/7.16.33)); grasping an object combines a feedforward grip force with feedback corrections when the object slips. Most movements use both.

The difficulty of feedback is delay. The simplest case shows the effect exactly.

**Proposition 29.2.1 (Delayed Proportional Feedback)** For the plant $\dot x = u$ with the delayed feedback law $u(t) = -k\,[x(t - T) - x^*]$, $k > 0$, the error $\varepsilon = x - x^*$ obeys $\dot\varepsilon(t) = -k\,\varepsilon(t - T)$. The fixed point $\varepsilon = 0$ is asymptotically stable if and only if $kT < \pi/2$. At $kT = \pi/2$ the system oscillates with period $4T$; for $1/\mathrm{e} < kT < \pi/2$ it approaches the target with damped oscillation.

**Proof** Solutions $\mathrm{e}^{\lambda t}$ satisfy the characteristic equation $\lambda = -k\,\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda T}$. For $T = 0$ the only root is $\lambda = -k < 0$; as $kT$ increases, roots can cross into the right half-plane only through the imaginary axis. Setting $\lambda = \mathrm{i}\omega$ gives $\mathrm{i}\omega = -k(\cos\omega T - \mathrm{i}\sin\omega T)$, so $\cos\omega T = 0$ and $\omega = k\sin\omega T$; the first crossing has $\omega T = \pi/2$ and $\omega = k$, hence $kT = \pi/2$ and period $2\pi/\omega = 4T$. Real roots exist only for $kT \le 1/\mathrm{e}$ (the function $\lambda\mathrm{e}^{\lambda T}$ has minimum $-1/(\mathrm{e}T)$); above that value the dominant roots are complex and the approach oscillates. $\square$

The result generalizes to every feedback loop: the product of gain and delay is limited, and a loop driven near its limit rings at a period of a few delays. For a visual loop with $T = 100\,\mathrm{ms}$ the critical gain is only $16\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$, so a movement corrected by vision alone could not be faster than a few hundred milliseconds without oscillating. Physiological tremor and pathological clonus are oscillations of short reflex loops of this kind ([7.14.44](/7.14.44#sec-44.3.2), where the same analysis is carried out for the **stretch reflex** with a second-order limb).

The remedy is prediction. If the controller knows how the plant responds to its commands, it can compute what the delayed sensory signal will show once the commands still "in flight" have taken effect.

**Definition 29.2.2 (Smith Predictor)** A **Smith predictor** (O. J. M. Smith, 1957) is a feedback controller for a plant with a known delay $T$ that uses as its feedback signal the delayed measurement plus the output of an internal model of the plant driven by the commands issued during the last $T$: $\hat x(t) = x(t - T) + \int_{t-T}^{t}\dot{\hat x}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s$. With an exact model $\hat x(t) = x(t)$, and the delay is removed from the loop.

With the plant $\dot x = u$ of Proposition 29.2.1 the internal model is a copy of the integrator, and the closed loop becomes $\dot\varepsilon = -k\varepsilon$, stable for any gain (figure below, B). The price is dependence on the model: a model with the wrong gain still converges for moderate errors but no longer as fast or as smoothly. Miall, Weir, Wolpert and Stein (1993) proposed that the cerebellum acts as a Smith predictor for visually guided movement, a hypothesis that leads directly to the internal models of the next section.

![Delayed feedback and prediction (plant $\dot x = u$, target $10\,\mathrm{cm}$; Proposition 29.2.1 and Definition 29.2.2). A: proportional feedback through a delay $T = 100\,\mathrm{ms}$: at $kT = 0.5$ the hand approaches the target without overshoot, at $kT = 1.2$ it rings, and at $kT = 1.8 > \pi/2$ the oscillation grows. B: the same high gain ($kT = 1.8$) with a Smith predictor: with a correct internal model the approach is the undelayed exponential; a model whose gain is wrong by a factor $0.7$ or $1.4$ still converges, with overshoot or a slower approach; without the predictor (dashed) the loop is unstable. ## 29.3. Internal Models

### 29.3.1. Forward and Inverse Models

**Definition 29.3.1 (Internal Model)** An **internal model** is a neural representation of the input–output relation of the body or of an object, used to predict or to control. A forward model maps the current state and a motor command to the predicted next state and its sensory consequences; it models causality, from command to consequence. An inverse model maps a desired state or trajectory to the motor command that produces it; it is what feedforward control requires.

The two are complementary. An inverse model is a controller; a forward model is a predictor, used for three purposes: to overcome delays (as in the Smith predictor), to estimate the state of the body by combining predictions with sensory signals (29.4), and to distinguish the sensory consequences of one's own actions from external events. It can also be used to train an inverse model, since the difference between predicted and actual consequences is an error signal in sensory coordinates that can be converted into an error in command coordinates (Jordan and Rumelhart, 1992; Kawato, 1999). The cerebellum is the best candidate for the site of both kinds of model ([7.16.33](/7.16.33)).

The strongest evidence for internal models is anticipation. When we lift an object and move it up and down, the grip force between the fingers rises and falls in phase with the load force that the acceleration produces, without the lag that feedback would introduce (Flanagan and Wing, 1997); when the experimenter drops a weight into a hand held by the subject, grip force rises only after the impact, but when the subject drops it from the other hand, grip force rises before. The nervous system therefore predicts the load from its own commands.

### 29.3.2. Efference Copy and the Cancellation of Self-Generated Sensation

**Definition 29.3.2 (Efference Copy)** An **efference copy** (corollary discharge) is an internal copy of a motor command sent to sensory or other brain areas, where, usually through a forward model, it predicts the sensory consequences of the movement. Von Holst and Mittelstaedt (1950) called the sensory signal caused by one's own movement *reafference* and that caused by external events *exafference*; subtracting the prediction from the sensory input isolates the exafference.

Every moving animal faces the problem. When the eye makes a **saccade**, the image sweeps across the retina, yet the world appears stable; pressing gently on the eyelid, which moves the eye without a command, makes the world jump. A pathway from the superior colliculus through the mediodorsal thalamus to the frontal eye field carries a corollary discharge of each saccade, and inactivating it impairs the ability to make a second saccade to a target flashed before the first one (Sommer and Wurtz, 2002). In the singing cricket, a single corollary discharge interneuron inhibits the auditory pathway during each chirp, so that the animal is not deafened by its own song (Poulet and Hedwig, 2006). In weakly electric fish, a cerebellum-like structure learns a "negative image" of the electrosensory consequences of the fish's own discharge and subtracts it (Bell, 1981; [7.16.33](/7.16.33)).

The same mechanism explains why one cannot tickle oneself. Self-produced touch is rated less intense and less ticklish than the same touch produced by another person, and the attenuation decreases when a robot interposed between the hands adds a delay of $100$–$300\,\mathrm{ms}$ or rotates the direction of the stroke (Blakemore, Frith and Wolpert, 1999): the better the prediction matches the sensation, the more it is cancelled. A consequence is force escalation: when two people take turns pressing on each other's finger with the instruction to match the force just felt, each underestimates the force he produces and the force grows by about $40\,\%$ per turn (Shergill and colleagues, 2003). A failure to attribute self-generated signals to oneself has been proposed to underlie hallucinations and delusions of control in schizophrenia ([7.16.52](/7.16.52)).

## 29.4. State Estimation

### 29.4.1. Combining Prediction and Sensation

To control the body the brain needs its current state: the positions and velocities of the limbs, and the properties of objects held. No single source gives it. Sensory signals are delayed and noisy; the prediction of a forward model driven by the efference copy is immediate but accumulates errors, because the motor noise is not known. As in the combination of two sensory cues (**cue integration**, Theorem 27.2.1 of [7.16.27](/7.16.27#sec-27.2.1)), the optimal estimate weights the two by their reliability, and for a linear system with Gaussian noise the weighting is computed recursively by the Kalman filter.

**Algorithm 29.4.1 (Kalman Filter)** Let the state evolve as $\boldsymbol{x}_{t+1} = A\boldsymbol{x}_t + B\boldsymbol{u}_t + \boldsymbol{w}_t$ with motor noise $\boldsymbol{w}_t \sim \mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0}, Q)$, and let the sensory signal be $\boldsymbol{y}_t = H\boldsymbol{x}_t + \boldsymbol{v}_t$, $\boldsymbol{v}_t \sim \mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0}, R)$. Starting from a prior mean $\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_0$ and covariance $\Sigma_0$, repeat for $t = 0, 1, \dots$:

1. Update with the measurement: $K_t = \Sigma_tH^{\mathsf T}(H\Sigma_tH^{\mathsf T} + R)^{-1}$, $\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_t^+ = \hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_t + K_t(\boldsymbol{y}_t - H\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_t)$, $\Sigma_t^+ = (I - K_tH)\Sigma_t$.
2. Predict with the forward model and the efference copy: $\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{t+1} = A\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_t^+ + B\boldsymbol{u}_t$, $\Sigma_{t+1} = A\Sigma_t^+A^{\mathsf T} + Q$.

For a linear-Gaussian system, $\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_t^+$ and $\Sigma_t^+$ are the exact mean and covariance of the posterior distribution of $\boldsymbol{x}_t$ given all measurements up to $t$; the estimate is the minimum-mean-square-error estimate (Kalman, 1960).

The update step is Bayes' rule with the prediction as the prior, which is why it reduces to the cue-combination weights of Eq. (27.2) for a scalar state: with prior variance $\sigma_{\mathrm{p}}^2$ and measurement variance $\sigma_{\mathrm{y}}^2$ the gain is $K = \sigma_{\mathrm{p}}^2/(\sigma_{\mathrm{p}}^2 + \sigma_{\mathrm{y}}^2)$, the weight of the measurement. The gain is large when the prediction is uncertain and small when the measurement is. Delays are handled by running the forward model ahead: the delayed measurement updates the estimate of the past state, which the model then propagates to the present with the commands issued in between, the Smith predictor in statistical form.

Wolpert, Ghahramani and Jordan (1995) asked subjects to move the unseen hand for various durations and then to indicate its position. The bias of the estimate grew with the duration of the movement up to about one second and then declined, and its variance grew and then levelled off, the pattern predicted by a Kalman filter whose forward model slightly overestimates the movement: the prediction error accumulates until the proprioceptive corrections balance it. The figure shows the three estimators on simulated reaches.

A: one movement: true position (black), sensory samples (gray dots), the prediction of a forward model driven by the efference copy alone (orange, dashed), which misses the effect of the motor noise, and the Kalman estimate (blue). C: the gain with which each sensory sample corrects the estimate rises as the prediction becomes uncertain and reaches a steady state, together with the posterior s.d.](./plots/kalman-reach.py)

### 29.4.2. Priors in Motor Behavior

The prior need not come only from the forward model. Körding and Wolpert (2004) shifted the visual feedback of the hand by an amount drawn from a Gaussian distribution and showed the hand's position only briefly, with more or less blur, in the middle of a reach. Subjects learned the distribution of shifts and corrected for it, weighting the prior more heavily when the feedback was blurred, as a **Bayesian observer** ([7.16.27](/7.16.27)) would. Bayesian estimation thus applies to the body as it applies to the world, and the same reliability-weighted combination appears in posture, in the estimation of the weight of an object from its size and in the timing of interception.

## 29.5. Optimal Control

### 29.5.1. Signal-Dependent Noise and Minimum Variance

Why do we reach along nearly straight paths with smooth, bell-shaped velocity profiles, when infinitely many trajectories reach the target? Earlier proposals minimized a smoothness cost, such as the integrated squared jerk (Flash and Hogan, 1985), but did not say why the brain should care about jerk. Harris and Wolpert (1998) based the answer on a property of muscle.

**Definition 29.5.1 (Signal-Dependent Noise)** **Signal-dependent noise** is variability of the force produced by a muscle whose standard deviation grows in proportion to the mean force (constant coefficient of variation), so that a command $u$ yields the output $u(1 + \kappa\xi)$ with $\xi$ of zero mean and unit variance.

The constant coefficient of variation follows from the **size principle** of **motor unit** recruitment: larger forces are produced by recruiting larger motor units, each of which adds a larger step of force ([7.16.30](/7.16.30#sec-30.1)). With signal-dependent noise, a movement made with large commands is imprecise, and the precision of a movement depends on the whole time course of the command, not only on its end.

**Proposition 29.5.1 (Minimum-Variance Command)** Let a linear plant with state $\boldsymbol{x}_t$ be driven by the commands $u_1, \dots, u_N$ corrupted by independent signal-dependent noise, and require the mean state at time $N$ to equal $\boldsymbol{x}^*$. The mean final state is $G\boldsymbol{u}$ and the positional variance accumulated over a post-movement period is $\sum_t c_tu_t^2$ with constants $c_t > 0$ that depend only on the plant. The command that minimizes this variance subject to $G\boldsymbol{u} = \boldsymbol{x}^*$ is
$$
\boldsymbol{u}^* = C^{-1}G^{\mathsf T}\bigl(GC^{-1}G^{\mathsf T}\bigr)^{-1}\boldsymbol{x}^*, \qquad C = \operatorname{diag}(c_1, \dots, c_N), \tag{29.1}
$$
and the minimal standard deviation is proportional to the distance $\lVert\boldsymbol{x}^*\rVert$.

**Proof** The mean and the noise of a linear plant superpose: the final state is $\sum_tG_t u_t(1 + \kappa\xi_t)$, with mean $G\boldsymbol{u}$ and with the position at a later time $s$ having variance $\kappa^2\sum_t g_{st}^2u_t^2$; summing over the post-movement period gives $c_t = \kappa^2\sum_s g_{st}^2$. Minimizing the positive-definite quadratic form $\boldsymbol{u}^{\mathsf T}C\boldsymbol{u}$ under linear constraints with a Lagrange multiplier $\boldsymbol{\lambda}$ gives $C\boldsymbol{u} = G^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol{\lambda}$, and substituting in the constraint gives Eq. (29.1). The solution is linear in $\boldsymbol{x}^*$, so the variance is quadratic in the distance. $\square$

The optimal commands avoid large peaks, because the variance grows with the square of the command, and they avoid commands late in the movement, whose noise has no time to be averaged out by the inertia of the limb. The result is a smooth, bell-shaped velocity profile like those of human reaches and saccades, without any smoothness assumption (figure below, A). Because shortening the movement requires larger commands, the minimal endpoint scatter rises steeply as the duration falls (B): the **speed–accuracy trade-off**, described empirically by Fitts' law (movement time increases linearly with $\log_2(2A/W)$ for amplitude $A$ and target width $W$), appears as a consequence of noise rather than as a separate principle. The same model predicts the main sequence of saccades and the shape of arm trajectories in the presence of obstacles.

### 29.5.2. Optimal Feedback Control

Minimum-variance planning computes a trajectory in advance. Todorov and Jordan (2002) proposed instead that the brain computes a feedback law: a mapping from the estimated state to the command that minimizes the expected cost of the whole movement, a weighted sum of task error and effort.

**Definition 29.5.2 (Optimal Feedback Control)** In **optimal feedback control** the motor command at each time is a function of the estimated state, $\boldsymbol{u}_t = \pi_t(\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_t)$, chosen to minimize the expected value of a cost that penalizes deviations from the task goal and the motor effort, for example $\mathbb{E}\bigl[\sum_t(\boldsymbol{x}_t^{\mathsf T}Q_t\boldsymbol{x}_t + \boldsymbol{u}_t^{\mathsf T}R\boldsymbol{u}_t)\bigr]$. For a linear plant, quadratic cost and Gaussian noise (the linear–quadratic–Gaussian problem), the optimal law is linear, $\boldsymbol{u}_t = -L_t\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_t$, with gains $L_t$ obtained backward in time from a **Riccati equation**, and with $\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_t$ given by the Kalman filter (Algorithm 29.4.1).

The separation of the problem into an estimator and a controller is exact for the linear–quadratic–Gaussian case (the separation principle) and is the basis of the scheme in which a forward model, a state estimator and a feedback controller work together. The cost function defines what the task is, and the most important consequence follows from that.

**Definition 29.5.3 (Minimum Intervention Principle)** The **minimum intervention principle** states that an optimal feedback controller corrects only those deviations of the state that interfere with the goal of the task and leaves deviations in task-irrelevant directions uncorrected, because correcting them costs effort and adds signal-dependent noise.

**Example 29.5.1** Two fingers press together so that the sum of their forces $f_1 + f_2$ matches a target. Each force fluctuates with independent noise. Deviations of the sum harm the task; deviations of the difference $f_1 - f_2$ do not. An optimal controller with an effort cost therefore applies feedback only to the sum, and the variability of the difference grows across trials until it is limited only by effort, while that of the sum stays small. The prediction is confirmed: in multifinger force production and in pointing with redundant arm postures, the trial-to-trial variance is much larger in the directions that leave the task variable unchanged, the "uncontrolled manifold" of Scholz and Schöner (1999), than in the task-relevant direction.

:::

Optimal feedback control explains why motor variability is structured, why responses to perturbations depend on the goal (pushing the arm toward a wide target produces little correction, toward a narrow one a strong correction) and why the long-latency responses of motor cortex are flexible ([7.16.32](/7.16.32)). It also dissolves the degrees-of-freedom problem: the brain does not choose a single trajectory but a **policy** that exploits redundancy. Optimal control of this kind is **reinforcement learning** with a known model, and the two theories meet in [7.16.39](/7.16.39).

## 29.6. Motor Learning

### 29.6.1. Adaptation

**Definition 29.6.1 (Motor Adaptation)** **Motor adaptation** is the trial-by-trial recalibration of movement in response to a systematic perturbation, driven by the error between the predicted and the actual sensory consequences (sensory prediction error), that returns performance to its unperturbed level; its signature is an aftereffect, an error in the opposite direction when the perturbation is removed.

The classic paradigms are prism glasses that shift the visual field, visuomotor rotations in which a cursor moves at an angle to the hand, and velocity-dependent force fields applied by a robotic manipulandum (Shadmehr and Mussa-Ivaldi, 1994). In a force field, reaches are first curved by the force; within a hundred trials they become straight again, and when the field is turned off they curve in the mirror direction. The aftereffect shows that the brain has not simply stiffened the arm but built an internal model of the new dynamics, which generalizes to other movements in a way that reveals its representation (for example, in joint rather than hand coordinates). Adaptation depends on the cerebellum: patients with cerebellar degeneration adapt little or not at all, while patients with lesions of the basal ganglia adapt normally ([7.16.33](/7.16.33)).

A state-space model describes adaptation from trial to trial. Let $x_n$ be the internal estimate of the perturbation on trial $n$ and $p_n$ the perturbation, so that the error is $e_n = p_n - x_n$; then $x_{n+1} = Ax_n + Be_n$, with a retention factor $A \le 1$ and an error sensitivity $B$. With a constant perturbation the error decays geometrically to a residual $(1 - A)p/(1 - A + B)$, since forgetting and learning balance.

### 29.6.2. The Two-State Model

A single process cannot explain several features of adaptation. Learning is faster the second time (savings), even after the aftereffect has been washed out; a perturbation learned for many trials and then reversed for a few trials reappears when errors are removed (spontaneous recovery); and adaptation decays with time. Smith, Ghazizadeh and Shadmehr (2006) showed that two processes with different time scales account for all of these.

**Formula 29.6.1 (Two-State Model of Motor Adaptation)** The adaptation $x_n = x_{\mathrm{f},n} + x_{\mathrm{s},n}$ on trial $n$ is the sum of a fast and a slow state driven by the same error $e_n = p_n - x_n$:
$$
x_{\mathrm{f},n+1} = A_{\mathrm{f}}x_{\mathrm{f},n} + B_{\mathrm{f}}e_n, \qquad x_{\mathrm{s},n+1} = A_{\mathrm{s}}x_{\mathrm{s},n} + B_{\mathrm{s}}e_n, \tag{29.2}
$$
with $A_{\mathrm{f}} < A_{\mathrm{s}} < 1$ and $B_{\mathrm{f}} > B_{\mathrm{s}}$: the fast state learns quickly and forgets quickly, the slow state learns slowly and retains.

During prolonged training the slow state gradually takes over from the fast one. A brief reversal drives the fast state strongly negative while the slow state, which learns slowly, is reduced only partly, so that the net adaptation returns to zero. If errors are then clamped to zero (the cursor is made to move straight to the target whatever the hand does), learning stops and only retention acts: the fast state decays within a few trials and the remaining slow state makes the old adaptation reappear (figure below, A). The model predicts the amount of spontaneous recovery quantitatively, and the slow state predicts long-term retention one day later (Joiner and Smith, 2008).

A: $300$ trials with a perturbation (force field, $p = 1$), the reversed field ($p = -1$) until the net adaptation is back at zero, and $200$ error-clamp trials (shaded): the net adaptation (black) returns to zero during the reversal because the fast state (red) becomes negative, and reappears in the error clamp as the fast state decays and the slow state (blue) remains, spontaneous recovery. ### 29.6.3. Error Sensitivity and Other Forms of Learning

The error sensitivity $B$ is not fixed. It decreases for large errors, which the nervous system attributes to external causes rather than to its own miscalibration, and it increases when errors are consistent from trial to trial and decreases when they alternate (Herzfeld and colleagues, 2014): the brain learns how much to learn from an error, a form of meta-learning that can account for savings without a second state. Adaptation is only one form of motor learning. Use-dependent learning biases movements toward recently repeated ones; reinforcement learning improves skills from the success or failure of actions, without a directed error, and depends on the **basal ganglia** and **dopamine** ([7.16.39](/7.16.39), [7.16.40](/7.16.40)); explicit strategies, such as aiming away from a target to compensate for a rotation, are fast but effortful and combine with the slow implicit recalibration (Mazzoni and Krakauer, 2006; Taylor, Krakauer and Ivry, 2014). Skill learning combines all of them and continues to improve speed and accuracy over years of practice.

## 29.7. Planning in Parietal and Frontal Cortex

Before a movement is controlled it must be chosen and planned, and here the boundary between perception, decision and action dissolves. The posterior parietal cortex lies between the visual and somatosensory areas and the frontal motor areas and transforms sensory information into coordinates for action, the dorsal stream of "vision for action" ([7.14.41](/7.14.41)). Mountcastle and colleagues (1975) found neurons there that fired when a monkey reached for or looked at an object of interest, but not during the same movements made without that intent. Its subdivisions are specialized by effector: the lateral intraparietal area (LIP) for saccades, the parietal reach region for arm movements and the anterior intraparietal area for grasping; in a task in which a monkey is cued to plan either a saccade or a reach to the same location, LIP neurons are active for the planned saccade and parietal reach region neurons for the planned reach (Snyder, Batista and Andersen, 1997). The activity of LIP also accumulates evidence during perceptual decisions, a subject of [7.16.47](/7.16.47).

Damage to the posterior parietal cortex causes optic ataxia, misreaching to objects that are seen well, and neglect of one side of space. Electrical stimulation of the human posterior parietal cortex evokes a conscious intention to move, and at higher intensity the belief of having moved although no movement occurred, whereas stimulation of premotor cortex evokes movements of which the patient is unaware (Desmurget and colleagues, 2009). The frontal motor areas (premotor cortex, supplementary motor area and, in rodents, the anterolateral motor cortex) hold the plan during delays and select among competing actions; according to the affordance competition hypothesis, several potential actions are specified in parallel in parietal and premotor cortex and compete until one is selected, with input from prefrontal cortex and the basal ganglia (Cisek, 2007). The neural dynamics of this preparation and of execution in motor cortex are the subject of [7.16.32](/7.16.32).

## Summary

| Problem | Computational solution | Section |
|---|---|---|
| Sensory delay | Prediction: Smith predictor, forward model | Proposition 29.2.1, Definition 29.2.2 |
| Distinguishing self from world | Efference copy and cancellation of reafference | Definition 29.3.2 |
| Uncertain state of the body | Kalman filter: reliability-weighted prediction and sensation | Algorithm 29.4.1 |
| Redundancy and noise | Minimum-variance planning; optimal feedback control | Proposition 29.5.1, Definition 29.5.2 |
| Changing dynamics | Error-driven adaptation with fast and slow processes | Formula 29.6.1 |

- Delayed feedback is stable only if gain times delay is small ($kT < \pi/2$ for an integrator, Proposition 29.2.1); fast movements therefore depend on feedforward control and prediction (Definition 29.2.1).
- Internal models (Definition 29.3.1) predict the consequences of commands and invert them; the efference copy (Definition 29.3.2) cancels self-generated sensation, which is why one cannot tickle oneself.
- The Kalman filter (Algorithm 29.4.1) combines the prediction of a forward model with sensory feedback, weighting each by its reliability; its gain is the cue-combination weight of Theorem 27.2.1.
- Signal-dependent noise (Definition 29.5.1) makes the precision of a movement depend on its plan; minimizing endpoint variance (Proposition 29.5.1) yields bell-shaped velocity profiles and the speed–accuracy trade-off. Optimal feedback control (Definition 29.5.2) corrects only task-relevant errors (Definition 29.5.3, Example 29.5.1).
- Motor adaptation (Definition 29.6.1) is well described by a fast and a slow process (Formula 29.6.1), which explain savings and spontaneous recovery; it depends on the cerebellum, whereas reinforcement-based learning depends on the basal ganglia.
- Posterior parietal and frontal cortex transform sensory information into effector-specific plans and select among potential actions.

## Questions

1. A loop with delay $T = 40\,\mathrm{ms}$ controls the plant $\dot x = u$ by proportional feedback. What is the largest gain for which the loop is stable, and at what frequency does it oscillate at that gain? How would doubling the delay change both answers?

   **Answer** By Proposition 29.2.1, $k_c = \pi/(2T) = 39\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$, and the oscillation at the boundary has period $4T = 160\,\mathrm{ms}$, a frequency of $6.25\,\mathrm{Hz}$. Doubling the delay halves the critical gain ($20\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$) and halves the frequency ($3.1\,\mathrm{Hz}$): longer loops are slower and oscillate at lower frequencies, which is why clonus of the ankle is slower than tremor of the fingers.

2. In a scalar Kalman filter the prediction has variance $4\,\mathrm{cm^2}$ and the proprioceptive measurement variance $1\,\mathrm{cm^2}$. Give the Kalman gain and the variance after the update. What happens to the gain if vision of the hand (variance $0.25\,\mathrm{cm^2}$) becomes available in addition?

   **Answer** $K = 4/(4 + 1) = 0.8$, and the posterior variance is $(1 - K)\cdot4 = 0.8\,\mathrm{cm^2}$, equal to $4\cdot1/(4 + 1)$, the cue-combination result. With vision added, the two measurements combine into one of variance $1\cdot0.25/1.25 = 0.2\,\mathrm{cm^2}$; the gain on this combined measurement is $4/4.2 = 0.95$ and the posterior variance $0.19\,\mathrm{cm^2}$. With a precise sense the estimate follows the measurement almost entirely; in the dark it relies more on prediction.

3. Explain, using the minimum intervention principle, why the variability of the joint angles during repeated pointing movements is larger than the variability of the fingertip position, and design an experiment to test that this structure is produced by feedback rather than by the plan.

   **Answer** Many joint configurations give the same fingertip position; deviations that leave the fingertip unchanged do not affect the task, and an optimal feedback controller with an effort cost leaves them uncorrected (Definition 29.5.3), so variance accumulates in the joint-space directions of the uncontrolled manifold while the fingertip variance stays small. To show that feedback is responsible, perturb the arm mechanically during the movement in a task-relevant and a task-irrelevant direction: optimal feedback control predicts a strong correction of the first and little or none of the second, already in the long-latency response, and the pattern should change when the task changes (for example, from a small round target to a long bar, which makes one direction task-irrelevant).

4. In the two-state model, why does a brief reversal of the perturbation return the net adaptation to zero without erasing the memory? Predict what happens if the reversal phase lasts 300 trials instead of the few needed to cancel the adaptation.

   **Answer** The reversal produces large negative errors that drive the fast state, with its large $B_{\mathrm{f}}$, strongly negative within a few trials, while the slow state, with small $B_{\mathrm{s}}$, decreases only a little; the net adaptation is zero because the two states cancel, not because both are zero. In the error clamp the fast state decays with $A_{\mathrm{f}}$ and the slow state is revealed. After a 300-trial reversal the slow state itself reverses and the fast state returns to a small value; the clamp then shows adaptation to the reversed field, with little or no recovery of the original one.

5. (Open question) Why is a forward model more useful for estimation and learning than an inverse model, given that control ultimately requires an inverse model?

   **Answer** A forward model can be trained directly by supervised learning, because the brain observes both the command (efference copy) and its consequence (sensory feedback); the error of its prediction is available in every movement. An inverse model needs the correct command as a target, which is never observed; it must be trained indirectly, for example by converting sensory errors into command errors through the forward model (distal teacher), by feedback-error learning, in which the output of a feedback controller serves as the error, or by reinforcement. The forward model also serves estimation (Kalman filter), cancellation of reafference and mental simulation of actions, so a single learned model has many uses.
