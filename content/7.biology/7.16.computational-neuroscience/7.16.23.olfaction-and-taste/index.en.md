The chemical senses solve a problem quite unlike that of vision or hearing. Light and sound vary along a few physical dimensions (wavelength, position, frequency, time), and sense organs can sample these dimensions with regular arrays of receptors. Odorants have no such ordering: the space of volatile molecules is vast and has no natural low-dimensional axes, so the olfactory system covers it with a very large family of receptors, each broadly tuned, and represents an odor by the pattern of activity across them. This chapter follows that strategy from the genes to the brain. The physiology of the olfactory epithelium, transduction, the olfactory bulb and the taste bud is in [7.14.43](/7.14.43); here we concentrate on the molecular logic discovered by genetics in mice, worms and flies, on the quantitative questions that the combinatorial code raises (how many odors can be told apart, how concentration is handled), and on the circuit computations that recur across species: normalization in the first relay and random expansion with sparse coding in the second. The sparse random expansion of the fly mushroom body reappears as the substrate of olfactory learning in [7.16.38](/7.16.38), and taste closes the chapter as the counterexample of a sense organized mainly by labeled lines.

## 23.1. Odorant Receptors

### 23.1.1. Discovery of the Receptor Family

By the late 1980s it was known that odorants raise cAMP in olfactory cilia through a G protein, which suggested that the receptors would be **G protein–coupled receptors**. Buck and Axel (1991) designed degenerate PCR primers against conserved transmembrane segments of known GPCRs and amplified cDNA from rat olfactory epithelium. Among the products they looked for a pool of related sequences rather than a single gene: a family that was expressed only in the olfactory epithelium and whose PCR products, when cut with a restriction enzyme, gave fragments adding up to far more than one gene. This search criterion embodied a hypothesis about the code, that a large number of receptors would be needed to cover chemical space, and it was correct.

**Definition 23.1.1 (Odorant Receptor)** An **odorant receptor** (OR) is a seven-transmembrane receptor, expressed in olfactory receptor neurons, that binds volatile molecules and couples to the olfactory G protein $\mathrm{G_{olf}}$; the vertebrate odorant receptors form the largest **gene family** of the genome, with about $1100$ intact genes in the mouse and $350$–$400$ in humans.

The OR genes lie in clusters on almost every chromosome, and their sequences are most variable in transmembrane helices 3–6, which line the ligand-binding pocket. A cryo-electron microscopy structure of a human OR bound to its ligand (OR51E2 with propionate, Billesbølle et al., 2023) confirmed that odorants bind in a pocket within the transmembrane bundle, as for other class A GPCRs; small changes in the residues of this pocket change the selectivity, which is how a large family can cover a large chemical space. Insects arrived at the same organization with unrelated molecules: their odorant receptors are odorant-gated ion channels formed with the coreceptor Orco ([7.14.43](/7.14.43#sec-43.5.1)).

### 23.1.2. One Neuron, One Receptor

**Definition 23.1.2 (One-Neuron–One-Receptor Rule)** The organization in which each olfactory receptor neuron expresses a single odorant receptor gene, from one allele, out of the whole repertoire is called the **one-neuron–one-receptor rule**.

The rule matters because it makes the receptor neuron, not the molecule, the unit of the code: the response profile of a neuron is the response profile of one receptor, and all neurons expressing that receptor form one channel. Its mechanism is a stochastic choice stabilized by feedback:

1. In immature neurons the OR genes are packed in silenced heterochromatin; a single OR allele escapes, assisted by enhancers (such as the H element) that interact in trans with the chosen promoter.
2. The OR protein that is produced triggers a feedback signal (involving the unfolded protein response, through the kinase PERK and translation factor ATF5, and the adenylyl cyclase Adcy3) that downregulates the demethylase LSD1 and so prevents a second OR gene from being activated.
3. If the chosen gene is a pseudogene and no functional receptor is made, the feedback does not occur, and the neuron switches to another OR gene.

The logic of "choose randomly, then lock" can be made quantitative. If activation events occur at random with rate $k$ per gene, and the feedback after the first success takes a time $\tau$ to silence the others, the probability that a second gene among the remaining $G - 1$ is activated during this window is $1 - \mathrm{e}^{-(G-1)k\tau}$; a rule that holds in nearly every neuron therefore requires that activation be slow compared with feedback, $(G - 1)k\tau \ll 1$. With $G \approx 1000$ genes, this means the expected time for a given gene to be activated must exceed the feedback delay by much more than a thousandfold, which is why OR choice is a rare event that takes days during neuronal maturation.

### 23.1.3. Convergence onto Glomeruli

Neurons expressing the same OR are scattered through a zone of the epithelium, but their axons converge on one or two **olfactory glomeruli** in each bulb ([7.14.43](/7.14.43#def-43.1.2)). Experiments that swapped the coding sequence of one OR for another showed that the receptor itself takes part in choosing the target: the axons then project to a new position, close to that of the substituted receptor (Mombaerts et al., 1996). The OR acts through its level of basal, odor-independent cAMP signaling, which sets the expression of guidance molecules such as neuropilin-1 and so the position of the glomerulus along the anterior–posterior axis; activity-dependent expression of adhesion molecules (Kirrel2 and Kirrel3, among others) then sorts axons with the same OR into a single glomerulus (Imai et al., 2006; Serizawa et al., 2006). How axon guidance and activity-dependent refinement build sensory maps in general is the topic of [7.16.21](/7.16.21). The result is a strong **convergence**, from thousands of neurons of one type onto one glomerulus, which improves the signal-to-noise ratio and turns "which receptor" into "where in the bulb".

## 23.2. Combinatorial Codes

### 23.2.1. The Receptor–Odorant Matrix

Malnic et al. (1999) recorded from single mouse olfactory neurons with calcium imaging and identified the OR of each responsive neuron by single-cell PCR. Each receptor responded to several odorants, and each odorant activated several receptors; aliphatic acids and alcohols that differed by a single carbon atom activated overlapping but distinct sets. This is **combinatorial coding** ([7.14.43](/7.14.43#def-43.2.1)). A simple model makes its consequences quantitative. Let receptor type $i$ bind odorant $j$ with dissociation constant $K_{ij}$; at concentration $c$ the fractional occupancy, and approximately the response, is

$$
a_{ij}(c) = \frac{c}{c + K_{ij}}. \tag{23.1}
$$

The matrix $A = (a_{ij})$ is the code: the column for odorant $j$ is its pattern across receptors. Measured affinities of a single odorant across receptors are spread over many orders of magnitude. As the concentration rises, the receptors with the highest affinity are recruited first and then more and more of the lower-affinity ones (panel A of the figure in Section 23.2.2), so that the code of one odorant at high concentration resembles that of a different odorant at low concentration. Many odorants indeed smell different at different concentrations (indole, for example, smells floral at low concentration and putrid at high). The brain must therefore separate identity from intensity; the normalization of Section 23.4.2 and the recurrent inhibition of piriform cortex (Section 23.3) are circuit answers to this problem, and the timing of activation within a sniff is another, since the highest-affinity receptors are always activated first (Wilson et al., 2017).

### 23.2.2. How Many Odors Can Be Distinguished?

With $N$ receptor types, an all-or-none code could represent $2^N$ patterns; even a set of binary neurons in a brain area of $10^3$–$10^6$ cells has an astronomically large capacity. Capacity in this sense says nothing about discrimination, which is limited by noise and by the structure of the code. Bushdid et al. (2014) tried to measure the limit directly. From $128$ odorants they made mixtures of $N = 10$, $20$ or $30$ components and asked subjects in triangle tests (two identical vials and one different) to pick the odd one out, varying the fraction of components by which two mixtures differed. Most pairs differing in more than about half of their components could be discriminated; counting the number of mixtures that could be packed in the space of all mixtures at this separation, they extrapolated to "at least $10^{12}$ odors".

The extrapolation is not robust. Meister (2015) showed that the same procedure assigns enormous numbers to sensory systems with only a few dimensions, so the result is a property of the method rather than of olfaction, and Gerkin and Castro (2015) showed that the estimate changes by many orders of magnitude with small changes of the discrimination criterion and of the number of subjects. What the experiments do establish is how discriminability of mixtures depends on their composition, and this has a simple explanation.

**Proposition 23.2.1 (Discriminability of Mixtures)** Let the codes $\boldsymbol{a}_j \in \mathbb{R}^R$ of the odorants be independent random vectors with mean $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ and covariance matrix $\Sigma$, let a mixture of $N$ components be coded by the mean $\boldsymbol{m} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{j \in \text{mixture}}\boldsymbol{a}_j$, and let two mixtures share a fraction $f$ of their components. Then

$$
\mathbb{E}\,\|\boldsymbol{m}_1 - \boldsymbol{m}_2\|^2 = \frac{2(1 - f)\operatorname{tr}\Sigma}{N}, \tag{23.2}
$$
and with independent noise of standard deviation $\sigma$ in each receptor channel, the squared discriminability is $d'^2 \approx 2(1 - f)\operatorname{tr}\Sigma/(N\sigma^2)$.

**Proof** The shared components cancel in $\boldsymbol{m}_1 - \boldsymbol{m}_2 = \frac{1}{N}\bigl(\sum_{j \in U_1}\boldsymbol{a}_j - \sum_{k \in U_2}\boldsymbol{a}_k\bigr)$, where $U_1$ and $U_2$ are the unshared components, $N(1 - f)$ of each. The difference has mean zero and covariance $\frac{2N(1 - f)}{N^2}\Sigma$; the expected squared norm of a zero-mean vector is the trace of its covariance. $\square$

Two predictions follow (panels B and C of the figure below). First, at a fixed fraction of differing components, mixtures with more components are harder to tell apart, because the mean of many random patterns converges to the average pattern $\boldsymbol{\mu}$. Weiss et al. (2012) found exactly this: mixtures of about 30 or more components of equal intensity, spanning the space of odorants, smell increasingly alike, a percept they called "olfactory white" by analogy with the white produced by mixing many wavelengths. Second, discriminability grows with the number of receptor channels, through $\operatorname{tr}\Sigma$, which is a sum over $R$ channels. With only $R = 3$ channels, as in color vision, disjoint mixtures quickly become indistinguishable (metamers), which is the point of the color analogy: a few random spectral lines added together look nearly the same, whereas a system with hundreds of receptor types keeps mixtures apart.

## 23.3. The Olfactory Bulb and Piriform Cortex

The circuit of the olfactory bulb (mitral and tufted cells, periglomerular and granule cells, reciprocal dendrodendritic synapses) and its main computations, gain control, contrast enhancement and synchronization to the sniff, are described in [7.14.43](/7.14.43#sec-43.2.1). Two quantitative findings refine that picture. In the zebrafish bulb, the activity patterns evoked by related amino acids are initially similar, reflecting the overlap of receptor input, but become progressively less correlated over the first few hundred milliseconds of the response, as recurrent inhibition through granule cells reshapes the mitral cell output (Friedrich and Laurent, 2001). This decorrelation is a dynamical form of pattern separation. In the mouse, optical imaging shows that the glomerular map is chemotopic only coarsely: neighboring glomeruli are not much more similar in their tuning than distant ones, so the bulb is not a smooth map of a chemical feature in the way that the cochlea is a map of frequency.

From the bulb, the olfactory pathway reaches cortex without a thalamic relay. In piriform cortex the spatial order is lost: neurons responding to a given odorant are scattered across the cortex without discernible pattern, and single mitral cell axons tracing from one glomerulus spread over the whole area (Stettler and Axel, 2009; Ghosh et al., 2011). Each piriform neuron samples a few percent of the glomeruli at random, an arrangement with the same computational structure as the mushroom body of the next section. Recurrent connections among piriform neurons and feedback inhibition keep the number of active neurons roughly constant across concentrations, and the earliest-activated glomeruli dominate the response; the resulting ensemble identifies the odor largely independently of its strength (Bolding and Franks, 2018). Because the connections are random, the meaning of an ensemble is not fixed by the wiring: it must be learned, which is consistent with the finding that piriform ensembles acquire associations with reward or punishment through experience (Choi et al., 2011).

## 23.4. Olfaction in Worms and Flies

### 23.4.1. *Caenorhabditis elegans*

The nematode uses the opposite design to vertebrates: about 1300 chemoreceptor genes are expressed in only 32 chemosensory neurons, so each neuron expresses many receptors, and the behavioral meaning is a property of the neuron. Moving the diacetyl receptor ODR-10 from an attractive neuron to a repulsive one turns attraction into avoidance ([7.14.43](/7.14.43#sec-43.5.1)). The worm thus implements the **labeled line principle** at the level of single neurons, and its odor discrimination relies on a small number of neuron classes and on adaptation within each. Even here there is a stochastic element: the two AWC neurons choose asymmetrically, through a calcium-dependent signaling cascade and gap-junctional communication between them, which of them expresses the receptor STR-2, so that left and right AWC detect different odorants.

### 23.4.2. The Antennal Lobe and Divisive Normalization

*Drosophila* has about 50 types of olfactory receptor neurons (ORNs), each expressing one odorant receptor with Orco and converging on one of about 50 glomeruli in the antennal lobe; roughly 150 uniglomerular projection neurons (PNs) carry the output of the glomeruli to the mushroom body and the lateral horn. Because the numbers are small and identified, the transformation from ORNs to PNs can be measured glomerulus by glomerulus (Bhandawat et al., 2007; Olsen et al., 2010). Three properties distinguish PN from ORN responses:

1. PN responses are more transient, emphasizing the onset of an odor, because the ORN–PN synapse depresses during sustained firing and because GABAergic local neurons inhibit ORN terminals presynaptically.
2. PN responses are more reliable from trial to trial: each PN pools many ORNs of its type, and the coefficient of variation of spike counts is lower.
3. Weak ORN inputs are amplified and strong ones saturate, so that PN responses to a panel of odors are spread more evenly over the available range of firing rates, and odors are farther apart in the PN coding space than in the ORN space.

The third property is captured by one equation.

**Definition 23.4.1 (Divisive Normalization)** A computation in which the response of a neuron to its driving input is divided by a quantity that grows with the summed activity of a pool of neurons,

$$
r = R_{\max}\frac{x^n}{\sigma^n + x^n + (m s)^n}, \tag{23.3}
$$
where $x$ is the driving input, $s$ the pooled activity, $\sigma$ a semisaturation constant, $n$ an exponent and $m$ the weight of the pool, is called **divisive normalization**.

In the antennal lobe, $x$ is the firing rate of the ORNs of the PN's own glomerulus and $s$ the total ORN input to the lobe, which drives the GABAergic local neurons; fits to paired recordings gave an exponent near $1.5$ and a semisaturation constant of about $10\,\mathrm{Hz}$ (Olsen, Bhandawat and Wilson, 2010). Equation (23.3) has two effects. Without lateral input it is a saturating curve whose steep part lies at low ORN rates, so it spreads the many weak inputs over a wide range of outputs: when the distribution of ORN rates is skewed towards small values, the distribution of PN rates is nearly flat (panel B of the figure below). This is the strategy of histogram equalization, which maximizes the information that a neuron with limited range can transmit about its input ([7.16.20](/7.16.20)). With lateral input the curve shifts to the right without changing its asymptote: a strong odor that activates many glomeruli reduces the gain of every PN, so that the pattern across PNs reflects the relative rather than the absolute input. The same form, with different meanings for $x$ and $s$, describes contrast normalization in the retina and primary visual cortex, the effects of attention, and multisensory integration; Carandini and Heeger (2012) proposed it as a canonical computation of the brain.

### 23.4.3. The Mushroom Body: Random Expansion and Sparse Coding

**Definition 23.4.2 (Mushroom Body and Kenyon Cell)** The paired neuropil of the insect brain formed by the parallel axons of a large population of intrinsic neurons, which receive olfactory (and in some insects visual) input from projection neurons in the calyx and send their axons through the peduncle into the lobes, where they contact the dendrites of a small number of output neurons, is called the **mushroom body**; its intrinsic neurons are called **Kenyon cells** (KCs).

Each mushroom body of *Drosophila* has about 2000 Kenyon cells, so the representation expands from about 50 glomerular channels to some 2000 dimensions. Each KC has on average about 6–7 dendritic claws, each receiving input from one PN bouton, and the glomeruli that connect to a given KC are drawn approximately at random: in a map of the inputs of 200 identified KCs, the frequency of each glomerulus matched its abundance, and the pairs of glomeruli converging on one KC showed no structure beyond chance (Caron et al., 2013). Since the connections differ between individuals, the KC representation of an odor is unique to each fly, in contrast to the stereotyped PN axons in the lateral horn (Section 23.5).

KC responses are sparse: a given odor activates about 5–10 % of the Kenyon cells, and each KC responds to few odors (Turner, Bazhenov and Laurent, 2008; Honegger et al., 2011). Two mechanisms produce this sparseness. A KC typically fires only when several of its inputs are active together, so its threshold is high relative to the input of one claw; and a single large GABAergic neuron, the anterior paired lateral (APL) neuron, receives input from all KCs and inhibits them all, forming a global feedback loop. Blocking the output of APL increases the fraction of responding KCs and impairs the fly's ability to learn to discriminate similar odors, while leaving the discrimination of dissimilar odors intact (Lin et al., 2014).

The functional logic of this architecture is the subject of a well-developed theory (Babadi and Sompolinsky, 2014; Litwin-Kumar et al., 2017). Random expansion followed by a threshold makes the representations of different odors less correlated than their inputs, because two odors whose PN patterns overlap partially drive largely different sets of KCs once only the most strongly driven KCs fire (panel A of the figure in Section 23.4.4). Decorrelated, high-dimensional representations are what a downstream linear readout needs: the output neurons of the mushroom body learn, by dopamine-gated plasticity at the KC–output synapses, to associate an odor with reward or punishment ([7.16.38](/7.16.38)), and a linear classifier can separate many more patterns in a 2000-dimensional sparse space than in a 50-dimensional dense one. The number of inputs per KC is close to the optimum for the dimension of the representation: with too few inputs a KC reports only one glomerulus and the expansion adds nothing, with too many all KCs see nearly the same mixture. Global inhibition in turn keeps the sparseness constant when concentration changes (panel B of the same figure), so the code reflects identity rather than intensity, the same problem met in Section 23.2.1.

### 23.4.4. The Fly Algorithm for Similarity Search

Dasgupta, Stevens and Navlakha (2017) noticed that the same architecture solves a basic problem of computer science, finding similar items in a large database. A **hash function** assigns each item a short tag; a *locality-sensitive hash* is one for which similar items receive similar tags, so that the nearest neighbors of a query can be found by comparing tags rather than full feature vectors. The classical construction projects the input onto a few random directions. The fly does something different.

**Algorithm 23.4.1 (Fly Hashing)** Input: a feature vector $\boldsymbol{x} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ (the PN responses, $d \approx 50$); parameters $m \gg d$ (the number of KCs), the number $c$ of inputs per KC, and the tag size $k$.

1. Center: subtract the mean of $\boldsymbol{x}$ over its components (and rescale), so that all inputs have the same mean, as divisive normalization does for odors of different concentrations.
2. Expand: compute $\boldsymbol{h} = W\boldsymbol{x}$, where $W$ is a sparse binary $m \times d$ matrix in which each row has $c$ ones in random positions.
3. Sparsify: keep the $k$ largest entries of $\boldsymbol{h}$ (winner-take-all by APL) and set them to 1, the others to 0. The binary vector of length $m$ with $k$ ones is the tag.

Similar inputs share many winners, so the overlap of tags is a measure of similarity. Compared with a dense random projection to $k$ dimensions of the same tag length, the expansion to $m \gg k$ followed by winner-take-all spends the same number of active units but has many more possible tags, which improves nearest-neighbor retrieval most when tags are short (panel C of the figure below); in benchmarks on image and text datasets the fly algorithm was competitive with standard locality-sensitive hashing. The example shows a general use of neuroscience for computation: a circuit with a well-defined input–output function can be abstracted into an algorithm whose properties are then understood mathematically, here random projections and the Johnson–Lindenstrauss guarantee that they approximately preserve distances. The same motif, a large random expansion with sparse activity followed by plastic output synapses, recurs in the cerebellar granule cell layer ([7.16.33](/7.16.33)) and in the dentate gyrus of the hippocampus ([7.16.42](/7.16.42)).

## 23.5. Innate and Learned Odor Responses

PN axons have two targets in the fly brain with complementary properties. In the mushroom body calyx their connections to KCs are random, as described above. In the lateral horn, by contrast, the axons of each PN type branch in a stereotyped pattern that is the same from fly to fly, and PNs conveying odors of similar behavioral significance (food odors, or pheromones) terminate in overlapping zones (Jefferis et al., 2007). Lateral horn neurons integrate input from particular combinations of PN types and drive innate approach or avoidance. Silencing the mushroom body abolishes learned odor responses but leaves innate responses largely intact (Heimbeck et al., 2001). The division of labor fits the wiring: a stereotyped circuit can carry a meaning that was fixed by evolution, whereas a random circuit can only carry meanings that the individual learns.

The clearest dedicated pathway carries the male pheromone 11-*cis*-vaccenyl acetate (cVA), a **pheromone** that males transfer to females during mating. cVA is detected by ORNs expressing Or67d, which project to the DA1 glomerulus; its PNs are narrowly tuned to cVA and terminate in a distinct part of the lateral horn. In males, cVA inhibits courtship of other males and of mated females; in females, it promotes receptivity (Kurtovic et al., 2007). Replacing Or67d with a moth pheromone receptor makes males respond to the moth pheromone with the same behavior, showing again that the behavioral meaning belongs to the neuron and its connections. The sexually dimorphic circuits downstream of DA1, which depend on the transcription factor Fruitless, are discussed in [7.16.36](/7.16.36).

Mammals show a similar division. Odors with innate significance, such as the predator odor trimethylthiazoline or the trace amines detected by TAARs, are processed partly through the cortical amygdala, and optogenetic activation of the bulb's projections to the cortical amygdala can elicit innate attraction or aversion, whereas activation of the same inputs to piriform cortex has no fixed effect until it is paired with reward or punishment (Root et al., 2014; Choi et al., 2011). Pheromones are detected mainly by the **vomeronasal organ** ([7.14.43](/7.14.43#sec-43.3)), whose projections through the accessory olfactory bulb reach the medial amygdala and hypothalamus without passing through cortex.

## 23.6. Taste

### 23.6.1. Receptor Cells and Labeled Lines

The five basic tastes are detected by distinct receptor cells in the **taste bud**: sweet and umami by T1R heterodimers, bitter by the T2R family, sour by the proton channel OTOP1, and low-concentration salt by ENaC ([7.14.43](/7.14.43#sec-43.4.2)). The organization differs from olfaction in two ways. First, a single bitter-sensing cell expresses many T2Rs, so bitter cells are broadly tuned to bitter compounds yet do not distinguish among them; animals need to detect many poisons but only to reject them all. Second, each modality is carried by its own cell type. Genetic experiments established that the cell, not the receptor, determines the meaning (Zhao et al., 2003; Mueller et al., 2005): mice expressing a human bitter receptor in their bitter cells avoid its ligand, whereas mice expressing the same receptor in their sweet cells prefer it.

At the level of afferent fibers and brainstem neurons the evidence is mixed: many neurons respond best to one taste but also to others. A classical way to quantify this is the breadth of tuning of Smith and Travers (1979). If a neuron's responses to the basic tastes, normalized to sum to one, are $p_1, \dots, p_K$, its breadth is the **entropy** of this profile normalized to the maximal value,

$$
H = -\frac{1}{\log K}\sum_{i=1}^{K}p_i\log p_i, \tag{23.4}
$$
which is 0 for a neuron responding to a single taste and 1 for a neuron responding equally to all.

**Example 23.6.1** A neuron in the nucleus of the solitary tract responds to sucrose, NaCl, HCl and quinine (and not to the fifth taste) with rates $20$, $5$, $3$ and $2\,\mathrm{Hz}$ above baseline. The profile is $p = (2/3, 1/6, 1/10, 1/15, 0)$, and with $K = 5$,

$$
H = -\frac{1}{\ln 5}\Bigl(\tfrac23\ln\tfrac23 + \tfrac16\ln\tfrac16 + \tfrac1{10}\ln\tfrac1{10} + \tfrac1{15}\ln\tfrac1{15}\Bigr) \approx \frac{0.270 + 0.299 + 0.230 + 0.181}{1.609} \approx 0.61.
$$
Such a neuron is "sucrose-best" but far from a labeled line; values between $0.4$ and $0.8$ are typical of brainstem taste neurons in rodents. Labeled-line and across-fiber pattern accounts then differ in what they claim the brain reads out: only the identity of the most active class of neurons, or the whole pattern.

### 23.6.2. The Gustatory Map in Insular Cortex

Two-photon calcium imaging of the primary gustatory cortex in the mouse revealed spatially segregated "hot spots" in the insula enriched in neurons responding to sweet, bitter, umami or salt, several hundred micrometers apart (Chen et al., 2011). Optogenetic activation of the sweet field was attractive to mice, and activation of the bitter field aversive, even in the absence of a tastant; silencing the sweet field reduced the ability to recognize sweet (Peng et al., 2015). These results support a labeled-line organization that extends from receptor cells to cortex, at least for sweet and bitter, the two tastes with the clearest opposite valences. The map is not purely sensory: the insula integrates taste with internal state, and the gustatory cortex projects to the amygdala, which assigns the hedonic value; inactivating these projections can change the valence of a taste without abolishing its identification.

In *Drosophila*, taste receptor neurons on the labellum, legs and wings express gustatory receptors (GRs) and ionotropic receptors; sugar-sensing neurons (expressing Gr5a and Gr64 family receptors) and bitter-sensing neurons (expressing Gr66a and others) project to separate regions of the subesophageal zone and drive proboscis extension or its suppression, a labeled-line design that parallels the mammalian one. As with olfaction, two lineages with unrelated receptor molecules converged on the same logic: broad cells for bitter and other aversive classes, segregated channels for opposite valences, and meaning carried by the identity of the activated neurons.

## Summary

- Odorant receptors (Definition 23.1.1) form the largest gene family of the genome. By the one-neuron–one-receptor rule (Definition 23.1.2), established by stochastic choice and feedback that requires activation to be slow compared with feedback, each receptor defines a channel; axons of a channel converge on one glomerulus, guided by the receptor's own cAMP signal.
- The code is combinatorial. In the occupancy model, Eq. (23.1), receptors are recruited in order of affinity as concentration rises, so identity and intensity are mixed and must be separated by circuits.
- The "trillion odors" estimate is not robust, but mixture discrimination follows Proposition 23.2.1: $d'^2 \propto (1 - f)\operatorname{tr}\Sigma/N$. Large mixtures converge to olfactory white, and many receptor channels keep mixtures apart, unlike the three channels of color vision.
- The olfactory bulb decorrelates similar odors over time; piriform cortex receives random input, keeps activity roughly constant across concentrations, and its ensembles acquire meaning by learning.
- In the fly antennal lobe, divisive normalization (Definition 23.4.1, Eq. (23.3)) equalizes the use of the PN firing range and normalizes for total input, a canonical computation also found in vision and attention.
- The mushroom body (Definition 23.4.2) expands 50 channels into about 2000 sparse, randomly wired Kenyon cells; APL feedback inhibition keeps sparseness constant. Expansion with sparsification decorrelates similar odors for learned readout and is an efficient locality-sensitive hash (Algorithm 23.4.1).
- Stereotyped lateral horn circuits (and the cortical amygdala in mammals) carry innate meanings such as the pheromone cVA; random mushroom body and piriform circuits carry learned ones.
- Taste is organized mainly by labeled lines from receptor cells to the gustatory map of insular cortex; brainstem neurons are more broadly tuned, as measured by the entropy of their response profile, Eq. (23.4).

## Questions

1. In the model of Section 23.1.2, suppose each of $G = 1000$ OR genes is activated as a Poisson event with rate $k$, and the feedback after the first activation takes $\tau = 1\,\mathrm{h}$ to silence the others. What must $k$ be for fewer than 1 % of neurons to express two receptors, and what is then the mean time until the first activation?

   **Answer** We need $1 - \mathrm{e}^{-(G-1)k\tau} < 0.01$, that is, $(G - 1)k\tau < 0.01005$, so $k < 1.0\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{h^{-1}}$ per gene. The first activation among all $G$ genes occurs at total rate $Gk \approx 0.01\,\mathrm{h^{-1}}$, so its mean waiting time is about $100\,\mathrm{h}$, roughly four days. The rule therefore forces OR choice to be a slow, rare event compared with the feedback, consistent with the several days that receptor neurons take to mature.
2. Using Proposition 23.2.1, how must the fraction $1 - f$ of differing components change as $N$ goes from 10 to 30 to keep $d'$ constant? Compare with the mixture figure of Section 23.2.2.

   **Answer** At constant $d'$, $(1 - f)/N$ must be constant, so the fraction of differing components must triple, for example from 0.1 to 0.3. In panel B of that figure, $d' = 1$ is reached at about 0.1 for $N = 10$ and at about 0.3 for $N = 30$, as predicted. Bushdid et al. likewise found that 30-component mixtures needed larger differences to be discriminated than 10-component mixtures.
3. Why does random connectivity in the mushroom body require learning, while the stereotyped connectivity in the lateral horn does not? What would happen to the meaning of a learned odor if the KC–PN connections were rewired after learning?

   **Answer** With random wiring, which KCs respond to an odor differs between individuals and has no relation to the odor's significance, so a fixed downstream circuit cannot interpret the KC code; the output synapses must be adjusted by experience (dopamine-dependent plasticity) to give a particular KC set a meaning. In the lateral horn, PN types end in the same positions in every fly, so downstream neurons can be wired by development to read a fixed meaning. If the KC inputs were rewired after learning, the odor would activate a different set of KCs, whose output synapses had not been modified, and the learned association would be lost even though the PN code was unchanged.
4. Show that divisive normalization, Eq. (23.3), with $n = 1$ and the pool $s$ equal to the sum of all inputs, makes the ratio of the responses of two PNs independent of a common scaling of all ORN inputs when the inputs are large.

   **Answer** If all inputs are scaled by $\lambda$, then $x_i \to \lambda x_i$ and $s \to \lambda s$. For large inputs the constant $\sigma$ is negligible, so $r_i \approx R_{\max}\lambda x_i/(\lambda x_i + m\lambda s) = R_{\max}x_i/(x_i + ms)$, independent of $\lambda$. The ratio $r_1/r_2 = x_1(x_2 + ms)/[x_2(x_1 + ms)]$ is likewise independent of $\lambda$; when $ms$ dominates both $x_i$ it tends to $x_1/x_2$, a relative code. Normalization thus separates the pattern of an odor from its concentration.
5. (Open question) Design an experiment to test whether the sparseness of the KC code is necessary for discriminating similar odors, and predict the outcome from the mushroom body model of Section 23.4.4.

   **Answer** One approach: silence the APL neuron (for example by expressing a temperature-sensitive dynamin allele or a light-gated chloride channel in APL during training and testing) and train flies in an aversive conditioning paradigm with pairs of odors of varying similarity (e.g. mixtures of two odorants in ratios 50:50 versus 40:60). Measure KC responses by calcium imaging to confirm that more KCs respond. Prediction: with denser codes the overlap of the KC representations of similar odors increases (the 20 % curve in panel A of the mushroom body figure lies above the 5 % curve), so generalization between similar odors increases and their discrimination falls, while dissimilar odors, whose codes still overlap little, are learned normally. This is what Lin et al. (2014) found.
