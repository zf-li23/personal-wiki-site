Working memory holds a small amount of information for seconds while it is used: a phone number between reading and dialing, the intermediate result of a mental calculation, the location of a target to which the eyes will move after a delay. It is the interface between perception, long-term memory and action, and its limited capacity is one of the most stable findings of psychology and a strong predictor of general intelligence. This chapter connects the psychology of working memory with its neural substrates and its models. We begin with the behavioral limits and Baddeley's model, turn to the delay-period activity of prefrontal neurons, and then develop two classes of mechanisms: persistent activity sustained by recurrent excitation, which uses the discrete and continuous attractors of [7.16.43](/7.16.43) and [7.16.44](/7.16.44), and "activity-silent" storage in the short-term facilitation of synapses, which uses the **Tsodyks–Markram model** of [7.16.12](/7.16.12#sec-12.5). The synaptic theory gives a quantitative account of the capacity of working memory and of how the order of items can be manipulated. The classification of memory systems is given in [7.14.46](/7.14.46#sec-46.5.3) and [7.16.38](/7.16.38); the role of working memory in decisions follows in [7.16.47](/7.16.47).

## 45.1. Short-Term and Working Memory in Psychology

### 45.1.1. Duration and Capacity

How long does information last in short-term memory without rehearsal? Peterson and Peterson (1959) presented three consonants (such as FZL) and then a number from which the participant counted backward by threes, to prevent rehearsal, until a recall signal after 3 to 18 s. Recall fell from about 80% after $3\,\mathrm{s}$ to about 12% after $18\,\mathrm{s}$. Much of this loss is due to interference from earlier lists rather than to time alone (Keppel and Underwood, 1962: on the first trial, recall after $18\,\mathrm{s}$ is almost perfect), but without rehearsal, unattended information is lost within seconds to tens of seconds.

How much can be held? Miller (1956) summarized digit span and absolute-judgment experiments as "the magical number seven, plus or minus two", and introduced *chunking*: the grouping of items into larger meaningful units, so that the span is limited in chunks rather than in letters or digits. A string such as 1-3-8-1-1-9-0-5-8-2-9 is hard to hold; grouped as a familiar telephone number it is three chunks. Later work, controlling for chunking and rehearsal, put the capacity lower, at about four chunks (Cowan, 2001). In the change-detection task of Luck and Vogel (1997), observers saw an array of colored squares for $100\,\mathrm{ms}$ and, after a delay of about a second, a second array in which one square might have changed color; accuracy was nearly perfect for up to three or four squares and then declined, and squares with several features (color and orientation) were remembered as well as squares with one, suggesting that the unit of storage is an object. Whether capacity is a fixed number of discrete "slots" or a continuous resource shared among items, so that precision falls gradually as more items are stored, is debated; the precision of recall, measured by asking observers to report a remembered color on a continuous wheel, declines smoothly with the number of items (Bays and Husain, 2008), which favors resource models or hybrids.

### 45.1.2. Baddeley's Multicomponent Model

Baddeley and Hitch (1974) replaced the passive short-term store of earlier models by a system that holds and manipulates information during complex tasks. Holding a few digits while reasoning slowed reasoning only slightly, which argued against a single store.

**Definition 45.1.1 (Working Memory)** **Working memory** is the limited-capacity system that temporarily maintains and manipulates information in the service of ongoing cognition, such as comprehension, reasoning and planning, over seconds. In Baddeley's model it consists of a *central executive*, an attentional control system, two modality-specific stores, the *phonological loop* (verbal and acoustic material, refreshed by subvocal rehearsal) and the *visuospatial sketchpad* (visual and spatial material), and an *episodic buffer* that binds information from the stores and long-term memory into integrated episodes (added in 2000).

The model is supported by dissociations: articulatory suppression (repeating "the the the") impairs verbal but not spatial span; a concurrent spatial tracking task impairs spatial but not verbal span; and patients with selective deficits of verbal or spatial span exist. The central executive maps onto the functions impaired by damage to the frontal lobes: patients with frontal lesions, of whom Phineas Gage (1848) is the historical example, are impulsive, poor at planning and distractible, deficits of attentional control rather than of storage.

## 45.2. Persistent Activity in Prefrontal Cortex

Jacobsen (1936) found that monkeys with lesions of the prefrontal cortex failed delayed-response tasks, in which they had to remember for a few seconds under which of two cups food had been placed, but not tasks without delay. Fuster and Alexander (1971) recorded in the prefrontal cortex during such a task and found neurons that fired throughout the delay, and in the same year Kubota and Niki reported similar activity. The paradigm that made the activity quantitative is the oculomotor delayed-response task (Funahashi, Bruce and Goldman-Rakic, 1989): while the monkey fixates, a target is flashed at one of eight locations, and after a delay of 1 to 6 s the monkey makes a saccade to the remembered location. Neurons in the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (around the principal sulcus) fire during the delay with a spatial tuning, a "memory field"; the activity ends at the response, and on error trials it decays prematurely.

**Definition 45.2.1 (Persistent Activity)** **Persistent activity** (delay activity) is stimulus-selective neuronal firing that outlasts a transient stimulus and is maintained during a memory delay, in the absence of the stimulus, until the information is used.

Delay tasks separate encoding, maintenance and retrieval in time, and persistent activity is found in many areas: prefrontal and posterior parietal cortex, the frontal eye fields, premotor cortex and subcortical structures such as the thalamus and superior colliculus. In the parametric working memory task of Romo, Brody, Hernández and Lemus (1999), monkeys compared the frequencies of two vibrations of a fingertip separated by a delay of several seconds, and prefrontal neurons encoded the first frequency during the delay in a monotonic (rather than bell-shaped) **rate code**, increasing or decreasing with frequency: a graded memory of a scalar, which requires a continuum of stable states, a **line attractor** (Machens, Romo and Brody, 2005). Persistent activity is also observed in the anterior lateral motor cortex of mice during the delay of a licking task, where optogenetic perturbations showed that it is maintained by a loop with the thalamus and is robust: after a brief silencing it returns to the trajectory it had before (Guo et al., 2017; Li, Daie, Svoboda and Druckmann, 2016), the signature of an attractor.

## 45.3. Persistent Activity as an Attractor

### 45.3.1. Bistability

The simplest mechanism is a population with recurrent excitation strong enough to sustain its own activity. In a firing-rate model,
$$
\tau\frac{\mathrm{d}r}{\mathrm{d}t} = -r + f(wr + I(t)), \tag{45.1}
$$
with a sigmoidal gain $f$, the fixed points satisfy $r = f(wr + I)$. If the recurrent weight $w$ times the maximal slope of $f$ exceeds 1, there are, in a range of inputs, three fixed points: a low (spontaneous) and a high (memory) state, both stable, separated by an unstable one, as in the toggle switch of [7.6.33](/7.6.33#sec-33.3). A brief excitatory input switches the network to the high state, where it stays; an inhibitory input, or a strong nonspecific excitation that drives the population into saturation and synchrony, erases it. A network with several such populations, connected by shared inhibition, is a discrete attractor network that can hold one of several items ([7.16.43](/7.16.43)); with enough selective excitation and moderate inhibition, it can hold several at once.

A: the fixed points are the intersections of $f(wr)$ with the identity line: stable states at $0$ and $100\,\mathrm{Hz}$ (filled) and an unstable state at $50\,\mathrm{Hz}$ (open). B: a $50\,\mathrm{ms}$ excitatory cue switches the population to the high state, which persists without input until an inhibitory pulse returns it to the low state ($\tau = 20\,\mathrm{ms}$). ### 45.3.2. Slow Synapses and Low Rates

Real delay activity is modest, often 10–$50\,\mathrm{Hz}$, and irregular, far below the saturated rates of simple bistable models. Wang (1999) showed in spiking networks that stable persistent states at low rates require slow recurrent excitation. With fast AMPA-receptor synapses, the strong positive feedback needed for bistability makes the high state synchronous and unstable: excitation and inhibition oscillate and the network falls back. The slow decay of **NMDA receptor** currents, about $100\,\mathrm{ms}$, smooths the recurrent input over many spikes, so that asynchronous low-rate states are stable; their saturation provides the nonlinearity. The prediction has been tested in monkeys: iontophoretic blockade of NMDA receptors on dorsolateral prefrontal neurons reduced their delay activity markedly, whereas **AMPA receptor** blockade had smaller effects on delay activity and larger ones on responses to the cue (Wang et al., 2013). This makes the NMDA receptor, already central to plasticity ([7.16.14](/7.16.14)), also central to working memory, and suggests a reason for the cognitive effects of NMDA receptor antagonists such as ketamine ([7.16.52](/7.16.52)).

### 45.3.3. Continuous Attractors and the Precision of Spatial Memory

The memory of a location among a continuum is held by a **ring attractor** ([7.16.44](/7.16.44)): a spiking network with structured excitation between neurons with similar memory fields and broad inhibition sustains a bump at the cued location (Compte, Brunel, Goldman-Rakic and Wang, 2000). The bump diffuses under noise and drifts under imperfections, so the model predicts that errors in the remembered location grow with the delay, with a variance increasing linearly with time, and that the direction of an error can be predicted from the drift of the bump. Both are observed. Memory-guided saccades in monkeys and humans become less precise with longer delays; in prefrontal recordings, the deviation of the decoded bump from the cue predicted the deviation of the saccade on single trials, and pairs of neurons had the M-shaped **noise correlations** of Eq. (44.7) (Wimmer, Nykamp, Constantinidis and Compte, 2014). Multiple items correspond to multiple bumps, which interact: nearby bumps attract and merge, which produces the attraction of remembered locations toward each other, and lateral inhibition limits the number of bumps the network can sustain, which gives a capacity limit and a loss of precision with load (Edin et al., 2009).

## 45.4. Activity-Silent Working Memory

### 45.4.1. Problems with Persistent Activity

Persistent activity is not the whole story. In many recordings, delay activity is weak or present in only a fraction of neurons, or it fades during part of the delay; when information is sustained, it is often carried by brief bursts of gamma activity separated by periods of baseline firing rather than by a constant rate (Lundqvist et al., 2016). Spikes are also metabolically expensive ([7.14.46](/7.14.46#sec-46.7)). In humans, an item that is held in memory but not currently attended can become undecodable from brain activity during the delay, and yet it can be recovered: a pulse of transcranial magnetic stimulation (Rose et al., 2016) or an uninformative visual "ping" (Wolff, Jochim, Akyürek and Stokes, 2017) made the unattended item decodable again. Information can therefore be held in a latent form, from which a nonspecific input reactivates it.

### 45.4.2. The Synaptic Theory

Mongillo, Barak and Tsodyks (2008) proposed that the latent form is the short-term facilitation of synapses. Excitatory synapses between prefrontal pyramidal neurons are facilitating (Wang et al., 2006), unlike the depressing synapses of sensory cortices: the release probability $u$ rises with each spike and decays with a time constant $\tau_{\mathrm{f}}$ of about $1$–$2\,\mathrm{s}$, much longer than the recovery time $\tau_{\mathrm{d}}$ of the resources $x$ ([Definition 12.5.2](/7.16.12#def-12.5.2)). When a population representing an item is briefly activated, its internal synapses facilitate; when the activity stops, the item remains stored for about $\tau_{\mathrm{f}}$ in the elevated $u$, without any spikes.

**Definition 45.4.1 (Activity-Silent Working Memory)** **Activity-silent working memory** is the maintenance of information during a memory delay in a latent, non-spiking form, such as short-term facilitation of synapses or other slow cellular variables, from which the information can be reactivated by a nonspecific input or by the need to use it.

**Formula 45.4.1 (Mean-Field Model of Synaptic Working Memory)** For $P$ excitatory clusters $a = 1, \dots, P$, one per memory item, with synaptic input $h_a$, rate $R_a$ and synaptic variables $u_a, x_a$ of their internal connections, and one inhibitory pool with input $h_I$,
$$
\begin{aligned}
\tau\frac{\mathrm{d}h_a}{\mathrm{d}t} &= -h_a + J_{EE}\,u_ax_aR_a - J_{EI}R_I + I_b + I_a(t), & R_a &= \alpha\ln\bigl(1 + \mathrm{e}^{h_a/\alpha}\bigr),\\
\tau\frac{\mathrm{d}h_I}{\mathrm{d}t} &= -h_I + J_{IE}\sum_aR_a, & R_I &= [h_I]_+,\\
\frac{\mathrm{d}u_a}{\mathrm{d}t} &= \frac{U - u_a}{\tau_{\mathrm{f}}} + U(1 - u_a)R_a, & \frac{\mathrm{d}x_a}{\mathrm{d}t} &= \frac{1 - x_a}{\tau_{\mathrm{d}}} - u_ax_aR_a,
\end{aligned} \tag{45.2}
$$
with synaptic time constant $\tau$ of a few milliseconds, background input $I_b$ and item inputs $I_a(t)$ (Mongillo, Barak and Tsodyks, 2008; Mi, Katkov and Tsodyks, 2017). The effective strength of the recurrent excitation of cluster $a$ is $J_{EE}u_ax_a$.

The behavior depends on the background input, which can be thought of as arousal or task engagement. At low $I_b$, the network has a single stable state of low activity. A loaded item leaves its cluster with high $u$; a weak nonspecific readout input to all clusters then pushes only the cluster with strengthened synapses over threshold, which responds with a brief synchronous burst, a population spike, recalling the item and, through the spikes, refreshing its facilitation. At higher $I_b$, the loaded cluster becomes an oscillator: its effective excitation $J_{EE}ux$ recovers between bursts as $x$ recovers with $\tau_{\mathrm{d}}$ while $u$ remains high, and when it exceeds the threshold for instability, the cluster fires the next population spike. The memory is then held by periodic reactivations, a form of rehearsal, with no persistent firing between them. Both regimes match the bursty, intermittent delay activity seen in prefrontal recordings, and they explain why an unattended item can be silent and yet recoverable.

## 45.5. The Capacity of Working Memory

### 45.5.1. Theta–Gamma Nesting

Why should capacity be about four items? Lisman and Idiart (1995) proposed a timing explanation. In the hippocampus and cortex, gamma oscillations ($30$–$100\,\mathrm{Hz}$) are nested within theta oscillations ($4$–$8\,\mathrm{Hz}$).

**Definition 45.5.1 (Theta–Gamma Coupling)** **Theta–gamma coupling** is the modulation of the amplitude or occurrence of gamma oscillations by the phase of a simultaneous theta oscillation, so that several gamma cycles occur at preferred phases within each theta cycle.

If each item is represented by an assembly that fires in one gamma cycle, and the items fire in turn within each theta cycle, the number of items that can be held is the number of gamma cycles per theta cycle, $T_\theta/T_\gamma$, about $7$ for $6\,\mathrm{Hz}$ theta and $40\,\mathrm{Hz}$ gamma. The theory predicts that items are reactivated in the order of presentation in successive gamma cycles, and that memory scanning time increases with the number of items by one gamma period per item: the slope of the Sternberg memory-scanning task, about $38\,\mathrm{ms}$ per item, is close to a gamma period. Recordings in human hippocampus and cortex have found that gamma power is phase-locked to theta during working memory maintenance, and that load changes the theta–gamma relation (Axmacher et al., 2010). What keeps the assemblies reactivating from one theta cycle to the next, in the original proposal an afterdepolarization of the neurons, is naturally provided by short-term facilitation.

### 45.5.2. Capacity in the Synaptic Theory

In the oscillatory regime of Formula 45.4.1 with several loaded clusters, the clusters compete through the shared inhibition: only one cluster can fire a population spike at a time, and the spike of one cluster briefly suppresses the others. Loaded clusters therefore take turns, producing a periodic sequence of population spikes, one per item, as in the theta–gamma scheme. The number of items that can be maintained follows from two times (Mi, Katkov and Tsodyks, 2017).

**Proposition 45.5.1 (Capacity of Synaptic Working Memory)** Let $T_{\max}$ be the longest interval between successive population spikes of a cluster for which it still reactivates, which is set by the recovery of the synaptic resources and is of the order of $\tau_{\mathrm{d}}$, and let $t_s$ be the minimal interval between population spikes of two different clusters, set by the width of a population spike and of the inhibitory response it triggers, of the order of the synaptic time constant $\tau$. Then the number of items that can be held is approximately
$$
N_{\mathrm{c}} \approx \frac{T_{\max}}{t_s} \propto \frac{\tau_{\mathrm{d}}}{\tau}. \tag{45.3}
$$

The argument is the same as for theta–gamma nesting: each item needs a slot of duration $t_s$ in every cycle, and the cycle cannot be longer than $T_{\max}$, beyond which a cluster's synapses would lose the advantage given by facilitation. A detailed analysis gives $T_{\max}$ as the time at which the synaptic efficacy $ux$ of a cluster, after a population spike, reaches its maximum, and $t_s$ as the sum of the widths of the population spike and the inhibitory pulse and the time for the next cluster to escape from inhibition; both can be estimated from the parameters, and the estimate matches simulations of the mean-field model and of networks of 20 000 integrate-and-fire neurons. With physiological values ($\tau_{\mathrm{d}} \approx 0.2$–$0.5\,\mathrm{s}$, $\tau \approx 5$–$10\,\mathrm{ms}$), the capacity is about 3 to 6 items, close to the behavioral estimates. Capacity is also controlled by the background input: below a critical value no item is maintained, and above it the capacity increases gradually with $I_b$, which links the variability of capacity to arousal and attention.

### 45.5.3. Primacy and Recency

When more items are presented than can be held, which ones survive? In the model, the answer depends on the presentation rate, as in human experiments. When items are presented rapidly, the first items have time to establish their population spikes in the cycle before later ones arrive, and the last items are freshest: both the first and the last items are retained more often than the middle ones, the primacy and recency effects. When items are presented slowly, each new item displaces one of the items held, with roughly equal probability, and the result is a pronounced recency effect, the probability of retaining an item falling geometrically with the number of items presented after it. The serial position effects of free recall ([7.16.43](/7.16.43#sec-43.6)) thus have a working-memory component, in addition to the contributions of long-term storage and context.

## 45.6. Manipulating Working Memory

Working memory is not only storage; its contents can be reordered, updated and prioritized. Huan Luo and colleagues developed a way to manipulate the relative strength of two items held in human working memory. Participants remembered the orientations of two sequentially presented colored bars and, after a delay of several seconds, reported one of them as cued; normally the second item is remembered better (a recency effect). During the delay, two color probes flickered with independent random luminance sequences, which carry no information about the orientations. When the luminance sequences of the two probes were identical, the recency effect was abolished; when the sequence of the probe of the first item led that of the second by about $200\,\mathrm{ms}$, the same order as at encoding, recency was preserved or enhanced, and when it led in the reverse order, recency was abolished or reversed into a primacy effect. A lag of $500\,\mathrm{ms}$ was ineffective. Temporally structured, nonspecific stimulation thus altered which item was remembered better.

A model that combines a continuous attractor for orientation with **short-term synaptic plasticity** and a shared inhibitory pool explains the effect. Each item is stored in the facilitated synapses of a neuronal group of the ring, and the memory strength of an item is its synaptic efficacy $ux$: the later item has the larger efficacy at the end of encoding, hence the recency effect. The weak flickering probes act as noisy inputs to the corresponding groups and occasionally trigger population spikes, by coherence resonance in the excitable network; each population spike refreshes the facilitation of the group that fires and, through the shared inhibition, briefly suppresses the other group.

With independent probe sequences these events are uncorrelated and leave the relative efficacies, and the recency effect, unchanged. With temporally correlated sequences, each fluctuation reaches the two groups in turn, and in the simulations the group that receives it second fires more often: probes in the order of encoding therefore strengthen the second item and enhance recency, and probes in the reversed order strengthen the first item until its efficacy overtakes, which produces primacy. The effect requires that the lag be shorter than the few hundred milliseconds over which a population spike and the inhibition it recruits interact with the other group, which is why $500\,\mathrm{ms}$ is ineffective. The model makes the general point that items held by synaptic variables can be manipulated by the timing of nonspecific inputs, which is how the theta–gamma code reorders sequences, and is consistent with the fast backward **replay** of sequentially memorized items found in human recordings (Huang, Jia, Han and Luo, 2018).

## 45.7. Distraction and Working Memory without Awareness

Working memory is vulnerable to distraction, and its two mechanisms predict different vulnerabilities. Persistent activity in an attractor network is robust to weak distractors, which do not move the network out of its basin, and fragile to strong ones, which switch it; this is the resistance to distractors observed in dorsolateral prefrontal neurons, whose delay activity survives intervening stimuli, in contrast to posterior parietal neurons, whose activity is reset by them (Suzuki and Gottlieb, 2013). Synaptic storage is erased only if the distractor activates the same synapses; a distractor that activates other clusters leaves the facilitation of the memory cluster intact but competes with it during retrieval.

A striking prediction of activity-silent storage is working memory without awareness. Trübutschek et al. (2017) flashed a target square at one of 20 locations, followed by a mask; participants rated its visibility and, after a delay of $2.5$–$4\,\mathrm{s}$, indicated its location. On trials rated as not seen, they still located the target above chance, and magnetoencephalography showed that the unseen target left a brief activation followed by a delay period without decodable persistent activity, with a late reactivation. Conscious perception, in contrast, was associated with sustained activity. In a **CANN** with short-term facilitation, a masked target leaves a weak trace in the synapses at its location; the mask, which activates all neurons, and the nonspecific recall signal then reactivate the location with a probability above chance, without any persistent bump (Trübutschek, Marti, Ueberschär and Dehaene, 2019). In this view, conscious working memory is maintained by persistent activity broadcast across areas ([7.16.49](/7.16.49)), and non-conscious working memory by silent synaptic traces in local circuits.

## Summary

- Short-term memory lasts seconds without rehearsal and holds about four chunks; working memory (Definition 45.1.1) maintains and manipulates information with a central executive and modality-specific stores.
- Prefrontal neurons show stimulus-selective persistent activity (Definition 45.2.1) in delay tasks, with memory fields for locations and monotonic codes for scalar values.
- A population with strong recurrent excitation is bistable (Eq. (45.1)); slow NMDA-receptor excitation stabilizes low-rate persistent states, and NMDA blockade in prefrontal cortex reduces delay activity. Ring attractors store locations; diffusion and drift of the bump explain the growth and direction of errors with delay.
- Activity-silent working memory (Definition 45.4.1) stores items in short-term facilitation (Formula 45.4.1); a nonspecific readout recalls them by population spikes, and at higher background input they are maintained by periodic reactivation.
- Capacity follows from the time available per cycle: $T_\theta/T_\gamma$ for theta–gamma coupling (Definition 45.5.1), $T_{\max}/t_s \propto \tau_{\mathrm{d}}/\tau$ in the synaptic theory (Proposition 45.5.1), with primacy and recency depending on presentation rate.
- Temporally structured nonspecific stimulation can reverse the relative strength of items held in working memory; silent synaptic traces can support working memory without awareness.

| Mechanism | Substrate | Activity during delay | Capacity limit | Readout |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Discrete attractor | Recurrent excitation (NMDA) | Persistent, stimulus selective | Inhibition between assemblies | Continuous |

## Questions

1. In the bistable model of Eq. (45.1) with $f(x) = r_{\max}/(1 + \mathrm{e}^{-(x - \theta)/\sigma})$, show that bistability is impossible if $wr_{\max} < 4\sigma$. Interpret the condition.

   **Answer** The fixed points are intersections of $g(r) = f(wr)$ with the line $r$. A sigmoid crosses a line of slope 1 three times only if its maximal slope exceeds 1. The slope of $f(wr)$ with respect to $r$ is $wf'(wr)$, and the maximum of $f'$ is $r_{\max}/(4\sigma)$, at $x = \theta$. Hence three fixed points require $wr_{\max}/(4\sigma) > 1$. The condition says that the recurrent loop gain, the change in input produced by a change in rate fed back through $w$ times the slope of the gain function, must exceed 1 somewhere: recurrent excitation must be strong enough to amplify its own activity. For the parameters of the figure, $wr_{\max}/(4\sigma) = 120/32 = 3.75$.

2. A ring attractor model of spatial working memory has a bump that diffuses with coefficient $D = 2\,\mathrm{deg^2\,s^{-1}}$. What is the standard deviation of the remembered location after delays of $1$, $3$ and $6\,\mathrm{s}$? How would systematic drift toward a few preferred locations change the error distribution?

   **Answer** $\operatorname{Var} = Dt$ gives standard deviations $\sqrt{2} = 1.4^\circ$, $\sqrt6 = 2.4^\circ$ and $\sqrt{12} = 3.5^\circ$: errors grow as the square root of the delay. With drift toward attracting locations, the errors are no longer centered on the cue: remembered locations are biased toward the attractors, more so at longer delays, and the error distribution depends on the cue position, small near attractors and large between them. Such biases toward oblique or cardinal locations are observed and are evidence for imperfect continuous attractors.

3. In the synaptic theory, the facilitation variable decays as $u(t) = U + (u_0 - U)\mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau_{\mathrm{f}}}$ after loading. With $U = 0.3$, $u_0 = 0.75$ and $\tau_{\mathrm{f}} = 1.5\,\mathrm{s}$, how long does it take for $u$ to fall to $0.5$ and to $0.4$? What does this imply for the maximal silent delay without readout?

   **Answer** The excess $u - U$ decays from $0.45$ with time constant $1.5\,\mathrm{s}$. It falls to $0.2$ ($u = 0.5$) after $t = 1.5\ln(0.45/0.2) = 1.2\,\mathrm{s}$ and to $0.1$ ($u = 0.4$) after $1.5\ln(0.45/0.1) = 2.3\,\mathrm{s}$. A purely silent memory therefore lasts one to a few seconds, of the order of $\tau_{\mathrm{f}}$, before the loaded cluster is no longer distinguishable from the others by a readout of limited selectivity. Longer delays require reactivation, either by readout signals or spontaneous population spikes, each of which refreshes $u$ and restarts the decay; this is why the model needs either attention-driven refreshing or the periodic regime for delays of more than a few seconds.

4. Using Proposition 45.5.1, estimate the change in capacity if a drug prolonged the decay time of excitatory synaptic currents from $8$ to $12\,\mathrm{ms}$ while leaving $\tau_{\mathrm{d}}$ unchanged. What other effect of such a drug would one have to consider?

   **Answer** $N_{\mathrm{c}} \propto \tau_{\mathrm{d}}/\tau$, so the capacity would decrease by a factor of $8/12$, for example from about 5 to about 3 items: population spikes and the inhibition they trigger would become wider, fewer would fit into a cycle. But prolonging the synaptic current also increases the total recurrent excitation per spike and therefore changes the effective background and the threshold for population spikes; if it shifts the network closer to the regime of persistent firing, the mechanism of storage itself could change. The proportionality holds only within the oscillatory regime.

5. (Open question) Design an experiment to distinguish whether an item in working memory is held by persistent activity or by activity-silent synaptic changes in a given cortical area of a mouse.

   **Answer** Record the population during the delay with two-photon calcium imaging or high-density electrodes and test whether the item can be decoded from activity throughout the delay; then perturb. If storage is by persistent activity, a brief optogenetic silencing of the area during the delay, long enough to let any persistent activity die, should erase the memory (unless another area restores it, which can be tested by silencing the loop partner). If storage is synaptic, a brief silencing should spare the memory, because facilitation decays over seconds without spikes, whereas a nonspecific excitatory pulse should transiently make the item decodable (an optogenetic "ping"). A third test uses the time constants: if storage depends on facilitation, manipulating presynaptic calcium buffering or synaptotagmin-7, which mediates facilitation, should shorten the maximal delay without affecting perception. The predictions of the two mechanisms also differ for distractors, as in Section 45.7.
