Psychiatric disorders (schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, depression, anxiety disorders), neurodevelopmental disorders (intellectual disability, autism spectrum disorder) and epilepsy together affect more people than any other class of brain disease, but, unlike the neurodegenerative diseases of [7.16.51](/7.16.51), most of them have no visible lesion. They are diagnosed by symptoms and behavior, and for most of the 20th century their biology was inferred from the drugs that happened to help. Three approaches have changed this: large-scale human genetics, which identifies risk genes without a prior hypothesis; models in animals and cells of the rare mutations with large effects; and computational models that link symptoms to abnormal computations in the circuits of earlier chapters (dopamine and reward learning in [7.16.39](/7.16.39), E–I networks in [7.16.15](/7.16.15)). This chapter presents the established mechanisms and the main hypotheses, with their quantitative support; it does not describe diagnosis or treatment protocols. Development of the brain is in [7.5](/7.5) and [7.16.21](/7.16.21); the genetics of complex traits and its methods in [7.4.24](/7.4.24), [7.4.25](/7.4.25) and [7.8.13](/7.8.13).

## 52.1. The Biology of Psychiatric Disorders

### 52.1.1. Genetic Architecture

Twin and family studies show that psychiatric disorders are highly heritable: the heritability of liability is about $0.8$ for schizophrenia and autism, $0.6$–$0.8$ for bipolar disorder, $0.7$–$0.8$ for attention deficit hyperactivity disorder and about $0.4$ for major depression. Yet none is inherited in a Mendelian way. The model that reconciles these observations is the threshold model of [Definition 24.6.1](/7.4.24#def-24.6.1): an underlying continuous **liability**, the sum of many genetic and environmental contributions, and disease when the liability exceeds a threshold set by the prevalence.

**Formula 52.1.1 (Liability-Threshold Model)** Let the liability $L \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ in the population, with heritability $h^2$, and let individuals with $L > T$ be affected, where $T$ satisfies $\mathbb{P}(L > T) = K$, the prevalence. If the liabilities of two relatives have correlation $r$ (for additive genetic effects without shared environment, $r = h^2$ for monozygotic twins, $h^2/2$ for first-degree relatives, $h^2/8$ for first cousins), the risk of a relative of an affected person is
$$
\mathbb{P}(L_2 > T \mid L_1 > T) = \frac{1}{K}\,\mathbb{P}(L_1 > T,\ L_2 > T), \tag{52.1}
$$
computed from the bivariate normal distribution with correlation $r$.

For schizophrenia ($K \approx 1\%$, $h^2 \approx 0.8$), Eq. (52.1) gives a risk of about $38\%$ for the monozygotic co-twin and about $9\%$ for a sibling, close to the observed probandwise concordance (about $33$–$50\%$ in different studies) and sibling risk (about $9$–$10\%$); the risk falls steeply with relatedness because the threshold lies far in the tail (figure below). The same model predicts that most affected people have no affected relative, as observed.

![The liability-threshold model (Formula 52.1.1). B: risk in relatives of an affected person against heritability, on a logarithmic axis, for monozygotic twins, first-degree relatives and first cousins. **Genome-wide association studies** ([7.4.25](/7.4.25)) have found the variants. The largest study of schizophrenia (Trubetskoy and colleagues, 2022) identified 287 associated loci, each with a tiny effect (odds ratios mostly $1.05$–$1.2$); together, common variants account for about a quarter of the variance in liability, and a **polygenic score** explains about $7\%$ of it in people of European ancestry. Rare variants have larger effects: **copy-number variants** such as the deletion at 22q11.2, which raises the risk of schizophrenia about 20-fold (about a quarter of carriers develop psychosis), and duplications and deletions at 16p11.2, 1q21.1 and 3q29, which raise the risk of schizophrenia, autism and intellectual disability alike; and rare protein-truncating variants in about a dozen genes (*SETD1A*, *GRIN2A*, *CUL1* and others; Singh and colleagues, 2022). Two lessons follow. The genetic risk is shared across disorders (genetic correlations between schizophrenia and bipolar disorder are about $0.7$), so diagnostic categories do not map onto distinct biology. And the risk genes are enriched in synaptic proteins and in genes expressed in neurons, particularly glutamate receptor subunits, calcium channels and the **postsynaptic density**.

### 52.1.2. Pharmacological Clues

The first effective drugs were found by chance: chlorpromazine (1952) for psychosis, lithium (1949) for mania, iproniazid and imipramine (1950s) for depression, benzodiazepines (1960s) for anxiety. Their targets suggested the first hypotheses, **dopamine** for psychosis, monoamines for depression, GABA for anxiety, which are partly right and partly an artifact of where the search started.

## 52.2. Schizophrenia

Schizophrenia has a lifetime prevalence of about $0.7\%$ and typically begins in late adolescence or early adulthood. Its symptoms are grouped as positive (hallucinations, delusions, disordered thought), negative (reduced motivation, emotion and speech) and cognitive (deficits of **working memory** and attention); cognitive impairment best predicts outcome.

**Definition 52.2.1 (Dopamine Hypothesis of Schizophrenia)** The **dopamine hypothesis of schizophrenia** holds that excessive dopamine signaling through D2 receptors in the striatum causes the positive symptoms. In its revised form (Howes and Kapur, 2009), the abnormality is an increased presynaptic dopamine synthesis and release in the associative striatum, which is the final common pathway of many genetic and environmental risk factors.

The evidence is that the clinical potency of antipsychotic drugs correlates linearly with their affinity for D2 receptors over three orders of magnitude (Seeman and colleagues, 1976; Creese, Burt and Snyder, 1976), that amphetamine, which releases **dopamine**, induces psychosis in healthy people and worsens it in patients, and that PET shows increased dopamine synthesis capacity in patients and in people at high risk before the first episode. In the reinforcement-learning terms of [7.16.39](/7.16.39), phasic dopamine signals **reward prediction errors**, which assign *salience* to events; the *aberrant salience* hypothesis (Kapur, 2003) proposes that chaotic dopamine release attributes significance to irrelevant stimuli and thoughts, and that delusions are the patient's explanations of these experiences. Antipsychotics reduce positive symptoms but have little effect on negative and cognitive symptoms, which points to other mechanisms.

The *glutamate hypothesis* addresses them: the **NMDA receptor** antagonists phencyclidine and ketamine produce in healthy people not only psychotic symptoms but also negative and cognitive ones, and autoimmune encephalitis with antibodies against NMDA receptors begins with psychosis. Hypofunction of NMDA receptors on parvalbumin interneurons would reduce inhibition and gamma oscillations ([7.16.15](/7.16.15#sec-15.5)), which are abnormal in patients, and disinhibit cortical projections that drive striatal dopamine, linking the two hypotheses. *GRIN2A*, encoding an NMDA receptor subunit, is among the genes with rare damaging variants.

The strongest common-variant association lies in the major histocompatibility complex, and Sekar and colleagues (2016) traced much of it to structural variation of the complement component 4 genes *C4A* and *C4B*: alleles that produce more C4A raise the risk in proportion to its expression, and C4 expression is higher in patients' brains. In the developing mouse brain, C4 and the complement components C1q and C3 tag synapses that are then engulfed by microglia (Stevens and colleagues, 2007; Schafer and colleagues, 2012), a mechanism of activity-dependent refinement ([7.16.21](/7.16.21)) that uses the machinery of innate immunity ([7.15.10](/7.15.10)). The cortex loses a large fraction of its synapses during adolescence, and patients have fewer dendritic spines in prefrontal cortex; excessive complement-dependent pruning during adolescence would explain the age of onset and the reduced cortical thickness, although this causal chain is not yet proven in humans.

## 52.3. Mood Disorders

Major depression has a lifetime prevalence of about $15$–$20\%$ and is a leading cause of disability worldwide; bipolar disorder (about $1\%$) adds episodes of mania. The *monoamine hypothesis* arose from the observations that reserpine, which depletes vesicular monoamines, can cause depression, and that drugs which increase synaptic **serotonin** and **norepinephrine**, by inhibiting monoamine oxidase or the reuptake transporters, relieve it. Its central difficulty is timing: selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors block the serotonin transporter within hours, but their clinical effect takes weeks, and depletion of monoamines does not cause depression in healthy people. The delay suggests that the drugs act through slow adaptations, such as receptor desensitization, neurotrophic signaling through BDNF, adult hippocampal neurogenesis (required for some behavioral effects of antidepressants in mice; Santarelli and colleagues, 2003) and synaptic plasticity.

The **NMDA receptor** antagonist ketamine, at doses below those used for anesthesia, relieves depression within hours and for about a week, including in patients resistant to other drugs (Berman and colleagues, 2000; Zarate and colleagues, 2006). Several mechanisms have experimental support: blocking NMDA receptors on inhibitory interneurons disinhibits pyramidal cells, causing a glutamate surge that activates **AMPA receptors**, BDNF release and mTOR signaling and restores dendritic spines in prefrontal cortex lost after chronic stress (Li and colleagues, 2010); and in the lateral habenula, a nucleus that signals negative outcomes and inhibits **dopamine** neurons, depression-like states in rodents are associated with NMDA receptor–dependent burst firing that ketamine blocks (Yang and colleagues, 2018). Ketamine thus shows that rapid antidepressant action is possible and places synaptic plasticity, not monoamine levels, at the center of current hypotheses.

*Computational psychiatry* describes symptoms as abnormal parameters of models that also explain normal behavior. Anhedonia, the reduced capacity for pleasure, can arise in a reinforcement-learning model either from a reduced *reward sensitivity* $\rho$ (rewards are worth less) or from a reduced learning rate $\alpha$; the two make different predictions (figure below). Fitting such models to the choices of patients in reward-learning tasks, Huys and colleagues (2013) found that anhedonia and depression were associated with reduced reward sensitivity rather than impaired learning. In **distributional reinforcement learning** ([7.16.50](/7.16.50#sec-50.6)), an imbalance between the learning rates for positive and negative prediction errors shifts all value predictions toward the pessimistic tail, a possible formal counterpart of the negative bias of depressed patients.

## 52.4. Anxiety and GABAergic Inhibition

Anxiety disorders are the most prevalent psychiatric disorders (lifetime prevalence near $30\%$). Fear and anxiety circuits centered on the **amygdala**, and their learning in **fear conditioning** and extinction, are described in [7.16.37](/7.16.37). Benzodiazepines, the classical anxiolytics, are positive allosteric modulators of GABA$_{\mathrm{A}}$ receptors: they bind at the interface of the $\alpha$ and $\gamma2$ subunits and increase the frequency of channel opening in the presence of GABA ([7.16.10](/7.16.10)), enhancing inhibition. Genetics separated their effects. In knock-in mice in which a histidine-to-arginine point mutation makes one $\alpha$ subunit insensitive to benzodiazepines, diazepam lost its sedative effect when the mutation was in $\alpha1$ and its anxiolytic effect when it was in $\alpha2$ (Rudolph and colleagues, 1999; Löw and colleagues, 2000). This dissection of a drug's actions by receptor subtype is the logic of selective drug design for brain disorders: different subunit combinations are expressed in different cells and circuits, and a drug selective for $\alpha2$- and $\alpha3$-containing receptors could relieve anxiety without sedation and dependence.

## 52.5. Intellectual Disability and Autism

**Definition 52.5.1 (Autism Spectrum Disorder)** **Autism spectrum disorder** is a neurodevelopmental disorder defined by persistent deficits in social communication and interaction, together with restricted, repetitive patterns of behavior, interests or activities (including atypical sensory responses), present from early childhood.

Autism is diagnosed in about 1 in 36 children in the United States (2020 data), about four times more often in boys; its heritability is about $0.8$. About a third of autistic people also have intellectual disability (an IQ below about 70 with deficits of adaptive function, affecting $1$–$3\%$ of the population). Unlike schizophrenia, autism and intellectual disability have a large contribution from *de novo* mutations, present in the child but not the parents: sequencing of families has identified more than 100 genes in which de novo damaging mutations raise the risk greatly, such as *CHD8*, *SCN2A*, *SYNGAP1* and *SHANK3* (Satterstrom and colleagues, 2020). The genes fall into two main groups, regulators of gene expression and chromatin, and synaptic proteins, and many cause both autism and intellectual disability. Several single-gene syndromes have become models for the field.

### 52.5.1. Rett Syndrome and the Reversibility of MeCP2 Loss

**Definition 52.5.2 (Rett Syndrome)** **Rett syndrome** is an X-linked neurodevelopmental disorder, affecting about 1 in 10 000 girls, in which apparently normal development for 6 to 18 months is followed by regression: loss of speech and purposeful hand use, stereotyped hand movements, breathing abnormalities, seizures and intellectual disability; it is caused by loss-of-function mutations in *MECP2*, which encodes the methyl-CpG-binding protein MeCP2.

MeCP2 binds methylated DNA (**DNA methylation**, at CpG and, in neurons, also at CA dinucleotides) and is about as abundant in mature neurons as histone octamers; it regulates the expression of many genes by small amounts rather than switching a few genes on or off. Affected girls are mosaics, because random X inactivation silences the mutant allele in about half of their cells; boys with null mutations usually die in infancy. Duplication of *MECP2* also causes a severe disorder, so neurons require the right dose. Neurons in Rett syndrome do not degenerate; they are smaller, with fewer dendritic branches and spines. Guy, Gan, Selfridge, Cobb and Bird (2007) made mice in which *Mecp2* was silenced by a removable stop cassette; they developed Rett-like symptoms, and when the gene was reactivated in adult, symptomatic mice by tamoxifen-induced Cre recombinase, the symptoms were largely reversed. The experiment showed that a neurodevelopmental disorder is not necessarily an irreversible defect of construction: the circuits had formed adequately, and their function depended on continued MeCP2. Similar rescue in adulthood was later obtained for *Shank3* (Mei and colleagues, 2016) and *Syngap1* mouse models, in part, which has motivated gene therapy approaches in which the dosage sensitivity of *MECP2* is the main challenge.

### 52.5.2. Fragile X Syndrome and the mGluR Theory

**Definition 52.5.3 (Fragile X Syndrome)** **Fragile X syndrome** is the most common inherited cause of intellectual disability (about 1 in 4000 males), often with autism; it is caused by expansion of a CGG repeat in the 5′ untranslated region of *FMR1* to more than 200 repeats (full mutation), which leads to methylation and silencing of the gene and loss of its product FMRP, an RNA-binding protein that represses the translation of many neuronal mRNAs.

Normal alleles have fewer than about 45 repeats; premutations of 55–200 are unstable and expand in transmission through the mother (**trinucleotide repeat expansion**, [7.16.51](/7.16.51#sec-51.4)). FMRP binds mRNAs of synaptic proteins and represses their translation at synapses; its loss produces an excess of long, thin, immature spines. Bear, Huber and Warren (2004) proposed that the core defect is exaggerated protein synthesis downstream of group I metabotropic glutamate receptors (mGluR1/5): mGluR-dependent LTD in the hippocampus, which normally requires new protein synthesis, is enhanced in *Fmr1* knockout mice and no longer requires it, because FMRP normally restrains the translation that mGluR activation stimulates. Reducing mGluR5 signaling genetically (by crossing in a heterozygous *Grm5* mutation) or pharmacologically corrected many phenotypes of the mice, including spine abnormalities, audiogenic seizures and abnormal learning (Dölen and colleagues, 2007). Clinical trials of mGluR5 antagonists in adolescents and adults, however, did not improve behavior. The failure is instructive: the mouse phenotypes measured may not correspond to the human symptoms, treatment may have to begin earlier in development, or tolerance may develop; it underlines the gap between correcting a mechanism in a model and treating a disorder.

### 52.5.3. Synapses and E–I Balance

Many autism genes encode proteins of the excitatory synapse: the cell adhesion molecules neurexins and neuroligins, which bridge presynaptic and postsynaptic membranes, and the scaffold proteins SHANK1–3 of the **postsynaptic density**. *Shank3* mutant mice show excessive self-grooming (a repetitive behavior), social deficits and defects of corticostriatal synapses (Peça and colleagues, 2011). A unifying hypothesis is that autism involves an increased ratio of excitation to inhibition in cortical circuits (Rubenstein and Merzenich, 2003): about a third of autistic people have epilepsy, many risk genes affect inhibitory interneurons or ion channels, and optogenetically increasing the E–I ratio in mouse prefrontal cortex, by exciting pyramidal cells, impaired social behavior, while increasing inhibition partly rescued it (Yizhar and colleagues, 2011). In the terms of [7.16.15](/7.16.15#sec-15.7), a change of the **balanced state** alters gain, noise and the dynamic range of cortical responses; it may explain the atypical sensory sensitivity of many autistic people. Different mutations shift the balance in opposite directions, however, and many changes in mouse models appear to be homeostatic compensation rather than primary defects, so "E–I imbalance" names a level of description, not a single mechanism.

## 52.6. Epilepsy

### 52.6.1. A Disorder of Network Excitability

**Definition 52.6.1 (Seizure and Epilepsy)** A **seizure** is a transient occurrence of signs or symptoms due to abnormal excessive or synchronous neuronal activity in the brain. **Epilepsy** is a disease characterized by an enduring predisposition to generate unprovoked seizures. Seizures are *focal* when they begin in a network limited to one hemisphere and *generalized* when they begin in bilaterally distributed networks.

About 50 million people have epilepsy (prevalence about $0.5$–$1\%$), and about a third of them have seizures that are not controlled by drugs. At the cellular level, the signature of a focus is the *paroxysmal depolarizing shift*, a large, long depolarization with a burst of action potentials, which appears synchronously in many neurons as an interictal spike in the EEG; a seizure is the spread and self-sustained reverberation of such activity. Generalized absence seizures, brief losses of awareness with $3\,\mathrm{Hz}$ spike-and-wave discharges, arise in the thalamocortical loop from the rebound bursts of thalamic neurons mediated by T-type calcium channels, the same mechanism that generates sleep spindles ([7.16.35](/7.16.35)); ethosuximide, which blocks T-type channels, is specific for absence seizures.

Mutations in ion channels (channelopathies) cause many genetic epilepsies, and their effects depend on which cells express the channel. Loss-of-function mutations in *KCNQ2* and *KCNQ3*, the subunits of the **M-current** ([7.16.4](/7.16.4)), cause benign familial neonatal seizures by increasing excitability. Paradoxically, loss-of-function mutations of *SCN1A*, encoding the sodium channel Na$_{\mathrm{V}}$1.1, cause Dravet syndrome, a severe epilepsy of infancy: Na$_{\mathrm{V}}$1.1 is the main sodium channel of parvalbumin-expressing inhibitory interneurons, whose fast firing fails, so the loss of a sodium channel disinhibits the network (Yu and colleagues, 2006), and sodium channel–blocking drugs worsen this syndrome. Antiseizure drugs act on the same targets in reverse: use-dependent block of sodium channels (phenytoin, carbamazepine, which bind preferentially to inactivated channels and therefore suppress high-frequency firing more than normal activity), enhancement of GABA$_{\mathrm{A}}$ receptors (benzodiazepines, barbiturates), block of T-type calcium channels, and binding to the synaptic vesicle protein SV2A (levetiracetam). Acquired epilepsies after brain injury develop through *epileptogenesis*, a period of months to years of cell loss, axonal sprouting (for example of the mossy fibers in the dentate gyrus) and changes of channels and inhibition, as modeled in animals by kindling: repeated brief stimulation that eventually produces spontaneous seizures.

### 52.6.2. The Dynamics of Seizure Onset and Offset

The transitions into and out of a seizure are qualitative changes of the dynamics of a network, which the bifurcation theory of [7.16.7](/7.16.7) classifies. In the simplest picture, an E–I network ([Definition 15.4.1](/7.16.15#def-15.4.1)) whose excitatory gain or recurrent excitation increases passes a bifurcation from a stable fixed point to a large-amplitude oscillation, the seizure. Because seizures start and stop spontaneously, a slow variable must move the network back and forth across the bifurcations, as in **bursting** neurons, where a slow current moves a fast spiking subsystem between rest and oscillation ([7.16.7](/7.16.7)).

**Formula 52.6.1 (Fast–Slow Model of Seizures)** Let the fast subsystem be the normal form of a subcritical Hopf bifurcation with a fold of limit cycles,
$$
\dot z = g\bigl(\mu + \lvert z\rvert^2 - \lvert z\rvert^4\bigr)z + \mathrm{i}\omega z, \qquad \dot\mu = \varepsilon\bigl(a - \lvert z\rvert^2\bigr), \tag{52.2}
$$
where $z$ is the complex amplitude of the network oscillation (its real part the recorded signal), $\mu$ a slow excitability variable (such as extracellular $\ce{K+}$ or energy reserves), $\varepsilon \ll g$, and $0 < a < 1/2$. For $\mu < -1/4$ only rest is stable, for $-1/4 < \mu < 0$ rest and a large limit cycle coexist (bistability), and for $\mu > 0$ only the cycle is stable. At rest $\mu$ rises at rate $\varepsilon a$ until the rest state loses stability (seizure onset at the Hopf bifurcation, or earlier by noise); during the seizure $\mu$ falls, since $\lvert z\rvert^2 > a$ on the cycle, until the cycle disappears in the fold (seizure offset).

This is the logic of the Epileptor (Jirsa, Stacey, Quilichini, Ivanov and Bernard, 2014), a five-variable model with a fast subsystem that produces the discharges, a second one that produces spike-and-wave events, and a slow "permittivity" variable whose rise and fall drive seizures in and out. Jirsa and colleagues found that the most common type of seizure in patients and in animal models (mouse, zebrafish, human) had the signatures of onset by a saddle-node bifurcation (a jump in baseline with constant frequency) and offset by a saddle homoclinic bifurcation (frequency slowing to zero as the seizure ends, with constant amplitude). Different bifurcation pairs leave different signatures on the recorded signal: amplitude growing as $\sqrt{\mu}$ from zero at a supercritical Hopf onset, frequency decreasing logarithmically toward a homoclinic offset, and a taxonomy of 16 classes of fast–slow bursters (Saggio and colleagues, 2020) provides a framework for classifying seizures from recordings. Model-based approaches also guide surgery: patient-specific brain network models built from the individual's **connectome** and fitted to intracranial recordings ("virtual epileptic patients") are being tested to predict the epileptogenic zone and the effect of resections.

A: the bifurcation diagram of the fast subsystem against the slow excitability $\mu$: stable rest for $\mu < 0$, stable large oscillation for $\mu > -1/4$ and an unstable cycle between them; the trajectory (thin line) circulates around the hysteresis loop, jumping up near $\mu = 0$ and down at $\mu = -1/4$. B: the signal $\mathrm{Re}\,z$ (blue) and $\mu$ (red; dotted lines at the two bifurcations): two seizures, each beginning abruptly with full amplitude when $\mu$ reaches the Hopf point and ending abruptly when it reaches the fold.](./plots/seizure-dynamics.py)

The fast–slow picture also gives a link to the **criticality** of [7.16.49](/7.16.49#sec-49.3). If seizures occur when a slowly varying parameter approaches a bifurcation, the network should show critical slowing down beforehand: the recovery from small perturbations becomes slower and the variance and autocorrelation of the signal rise. Such early-warning signals have been reported before some seizures, in recordings over hours to days, and seizure risk follows circadian and multiday cycles that can be forecast from long-term recordings (Baud and colleagues, 2018). Whether a given seizure begins by a bifurcation, where warning signs exist, or by a noise-driven jump in the bistable region, where they need not, is a question about the dynamics that the model makes explicit.

## Summary

- Psychiatric disorders are highly heritable but polygenic; the liability-threshold model (Formula 52.1.1) explains high twin concordance with steeply falling risk in more distant relatives. GWAS loci of tiny effect, copy-number variants and rare damaging variants converge on synaptic genes and are shared across disorders.
- Schizophrenia: excess striatal dopamine underlies positive symptoms (Definition 52.2.1), NMDA receptor hypofunction contributes to negative and cognitive ones, and complement C4–mediated synaptic pruning links genetics to adolescent onset.
- Mood disorders: the delayed action of monoamine drugs and the rapid action of ketamine point to synaptic plasticity; computational models attribute anhedonia to reduced reward sensitivity.
- Anxiolysis and sedation by benzodiazepines are mediated by different GABA$_{\mathrm{A}}$ receptor subtypes.

| Disorder | Main genetic architecture | Key mechanism | Lesson |
|---|---|---|---|
| Schizophrenia | Polygenic, CNVs (22q11.2), *C4A* | Dopamine excess, NMDA hypofunction, pruning | Drugs find the symptoms, genes find the causes |
| Depression | Polygenic, $h^2 \approx 0.4$ | Plasticity, reward sensitivity | Rapid ketamine effect |
| Autism, ID | De novo mutations, ~100 genes | Synapse and chromatin genes, E–I balance | Mechanisms converge, phenotypes diverge |
| Rett syndrome | *MECP2* (X-linked) | Dosage of a chromatin regulator | Reversible in adult mice |
| Fragile X | *FMR1* CGG expansion | Excess mGluR-dependent translation | Mouse rescue did not translate |
| Epilepsy | Channelopathies, acquired | Network excitability, bifurcations | Cell type determines the effect of a mutation |

- Autism (Definition 52.5.1), Rett syndrome (Definition 52.5.2) and fragile X syndrome (Definition 52.5.3) involve synaptic and chromatin genes; adult reactivation of *Mecp2* reverses symptoms in mice.
- Epilepsy (Definition 52.6.1) results from imbalances of excitation and inhibition, often through channels in specific cell types (Dravet syndrome). Seizure onset and offset are bifurcations of a fast subsystem driven by a slow variable (Formula 52.6.1; the Epileptor).

## Questions

1. Using Formula 52.1.1 with $K = 1\%$, what would the monozygotic concordance be if the heritability of schizophrenia were $0.4$ instead of $0.8$? What does the comparison imply about inferring heritability from concordance?

   **Answer** The threshold is $T = 2.33$. For $r = 0.8$, the bivariate normal gives $\mathbb{P}(L_1 > T, L_2 > T) \approx 0.0038$, so the concordance (risk of the co-twin) is about $38\%$. For $r = 0.4$ the joint probability falls to about $0.0009$, a concordance of about $9\%$. Concordance is a steeply nonlinear function of the liability correlation when the prevalence is low, so a moderate concordance (for example $38\%$) implies a high heritability of liability, and small changes in concordance correspond to large differences in $h^2$; conversely, the fact that about $60\%$ of co-twins remain unaffected does not by itself imply a large environmental component of the kind that a linear intuition suggests. The model also assumes that liability is normal and that the environment is not shared, both of which must be checked.

2. In the bandit model of the figure in 52.3, show that the asymptotic value difference between the options is proportional to $\rho$ and independent of $\alpha$, and explain how this lets one separate the two parameters from choice data.

   **Answer** At stationarity, the expected change of $q_k$ is zero when $q_k = \rho\,\mathbb{E}[r \mid k] = \rho p_k$ (the delta rule converges in mean to the expected scaled reward of the chosen option, independently of how often it is chosen, as long as it is sampled). The difference is $\rho(p_1 - p_2) = 0.5\rho$, independent of $\alpha$, which only sets the speed of convergence (the time constant is about $1/(\alpha\,\mathbb{P}(\text{choose }k))$ trials) and the size of the fluctuations around the mean. Choice probability through the softmax depends on $\beta\rho(p_1 - p_2)$, so the asymptotic preference identifies the product $\beta\rho$, while the learning curve identifies $\alpha$. (The product $\beta\rho$ cannot be separated by choices alone: reduced reward sensitivity and increased choice randomness are equivalent; additional measures, such as ratings of rewards or neural responses, are needed.)

3. Explain why a loss-of-function mutation of a sodium channel can cause epilepsy, and why sodium channel blockers then worsen the seizures.

   **Answer** Network excitability depends on the balance of excitation and inhibition, not on the excitability of all neurons together. Na$_{\mathrm{V}}$1.1 is the dominant sodium channel in parvalbumin-positive interneurons (and in some other inhibitory cells), and these interneurons fire at high rates that require a large density of rapidly recovering sodium channels. With one copy of *SCN1A* lost, they fail to sustain fast firing, while excitatory neurons, which rely mostly on Na$_{\mathrm{V}}$1.2 and Na$_{\mathrm{V}}$1.6, are little affected; inhibition fails first, and the network becomes hyperexcitable. Sodium channel blockers reduce the remaining sodium current further, again affecting most the cells with the least reserve, the interneurons, so they aggravate the imbalance.

4. In Formula 52.6.1, compute the durations of the seizure and of the interval between seizures, neglecting the fast transients and noise, for $\varepsilon = 0.02\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$ and $a = 0.4$. How could the model explain why a drug that speeds the drift of the slow variable during seizures shortens them?

   **Answer** Between seizures $\lvert z\rvert^2 \approx 0$, so $\dot\mu = \varepsilon a = 0.008\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$; $\mu$ must rise from $-1/4$ to $0$, which takes $0.25/0.008 \approx 31\,\mathrm{s}$. During a seizure the state is on the large cycle, $\lvert z\rvert^2 = (1 + \sqrt{1 + 4\mu})/2$, which falls from $1$ at $\mu = 0$ to $1/2$ at $\mu = -1/4$; with $\dot\mu = \varepsilon(a - \lvert z\rvert^2)$ between $-0.012$ and $-0.002\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$, integrating $\mathrm{d}\mu/\dot\mu$ from $0$ to $-1/4$ gives about $32\,\mathrm{s}$ (the descent slows as the cycle shrinks), close to the simulated seizures. In the model, seizure duration is the time for the slow variable to travel across the bistable range, so it can be shortened by making the slow variable move faster during the seizure or by narrowing the bistable range; a treatment that moves the slow variable more slowly at rest, or shifts the onset bifurcation to higher values, lengthens the interval between seizures. The model thus separates the parameters controlling frequency and duration, which can be estimated from recordings.
