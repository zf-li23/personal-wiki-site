The body surface and the interior of the body are monitored by the somatosensory system: touch, vibration, stretch and body position, warmth and cold, pain and itch. All of these begin in a single class of cells, the primary sensory neurons of the dorsal root and trigeminal ganglia, whose peripheral endings are the transducers. The variety of somatic sensations therefore raises two questions that this chapter follows. At the periphery: which molecules convert force and temperature into current, and how are the many types of sensory neurons specialized? Centrally: are the qualities of sensation carried by dedicated lines, or computed by comparing the activity of several types? The receptor organs of the skin, their adaptation and viscoelastic filtering, the ascending pathways and the basic physiology of pain, temperature and itch are covered in [7.14.39](/7.14.39) and [7.14.40](/7.14.40); the structure and gating of Piezo channels in [7.6.25](/7.6.25#sec-25.6.2) and the thermodynamics of two-state gating in [7.6.4](/7.6.4). Here we add the genetic discoveries, the quantitative models of thermal gating and texture coding, and a circuit model of the spinal gate for pain that explains rubbing, allodynia and wind-up within one framework. The encoding concepts used here, tuning and the comparison of neural and behavioral discrimination, are developed in [7.16.16](/7.16.16) and [7.16.26](/7.16.26).

## 25.1. Somatosensory Neurons

### 25.1.1. Classes of Primary Afferents

Dorsal root ganglion (DRG) neurons are pseudounipolar: one axon branches into a peripheral process ending in the skin, muscle or viscera and a central process entering the spinal cord. The classical classification is by axon diameter and myelination, which determine conduction velocity. For myelinated fibers, velocity grows roughly in proportion to diameter, about $6\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ per micrometer of outer diameter (Hursh, 1939), because the internodal distance scales with diameter and the time per internode stays roughly constant; for unmyelinated fibers it grows only as the square root of diameter, as the cable equation predicts ([7.6.30](/7.6.30)).

| Class | Diameter | Conduction velocity | Main modalities |
|---|---|---|---|
| Aα (Ia, Ib) | $12$–$20\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ | $70$–$120\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ | muscle spindles, Golgi tendon organs (proprioception) |
| Aβ | $6$–$12\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ | $35$–$70\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ | low-threshold mechanoreceptors of the skin (touch, vibration) |
| Aδ | $1$–$5\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ | $5$–$30\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ | cold, first (sharp) pain, some hair follicle receptors |
| C | $0.2$–$1.5\,\mu\mathrm{m}$, unmyelinated | $0.5$–$2\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ | warmth, second (burning) pain, itch, pleasant touch |

The difference in velocity explains the two waves of pain after a sudden injury ([7.14.40](/7.14.40#def-40.1.2)): over the $1\,\mathrm{m}$ from foot to spinal cord, an Aδ signal arrives after about $50\,\mathrm{ms}$, a C signal after about $1\,\mathrm{s}$. Molecular methods have refined the classical scheme. Single-cell RNA sequencing of mouse DRG neurons distinguishes about a dozen to two dozen transcriptional types (Usoskin et al., 2015, and later atlases), each with characteristic receptors and channels, such as the low-threshold mechanoreceptors marked by TrkB or Ret, the peptidergic nociceptors expressing CGRP and TRPV1, the nonpeptidergic nociceptors expressing MrgprD, and C-fiber low-threshold mechanoreceptors marked by tyrosine hydroxylase that respond to gentle stroking. Genetic access to these types has made it possible to test which sensations each one is necessary and sufficient for, the logic of the experiments below.

### 25.1.2. Mechanotransduction Channels

The search for mechanically gated channels proceeded through genetics, because these channels are rare and cannot be purified by binding a ligand. Forward genetic screens for touch-insensitive *Caenorhabditis elegans* identified the *mec* genes (Chalfie and colleagues), including *mec-4* and *mec-10*, which encode subunits of a DEG/ENaC sodium channel in the six touch receptor neurons; dominant mutations that hold the channel open cause the touch neurons to swell and degenerate. Screens for *Drosophila* larvae lacking a mechanoreceptor potential in their bristles identified NOMPC (no mechanoreceptor potential C), a channel of the TRP family whose 29 ankyrin repeats form a helical spring that tethers the channel to microtubules; NOMPC is sufficient to confer mechanosensitivity on cells that normally lack it. In mammals, an RNA interference screen in a mechanosensitive cell line identified Piezo1 and Piezo2 (Coste et al., 2010); Piezo2 is the principal transducer of light touch and proprioception in mice and humans ([7.14.39](/7.14.39#sec-39.5.2)).

Two gating mechanisms are realized by these channels. In the "force-from-lipid" mechanism (Piezo), the channel senses membrane tension $\gamma$ directly: if opening increases the in-plane area of the channel by $\Delta A$, the tension does work $\gamma\Delta A$, and the **open probability** is the Boltzmann function $p_{\mathrm{o}} = 1/\{1 + \exp[(\Delta G_0 - \gamma\Delta A)/k_{\mathrm{B}}T]\}$; Piezo's large blades make $\Delta A$ large, so that physiological tensions of a few $\mathrm{mN\,m^{-1}}$ suffice. In the "force-from-tether" mechanism (NOMPC, and the hair cell channel with its tip link, [7.16.24](/7.16.24)), force is delivered through a protein link to the cytoskeleton or extracellular matrix, and the gating energy is the work done by the tether. Both are instances of the same principle: a mechanical variable couples to a conformational change, and the free energy of gating is shifted by force times displacement, exactly as voltage shifts it by charge times potential.

## 25.2. Temperature Sensing by TRP Channels

### 25.2.1. Discovery

Capsaicin, the pungent compound of chili peppers, excites a subset of small DRG neurons and produces a burning pain. Caterina et al. (1997) used this to clone its receptor by expression cloning: they transfected pools of cDNA from DRG neurons into non-neuronal cells and looked for pools that conferred capsaicin-induced calcium influx, then subdivided the positive pool until one clone remained.

**Definition 25.2.1 (TRPV1)** The nonselective cation channel of the transient receptor potential vanilloid family that is activated by capsaicin, by noxious heat above about $43\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$ and by extracellular protons, and that is expressed mainly in nociceptive C and Aδ neurons, is called **TRPV1** (the capsaicin receptor).

The same channel was thus found to respond both to a pungent chemical and to the physical stimulus whose sensation it mimics, which explained why chili feels hot. Mice lacking TRPV1 have reduced responses to capsaicin and to inflammatory heat hyperalgesia, but acute responses to noxious heat are only partly reduced; acute heat sensing is abolished only when TRPV1, TRPM3 and TRPA1 are all deleted (Vandewauw et al., 2018), a striking example of redundancy among sensors. The analogous strategy with menthol identified the cold receptor (McKemy, Neuhausser and Julius, 2002; Peier et al., 2002).

**Definition 25.2.2 (TRPM8)** The nonselective cation channel of the transient receptor potential melastatin family that is activated by cooling below about $25\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$ and by menthol and icilin, and that is expressed in cold-sensitive Aδ and C neurons, is called **TRPM8** (the menthol receptor).

Mice lacking TRPM8 are profoundly deficient in sensing innocuous cool temperatures but still avoid extreme cold, showing that other mechanisms detect noxious cold. Julius shared the 2021 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with Patapoutian, who discovered the Piezo channels.

### 25.2.2. Thermodynamics of Thermal Gating

How can a protein be steeply temperature-dependent? In a **two-state system** with free energy of opening $\Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S$, the equilibrium constant is $K = p_{\mathrm{o}}/(1 - p_{\mathrm{o}}) = \exp(-\Delta G/RT)$, and

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}\ln K}{\mathrm{d}T} = \frac{\Delta H}{RT^2} \tag{25.1}
$$
(the van 't Hoff relation). A steep temperature dependence requires a large enthalpy change, positive for heat-activated and negative for cold-activated channels; since the channel is half open at $T_{1/2} = \Delta H/\Delta S$, a large entropy change of the same sign is needed to keep $T_{1/2}$ in the physiological range ([7.6.4](/7.6.4#sec-4.2.3); [7.14.40](/7.14.40#sec-40.6.2)). The temperature coefficient of the equilibrium follows by integrating Eq. (25.1) over $10\,\mathrm{K}$:

$$
Q_{10} \approx \exp\Bigl(\frac{10\,\mathrm{K}\cdot\Delta H}{RT^2}\Bigr). \tag{25.2}
$$

**Example 25.2.1** For $\Delta H = 280\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ near $T = 316\,\mathrm{K}$ ($43\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$), $10\Delta H/(RT^2) = 2.8\times10^6/(8.314\times9.99\times10^4) \approx 3.37$, so $Q_{10} \approx 29$. A typical enzymatic reaction with an activation enthalpy of $50\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ has $Q_{10} \approx 1.8$. The measured $Q_{10}$ of TRPV1 currents is of the order of 10–40, of TRPM8 currents similar with the opposite sign of $\Delta H$, which is why these channels can act as thermometers.

:::

The large enthalpy and entropy changes are thought to reflect changes in the hydration of many residues or the melting of a structured region when the channel opens, distributed over the protein rather than localized in a single "temperature sensor" domain; mutations in several regions shift the temperature sensitivity, and the issue remains under study.

### 25.2.3. Convergence of Temperature, Voltage and Ligands

Thermosensitive TRP channels are also weakly voltage-dependent, with a gating charge $z$ of less than one elementary charge, and are modulated by ligands. Voets et al. (2004) showed that their gating can be described by a single two-state scheme in which all stimuli add to the free energy of opening. With membrane potential $V$ and a ligand or modulator contributing $\Delta G_{\mathrm{lig}}$,

$$
p_{\mathrm{o}} = \frac{1}{1 + \exp\bigl[-zF\bigl(V - V_{1/2}(T)\bigr)/RT\bigr]}, \qquad V_{1/2}(T) = \frac{\Delta H - T\Delta S + \Delta G_{\mathrm{lig}}}{zF}. \tag{25.3}
$$

At physiological temperatures the half-activation voltage lies far positive, so at the resting potential only a small fraction of channels is open. Cooling (for TRPM8) or heating (for TRPV1) shifts $V_{1/2}$ towards negative potentials, by several millivolts per degree, until channels open at rest (figure below). Ligands act in the same way: menthol shifts the TRPM8 activation curve to more negative voltages, which is why menthol feels cool at skin temperature, and capsaicin does the same for TRPV1.

The scheme also explains inflammatory heat **hyperalgesia**. Inflammatory mediators (protons, bradykinin and prostaglandins acting through protein kinase C and A, and nerve growth factor) sensitize TRPV1, which in the model is a negative $\Delta G_{\mathrm{lig}}$ of a few kilojoules per mole. Because the temperature dependence of $V_{1/2}$ is steep, a shift of $5\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ lowers the heat threshold by about $\Delta G_{\mathrm{lig}}/\Delta S \approx 5.5\,\mathrm{K}$, from about $43\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$ to near body temperature: warm water then hurts on sunburned skin. The coupling of several stimuli to one gate gives polymodal nociceptors their polymodality.

## 25.3. Labeled Lines and Central Integration

The **labeled line principle** states that the quality of a sensation is determined by which fibers are active ([7.14.39](/7.14.39#def-39.1.2)), and many experiments with genetically defined DRG types support it: activating TRPV1-expressing neurons produces pain behavior, and activating MrgprB4-expressing C fibers, which innervate hairy skin, produces signs of reward. But several sensations cannot be explained by a single line, and two examples show how the central nervous system combines lines.

The thermal grill illusion: touching a grill of interlaced bars, alternately warm ($40\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$) and cool ($20\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$), neither painful alone, produces a burning, painful sensation (Craig and Bushnell, 1994). The leading explanation is that cold-specific lamina I neurons normally inhibit the activity of polymodal "heat–pinch–cold" neurons that signal burning pain; the grill excites the polymodal neurons while recruiting less cold-specific inhibition than uniform cold, so the balance shifts towards pain. Sensation here is computed from the ratio of two channels, like color from the cone ratios.

**Itch** is defined as an unpleasant sensation that elicits the desire to scratch ([7.14.40](/7.14.40#def-40.7.1)). Most itch-sensing neurons also express TRPV1 and respond to painful stimuli, which raised the question of how itch and pain are distinguished. An experiment by Han et al. (2013) separated them. The receptor MrgprA3, which binds the antimalarial drug chloroquine (whose side effect is itch), marks about 4 % of DRG neurons. In mice lacking TRPV1 everywhere except in MrgprA3 neurons, capsaicin injected into the cheek, which normally evokes pain (wiping with the forepaw), evoked scratching with the hind paw: activating these neurons by any means produced itch, not pain. Itch is thus signaled by a dedicated population, even though that population is polymodal at the level of its receptors. In the spinal cord, these neurons excite interneurons that release gastrin-releasing peptide (GRP), which act on GRP receptor–expressing neurons; ablating the GRPR neurons abolishes scratching to all itch-inducing agents tested, without affecting pain behavior (Sun et al., 2009). Scratching suppresses itch because nociceptive input activates inhibitory interneurons (marked by the transcription factor Bhlhb5) that inhibit the itch pathway; mice lacking these interneurons scratch until their skin is injured (Ross et al., 2010). Itch and pain are therefore separate lines that interact through inhibition, which is the general pattern in the dorsal horn.

## 25.4. The Coding of Touch

### 25.4.1. Spatial and Temporal Codes for Texture

The four classes of low-threshold mechanoreceptive afferents of the glabrous skin (SA1, RA1, SA2 and RA2, the last ending in Pacinian corpuscles; [7.14.39](/7.14.39#sec-39.5.3)) divide the work of touch. Texture perception illustrates how they combine (Johnson, 2001; Weber et al., 2013). Coarse textures, with elements more than about a millimeter apart, are encoded spatially: SA1 afferents have small receptive fields (2–3 mm in diameter), respond with sustained firing to the local indentation, and their population activity reproduces the pattern of raised elements like an image, sharpened by their high sensitivity to edges and curvature. Perceived roughness of coarse surfaces is predicted by the spatial variation of SA1 activity (Connor and Johnson, 1992). Fine textures, with spatial periods below a few hundred micrometers, are too fine for this spatial image; they are encoded temporally instead. When a finger scans a surface with spatial period $\lambda$ at speed $v$, the skin vibrates at frequency

$$
f = \frac{v}{\lambda}, \tag{25.4}
$$
and Pacinian afferents, tuned to vibrations of about $40$–$800\,\mathrm{Hz}$, respond with spike patterns that are precisely timed and specific to the texture. Their temporal patterns dilate or contract in proportion to scanning speed, while the perceived texture does not change with speed: the brain must therefore read the temporal pattern relative to the speed of the movement, for which it has efference copies and proprioceptive signals ([7.16.29](/7.16.29)). Because Eq. (25.4) maps period onto frequency, the receptive range of the Pacinian channel corresponds to spatial periods from about $50\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ to $1\,\mathrm{mm}$ at ordinary scanning speeds, and humans can distinguish surfaces whose elements differ by tens of nanometers in height through the vibrations they produce.

### 25.4.2. Cortical Representation and Discrimination

Primary somatosensory cortex contains four areas with partly separate inputs: area 3a (proprioceptive), 3b (cutaneous, small receptive fields, the main target of SA1 and RA1 signals), 1 (texture and motion) and 2 (shape, combining cutaneous and proprioceptive input), each with a somatotopic map ([7.14.39](/7.14.39#sec-39.6.2)). Mountcastle (1957) discovered here the columnar organization of cortex: electrode penetrations perpendicular to the surface encountered neurons of the same modality and receptive field location, whereas oblique penetrations crossed between modalities. In rodents, each whisker is represented by a discrete column, the barrel, whose development depends on input from the whisker ([7.16.21](/7.16.21)).

A series of experiments by Romo and colleagues traced the neural basis of a simple perceptual decision through the somatosensory system. Monkeys compared the frequencies of two vibrations ("flutter", $5$–$50\,\mathrm{Hz}$) applied to a fingertip a few seconds apart and reported whether the second was higher. RA1 afferents and neurons of area 3b fire in phase with each vibration cycle, a **temporal code**; neurons of area 1 and the secondary somatosensory cortex convert it into a **firing rate** that increases or decreases monotonically with frequency; neurons of the prefrontal cortex maintain this rate during the delay (parametric working memory, [7.16.45](/7.16.45)) and finally reflect the comparison. Replacing the first vibration by electrical microstimulation of a column in area 3b at the same frequency allowed the monkeys to perform the task almost as well as with real vibrations (Romo et al., 1998): activity in this small population is sufficient for the perception of flutter. Comparing the discrimination thresholds of single neurons with those of the animal (neurometric and psychometric curves) follows the method of [7.16.26](/7.16.26).

## 25.5. The Modulation of Pain

### 25.5.1. A Rate Model of the Spinal Gate

The **gate control theory** ([7.14.40](/7.14.40#def-40.4.2)) proposed that inhibitory interneurons in the dorsal horn, driven by large-diameter touch fibers and inhibited by small nociceptive fibers, regulate the transmission of noxious input to projection neurons. The theory is best understood as a circuit model with explicit rates. Let $A$ and $C$ be the **firing rates** of the touch (Aβ) and nociceptive (C and Aδ) inputs, $D$ a descending drive, $I$ the rate of the inhibitory interneuron and $T$ that of the projection ("transmission") neuron, with rectified-linear units $[x]_+ = \max(x, 0)$:

$$
\tau\frac{\mathrm{d}I}{\mathrm{d}t} = -I + [w_{AI}A + D - w_{CI}C]_+, \qquad \tau\frac{\mathrm{d}T}{\mathrm{d}t} = -T + [w_{AT}A + w_{CT}C - g\,w_{IT}I]_+, \tag{25.5}
$$
where $g$ scales the strength of inhibition. At steady state $I$ and $T$ are piecewise-linear functions of the inputs, and three phenomena follow (figure below):

1. Rubbing: touch input increases $I$ more than it excites $T$ directly ($w_{AI}w_{IT} > w_{AT}$), so adding $A$ raises the threshold and lowers the gain of the response to $C$. The same holds for a descending drive to the interneuron, the spinal component of stress-induced analgesia.
2. Disinhibition and allodynia: if inhibition is weakened ($g < w_{AT}/(w_{AI}w_{IT})$), touch input alone drives the projection neuron. After peripheral nerve injury, microglia release BDNF, which downregulates the chloride transporter KCC2 in dorsal horn neurons; their chloride **reversal potential** rises, and GABA and glycine become less inhibitory (Coull et al., 2005). In the model this is a reduction of $g$, and the prediction, pain from light touch (**allodynia**, [7.14.40](/7.14.40#def-40.2.1)), is what patients with neuropathic pain experience. A polysynaptic pathway from Aβ fibers to lamina I projection neurons through excitatory interneurons expressing protein kinase Cγ, normally held silent by glycinergic inhibition, has been identified as one substrate (Lu et al., 2013).
3. The original model's specific claim that C fibers inhibit the interneurons ($w_{CI} > 0$) has had little direct support; with $w_{CI} = 0$ the model still produces rubbing and allodynia, which shows which parts of the theory carry its explanatory weight.

### 25.5.2. Wind-Up and Central Sensitization

Repeated stimulation of C fibers at rates above about $0.3\,\mathrm{Hz}$ causes a progressive increase in the response of dorsal horn neurons to each volley, called wind-up (Mendell, 1966). Each C-fiber volley produces, besides a fast EPSP, a slow depolarization lasting seconds, mediated by **NMDA receptors** (whose **magnesium block** is relieved by depolarization) and by **neuropeptides** such as substance P acting on NK1 receptors. If the next volley arrives before the slow depolarization has decayed, the depolarizations summate and each volley evokes more spikes; the response saturates when the slow component reaches a balance between increment and decay. A minimal model gives the steady level directly: if each volley adds $\delta$ to a variable $u$ that decays with time constant $\tau_{\mathrm{s}}$, then just after the $n$-th volley at interval $\Delta t$, $u_n = \delta\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\mathrm{e}^{-k\Delta t/\tau_{\mathrm{s}}} \to \delta/(1 - \mathrm{e}^{-\Delta t/\tau_{\mathrm{s}}})$, which is large when $\Delta t \ll \tau_{\mathrm{s}}$ and close to $\delta$ when $\Delta t \gg \tau_{\mathrm{s}}$, the frequency dependence observed.

**Definition 25.5.1 (Central Sensitization)** An increase in the excitability and synaptic efficacy of neurons in central nociceptive pathways, produced by intense or sustained nociceptor input, injury or inflammation, by which normal inputs evoke amplified responses and normally innocuous inputs evoke pain, is called **central sensitization** (Woolf, 1983).

Wind-up is a short-lived form of central sensitization; longer-lasting forms involve the same mechanisms as long-term potentiation in the hippocampus, NMDA receptor–dependent calcium entry and kinase activation in dorsal horn neurons ([7.16.14](/7.16.14)), together with disinhibition and changes in gene expression. Central sensitization explains secondary hyperalgesia, the increased sensitivity of uninjured skin around an injury, which is mediated by the central neurons that receive input from both regions rather than by the peripheral nerves. It is a target for treatment: blocking NMDA receptors with ketamine reduces wind-up and some forms of chronic pain.

### 25.5.3. Descending Control, Opioids and the Experience of Pain

The periaqueductal gray of the midbrain and the rostral ventromedial medulla form a descending system that can either inhibit or facilitate dorsal horn transmission ([7.14.40](/7.14.40#sec-40.4.1)). Electrical stimulation of the periaqueductal gray produces analgesia profound enough for surgery in rats (Reynolds, 1969), and the analgesic action of morphine depends largely on this system. The rostral ventromedial medulla contains "ON cells", which increase firing just before a withdrawal reflex and facilitate pain, and "OFF cells", which pause and normally inhibit it; opioids silence ON cells and activate OFF cells (Fields and colleagues). Opioid receptors (μ, δ, κ) are **G protein–coupled receptors** that open potassium channels and close calcium channels, hyperpolarizing neurons and reducing transmitter release, and endogenous opioid peptides (enkephalins, β-endorphin, dynorphins) are their natural ligands. Placebo analgesia is partly opioid-mediated: it is reduced by the opioid antagonist naloxone (Levine et al., 1978), and imaging shows that expectation of relief activates the same descending circuit.

At the molecular level, the voltage-gated sodium channel $\mathrm{Na_V1.7}$, encoded by *SCN9A*, is required for pain in humans: loss-of-function mutations cause congenital insensitivity to pain with otherwise normal sensation (Cox et al., 2006), and gain-of-function mutations cause inherited erythromelalgia, episodes of burning pain. Such genes have become targets for analgesics without the addictive properties of opioids.

In the cortex, pain is represented in a distributed network. The somatosensory cortices encode the location and intensity of a noxious stimulus, while the anterior cingulate cortex and the insula are related to its unpleasantness: under hypnotic suggestion that changed the unpleasantness of a fixed painful stimulus without changing its intensity, activity in the anterior cingulate cortex changed, whereas activity in S1 did not (Rainville et al., 1997). Pain is thus not a fixed readout of nociceptor activity: it is the output of a network whose gain is set at the spinal gate, by descending control and by expectation, which is why the same injury can hurt very differently in different circumstances.

## Summary

- Somatosensory neurons are classified by fiber diameter and conduction velocity (Aα, Aβ, Aδ, C) and, more finely, by transcriptional type; genetic access to the types tests their necessity and sufficiency for each sensation.
- Mechanotransduction channels were found by genetics: MEC-4/MEC-10 (DEG/ENaC) in worms, NOMPC (TRP, tethered by an ankyrin spring) in flies, Piezo2 in mammals; force gates them through membrane tension or tethers, shifting the free energy of opening.
- TRPV1 (Definition 25.2.1) and TRPM8 (Definition 25.2.2) are gated by heat and cold through large enthalpy and entropy changes; Eqs. (25.1)–(25.2) relate $\Delta H$ to $Q_{10}$. Temperature, voltage and ligands add to one free energy of opening (Eq. (25.3)), explaining the cooling of menthol, the burning of capsaicin and inflammatory heat hyperalgesia.
- Labeled lines carry pain and itch (MrgprA3 neurons, GRP–GRPR spinal circuit), but sensation is also computed by comparing lines (thermal grill) and by inhibition between them (scratching suppresses itch).
- Coarse textures are coded spatially by SA1 populations, fine textures temporally by Pacinian afferents responding at $f = v/\lambda$ (Eq. (25.4)). S1 is columnar and somatotopic; microstimulation of area 3b can substitute for flutter vibration in a discrimination task.
- The rate model of the dorsal horn gate (Eq. (25.5)) explains why rubbing and descending drive reduce pain and why disinhibition after nerve injury causes allodynia. Wind-up and central sensitization (Definition 25.5.1) amplify nociceptive transmission through NMDA receptors and LTP-like mechanisms.
- Descending control (PAG–RVM, ON and OFF cells, opioids), placebo, $\mathrm{Na_V1.7}$ and a distributed cortical network set the gain of pain.

1. A myelinated Aβ fiber has an outer diameter of $10\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ and a C fiber a diameter of $1\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ with a conduction velocity of $1\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. Estimate the arrival times at the spinal cord of signals from a toe $1.1\,\mathrm{m}$ away. What would the C fiber's velocity be if its diameter were $4\,\mu\mathrm{m}$?

   **Answer** With $v \approx 6\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ per micrometer, the Aβ fiber conducts at about $60\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ and its signal arrives after $1.1/60\,\mathrm{s} \approx 18\,\mathrm{ms}$; the C fiber signal arrives after $1.1\,\mathrm{s}$. For an unmyelinated axon $v \propto \sqrt{d}$, so a fourfold increase of diameter doubles the velocity to $2\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$; a C fiber would need a diameter of $3600\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ to match the Aβ fiber, which is why myelination is indispensable for fast conduction in vertebrates.

   **Answer** $zF = 0.8\times96\,485 = 77\,188\,\mathrm{C\,mol^{-1}}$. At $308.15\,\mathrm{K}$: $\Delta H - T\Delta S = -202\,000 + 308.15\times695 = 12\,164\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}}$, so $V_{1/2} = 0.158\,\mathrm{V} = 158\,\mathrm{mV}$. At $288.15\,\mathrm{K}$: $-202\,000 + 200\,264 = -1736$, $V_{1/2} = -22\,\mathrm{mV}$. The shift is $\Delta S/(zF) = 695/77\,188\,\mathrm{V\,K^{-1}} \approx 9\,\mathrm{mV}$ per degree: cooling by $20\,\mathrm{K}$ moves activation by $180\,\mathrm{mV}$, from far positive to near the resting potential.
3. In the steady state of Eq. (25.5) with $D = 0$ and $w_{CI} = 0$, derive the condition on the weights under which touch input alone never activates $T$, and the threshold value of $C$ above which $T$ responds when touch input $A$ is present.

   **Answer** With $C = 0$, $I = w_{AI}A$ and $T = [(w_{AT} - g\,w_{IT}w_{AI})A]_+$, which is zero for all $A$ if and only if $g\,w_{IT}w_{AI} \ge w_{AT}$: inhibition routed through the interneuron must outweigh direct excitation. With touch present and $w_{CI} = 0$, $I = w_{AI}A$ does not depend on $C$, and $T = [w_{CT}C - (g\,w_{IT}w_{AI} - w_{AT})A]_+$, so $T > 0$ when $C > (g\,w_{IT}w_{AI} - w_{AT})A/w_{CT}$. The threshold for pain transmission rises linearly with the touch input, the "closing of the gate"; with the figure's weights it is $1.2A$, that is $24\,\mathrm{Hz}$ for $A = 20\,\mathrm{Hz}$ (the figure, with $w_{CI} = 0.1$, gives $20\,\mathrm{Hz}$).
4. Why does the experiment in which TRPV1 was restored only in MrgprA3 neurons show that itch is a labeled line, even though MrgprA3 neurons also respond to noxious heat and mechanical stimuli?

   **Answer** The experiment fixed the stimulus (capsaicin) and changed which neurons could respond to it. When only MrgprA3 neurons could detect capsaicin, the behavior was scratching, the itch response, rather than wiping, the pain response. The quality of the sensation therefore depended on the identity of the activated neurons and their central connections, not on the stimulus or on the receptor; that the same neurons respond to heat in natural conditions means that, in isolation, their activity is read as itch, and that pain from such stimuli must normally come from other, co-activated populations that also suppress itch. This is the labeled-line principle applied to a genetically defined population.
5. (Open question) Propose an experiment to test whether the anterior cingulate cortex is necessary for the unpleasantness of pain but not for its detection in mice.

   **Answer** One design: train mice in a conditioned place avoidance paradigm, in which a chamber is paired with a noxious stimulus (for example a mild hind paw injection of formalin or a heat stimulus), and separately measure reflexive withdrawal thresholds (von Frey filaments, Hargreaves heat test). Silence the anterior cingulate cortex chemogenetically or optogenetically during pairing. Prediction if the hypothesis holds: withdrawal thresholds, which depend on spinal and brainstem circuits and on the detection of the stimulus, are unchanged, while place avoidance, which reflects the aversiveness of the experience, is reduced. Controls must exclude effects on learning or locomotion (for example by testing conditioned avoidance with a non-painful aversive stimulus). Experiments of this kind (Johansen et al., 2001, with lesions; later optogenetic studies) found reduced avoidance with intact reflexes.
