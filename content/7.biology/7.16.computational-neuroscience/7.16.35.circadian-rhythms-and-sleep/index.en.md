Two clocks govern when animals sleep. A circadian clock, present in almost every cell and synchronized by a master pacemaker in the hypothalamus, tells the time of day; a homeostatic process measures how long the animal has been awake. This chapter treats both as dynamical systems. The circadian clock is a biochemical oscillator built from a delayed negative feedback loop of transcription and translation, one of the first behaviors whose mechanism was traced from a gene to a behavior; its synchronization by light is described by a phase response curve. Sleep is controlled by mutually inhibitory populations of sleep- and wake-promoting neurons that form a switch, pushed by the two processes. The physiology of sleep stages, the arousal system and the two-process model of Borbély are in [7.14.46](/7.14.46#sec-46.4); the general mathematics of biochemical oscillators, including the Goodwin model and the Hopf bifurcation, is in [7.6.33](/7.6.33#sec-33.5). Here we add the genetics of the clock, the theory of entrainment and the circuit-level model of sleep switching.

## 35.1. The Molecular Clock

### 35.1.1. Circadian Rhythms

A **circadian rhythm** has three defining properties. It persists in constant conditions with a free-running period $\tau$ close to, but not exactly, 24 hours (about $24.2\,\mathrm{h}$ in humans, $23.7\,\mathrm{h}$ in mice); it is entrained by environmental time cues (zeitgebers: light, temperature, feeding) to exactly 24 hours; and its period is temperature compensated, with a $Q_{10}$ close to 1 (0.8–1.2), although most biochemical rates have $Q_{10} \approx 2$–3. The third property shows that a circadian clock is not simply the sum of a few reaction rates: its period is a robust, regulated property of the network.

### 35.1.2. per, tim and the Feedback Loop in Flies

Konopka and Benzer (1971) screened for mutant flies with altered rhythms of eclosion and locomotor activity and found three alleles of one X-linked gene, *period* (*per*): $per^{\mathrm{S}}$ with a period of 19 h, $per^{\mathrm{L}}$ with 28 h and $per^{0}$ arrhythmic. The cloning of *per* (1984; the groups of Hall and Rosbash and of Young) and later work showed that *per* mRNA and PER protein oscillate, with PER lagging its mRNA by about 6 hours, and that PER represses its own transcription (Hardin, Hall and Rosbash, 1990). The partner TIMELESS (TIM), the transcriptional activators CLOCK (CLK) and CYCLE (CYC), and the kinase DOUBLETIME (a casein kinase 1, CK1ε homolog) complete the core loop; the three discoverers shared the 2017 Nobel Prize.

**Definition 35.1.1 (Transcription–Translation Feedback Loop)** A **transcription–translation feedback loop** is a gene regulatory circuit in which transcriptional activators drive the expression of repressor proteins that, after a delay due to translation, modification, complex formation and nuclear entry, inhibit the activators and thus their own transcription, until the repressors are degraded and the cycle restarts.

The loop in flies runs as follows. CLK–CYC heterodimers bind E-box enhancers and activate *per* and *tim* in the late day. PER is unstable unless bound to TIM; PER–TIM complexes accumulate in the cytoplasm during the early night, enter the nucleus around midnight, and inhibit CLK–CYC. Phosphorylation of PER by DOUBLETIME targets it for degradation by the ubiquitin–proteasome system; when PER and TIM are gone, in the late night and morning, CLK–CYC is released and a new cycle starts. The slowness of the steps (hours for accumulation, phosphorylation and nuclear entry) sets the 24-hour period: $per^{\mathrm{S}}$ and the *doubletime* short-period alleles accelerate PER phosphorylation and degradation.

### 35.1.3. The Mammalian Clock

Mammals use the same design with partly different genes. Takahashi and colleagues found the *Clock* mutation in a forward genetic screen in mice (Vitaterna and colleagues, 1994): heterozygotes have a 1-hour longer period and homozygotes become arrhythmic in constant darkness. CLOCK and BMAL1 activate the *Per1*, *Per2*, *Cry1* and *Cry2* genes; PER–CRY complexes, phosphorylated by CK1δ/ε, enter the nucleus and inhibit CLOCK–BMAL1. A second loop through the nuclear receptors REV-ERBα (repressor) and ROR (activator) drives antiphasic rhythms of *Bmal1* transcription and adds robustness. The mutations confirm the role of the phosphorylation steps: the *tau* mutation of the Syrian hamster, a gain-of-function allele of CK1ε, shortens the period to 20 h, and familial advanced sleep phase syndrome in humans (sleep onset around 19:00, waking around 4:00) is caused by a mutation of a CK1 phosphorylation site in PER2 (Toh and colleagues, 2001) or by a mutation in CK1δ.

The clock controls physiology through output genes: about 10% of the transcripts of a typical tissue, and over 40% of all protein-coding genes in at least one tissue, oscillate with a circadian period. Peripheral clocks in liver, muscle and other organs are synchronized by the SCN through neural and hormonal signals and body temperature, and can be reset independently by the timing of feeding.

### 35.1.4. A Goodwin-Type Model

The simplest mathematical abstraction of the loop is the **Goodwin oscillator** ([7.6.33](/7.6.33#sec-33.5)). In nondimensional form, with mRNA $M$, cytoplasmic protein $P$ and nuclear repressor $P_{\mathrm{n}}$,
$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}M}{\mathrm{d}t} = \frac{1}{1 + P_{\mathrm{n}}^h} - b M, \qquad \frac{\mathrm{d}P}{\mathrm{d}t} = b(M - P), \qquad \frac{\mathrm{d}P_{\mathrm{n}}}{\mathrm{d}t} = b(P - P_{\mathrm{n}}). \tag{35.1}
$$
With equal rates $b$ for the three stages, the fixed point loses stability in a Hopf bifurcation only if the Hill coefficient exceeds $h = 8$ (the secant condition for a three-stage loop); at the bifurcation the frequency is $\omega = \sqrt{3}\,b$, so the period is about $2\pi/(\sqrt{3}\,b)$. A 24-hour period then requires $b \approx 0.15\,\mathrm{h^{-1}}$: mRNA and protein half-lives of about 4.6 hours, close to the measured values for PER proteins. The steep repression required by the minimal model is not realistic for a single repressor binding a promoter; in real clocks the effective steepness comes from additional stages (more delay), from sequestration of the activator by a repressor that binds it tightly at a 1:1 ratio (which gives ultrasensitivity without cooperativity; Kim and Forger, 2012), and from multisite phosphorylation.

The model explains why the period depends mainly on the degradation rates: in Eq. (35.1) time scales as $1/b$, and mutations that speed up phosphorylation-dependent degradation ($per^{\mathrm{S}}$, *tau*, the human PER2 mutation) shorten the period. Temperature compensation is not explained by the model with a single rate; it requires opposing temperature dependences of different steps, for example of the phosphorylation of different sites of PER2 by CK1, which have been demonstrated.

## 35.2. Entrainment and the Suprachiasmatic Nucleus

### 35.2.1. The Light Input

In mammals, light entrains the clock only through the eyes: blind mice without rods and cones still entrain, through **intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells** that express melanopsin and project through the retinohypothalamic tract to the **suprachiasmatic nucleus** ([7.14.46](/7.14.46#sec-46.4.5)). Glutamate and the neuropeptide PACAP released onto SCN neurons raise $\ce{Ca^2+}$ and cAMP, activate CREB and induce *Per1* and *Per2* transcription within an hour. In flies, light acts cell-autonomously: the blue-light photoreceptor CRYPTOCHROME, activated by light, binds TIM and targets it for degradation, so light resets the clock by destroying the repressor complex.

### 35.2.2. The Phase Response Curve

The effect of a brief stimulus on an oscillator depends on its phase. For a clock, the **phase response curve** (the concept is defined for neural oscillators in [7.16.15](/7.16.15)) plots the phase shift produced by a light pulse given in constant darkness against the circadian time (CT) of the pulse, where CT 0 is the start of the subjective day. The PRC of nocturnal and diurnal mammals alike has three parts: light in the subjective day has little effect (the dead zone), light in the early subjective night delays the clock and light in the late subjective night advances it. Weak pulses give small shifts that vary continuously with phase (type 1 PRC); strong pulses can give shifts of up to 12 hours, with a discontinuity (type 0 PRC).

**Proposition 35.2.1 (Condition for Entrainment)** Let an oscillator with free-running period $\tau$ receive one brief light pulse every 24 hours, and let $\Delta\phi(\theta)$ (in hours, advances positive) be its PRC. A periodic entrained state at circadian phase $\theta^*$ exists if $\Delta\phi(\theta^*) = \tau - 24\,\mathrm{h}$, and it is stable if $-2 < \Delta\phi'(\theta^*) < 0$.

**Proof** Between pulses the phase advances by 24 h of real time, which is $24$ circadian hours minus the mismatch $\tau - 24$: measured on the oscillator's own cycle, the phase of the next pulse is $\theta_{k+1} = \theta_k + 24 - \tau + \Delta\phi(\theta_k)$ (mod $\tau$). A fixed point satisfies $\Delta\phi(\theta^*) = \tau - 24$. The map $F(\theta) = \theta + 24 - \tau + \Delta\phi(\theta)$ has derivative $F'(\theta^*) = 1 + \Delta\phi'(\theta^*)$, and the fixed point is stable iff $\lvert F'(\theta^*)\rvert < 1$, i.e. $-2 < \Delta\phi'(\theta^*) < 0$. $\square$

A clock that runs slow ($\tau > 24\,\mathrm{h}$) must be advanced every day, so it entrains with light falling at a phase on the advance part of the PRC, where the curve decreases; a fast clock entrains with light on the delay part. This predicts the phase of entrainment from $\tau$ (people with longer intrinsic periods are later chronotypes) and why bright light in the late night and early morning advances human rhythms, the basis of light therapy for delayed sleep phase and jet lag.

The Goodwin model reproduces a PRC if light adds to the transcription rate, as *Per* induction does: a pulse while $M$ is rising advances the cycle and a pulse while it is falling delays it.

### 35.2.3. Coupling in the SCN

Each of the roughly 20 000 neurons of the mouse SCN is a cell-autonomous oscillator: dispersed SCN neurons in culture fire with circadian rhythms of different periods (Welsh and colleagues, 1995). In the intact nucleus they are synchronized by coupling, mainly through the neuropeptide VIP acting on VPAC2 receptors, together with GABA and gap junctions; mice lacking VIP or VPAC2 have weak, desynchronized rhythms. Coupling has three consequences that follow from the theory of coupled oscillators ([7.16.15](/7.16.15)). The ensemble period is more precise than that of single cells, because independent fluctuations average out; the network is resistant to perturbations, which explains why the SCN, unlike peripheral tissues, is hard to reset by temperature or feeding and why jet lag takes days: the clock can shift only about an hour per day. And strong coupling can sustain rhythms in cells that would be damped oscillators on their own: *Cry1*- or *Per1*-deficient peripheral cells are arrhythmic, but the coupled SCN of the same mutants still oscillates (Liu and colleagues, 2007).

## 35.3. Sleep as a Behavior

### 35.3.1. A Behavioral Definition

Sleep is defined by behavior, not by brain waves, so that it can be identified in animals without a cortex. Sleep is a state of reversible behavioral quiescence (distinguishing it from coma and torpor) with a species-specific posture and site, a raised arousal threshold (stronger stimuli are needed to elicit a response), and homeostatic regulation: deprivation is followed by a rebound of more or deeper sleep. By these criteria sleep is found in all animals studied carefully: mammals and birds, fish (zebrafish larvae), insects (Hendricks and colleagues, 2000; Shaw and colleagues, 2000, in *Drosophila*), nematodes (lethargus between larval molts), and even the jellyfish *Cassiopea* and the cnidarian *Hydra*, which have no centralized nervous system. Sleep is therefore ancient, and its basic function must be one that nerve nets already needed.

### 35.3.2. Sleep Stages

Mammals and birds have two kinds of sleep, **non-rapid eye movement sleep** with synchronized slow waves and spindles, and **rapid eye movement sleep** with a desynchronized EEG and muscle atonia; their stages, the 90-minute cycle in humans and the physiological changes are described in [7.14.46](/7.14.46#sec-46.4.2). The slow oscillation of NREM sleep (below $1\,\mathrm{Hz}$) is an alternation of up states and down states of cortical neurons, generated by recurrent cortical networks with adaptation, a relaxation oscillation of the kind analyzed in [7.16.15](/7.16.15); thalamic reticular neurons generate spindles through their T-type calcium currents.

## 35.4. Circuits That Control Sleep and Wakefulness

### 35.4.1. The Flip-Flop Switch

Wakefulness is maintained by the ascending arousal system: monoaminergic (locus coeruleus, raphe, tuberomammillary nucleus), cholinergic (basal forebrain, pedunculopontine and laterodorsal tegmental nuclei) and glutamatergic (parabrachial) neurons, stabilized by orexin neurons of the lateral hypothalamus ([7.14.46](/7.14.46#sec-46.4.1)). Sleep is promoted by GABAergic and galaninergic neurons of the ventrolateral and median preoptic area, which inhibit the arousal nuclei and are inhibited by them.

**Definition 35.4.1 (Flip-Flop Switch)** A **flip-flop switch** is a pair of neural populations that inhibit each other strongly enough that the system has two stable states, one population active and the other silent, and moves between them by rapid transitions when an external drive crosses a threshold; in sleep regulation the populations are the sleep-promoting preoptic neurons and the wake-promoting arousal nuclei (Saper, Chou and Scammell, 2001).

A flip-flop explains why transitions between sleep and wakefulness take seconds to minutes although sleep pressure and the circadian signal change over hours: the switch is **bistable**, and a slowly changing drive flips it only when it reaches the end of the bistable range, a saddle-node bifurcation of the fast subsystem. Orexin neurons add a bias toward the wake state; without it the switch is unstable, which explains the fragmented sleep and wakefulness of narcolepsy. A second flip-flop between REM-on neurons (glutamatergic neurons of the sublaterodorsal nucleus of the pons) and REM-off neurons (GABAergic neurons of the ventrolateral periaqueductal gray) switches between NREM and REM sleep.

### 35.4.2. Genetic Identification of Sleep-Regulating Neurons

The tools of [7.16.3](/7.16.3) have identified many sleep-regulating populations. In mice, chemogenetic activation of GABAergic neurons of the parafacial zone in the medulla induces slow-wave sleep within minutes; preoptic galanin neurons are activated during sleep and by warmth, linking sleep to thermoregulation; and activation of GABAergic neurons of the ventral medulla induces REM sleep. In *Drosophila*, neurons projecting to the dorsal fan-shaped body of the central complex promote sleep, and their excitability increases with sleep pressure, through changes in potassium channels that switch them between an electrically silent and an active mode; neurons of the ellipsoid body (R2 neurons) increase their activity with time awake and encode sleep pressure (Liu and colleagues, 2016).

Unbiased screens have found genes that set sleep amount. In flies, mutations of the potassium channel *Shaker* and of its regulator *sleepless* reduce sleep to a third of normal. In mice, a forward screen of thousands of mutagenized animals recorded by EEG found *Sleepy*, a splice mutation of the kinase gene *Sik3* that increases NREM sleep time and slow-wave activity, as if sleep need were constantly high, and *Dreamless*, a mutation of the sodium leak channel *Nalcn* that reduces REM sleep (Funato and colleagues, 2016). SIK3 activity and the phosphorylation of a set of synaptic proteins increase with time awake and decrease during sleep (Wang and colleagues, 2018), which makes phosphorylation a candidate molecular record of sleep need; adenosine, which accumulates during wakefulness, is another ([7.14.46](/7.14.46#sec-46.4.4)).

## 35.5. A Quantitative Model of Sleep Regulation

The **two-process model** ([7.14.46](/7.14.46#sec-46.4.5)) describes sleep timing with a homeostatic variable $H$ (process S) that rises during wakefulness and falls during sleep, and two thresholds modulated by the circadian process C: sleep begins when $H$ reaches the upper threshold and ends when it falls to the lower one. The model is phenomenological: it does not say what the thresholds are. A flip-flop provides them (Phillips and Robinson, 2007).

**Formula 35.5.1 (Flip-Flop Model of Sleep–Wake Regulation)** Let $m$ be the activity of the wake-active arousal population and $v$ that of the sleep-active preoptic population, with gain function $S(x) = 1/(1 + \mathrm{e}^{-x/\sigma})$:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\tau\,\frac{\mathrm{d}m}{\mathrm{d}t} &= -m + S\bigl(I_{\mathrm{w}} + A(t) - w v\bigr), \qquad \tau\,\frac{\mathrm{d}v}{\mathrm{d}t} = -v + S\bigl(D(t) - w m\bigr),\\
D(t) &= k\bigl(H(t) - L_0\bigr) - c\,C(t), \qquad \frac{\mathrm{d}H}{\mathrm{d}t} = \begin{cases}(1 - H)/\chi_{\mathrm{w}} & \text{awake},\\ -H/\chi_{\mathrm{s}} & \text{asleep},\end{cases}
\end{aligned}
$$
where $\tau$ is minutes, $w$ the mutual inhibition, $I_{\mathrm{w}}$ a tonic drive to the arousal system (orexin), $A(t)$ an external arousing input, $C(t)$ a wake-promoting circadian signal from the SCN, and $\chi_{\mathrm{w}} = 18.2\,\mathrm{h}$, $\chi_{\mathrm{s}} = 4.2\,\mathrm{h}$ the time constants of process S.

With steep gain functions ($\sigma \to 0$) the fast subsystem can be analyzed by hand. In the wake state $m \approx 1$, and $v = S(D - w)$ stays near 0 as long as $D < w$; in the sleep state $v \approx 1$, and $m = S(I_{\mathrm{w}} - w v)$ stays near 0 as long as $I_{\mathrm{w}} < w v$, which with $I_{\mathrm{w}} = w/2$ holds as long as $v = S(D) > 1/2$, that is $D > 0$. The switch is therefore bistable for $0 < D < w$: wake turns into sleep when $D$ rises above $w$, and sleep into wake when $D$ falls below 0. Choosing $k = w/(H_0 - L_0)$ and $c = 0.1\,k$, the thresholds expressed in terms of $H$ are $H_0 + 0.1\,C(t)$ and $L_0 + 0.1\,C(t)$: the upper and lower thresholds of the two-process model are the two ends of the hysteresis loop of the flip-flop.

A: steady states of the fast switch as the drive $D$ is slowly increased (red) and decreased (blue, dashed); the switch is bistable between $D = 0$ and $D = w$. B: the homeostatic variable $H$ (red) between the sleep-onset and waking thresholds (dashed and dotted) generated by the switch; blue bars mark sleep, which normally runs from about 23:00 to 7:00. Enforced wakefulness (shaded, $A = 2$, from 22:00 on day 3 to 7:00 on day 5) holds the switch in the wake state while $H$ rises toward its asymptote; recovery sleep begins as soon as the input stops, at $H \approx 0.94$, but lasts only about 6 h because the rising circadian waking threshold ends it around midday.](./plots/sleep-flipflop.py)

The model makes predictions that a purely phenomenological description does not. Sleep deprivation raises $H$ far above its normal value at sleep onset, so recovery sleep is deeper (higher slow-wave activity, which the model identifies with $H$) but not proportionally longer, and its length depends on the circadian phase at which it starts: recovery sleep that begins in the morning is short, as observed in shift workers. Reducing the tonic orexin drive $I_{\mathrm{w}}$ destabilizes the wake state: with $\sigma = 0.1$, lowering $I_{\mathrm{w}}$ from 1 to 0.1 moves the sleep-onset threshold from $D \approx 1.9$ to $D \approx 0.9$ while the waking threshold stays near 0, and for $I_{\mathrm{w}} \to 0$ no stable wake state remains. The narrowed bistable range means that the animal falls asleep at low sleep pressure and that noise flips the switch often, a model of the state instability of narcolepsy. Because the switch sits near a saddle-node bifurcation close to each transition, noise causes brief awakenings preferentially near the transitions, as in human sleep at the beginning and end of the night.

## 35.6. Why Do We Sleep?

Sleep deprivation is lethal in rats within weeks and impairs attention, learning and metabolism in humans ([7.14.46](/7.14.46#sec-46.4.6)), yet no single function of sleep is established. The main hypotheses make different quantitative predictions.

| Hypothesis | Claim | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Synaptic homeostasis (Tononi and Cirelli) | Wakefulness strengthens synapses through learning; sleep renormalizes them, restoring the capacity to learn and saving energy | The axon–spine interface of cortical synapses is about 18% smaller after sleep than after wakefulness (de Vivo and colleagues, 2017, serial electron microscopy); AMPA receptor levels and slow-wave activity rise with time awake |
| Clearance of metabolites | The extracellular space expands in NREM sleep and flow of cerebrospinal fluid through the brain parenchyma increases | Clearance of injected amyloid-β twice as fast in sleep (Xie and colleagues, 2013); pulsations of CSF coupled to slow waves in humans |
| Memory consolidation | Replay of waking activity in NREM sleep, coordinated by slow oscillations, spindles and hippocampal sharp-wave ripples, transfers memories to the cortex | Disrupting ripples during sleep impairs spatial learning; [7.16.42](/7.16.42) |
| Energy and repair | Reduced metabolic rate and increased synthesis and repair | Sleep amount across species correlates with metabolic rate; sleep reduces DNA damage in zebrafish neurons |

The hypotheses are not exclusive; the homeostatic component of sleep (process S) suggests that some quantity accumulates during wakefulness and is cleared during sleep, whether synaptic strength, metabolites, protein phosphorylation or damage, and a single such quantity would explain why sleep pressure can be described by one variable.

## Summary

- Circadian rhythms free-run with a period near 24 h, are entrained by zeitgebers and are temperature compensated. The molecular clock is a transcription–translation feedback loop (Definition 35.1.1): CLK–CYC (flies) or CLOCK–BMAL1 (mammals) activate *per*/*tim* or *Per*/*Cry*, whose proteins inhibit their activators after a delay; phosphorylation by CK1 sets the degradation rate and the period.
- A Goodwin model (Eq. (35.1)) oscillates when repression is steep enough ($h > 8$ for three equal stages); its period scales as $1/b$, about $2\pi/(\sqrt{3}\,b)$ near onset.
- Light entrains the mammalian clock through melanopsin ganglion cells and *Per* induction in the SCN; the PRC has a dead zone, delays in the early and advances in the late subjective night. Entrainment requires $\Delta\phi(\theta^*) = \tau - 24\,\mathrm{h}$ on a decreasing part of the PRC (Proposition 35.2.1). Coupling through VIP makes the SCN precise and robust.
- Sleep is defined behaviorally (reversible quiescence, raised arousal threshold, homeostatic rebound) and occurs in all animals examined.
- Sleep- and wake-promoting populations form a flip-flop switch (Definition 35.4.1); genetic screens found *Sik3*, *Nalcn*, *Shaker* and *sleepless* as regulators of sleep amount.
- In the flip-flop model (Formula 35.5.1), the two thresholds of the two-process model are the ends of the hysteresis loop of the switch; it predicts deep but short recovery sleep and state instability with reduced orexin drive.

| Process | Variable | Time scale | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|---|
| Circadian (C) | Clock phase | 24 h | Transcription–translation feedback loop, SCN |
| Homeostatic (S) | Sleep pressure $H$ | $18\,\mathrm{h}$ rise, $4\,\mathrm{h}$ decay | Adenosine, protein phosphorylation, synaptic strength (candidates) |
| Switching | Activity of arousal vs preoptic neurons | Seconds to minutes | Mutual inhibition, bistability |

## Questions

1. In the Goodwin model with equal rates, why is the threshold Hill coefficient 8 and the onset frequency $\sqrt{3}\,b$? What happens to the required steepness if the loop has more stages?

   **Answer** At the fixed point all three variables are equal, $\bar M = \bar P = \bar P_{\mathrm{n}} = x$ with $f(x) = b x$, where $f(x) = 1/(1 + x^h)$. Linearizing, $\delta\dot M = -b\,\delta M - \lvert f'(x)\rvert\,\delta P_{\mathrm{n}}$, $\delta\dot P = b(\delta M - \delta P)$, $\delta\dot P_{\mathrm{n}} = b(\delta P - \delta P_{\mathrm{n}})$, so the characteristic equation is $(s + b)^3 + G b^3 = 0$ with loop gain $G = \lvert f'(x)\rvert/b = \lvert f'(x)\rvert x/f(x) = h x^h/(1 + x^h)$, the logarithmic sensitivity of the repression. The roots are $s = -b + b\,G^{1/3}\mathrm{e}^{\pm\mathrm{i}\pi/3}$ (and a real negative root); their real part vanishes when $G^{1/3}\cos(\pi/3) = 1$, i.e. $G = 8$, and then $\omega = b\,G^{1/3}\sin(\pi/3) = \sqrt{3}\,b$. Since $G < h$, oscillation requires $h > 8$. For $N$ equal stages the condition becomes $G > \sec^N(\pi/N)$: 8 for $N = 3$, 4 for $N = 4$, about 2.9 for $N = 5$, approaching 1 for many stages; more delay stages relax the steepness requirement.

2. A person has an intrinsic period of $24.5\,\mathrm{h}$ and lives under a light–dark cycle. Using Proposition 35.2.1, at what part of the PRC must the light fall each day? What happens to entrainment if the light is too weak for the maximal advance to exceed 0.5 h?

   **Answer** The clock runs 0.5 h slow each day, so the light must produce a daily advance of 0.5 h: $\Delta\phi(\theta^*) = +0.5\,\mathrm{h}$ at a phase where the PRC decreases, which is on the falling flank of the advance region (late subjective night to early subjective day, after the peak of advances). If the maximal advance is below 0.5 h, no fixed point exists; the clock free-runs, drifting about 0.5 h later every day minus the partial correction it receives, and the phase of light relative to the clock cycles through all phases (relative coordination). This is the situation of many blind people without light perception, whose rhythms free-run.

3. In Formula 35.5.1 with steep gain functions, show that the switch is bistable for $0 < D < w$ when $I_{\mathrm{w}} = w/2$. What happens if $I_{\mathrm{w}}$ is reduced to $w/4$ (a model of orexin loss)?

   **Answer** Wake state: $m \approx 1$ requires $v = S(D - w) \approx 0$, i.e. $D < w$, and then $m = S(I_{\mathrm{w}}) \approx 1$ because $I_{\mathrm{w}} \gg \sigma$. Sleep state: $m \approx 0$ gives $v = S(D)$, and $m = S(I_{\mathrm{w}} - w v)$ stays near 0 if $w v > I_{\mathrm{w}}$, i.e. $S(D) > 1/2$, i.e. $D > 0$. Both states exist for $0 < D < w$. With $I_{\mathrm{w}} = w/4$ the sleep state requires only $S(D) > 1/4$, which extends it slightly to negative $D$; in the steep limit the wake state is unchanged, but with finite steepness $m = S(I_{\mathrm{w}} - w v)$ in the wake state is no longer close to 1, the inhibition it exerts on $v$ weakens, and the sleep-onset threshold falls (to $D \approx 1.7$ for $\sigma = 0.1$, from $1.9$, and to $0.9$ for $I_{\mathrm{w}} = 0.1$). The bistable range narrows, so the animal falls asleep at lower pressure and noise produces frequent transitions: wakefulness is fragmented although total sleep changes little, as in narcolepsy.

4. (Open question) The synaptic homeostasis hypothesis predicts that synapses weaken during sleep. How could you test whether this weakening is a cause of the recovery of learning ability, rather than a side effect of reduced activity?

   **Answer** Approach: measure synaptic strength and learning capacity in the same animals; then dissociate sleep from synaptic downscaling. Examples: (i) block the molecular pathway of sleep-dependent downscaling (for example the mGluR5–Homer1a pathway reported to mediate it) in a cortical area during sleep and test whether learning that depends on that area recovers after sleep; (ii) induce downscaling during wakefulness (optogenetically driven slow oscillations, or pharmacological induction of synaptic scaling) and test whether it substitutes for sleep; (iii) track identified spines across sleep and wakefulness with two-photon imaging and relate their changes to subsequent learning-induced potentiation in the same dendrites. A causal role predicts that preventing downscaling prevents the recovery of learning and saturation of LTP, and that imposed downscaling restores them; correlation alone cannot distinguish cause from consequence, because reduced firing during sleep would also reduce synaptic strength through activity-dependent plasticity.
