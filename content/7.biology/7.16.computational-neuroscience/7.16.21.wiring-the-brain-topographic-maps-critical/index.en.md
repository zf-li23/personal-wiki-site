The brain is not wired by a blueprint that specifies each of its $10^{14}$ synapses, and it is not wired by experience alone. A first, coarse circuit is laid down by molecular cues that need no neural activity; it is then refined by spontaneous and sensory-evoked activity, in a process that depends on competition between inputs. This chapter treats the visual system, in which both stages are best understood, from the retinotectal projection to the ocular dominance columns of the cortex ([7.16.18](/7.16.18#sec-18.3)). The molecular biology of **axon guidance** and synapse formation is the subject of [7.5.21](/7.5.21) and is only summarized here; the emphasis is on the quantitative principles, which are used in [7.16.22](/7.16.22) to model the self-organization of receptive fields and maps.

## 21.1. The Retinotectal Projection and Chemoaffinity

The axons of retinal ganglion cells project onto the optic tectum (the superior colliculus in mammals) in an orderly, inverted map: nasal retina to the posterior tectum, temporal retina to the anterior tectum ([7.5.21](/7.5.21#sec-21.5.1)). Sperry's **chemoaffinity hypothesis** explained the map by matching molecular labels: axons from a given retinal position carry a label, targets carry the complementary label, and each axon stops where the labels match. His evidence was that regenerating axons of an amphibian return to their original tectal positions after the optic nerve is cut and the eye is rotated by $180^\circ$; the animal then sees the world upside down.

A strict reading of chemoaffinity predicts that each axon has a fixed destination that does not depend on the other axons. Experiments on regenerating fish and frogs showed that this cannot be the whole story. When half of the tectum is removed before regeneration, the axons from the entire retina connect to the remaining half, in order: the map is compressed (Gaze and Sharma 1970). When half of the retina is removed, the axons from the remaining half spread over the entire tectum: the map expands (Yoon 1972). In both cases the map remains continuous and topographic, but the position of an axon on the tectum depends on which other axons are present. The matching of labels must therefore be relative, a conclusion supported by the knock-in experiments in which a fraction of the ganglion cells receives an extra EphA receptor ([7.5.21](/7.5.21#sec-21.5.2)).

## 21.2. Gradient Matching with Competition

The following model, a simplified form of the models of Gierer (1983) and Koulakov and Tsigankov (2004), reproduces compression and expansion. The *ephrins* are membrane-bound ligands of the Eph receptor tyrosine kinases: ephrin-As are anchored to the membrane by GPI and bind mainly EphA receptors, ephrin-Bs are transmembrane proteins that bind EphB receptors, and both signal into the receptor-bearing cell (forward signaling) and into the ligand-bearing cell (reverse signaling). Axons are labeled by the level of the receptor EphA, which rises from nasal to temporal retina, and the tectum by the level of ephrin-A, which rises from anterior to posterior; forward signaling repels axons from tectal sites with a high ligand level in proportion to their receptor level ([7.5.21](/7.5.21#sec-21.5.1)). Axons also compete for tectal territory, so that terminals cannot pile up on one site.

**Formula 21.2.1 (Repulsion and Competition in a One-Dimensional Map)** Let $n$ axons be ordered by decreasing receptor level $R_1 \ge \dots \ge R_n$, with $R_k = u_k$ the retinal position (temporal $= 1$), and let axon $k$ terminate at tectal position $x_k$ with $x_1 < \dots < x_n$, all within the available interval $[a, b]$. With the ligand level $L(x) = x$, the energy of the map is

$$
E = \alpha\sum_{k=1}^n R_k\,x_k + \beta\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\frac{1}{x_{k+1} - x_k}, \tag{21.1}
$$
where $\alpha$ measures the strength of repulsion and $\beta$ that of competition (the second term penalizes a high density of terminals, $\int\rho^2\,\mathrm{d}x$ in the continuum), and the map is the minimum of $E$.

The first term is lowest when strongly repelled axons (high $R$) are placed at low $L$, that is anteriorly: it enforces the inverted order of the map. The second term spreads the terminals out. Their balance explains the compression and expansion experiments.

**Theorem 21.2.1 (Compression and Expansion)** For $\alpha = 0$, the minimum of (21.1) is the map with equal spacing that fills the whole available interval: $x_k = a + (k-1)(b-a)/(n-1)$. In particular, removing the posterior half of the tectum ($[a, b] = [0, \tfrac12]$) compresses the map of the entire retina into the anterior half, and removing the temporal half of the retina, which leaves $n/2$ axons, expands the remaining axons over the whole tectum, with the spacing approximately doubled ($(b-a)/(n/2-1)$ instead of $(b-a)/(n-1)$).

**Proof** Let $\Delta_k = x_{k+1} - x_k > 0$. By the inequality between the arithmetic and the harmonic mean, $\sum_k 1/\Delta_k \ge (n-1)^2/\sum_k\Delta_k$, and $\sum_k\Delta_k = x_n - x_1 \le b - a$. Hence $E \ge \beta(n-1)^2/(b-a)$, with equality if and only if all $\Delta_k$ are equal and $x_1 = a$, $x_n = b$. $\square$

For $\alpha > 0$ the repulsion pushes the high-$R$ axons toward the anterior end and leaves the low-$R$ axons less constrained, so that the terminals are denser anteriorly; for small $\alpha/\beta$ the deviation from the linear map is slight, for large $\alpha/\beta$ the map becomes strongly nonlinear (panel A of the figure). The model does not contradict chemoaffinity, since the order of the map is set by the gradients, but it makes the scale of the map a property of the whole population. The relative-signaling experiments of [7.5.21](/7.5.21#sec-21.5.2) are another consequence: only the rank order of the receptor levels matters. In vivo, forward and reverse signaling, branch formation along the length of the axon and refinement by activity contribute as well.

## 21.3. The Optic Chiasm

At the chiasm the axons of the two optic nerves meet, and each ganglion cell axon decides whether to cross or to stay on the same side. In animals with laterally placed eyes almost all axons cross, in animals with frontal eyes a substantial fraction does not, so that the visual fields of both eyes are combined in the same hemisphere.

| Species | Fraction of axons that do not cross | Binocular zone |
|---|---|---|
| Most fish, larval amphibians, most birds | 0 or nearly 0 | None or small |
| Mouse | about 3–5 % | Small, in the temporal visual field |
| Cat | about 30 % | About $90^\circ$ |
| Macaque, human | slightly below 50 % | Most of the visual field |

The decision is made by the cell's origin: axons from the ventrotemporal retina are those that stay ipsilateral; they express the receptor EphB1 and the transcription factor Zic2, and are repelled by ephrin-B2 on the radial glia at the midline ([7.5.21](/7.5.21#sec-21.5.1)); their number sets the extent of the binocular zone. The consequence for map formation is that two maps must be built in the same target, one from each eye, and brought into register: a point in space must be represented at the same place by the left and the right eye. The molecular cues of Section 21.2 cannot guarantee this to a precision of one terminal arbor, which is part of the reason why activity is needed.

## 21.4. Experience and the Critical Period

### 21.4.1. Monocular Deprivation

In the 1960s Hubel and Wiesel closed one eyelid of newborn kittens and, after several weeks, recorded from cells in V1. They grouped cells by ocular dominance into seven classes, from class 1 (driven only by the contralateral eye) through class 4 (equal) to class 7 (driven only by the ipsilateral eye). In normal animals most cells are binocular, with a bias toward the contralateral eye (the first panel of the figure below). After closure of one eye, the great majority of cells responded only to the open eye; the animals were behaviorally blind in the deprived eye even though the retina and the LGN were normal (Wiesel and Hubel 1963). In monkeys, labeling with a transneuronal tracer showed that the stripes of layer 4 that belong to the deprived eye shrink, and those of the open eye widen (Hubel, Wiesel and LeVay 1977): the ocular dominance columns of [7.16.18](/7.16.18#sec-18.3.1) are the result of competition, not just of anatomy.

The effect depends on age. In the cat the sensitive window opens abruptly about four weeks after birth and closes gradually by about twelve weeks; a few days of closure in the fifth week is devastating, whereas months of closure in an adult have almost no effect. This window is the **critical period** of ocular dominance plasticity. The damage caused within it is largely irreversible by simply reopening the eye, unless the other eye is closed (reverse suture) within the window.

### 21.4.2. Competition, Not Disuse

Closing both eyes has a much milder effect on ocular dominance than closing one: although many cells respond abnormally, most remain driven by both eyes, and the ocular dominance distribution stays close to normal (Wiesel and Hubel 1965). The loss of the deprived eye's input is therefore not the result of its inactivity alone, but of its inactivity relative to the other eye: the two sets of afferents compete for postsynaptic targets, and the input with the weaker correlation with the postsynaptic cell loses.

The same principle underlies the segregation of the afferents in normal development ([7.16.22](/7.16.22#sec-22.5)). In humans, an imbalance between the eyes in childhood (strabismus, a unilateral cataract or large differences in refraction) leads to *amblyopia*, a loss of vision in the weaker eye that cannot be corrected by glasses later in life; this is the clinical form of monocular deprivation, and the treatment is to patch the dominant eye in childhood.

### 21.4.3. What Opens and Closes the Window

The critical period is controlled by the maturation of inhibition. Mice lacking the synthetic enzyme GAD65 for GABA have a reduced inhibitory drive and no critical period for monocular deprivation; plasticity appears when inhibition is raised pharmacologically with a benzodiazepine, and only within a window of age (Hensch et al. 1998; Fagiolini and Hensch 2000). In wild-type mice the critical period starts at about postnatal day 19–21, has its peak at about day 28 and ends at about day 35–40, in step with the maturation of the fast-spiking parvalbumin (PV) interneurons that provide perisomatic inhibition.

Dark rearing delays both the maturation of inhibition and the closing of the window. The picture that has emerged is that plasticity requires an inhibition that exceeds a lower threshold (onset), which makes the cortical response competitive, and is terminated when it exceeds an upper threshold, together with the accumulation of molecular brakes: perineuronal nets of extracellular matrix around PV cells, myelin-associated proteins acting through the Nogo receptor, and Lynx1, a negative modulator of nicotinic signaling. These are schematic thresholds, not measured values.

### 21.4.4. Reopening

The adult cortex is not strictly closed. Removal of perineuronal nets with chondroitinase, chronic treatment with fluoxetine, a period of dark exposure and an enriched environment all restore ocular dominance plasticity and allow recovery from amblyopia in adult rodents (Pizzorusso et al. 2002; He, Hodos and Quinlan 2006; Sale et al. 2007; Maya Vetencourt et al. 2008), in most cases by reducing intracortical inhibition. Translation to humans is under study; the improvement of amblyopia in adults by perceptual learning and by video-game training is modest. The critical period is thus better described as a period in which plasticity is high and the brakes are not yet in place, than as a point of no return.

## 21.5. Spontaneous Activity: Retinal Waves

Activity can sharpen the map before the eyes open, since a developing retina is spontaneously active. Recordings from retinal explants with multielectrode arrays and calcium imaging show retinal waves: a patch of ganglion cells fires, the activity spreads to the neighbors and stops after a few hundred micrometers; the next wave starts elsewhere, about once per minute (Meister et al. 1991; Feller et al. 1996). In the mouse, the waves of the first postnatal week are driven by the cholinergic starburst amacrine cells acting on nicotinic receptors of ganglion cells (second stage); from about postnatal day 10 until eye opening, glutamate released by the bipolar cells takes over, as the circuits mature (third stage). The wave properties relevant for the wiring are statistical: nearby ganglion cells of the same eye are active together, distant cells are not, and cells of the two eyes are independent.

**Definition 21.5.1 (Retinal Wave)** A **retinal wave** is a spontaneous, propagating burst of activity that spreads across a patch of neighboring retinal ganglion cells in the developing retina, before photoreceptors drive the circuit.

The generation of waves can be described by an excitable medium: each cell is either at rest, active or refractory, resting cells are excited by active neighbors and at a low rate spontaneously, and a long refractory period makes the waves finite, because a patch that has fired cannot fire again for tens of seconds. The first-order statistics are then those of the figure: the size of the waves is set by the ratio of the coupling to the refractory time, the correlation of the activity decays over a distance comparable with the wave size, and independent eyes have zero correlation.

![Retinal waves in a one-dimensional excitable medium (400 cells; each cell rests, is active for 3 steps, then refractory for 150 steps; a resting cell fires spontaneously with probability $10^{-5}$ per step, or with probability 0.32 per step if a cell within 2 positions is active). A: raster of one retina (cell position against time): waves are short oblique streaks that start at random places and stop after tens of cells. In ferrets, whose retina is less mature at birth than in the cat, the segregation of the afferents of the two eyes in the LGN occurs during the first ten postnatal days, before the eyes open. The injection of epibatidine, an agonist that desensitizes nicotinic receptors and blocks cholinergic waves, prevented the layers from forming, so that axons of the two eyes remained intermingled; injection into one eye only made its axons lose territory to the other eye (Penn et al. 1998). Mice lacking the $\beta2$ subunit of the nicotinic receptor have disturbed retinal waves and incomplete eye-specific segregation and retinotopic refinement (Rossi et al. 2001; McLaughlin et al. 2003). Retinal waves thus supply the correlation structure that the Hebbian mechanism of the next section needs: the activity of neighbors is correlated, whereas the activity of the two eyes is not, and that is sufficient for the axons of each eye to gather in a territory of their own.

## 21.6. Hebb's Rule and the NMDA Receptor

**Definition 21.6.1 (Hebb's Rule)** **Hebb's rule** states that a synapse is strengthened when the presynaptic neuron participates in firing the postsynaptic neuron, that is, when the two are active together (Hebb 1949). Its modern form, summarized as "cells that fire together wire together" (Shatz 1992), adds that the synapse is weakened when the activities are uncorrelated. In the developing visual system the rule, applied to correlated inputs, leads to the strengthening of synapses between axons that fire together and the elimination of those that do not.

The rule needs a molecular detector of coincidence. The NMDA receptor is one: it is a glutamate-gated channel that is blocked by $\mathrm{Mg}^{2+}$ at the resting potential and conducts only if the postsynaptic membrane is depolarized at the same time as glutamate is bound; its calcium entry is the signal for synaptic strengthening ([7.16.10](/7.16.10), [7.16.14](/7.16.14)). Since the NMDA receptor reports the conjunction of presynaptic release and postsynaptic depolarization, it implements Hebb's rule at the level of a synapse. The prediction that the NMDA receptor is required for activity-dependent refinement has been confirmed in several systems. Blocking NMDA receptors desegregates the eye-specific stripes in the tectum of three-eyed frogs (Cline, Debski and Constantine-Paton 1987) and reduces ocular dominance plasticity in the kitten visual cortex (Kleinschmidt, Bear and Singer 1987).

In the mouse somatosensory cortex, the whisker-related barrels of layer 4 are formed by thalamocortical axons, one barrel for each whisker (Woolsey and Van der Loos 1970); the barrel pattern fails to form in mice in which the NR1 (GluN1) subunit is removed from cortical neurons alone (Iwasato et al. 2000), and cutting the whisker follicle in the first days after birth makes the corresponding barrel shrink or disappear, within a critical period of a few days. The barrel cortex is thereby a model for the visual cortex, with a clear anatomical read-out. The quantitative form of Hebb's rule and its use for the development of maps are discussed in [7.16.22](/7.16.22).

## 21.7. Molecules and Activity Together

Neither mechanism alone suffices. Mice lacking ephrin-A2, -A3 and -A5 keep a coarse retinotopic order but have ectopic and diffuse terminal zones, and mice lacking the $\beta2$ subunit keep the order but have terminal zones that are too broad; in mice with both mutations the map along the nasotemporal axis is nearly abolished (Pfeiffenberger et al. 2006). The most natural interpretation is that the molecular cues provide the long-range order, and activity-dependent competition provides the precision, by removing the terminals that are not correlated with their neighbors; the two mechanisms are partly redundant. In the model of Section 21.2, the gradients correspond to the energy of repulsion, while the competition term would be implemented by activity; in the model of [7.16.22](/7.16.22#sec-22.5) the competition is the consequence of a Hebbian rule with normalization.

## 21.8. Connection Specificity in Other Systems

The same principles, molecular matching, competition and activity-dependent pruning, are used throughout the nervous system, with variations that are best discussed with the examples.

- *Olfactory axons sort by receptor.* Each olfactory sensory neuron expresses one odorant receptor gene, and the axons of all neurons that express the same receptor converge on one or two glomeruli of the olfactory bulb. The identity of the receptor, and not the position of the neuron in the epithelium, determines the glomerulus: replacing the coding sequence of one receptor with another redirects the axons to a new glomerulus (Mombaerts et al. 1996), and the level of cAMP signaling by the receptor sets the expression of the guidance molecules neuropilin-1 and semaphorin-3A, so that receptor activity is used for the axon's address (Imai et al. 2006; [7.16.23](/7.16.23)).
- *Self-avoidance by recognition molecules.* Branches of the same neuron must not synapse with each other but must cover the territory independently of the other neurons of the same type. In *Drosophila*, the gene *Dscam1* generates by alternative splicing 38,016 isoforms, with 19,008 variants of the extracellular domain ($12\times48\times33$ choices for three variable immunoglobulin domains), which bind homophilically and only if all three variable domains are identical; each neuron expresses a random subset of isoforms, so that sister branches recognize one another and repel while branches of other neurons almost never share all their isoforms. In vertebrates the clustered protocadherins play an analogous role.
- *Synapse formation and elimination.* Synapses are initially formed in excess, and the excess is removed. At the **neuromuscular junction** of newborn mammals each muscle fiber is contacted by several motor axons, and within two weeks all but one are eliminated, by a competition in which the more active axon wins and the others withdraw (Lichtman and colleagues); the loss of climbing-fiber synapses on Purkinje cells is similar. In the human cerebral cortex synaptic density increases rapidly after birth and peaks in early childhood, and then falls during childhood and adolescence to the adult level (Huttenlocher 1979). The competition for neurotrophins that helps to explain the numbers and the selection of axons is a model that uses the same nonlinear positive feedback as the models of this chapter ([7.5.21](/7.5.21#sec-21.7.2)).

## Questions

1. In the model of Formula 21.2.1 with $\alpha = 0$, the whole retina ($n = 41$ axons) is mapped onto a tectum of length 1. What is the spacing of the terminals? What is it after the posterior 60 % of the tectum has been removed, and after the nasal half of the retina has been removed (the temporal 21 axons remain, tectum of length 1)?

   **Answer** By Theorem 21.2.1 the spacing is $(b-a)/(n-1)$. Normal: $1/40 = 0.025$. Posterior 60 % removed ($b - a = 0.4$): $0.4/40 = 0.01$, a compression by a factor of 2.5 in the spacing. Nasal half removed ($n = 21$): $1/20 = 0.05$, an expansion by a factor of 2. In both cases the order is that of the retina.

2. Why does closing both eyes of a kitten during the critical period affect the cortex less than closing one eye? What does it say about the mechanism?

   **Answer** If the mechanism were disuse (atrophy of the synapses of inactive afferents), closing both eyes would be at least as harmful. Instead, the cells keep their responses to both eyes, whereas after monocular closure nearly all cells are driven only by the open eye. The deprived afferents lose because they compete with active afferents of the other eye for the same postsynaptic cells; when both are deprived, neither has an advantage. This is the signature of a competitive, correlation-based (Hebbian) rule in which synapses are strengthened by correlated activity with the postsynaptic cell and weakened otherwise.

3. In the retinal wave model of the figure, a wave covers a patch of about 30 cells. Two ganglion cells in the same retina lie 5 and 40 cell spacings apart. Which pair will have the stronger correlation of activity, and what does a Hebbian rule do with the synapses of these cells onto a common tectal neuron?

   **Answer** The pair 5 spacings apart: the correlation falls from 1 to about 0 within roughly 15 spacings (panel B), so that the pair at 40 spacings is not correlated. By Hebb's rule the synapses of the cells at 5 spacings, which fire together, are strengthened on the same target neuron and become stabilized as neighbors; the cells at 40 spacings are never active together and their synapses on a common target are weakened and removed. The result is a map with a finer resolution than the molecular cues, and the segregation of the two eyes follows the same argument, with the correlation between eyes equal to zero.

4. (Open question) Design an experiment, with a genetically modified mouse, to distinguish whether the critical period of ocular dominance plasticity is closed by the maturation of PV inhibition or by the accumulation of perineuronal nets.

   **Answer** One design: delay the maturation of PV cells (dark rearing or a conditional knockout of a gene that is necessary for it) and measure the critical period; if the period closes only after the PV interneurons mature, it is controlled by inhibition. In a second group, degrade perineuronal nets enzymatically in adult animals without altering the inhibitory strength (monitored by whole-cell recordings of inhibitory currents) and measure whether plasticity returns. If plasticity returns with unchanged inhibition, nets act in parallel; if it requires a decrease in inhibition, they act through it. Intermediate outcomes (both manipulations partly restoring plasticity) are probable, and a factorial design is needed to estimate their interaction.
