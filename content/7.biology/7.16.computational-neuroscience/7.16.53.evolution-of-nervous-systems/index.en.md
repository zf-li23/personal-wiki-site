Every chapter of this course has described the nervous system of a few model animals, mostly mammals, flies and worms. This chapter asks where these systems came from. Evolution explains why neurons use the molecules they do, why the same **circuit motifs** and maps recur in distant animals, and why some solutions (myelin, camera eyes, centralized brains) appear several times. It also gives the most powerful comparative method of neuroscience: the differences between species are natural experiments, and their genetic bases can now be identified and tested. We follow the order of increasing complexity: the origin of neurons ([7.16.2](/7.16.2)), of channels and synapses ([7.16.4](/7.16.4), [7.16.9](/7.16.9)), of sensory receptors ([7.16.17](/7.16.17), [7.16.23](/7.16.23)), of body plans and brains, and of behavior ([7.16.36](/7.16.36)). The methods of phylogenetics and the population genetics of gene duplication are in [7.10](/7.10), in particular [7.10.2](/7.10.2) and [7.10.18](/7.10.18); the developmental mechanisms of brain growth are in [7.5.19](/7.5.19); brain size and its scaling across vertebrates in [7.14.46](/7.14.46#sec-46.8.2).

## 53.1. The Origin of Nervous Systems

### 53.1.1. Animals With and Without Neurons

Comparisons between species are interpreted on a **phylogenetic tree**: a trait shared by two species because their common ancestor had it is a **homology**, a trait that arose independently in two lineages is a product of **convergent evolution**, and a trait can be lost as well as gained ([7.10.2](/7.10.2#sec-2.3)). Among the earliest-branching animal lineages, sponges (Porifera) and placozoans have no **neurons**, while comb jellies (Ctenophora) and cnidarians (jellyfish, sea anemones, *Hydra*) have them, organized as nerve nets.

**Definition 53.1.1 (Nerve Net)** A **nerve net** is a diffuse nervous system in which neurons are distributed over the body wall, connected by synapses (or, in some ctenophores, by fusion into a syncytium) into a network without a central ganglion; excitation spreads from any point in all directions.

Nerve nets conduct slowly and integrate little, but they coordinate the contraction of a body wall, the beating of a bell or the swimming of comb rows. Cnidarians already have condensations of the net (nerve rings at the margin of the jellyfish bell) and pacemaker neurons, and their neurons use **neuropeptides** abundantly, besides classical transmitters. The shared view was that neurons originated once, in the common ancestor of cnidarians and bilaterians, after the sponges had diverged.

Genome sequencing of ctenophores (*Mnemiopsis leidyi*, Ryan and colleagues, 2013; *Pleurobrachia bachei*, Moroz and colleagues, 2014) put this view in doubt. Most phylogenomic analyses, and the conservation of chromosomal gene linkages (Schultz and colleagues, 2023), place ctenophores, not sponges, as the sister group of all other animals (figure below). If so, either the nervous system arose before the ctenophores diverged and was lost in sponges and placozoans, or neurons arose twice, once in ctenophores and once in the ancestors of cnidarians and bilaterians. The second possibility is supported by the peculiarities of ctenophore neurons: they lack the genes for the synthesis of most classical transmitters (**serotonin**, **dopamine**, **norepinephrine**, **acetylcholine**), rely on glutamate and dozens of neuropeptides, and the subepithelial nerve net of *Mnemiopsis* is a syncytium of fused cells rather than a network of synapses (Burkhardt and colleagues, 2023). Neither alternative is settled, and they illustrate two principles of evolution: losses are as important as gains, and similar solutions can be assembled independently from common parts.

The "animals without neurons" are not simply simpler. Sponges have most postsynaptic scaffold proteins and, in the freshwater sponge *Spongilla*, "neuroid" cells that extend processes to the feeding chambers and express secretory machinery (Musser and colleagues, 2021). Placozoans, which glide on cilia, have peptidergic secretory cells whose peptides change the animal's behavior and whose gene expression programs resemble those of neuronal progenitors (Najle and colleagues, 2023). Neurons may therefore have arisen from secretory cells that already communicated by released signals, the gain being the directed, fast and local signaling through synapses.

## 53.2. Channels, Myelin and Synapses

### 53.2.1. The Stepwise Origin of Ion Channels

The voltage-gated **ion channels** of the action potential ([7.16.4](/7.16.4), [7.16.5](/7.16.5)) arose in sequence, by **gene duplication** and divergence. Potassium channels are the oldest: bacteria have K⁺ channels with two transmembrane segments around a pore, and others with six, in which segments S1–S4 form a voltage sensor; their function in prokaryotes is the same as in neurons, setting a negative resting potential. Calcium channels came next. Two rounds of duplication and fusion of a six-segment channel gene produced the 24-segment channel of four homologous domains, already present in yeast and green algae, which made $\ce{Ca^2+}$ a signaling ion in all eukaryotes. Sodium channels derive from calcium channels: each of the four domains of a sodium channel is more similar to the corresponding domain of a calcium channel than to the other domains of its own protein, so the duplications that made the four-domain channel preceded the split between the two.

Choanoflagellates already have a sodium channel homolog, but its selectivity filter suggests that it conducts $\ce{Ca^2+}$ as well as $\ce{Na+}$; channels selective for $\ce{Na+}$ evolved at least twice, in bilaterians (the DEKA filter of vertebrate Na$_{\mathrm{V}}$ channels) and in one group of cnidarians. Sodium action potentials freed calcium from carrying electrical signals, so that its concentration could be reserved for signaling (release, plasticity, gene expression), and allowed large, fast spikes, since cells tolerate large fluxes of $\ce{Na+}$ far better than of $\ce{Ca^2+}$. The sodium channel was later lost in nematodes: *C. elegans* neurons, in a body of 1 mm, signal by graded potentials and calcium spikes. Within vertebrates, duplicated channel genes acquired new functions (**neofunctionalization**): in two unrelated groups of electric fish, mormyrids and gymnotiforms, one of the two teleost copies of the muscle sodium channel gene stopped being expressed in muscle, became expressed in the electric organ and accumulated amino acid changes that alter its kinetics, a molecular case of convergent evolution (Zakon and colleagues, 2006).

### 53.2.2. Myelin, a Convergent Solution

Large animals need fast conduction over long distances. An unmyelinated axon conducts at a speed proportional to the square root of its diameter, a myelinated one at a speed roughly proportional to the diameter (**conduction velocity**, [7.14.4](/7.14.4)), and the difference becomes large quickly.

**Example 53.2.1** The squid giant axon, about $0.5\,\mathrm{mm}$ in diameter, conducts at about $25\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. A mammalian myelinated axon conducts at about $6\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ per micrometer of outer diameter, so a $10\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ fiber reaches $60\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. How thick must an unmyelinated axon be to do the same?

With $v = k\sqrt{d}$ and $k$ fixed by the squid axon, $d = 0.5\,\mathrm{mm} \times (60/25)^2 \approx 2.9\,\mathrm{mm}$, almost 300 times the diameter and about $8 \times 10^4$ times the cross-sectional area of the myelinated fiber. A human optic nerve with its million axons built in this way would be meters thick. Myelin, which raises the membrane resistance, lowers its capacitance and confines active currents to the nodes (**saltatory conduction**), buys speed with volume and with energy, since only the nodes need to restore their ion gradients.

:::

**Myelin** formed by **glial cells** appeared in vertebrates once, in the jawed fishes (lampreys and hagfish have no myelin). Glial sheaths of similar appearance evolved independently in several invertebrate groups, in annelids, in calanoid copepods and in penaeid shrimps, whose myelinated giant fibers conduct at over $100\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, faster than any vertebrate axon. Their proteins and the structure of their nodes differ from those of vertebrate myelin and from one another, so the resemblance is a convergence driven by the same physics ([7.6.30](/7.6.30)). The other solution, giant axons for escape reflexes, also arose many times: the squid giant axon, the giant fibers of crayfish, earthworms and *Drosophila*, and the Mauthner cells of fish.

### 53.2.3. Synapses from Junctions and Secretion

A **chemical synapse** is an asymmetric cell junction with a secretory specialization on one side and a receptive one on the other. Both parts are older than neurons (figure above). The vesicle fusion machinery is that of the general secretory pathway: **SNARE** proteins mediate all membrane fusion in eukaryotes, and in the early 1990s the studies of secretion in yeast and of transmitter release in neurons converged on the same protein families ([7.16.9](/7.16.9)). Homophilic adhesion molecules such as cadherins, present already in choanoflagellates, held cells together; the heterophilic pairs that give the synapse its polarity (neurexin and neuroligin, ephrin and Eph) appeared later, with cnidarians and bilaterians, whose synapses look alike in the electron microscope. Postsynaptic scaffolds (PSD-95, Homer, Shank) are present in choanoflagellates, metabotropic glutamate receptors first appear in sponges, and the ionotropic glutamate receptors expanded in ctenophores, which use glutamate as a main transmitter. Many of these proteins may have served first to sense chemicals in the environment.

**Electrical synapses** formed by **gap junctions** arose twice. Invertebrates use innexins; vertebrates use **connexins**, a chordate innovation, while their innexin homologs (the pannexins) no longer form gap junctions. The overall pattern is one of recruitment: the first nervous systems were assembled mostly from proteins that already existed, with new components added as signaling became more elaborate, the "tinkering" of evolution in François Jacob's phrase.

## 53.3. The Evolution of Chemical Senses

### 53.3.1. From Bacteria to Animals

Chemosensation is the oldest sense. *Escherichia coli* swims up gradients of attractants by a biased random walk: runs are prolonged when the concentration rises and interrupted by tumbles when it falls, which requires the bacterium to compare the concentration now with that a moment ago, because its body is too short to compare its two ends ([7.6.35](/7.6.35#sec-35.2)). Receptor clusters amplify the signal so that it can respond to about $10\,\mathrm{nM}$ (some ten molecules in the volume of a cell), and methylation of the receptors adapts them over a $10^5$-fold range of concentrations. **Chemotaxis** in animals faces the same problems of detection, amplification and adaptation: *C. elegans* uses the same temporal strategy (runs and turns) together with head swings that sample the gradient in space, and insects and mammals compare the signals of two antennae or two nostrils.

**G protein–coupled receptors** are found in all eukaryotes but not in prokaryotes. The budding yeast has only three: two receptors for the peptide pheromones of the opposite mating type, which trigger a MAP kinase cascade and polarized growth, and a glucose receptor that activates adenylyl cyclase. The signaling modules of these unicellular receptors (trimeric G proteins, kinase cascades, small GTPases) are those of neuronal GPCRs, and their ancestral function was the sensing of nutrients and **pheromones**. Vertebrate olfaction, taste of sweet, bitter and umami and vision all use GPCRs; the roughly 800 GPCR genes of the human genome are about half chemosensory.

### 53.3.2. Insects: Ionotropic Odorant Receptors

The **odorant receptors** of insects have seven transmembrane segments, like those of vertebrates ([7.16.23](/7.16.23#sec-23.1)), but they are unrelated to GPCRs: their topology is inverted, with the N terminus inside the cell, and each receptor forms a heteromeric ligand-gated cation channel with a common coreceptor, Orco. A second family, the ionotropic receptors of *Drosophila*, derives from **ionotropic receptor** subunits for glutamate and detects acids and amines. Insects thus sense odors with ion channels while vertebrates and nematodes use GPCRs, perhaps because a channel responds faster than a second-messenger cascade, which matters for an animal that tracks odor plumes in flight. Despite the different molecules, the organization of the two olfactory systems is the same: one receptor per sensory neuron and convergence of all neurons expressing the same receptor onto one glomerulus, a convergent solution to the problem of representing a large chemical space.

### 53.3.3. Expansion and Loss of Receptor Families

Chemoreceptor genes form the largest and most dynamic **gene families** of animal genomes: about $1100$ intact odorant receptor genes in the mouse, about $400$ in humans, about $2000$ in the African elephant, and about $1300$ chemoreceptor genes in *C. elegans*; toothed whales have lost almost all of theirs. Such families evolve by repeated duplication (birth) and inactivation (death) of their members, with inactivated copies remaining for a while as **pseudogenes** ([7.10.18](/7.10.18#sec-18.2)).

**Formula 53.3.1 (Birth–Death Model of a Gene Family)** Let each intact gene duplicate at rate $\beta$ and become a pseudogene at rate $\mu$, and let pseudogenes be deleted at rate $\delta$. The number $N(t)$ of intact genes is a linear **birth–death process**, and with $r = \beta - \mu$, starting from $N_0$ genes,
$$
\mathbb{E}[N(t)] = N_0\mathrm{e}^{rt}, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(N(t)) = N_0\frac{\beta + \mu}{\beta - \mu}\,\mathrm{e}^{rt}\bigl(\mathrm{e}^{rt} - 1\bigr). \tag{53.1}
$$
The expected number $\Psi$ of pseudogenes obeys $\mathrm{d}\Psi/\mathrm{d}t = \mu N - \delta\Psi$ and approaches the ratio $\Psi/N = \mu/(\delta + r)$ when $\delta + r > 0$.

The model makes two points (figure below). First, the size of a family is the product of two rates acting over tens of millions of years: a difference of $0.01$ per million years between birth and death changes the repertoire by a factor of 2.5 in 90 million years. Second, the pseudogene fraction measures the death rate relative to the turnover: the human repertoire, about half pseudogenes, has lost genes faster than it gained them, while the mouse repertoire, with about a fifth, has been nearly stable. The loss in primates was once attributed to the evolution of trichromacy, which might have made smell less important (Gilad and colleagues, 2004), but the pseudogene fraction did not prove to correlate with color vision when more species were sequenced (Matsui, Go and Niimura, 2010). The repertoire follows ecology: aquatic mammals have lost olfactory receptors, and the stochastic spread of Eq. (53.1) means that two lineages with identical rates also differ by chance.

## 53.4. Cell Types, Eyes and Color Vision

### 53.4.1. The Diversification of Cell Types

New functions require new **cell types**, and the main route to them is the duplication of a cell type followed by divergence: a sister cell type inherits the gene regulatory program of its parent and modifies part of it, as duplicated genes do. The diversification can be followed in the photoreceptors.

**Definition 53.4.1 (Opsin)** An **opsin** is a GPCR that binds the chromophore 11-*cis*-retinal by a Schiff base to a lysine of its seventh transmembrane helix; absorption of a photon isomerizes retinal to all-*trans* and activates the receptor. Opsins with their chromophore are the visual pigments of all animals, such as the **rhodopsin** of vertebrate rods.

**Definition 53.4.2 (Ciliary and Rhabdomeric Photoreceptors)** A **ciliary photoreceptor** stores its opsin in membranes derived from a cilium and transduces light through a transducin-type G protein and a cGMP cascade that closes channels and hyperpolarizes the cell (vertebrate rods and cones). A **rhabdomeric photoreceptor** stores its opsin in microvilli and transduces light through $\mathrm{G_q}$ and phospholipase C, opening TRP channels and depolarizing the cell (the photoreceptors of insect and cephalopod eyes).

Both types existed in the common ancestor of bilaterians: the marine annelid *Platynereis* has rhabdomeric photoreceptors in its eyes and ciliary ones in its brain (Arendt and colleagues, 2004). Vertebrates built their eyes from ciliary photoreceptors, while the rhabdomeric lineage, with its opsin melanopsin, survives in the intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells that set the circadian clock ([7.16.17](/7.16.17)). Eye development also shares a regulator across phyla: the transcription factor Pax6 (*eyeless* in *Drosophila*) is required for eyes in flies, mice and humans, and the mouse gene induces ectopic compound eyes when expressed in fly imaginal discs (Halder, Callaerts and Gehring, 1995), a case of **deep homology** ([7.5.39](/7.5.39#sec-39.3)) in which the regulatory switch is conserved although the eyes themselves evolved independently.

**Example 53.4.1** Darwin found the eye the hardest organ to explain by natural selection. Nilsson and Pelger (1994) estimated how long it would take to evolve a camera eye from a flat patch of photoreceptors over a pigment layer, by small steps each of which improves spatial resolution: invagination into a cup, narrowing of the aperture and a graded-index lens. The whole sequence required a total change of the morphology equivalent to $1829$ steps of $1\,\%$. Assuming a heritability of $0.5$, a coefficient of variation of $1\,\%$ and a selection intensity of $0.01$, the response to selection is $0.005\,\%$ per generation, and the number of generations is

$$
n = \frac{1829\,\ln 1.01}{\ln 1.00005} \approx 3.6 \times 10^5.
$$
With one generation a year, fewer than half a million years suffice, a geological instant compared with the 500 million years since the Cambrian; eyes appear in the fossil record soon after the Cambrian radiation and have evolved independently many times.

### 53.4.2. Trichromatic Vision in Primates

Ancestral vertebrates had four classes of cone opsins, SWS1 (ultraviolet), SWS2 (blue), RH2 (green) and LWS (red), probably products of the two rounds of **whole-genome duplication** at the origin of vertebrates, and the rod opsin arose from a further duplication in jawed vertebrates. Many fishes, reptiles and birds retain all four and are tetrachromatic. During the long nocturnal phase of early mammalian evolution, two classes were lost, so that most mammals are dichromatic.

**Definition 53.4.3 (Dichromacy and Tetrachromacy)** An animal is **dichromatic** when its color vision rests on two classes of cones with different spectral sensitivities, so that any light can be matched by a mixture of two primaries, and **tetrachromatic** when it rests on four; **trichromacy** rests on three.

The **trichromacy** of humans ([7.16.17](/7.16.17)) is recent. In the ancestor of the catarrhines (Old World monkeys and apes), about 35 million years ago, the X-linked LWS gene was duplicated, probably by **unequal crossing over**, into adjacent genes for the M (peak near $530\,\mathrm{nm}$) and L (near $560\,\mathrm{nm}$) opsins; three amino acid substitutions in the transmembrane helices account for most of the spectral shift. New World monkeys show how this could have happened. Most of them have a single X-linked LWS gene with several alleles whose peaks range from about $535$ to $563\,\mathrm{nm}$. Males and homozygous females are dichromatic, but heterozygous females are trichromatic because **X-chromosome inactivation** expresses one allele in each cone. Howler monkeys duplicated the gene independently and are uniformly trichromatic, and their M and L alleles resemble the polymorphic alleles of other New World monkeys: the allelic diversity came first, and duplication fixed it on one chromosome (figure below).

Trichromacy needs two more things. Each cone must express only one of the two adjacent genes; in catarrhines a shared upstream locus control region pairs with one of them at random. The retina must also compare M and L signals; the midget pathway of the fovea does so because each midget bipolar cell contacts a single cone, so that the center of its receptive field is one cone class while its surround is mixed. The advantage of trichromacy is thought to be the detection of ripe fruits and young, reddish leaves against mature foliage, and the evolution of colored fruits and of trichromatic frugivores has been mutual.

The predicted gain in discrimination can be quantified from the cone spectra. Two lights that differ slightly in wavelength change the logarithms of the cone excitations $q_i$; in the receptor-noise-limited model (Vorobyev and Osorio, 1998) they are discriminable when the change in the differences of these logarithms exceeds the noise of the cones. For a dichromat with noise $\omega_i$ in cone class $i$,

$$
\Delta S = \frac{\lvert\Delta\ln q_1 - \Delta\ln q_2\rvert}{\sqrt{\omega_1^2 + \omega_2^2}}, \tag{53.2}
$$
and one just-noticeable difference is $\Delta S = 1$. A dichromat discriminates well only where the two sensitivity curves have different slopes, near $490\,\mathrm{nm}$ for S and L′ cones, and poorly at long wavelengths, where only one cone absorbs; a third cone class between the two restores discrimination over the middle and long wavelengths (panel C).

### 53.4.3. Adding a Cone Opsin

Two experiments simulated this step of evolution. Mice are dichromatic (an ultraviolet S cone and an M cone near $510\,\mathrm{nm}$, X-linked). Jacobs and colleagues (2007) replaced the mouse M gene by the human L gene on one X chromosome; heterozygous females, mosaics of M and L cones like New World monkey females, were trained to report which of three lights differed in color, and discriminated wavelengths between $500$ and $600\,\mathrm{nm}$ that normal mice could not. Mancuso and colleagues (2009) injected an adeno-associated virus carrying the human L opsin into the retina of adult male squirrel monkeys, which are dichromats; after about 20 weeks the monkeys discriminated colors, including near their former neutral point at about $490\,\mathrm{nm}$, that they had never distinguished. In both cases the brain used a new input without any genetic change in its circuits, presumably through the existing random wiring of the midget system and the plasticity of cortex. A single gene change in the receptor can therefore produce a new perceptual dimension at once, which makes the evolution of trichromacy easier to understand.

## 53.5. A Shared Body Plan

Bilaterians, animals with a left–right symmetric body and a central nervous system, have two main branches: protostomes (most invertebrates), whose nerve cord runs ventrally, and deuterostomes, which include the vertebrates, whose spinal cord is dorsal. In 1822 Étienne Geoffroy Saint-Hilaire proposed that an arthropod turned upside down resembles a vertebrate. Molecular embryology confirmed the idea: in the fly the ventral neuroectoderm is specified where the secreted antagonist Sog blocks Dpp, and in the frog the dorsal neuroectoderm where its homolog Chordin blocks BMP4, the homolog of Dpp ([7.5.16](/7.5.16#sec-16.6)); frog Chordin promotes ventral (neural) fate in fly embryos and fly Sog promotes dorsal fate in frogs. Within the nerve cord, homologous transcription factors (Vnd/Nkx2.2, Ind/Gsh, Msh/Msx) are expressed in the same order from the midline in the fly nerve cord and the vertebrate neural tube. The dorsoventral axis appears to have been inverted in an early chordate.

The anteroposterior axis is patterned in all bilaterians by **Hox genes** ([7.5.15](/7.5.15#sec-15.5)), transcription factors whose mutations cause **homeotic transformations** and whose order on the chromosome matches the order of their expression domains along the body. Vertebrates have four Hox clusters, from the same two genome duplications that produced the four cone opsin classes. Hox genes specify segmental identity in the hindbrain and spinal cord, including the pools of motor neurons that innervate limbs; in skates, which walk on their pelvic fins, the same Hox-dependent program that directs limb motor neurons in tetrapods directs fin motor neurons, suggesting that the circuits for walking predate the move to land (Jung and colleagues, 2018). Whether the last common bilaterian ancestor had a centralized nervous system is still debated: in several lineages that branch near the base of the bilaterians, the dorsoventral patterning genes are not expressed in the arrangement shared by flies and vertebrates (Martín-Durán and colleagues, 2018), so nerve cords may have been centralized more than once.

## 53.6. The Expansion of the Neocortex

### 53.6.1. From Dorsal Cortex to Neocortex

The **neocortex** is a mammalian innovation, homologous as a region to the dorsal cortex of reptiles, which has three layers with excitatory neurons concentrated in the middle one. Single-cell transcriptomes of turtle and lizard cortex show that their GABAergic interneuron classes correspond one to one to those of the mammalian neocortex, while their excitatory neurons mix markers of superficial and deep neocortical layers, so that the excitatory classes diversified separately in the two lineages from those of the common amniote ancestor (Tosches and colleagues, 2018). Since mammals diverged from reptiles, more than 300 million years ago, the neocortex has gained layers and areas and expanded enormously, largely after the extinction of the non-avian dinosaurs. It occupies about $75\,\%$ of the volume of the human brain, against about $40\,\%$ in the mouse lemur, a small primate, and $30\,\%$ in the rat.

The developmental basis of the expansion is in the progenitors ([7.5.19](/7.5.19#sec-19.3)): each additional round of symmetric divisions of the founder cells doubles the number of radial units and so the surface of the cortex, and primates add a large outer subventricular zone in which **outer radial glia** divide repeatedly and produce **intermediate progenitors**, multiplying the output per unit of ventricular surface ([7.5.19](/7.5.19#sec-19.5)). A large cortex of nearly constant thickness folds; gyri and sulci have arisen independently in many mammalian lineages, because folding follows from mechanical instability once the outer layer expands faster than the inner one. Brain size and neuron number scale with body size in ways that differ between rodents and primates ([7.14.46](/7.14.46#sec-46.8.2)); the cost of a large brain, the most expensive tissue of the body, constrains its evolution.

### 53.6.2. Human-Specific Genes

The human and chimpanzee genomes differ by about $35$ million single-nucleotide substitutions and $5$ million insertions and deletions, and the human brain has about three times as many neurons; most of the differences are neutral, and the task is to find those that matter ([7.10.33](/7.10.33#sec-33.4)). Candidates come from several sources.

| Change | Origin | Effect in experimental models |
|---|---|---|
| *SRGAP2C* | human-specific partial duplication of *SRGAP2*; the truncated protein inhibits the ancestral one | in mouse cortex, delays neuronal migration and spine maturation and increases spine density (Charrier and colleagues, 2012) |
| *ARHGAP11B* | human-specific partial duplication of *ARHGAP11A*; a point mutation creates a new protein through altered splicing | amplifies basal progenitors in mouse (Florio and colleagues, 2015) and ferret, and induces folding in marmoset (Heide and colleagues, 2020) |
| *NOTCH2NL* | human-specific paralogs of *NOTCH2* | delay the differentiation of radial glia and enlarge cortical **organoids** (Fiddes and colleagues; Suzuki and colleagues, 2018) |
| Deletion near *GADD45G* | one of 510 sequences conserved in other mammals but deleted in humans | the deleted enhancer drives expression of a cell-cycle inhibitor in the mouse subventricular zone; its loss may extend proliferation (McLean and colleagues, 2011) |

Modern humans differ from **Neanderthals** and **Denisovans** far less: about $30\,000$ single-nucleotide changes fixed in all modern humans, of which only 96 change the amino acid sequence of 87 proteins. One of them, a lysine-to-arginine substitution in the enzyme TKTL1, increases the production of basal radial glia in mouse and ferret cortex and in human organoids (Pinson and colleagues, 2022), but whether modern and archaic humans differed in neuron number is unknown. Brain organoids from human and chimpanzee stem cells, and genome editing in them, now allow such candidate changes to be tested directly in the cells of both species; the late stages of development, and the behavioral consequences, remain out of reach.

### 53.6.3. Areas Shaped by Their Inputs

How do new cortical areas acquire their functions? The coarse map of areas is set by morphogens and transcription factors during development, but the input tunes it. When retinal axons were redirected in newborn ferrets to the auditory thalamus, so that the primary auditory cortex received visual input, its neurons became orientation selective and formed an **orientation map** with **pinwheels** like that of visual cortex (Sharma, Angelucci and Sur, 2000), and the animals responded to light stimuli presented to the rewired pathway as visual (von Melchner, Pallas and Sur, 2000). In nature, species expand the cortical representations of the senses they rely on: echolocating bats have large auditory areas tuned to the frequencies of their calls, rodents have one barrel of somatosensory cortex per whisker ([7.16.21](/7.16.21)), and in the star-nosed mole, which hunts by touch with 22 fleshy appendages around its nostrils, the pair used for fine discrimination, like a fovea, has a disproportionately large cortical representation. A cortex whose modules organize themselves according to their input allows a change in the periphery, a new receptor or a new sensory organ, to be used immediately, as the trichromatic mice and monkeys of 53.4.3 show.

## 53.7. The Evolution of Behavior

Differences in behavior between closely related species are a test case for the genetic and neural basis of evolution. Courtship of *Drosophila* males is well suited ([7.16.36](/7.16.36)): it is innate, quantifiable and differs between species that must avoid hybridization.

- *A changed balance in a conserved circuit.* The female cuticular **pheromone** 7,11-heptacosadiene promotes courtship by *D. melanogaster* males and suppresses it in *D. simulans*, a species that diverged 2–3 million years ago. In both species the pheromone is detected by homologous gustatory neurons in the foreleg and relayed through an excitatory and an inhibitory pathway that converge on the P1 neurons that command courtship; in *D. melanogaster* the excitatory pathway prevails, in *D. simulans* the inhibitory one (Seeholzer and colleagues, 2018). In the language of a rate model, P1 activity is $r = [w_{\mathrm{E}}s - w_{\mathrm{I}}s]_+$ for a pheromone input $s$, and evolution changed the sign of $w_{\mathrm{E}} - w_{\mathrm{I}}$ without changing the wiring diagram.
- *A single mutation with a specific effect.* Males sing to females by vibrating one wing, and the carrier frequency of the sine song differs between *D. simulans* and *D. mauritiana*, which diverged about 240 000 years ago. Genetic mapping identified a **retrotransposon** inserted in an intron of *slowpoke*, the gene of a $\ce{Ca^2+}$-activated $\ce{K+}$ channel, in *D. simulans*; it changes the **alternative splicing** of an adjacent exon, and its precise removal by genome editing raised the song frequency by $8.7\,\mathrm{Hz}$, most of the species difference (Ding and colleagues, 2016). A gene required everywhere in the nervous system can thus be modified to change one parameter of one behavior.

These cases illustrate the principle of the chapter at the level of behavior: evolution rarely builds new circuits from nothing, but changes the strengths of connections, the expression of genes and the tuning of receptors within conserved architectures. The ecology and evolution of behavior itself (optimal foraging, evolutionarily stable strategies, communication) belong to the study of animal behavior; the neural mechanisms of sexual and social behavior are in [7.16.36](/7.16.36).

## Summary

- Neurons and nerve nets (Definition 53.1.1) exist in ctenophores and in cnidarians plus bilaterians but not in sponges and placozoans; with ctenophores as the sister of all other animals, neurons either arose once and were lost or arose twice. Many synaptic proteins and secretory cell types predate neurons.
- The molecular toolkit was assembled stepwise from older parts:

| Component | Origin | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| K⁺ channels | prokaryotes | ancestral |
| Ca$_{\mathrm{V}}$ channels, GPCRs, SNAREs | early eukaryotes | duplication and fusion; secretion |
| Na$_{\mathrm{V}}$ channels | from Ca$_{\mathrm{V}}$; Na⁺ selectivity twice | duplication, divergence |
| Postsynaptic scaffolds | choanoflagellates | recruitment |
| Neurexin–neuroligin, iGluRs of AMPA/NMDA type | cnidarians + bilaterians | new heterophilic adhesion |
| Myelin | jawed vertebrates; convergent in invertebrates | glial wrapping |
| Connexins | chordates | replaced innexins |

- Myelin is a convergent solution: it gives a velocity proportional to diameter, against the square root for bare axons (Example 53.2.1).
- Chemoreceptors: GPCRs began as nutrient and pheromone sensors of unicellular eukaryotes; insects evolved ionotropic odorant receptors. Receptor families change size by birth and death of genes (Formula 53.3.1); the pseudogene fraction records the balance.
- Opsins (Definition 53.4.1) serve two photoreceptor types (Definition 53.4.2); Pax6 is a deep homology of eyes; a camera eye can evolve in a few hundred thousand generations (Example 53.4.1). Mammals lost two of four cone classes; catarrhines regained trichromacy by duplicating LWS (Definition 53.4.3), and adding an L opsin gives dichromats a new dimension of color at once (Eq. (53.2)).
- Bilaterians share Hox-patterned anteroposterior axes and an inverted dorsoventral patterning system; the neocortex expanded through progenitor amplification, human-specific gene duplications and regulatory deletions, while areas are tuned by their input.
- Behavioral differences between species arise from changes in the balance of conserved circuits and in single genes.

## Questions

1. Using the dependence of conduction velocity on diameter, explain why myelin is an advantage mainly for animals with many long axons, and why a small animal such as *C. elegans* can do without voltage-gated sodium channels altogether.

   **Answer** For an unmyelinated axon $v \propto \sqrt{d}$, so doubling the speed costs four times the diameter and sixteen times the cross-sectional area; for a myelinated axon $v \propto d$, and the same doubling costs four times the area. An animal that needs many fast axons (a large vertebrate with millions of long fibers) cannot afford giant axons for all of them, so myelin pays off; an animal that needs one fast pathway for escape can use a giant axon. In a body $1\,\mathrm{mm}$ long, with neurons whose processes are short compared with their length constant, passive electrotonic spread and graded or calcium potentials reach the terminals in milliseconds (the delay over a length $\ell$ is of order $\tau_{\mathrm{m}}(\ell/\lambda)$ or less), so the regenerative, all-or-none sodium spike is not needed, and the cost of maintaining the channels and of restoring sodium gradients is saved.

2. In Formula 53.3.1, take $N_0 = 780$, $\beta = 0.012$ and $\mu = 0.019\,\mathrm{Myr^{-1}}$ for 90 million years. Compute the expected number of intact genes and its standard deviation, and the equilibrium pseudogene fraction for $\delta = 0.025\,\mathrm{Myr^{-1}}$.

   **Answer** With $r = -0.007$, $\mathrm{e}^{rT} = \mathrm{e}^{-0.63} \approx 0.53$, so $\mathbb{E}N \approx 780 \times 0.53 \approx 415$. The variance is $780 \times \frac{0.031}{-0.007} \times 0.53 \times (0.53 - 1) \approx 780 \times 4.43 \times 0.25 \approx 860$, a standard deviation of about $29$ genes. The ratio of pseudogenes to intact genes approaches $\mu/(\delta + r) = 0.019/0.018 \approx 1.06$, so pseudogenes make up about $1.06/2.06 \approx 51\,\%$ of the family, as observed in humans. A net loss of less than one percent per million years is enough to halve the repertoire.

3. A New World monkey species has three LWS alleles with frequencies $0.5$, $0.3$ and $0.2$ in the population. What fraction of females and of males is trichromatic, and why might natural selection maintain the polymorphism?

   **Answer** Males have one X chromosome and are all dichromatic. A female is trichromatic if she is heterozygous at the LWS locus, with probability $1 - \sum_i p_i^2 = 1 - (0.25 + 0.09 + 0.04) = 0.62$ under Hardy–Weinberg proportions. So $62\,\%$ of females, and $31\,\%$ of the population, see in three colors. If trichromats find ripe fruit more efficiently, heterozygous females have higher fitness than homozygotes, which is heterozygote advantage (overdominance) and maintains several alleles; a rare allele gives its carriers trichromacy more often, so selection is also frequency-dependent. Some field studies, however, found that dichromats detect camouflaged insects better, which could add a benefit to dichromacy and help maintain the polymorphism.

4. In the knock-in mice of Jacobs and colleagues, only heterozygous females with a balanced ratio of M and L cones discriminated long wavelengths. Explain this in terms of Eq. (53.2) and of the retinal wiring, and state what the experiment implies for the evolution of trichromacy in catarrhines.

   **Answer** Discrimination in the M–L range depends on the difference of the logarithmic excitations of M and L cones, which must be read by circuits that compare neighboring cones. Males and homozygous females have only one long-wavelength class, so the M–L term of the three-cone formula is absent and the threshold at long wavelengths is that of a dichromat. In heterozygous females random X-chromosome inactivation creates a mosaic; if one class is rare, few receptive fields have centers and surrounds dominated by different classes, the comparison is noisy (the effective $\omega$ is large), and discrimination fails. With balanced numbers the existing center–surround wiring, which samples single cones at random, carries an M–L opponent signal that downstream circuits can use. For catarrhines this means that once the gene duplication and the mutually exclusive expression of M and L existed, no new circuitry was needed for a functional third color dimension: the first trichromats benefited immediately, which is why selection could fix the duplication.
