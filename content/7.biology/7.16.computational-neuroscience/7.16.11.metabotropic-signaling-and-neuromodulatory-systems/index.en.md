Ionotropic receptors turn a transmitter into a current within a millisecond ([7.16.10](/7.16.10)). Most receptors in the brain are instead **G protein–coupled receptors**: they act through intracellular messengers over hundreds of milliseconds to minutes, change the excitability of neurons and the strength of synapses rather than producing a postsynaptic potential of their own, and can act far from the site of release. This chapter treats the metabotropic mode of signaling quantitatively (how a cascade amplifies and slows a signal) and then follows it to the level of systems: a few thousand neurons in the brainstem and basal forebrain that release dopamine, norepinephrine, serotonin and acetylcholine over the entire brain, and the circuit-level principle, worked out in a crustacean ganglion of thirty neurons, that modulators reconfigure what a fixed anatomical circuit does. The general cell biology of GPCRs, heterotrimeric G proteins and second messengers is in [7.3.18](/7.3.18) and [7.1.14](/7.1.14); this chapter concentrates on what is specific to neurons. The dopamine system returns in the chapters on reward and reinforcement learning ([7.16.37](/7.16.37), [7.16.39](/7.16.39)), and neuromodulators as "third factors" of plasticity in [7.16.14](/7.16.14).

## 11.1. The GPCR Signaling Paradigm

### 11.1.1. From Receptor to Kinase

The best-understood pathway is the one by which norepinephrine and epinephrine accelerate the heart and strengthen its contraction, studied by Sutherland, Gilman, Rodbell, Krebs and Lefkowitz among others. Its steps are the template for most neuronal metabotropic signaling:

1. the ligand binds a β-adrenergic receptor, a seven-transmembrane protein, and stabilizes its active conformation;
2. the active receptor catalyzes exchange of GDP for GTP on the α subunit of the **heterotrimeric G protein** $\mathrm{G_s}$, which dissociates into $\mathrm{G\alpha_s}$-GTP and $\mathrm{G}\beta\gamma$;
3. $\mathrm{G\alpha_s}$-GTP activates **adenylyl cyclase**, which converts ATP into **cyclic AMP**;
4. cAMP binds the regulatory subunits of **protein kinase A**, releasing the catalytic subunits, which phosphorylate target proteins on serines and threonines (in the heart the L-type $\ce{Ca^2+}$ channel and phospholamban);
5. the signal ends when $\mathrm{G\alpha}$ hydrolyzes its GTP (the intrinsic GTPase, accelerated by RGS proteins), **cyclic nucleotide phosphodiesterase** degrades cAMP, phosphatases dephosphorylate the targets, and the receptor is phosphorylated by GPCR kinases and bound by **arrestin**, which uncouples and internalizes it.

The G protein is a molecular timer: active from GTP binding to GTP hydrolysis. Three main families couple to neuronal receptors.

| G protein | Effector | Second messengers and targets | Neuronal receptors (examples) |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\mathrm{G_s}$ ($\mathrm{G_{olf}}$ in olfactory neurons and striatum) | Adenylyl cyclase stimulated | cAMP, PKA; cyclic-nucleotide-gated and HCN channels | $\beta$-adrenergic, $\mathrm{D_1}$/$\mathrm{D_5}$, $\mathrm{5\text{-}HT_4}$, $\mathrm{5\text{-}HT_6}$, $\mathrm{5\text{-}HT_7}$, odorant receptors |
| $\mathrm{G_{i/o}}$ | Adenylyl cyclase inhibited; $\mathrm{G}\beta\gamma$ opens GIRK, inhibits $\mathrm{Ca_V2}$ | cAMP decreased | $\mathrm{D_2}$–$\mathrm{D_4}$, $\alpha_2$-adrenergic, $\mathrm{M_2}$/$\mathrm{M_4}$, $\mathrm{GABA_B}$, $\mathrm{mGluR_{2/3}}$, $\mathrm{mGluR_{4\text{–}8}}$, $\mu$/$\delta$/$\kappa$ opioid, $\mathrm{CB_1}$, $\mathrm{A_1}$ adenosine, $\mathrm{5\text{-}HT_{1A}}$ |
| $\mathrm{G_q}$ | **Phospholipase C** $\beta$ | **Inositol 1,4,5-trisphosphate** and $\ce{Ca^2+}$ release; **diacylglycerol** and **protein kinase C**; depletion of $\mathrm{PIP_2}$ | $\alpha_1$-adrenergic, $\mathrm{M_1}$/$\mathrm{M_3}$/$\mathrm{M_5}$, $\mathrm{mGluR_1}$/$\mathrm{mGluR_5}$, $\mathrm{5\text{-}HT_2}$, many peptide receptors |

### 11.1.2. Amplification and Time Scales

A cascade does two things to a signal at once: it multiplies it and it slows it. One active receptor activates G proteins one after another while it remains bound; each $\mathrm{G\alpha_s}$-GTP keeps an adenylyl cyclase active for about its lifetime of GTP, producing hundreds of cAMP molecules; each PKA catalytic subunit phosphorylates many substrates. In rod photoreceptors, where the cascade is fastest and best measured, one photoisomerized rhodopsin activates more than a hundred **transducin** molecules per second ([7.16.17](/7.16.17)). The kinetics can be described by a chain of first-order stages.

**Formula 11.1.1 (Minimal Cascade Model)** Let $R(t) = A/(A + K_{\mathrm{d}})$ be the fractional occupancy of the receptors by agonist $A$, $G(t)$ the fraction of active G protein, $c(t)$ the cAMP concentration and $P(t)$ the fraction of active PKA. Then
$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\mathrm{d}G}{\mathrm{d}t} &= k_{\mathrm{G}}R\,(1 - G) - \frac{G}{\tau_{\mathrm{G}}}, &
\frac{\mathrm{d}c}{\mathrm{d}t} &= k_{\mathrm{c}}G - \frac{c}{\tau_{\mathrm{c}}}, &
P &= \frac{c^{n}}{c^{n} + K_{\mathrm{P}}^{n}},
\end{aligned}
$$
where $k_{\mathrm{G}}$ is the rate at which an active receptor population activates G proteins, $\tau_{\mathrm{G}}$ the lifetime of $\mathrm{G\alpha}$-GTP, $k_{\mathrm{c}}$ the rate of cAMP synthesis per active G protein, $\tau_{\mathrm{c}}$ the lifetime of cAMP set by phosphodiesterase, and $n \approx 2$ reflects the two cAMP molecules bound by each regulatory subunit.

Each stage is a low-pass filter with its own time constant, so the response lags the agonist by the sum of the delays (figure below, A): a G protein–gated current rises in $30$–$100\,\mathrm{ms}$, a cAMP-dependent current in seconds, and effects that require gene expression in tens of minutes. At steady state the stages multiply. From the formula, $G_\infty = k_{\mathrm{G}}\tau_{\mathrm{G}}R/(1 + k_{\mathrm{G}}\tau_{\mathrm{G}}R)$, which reaches half its maximum at $R = 1/(k_{\mathrm{G}}\tau_{\mathrm{G}})$; with $k_{\mathrm{G}}\tau_{\mathrm{G}} \gg 1$ the G protein saturates while most receptors are still free. This is the origin of the "spare receptors" of pharmacology: the downstream response is half-maximal at agonist concentrations well below $K_{\mathrm{d}}$, and the later the stage, the further the dose–response curve is shifted to the left and the steeper it becomes (B). The same arithmetic makes metabotropic signaling sensitive to the low, diffuse concentrations of modulators described in 11.3.

## 11.2. How Metabotropic Receptors Act on Ion Channels

Metabotropic receptors change the behavior of neurons mainly by modulating ion channels, through three routes that differ in speed and spread.

### 11.2.1. Membrane-Delimited Action of Gβγ

Acetylcholine slows the heart by opening $\ce{K+}$ channels in pacemaker cells. In cell-attached patch recordings, acetylcholine opened channels in the patch only when it was included in the pipette, not when it was applied to the rest of the cell (Soejima and Noma, 1984): the signal does not travel through the cytosol but stays within the membrane, a few hundred nanometers from the receptor. The messenger is the $\mathrm{G}\beta\gamma$ dimer released from $\mathrm{G_{i/o}}$, which binds and opens G protein–gated inwardly rectifying $\ce{K+}$ (GIRK, $\mathrm{K_{ir}3}$) channels (Logothetis and colleagues, 1987). The same mechanism produces the slow IPSP of $\mathrm{GABA_B}$ receptors, with rise time about $50\,\mathrm{ms}$ and decay about $200\,\mathrm{ms}$, and the inhibition by opioids, adenosine, dopamine $\mathrm{D_2}$ receptors and serotonin $\mathrm{5\text{-}HT_{1A}}$ receptors. $\mathrm{G}\beta\gamma$ also binds presynaptic $\mathrm{Ca_V2.1}$ and $\mathrm{Ca_V2.2}$ channels and shifts them into a "reluctant" state that opens only with strong depolarization; this inhibition is partly relieved by a large depolarizing prepulse, its diagnostic signature. Because release depends on about the fourth power of $\ce{Ca^2+}$ influx ([7.16.9](/7.16.9#sec-9.3.1)), a modest reduction of $\ce{Ca^2+}$ current strongly reduces release, which makes presynaptic $\mathrm{G_{i/o}}$-coupled receptors (autoreceptors for the cell's own transmitter, and heteroreceptors for others) a universal brake on release.

### 11.2.2. Phosphorylation by Kinases

PKA and PKC phosphorylate channels and change their gating or number. In *Aplysia* sensory neurons, serotonin acting through cAMP and PKA closes a resting $\ce{K+}$ conductance, broadens the **action potential** and increases $\ce{Ca^2+}$ influx and release, the presynaptic mechanism of behavioral sensitization described by Kandel and colleagues ([7.16.38](/7.16.38)). In the heart and in neurons, PKA increases the current through L-type $\ce{Ca^2+}$ channels.

### 11.2.3. Lipid Signaling

$\mathrm{G_q}$-coupled receptors activate phospholipase C, which hydrolyzes the membrane lipid $\mathrm{PIP_2}$. Many channels require $\mathrm{PIP_2}$ to open; among them the KCNQ ($\mathrm{K_V7}$) channels that carry the **M current**, a slow, non-inactivating $\ce{K+}$ current active near threshold, named for its suppression by muscarinic receptors (Brown and Adams, 1980). Closing it depolarizes the neuron, reduces **spike-frequency adaptation** and converts a phasic neuron into a tonically firing one; its role in setting excitability is described in [7.16.5](/7.16.5).

The three routes, with the ionotropic and transcriptional modes for comparison:

| Route | Example | Latency | Duration | Spread |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ionotropic receptor | AMPA EPSC | $< 1\,\mathrm{ms}$ | $1$–$5\,\mathrm{ms}$ | One synapse |
| $\mathrm{G}\beta\gamma$, membrane-delimited | $\mathrm{GABA_B}$–GIRK IPSP | $30$–$50\,\mathrm{ms}$ | $0.1$–$1\,\mathrm{s}$ | $< 1\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ |
| Second messenger and kinase | Serotonin–cAMP–PKA closing $\ce{K+}$ channels | $0.1$–$1\,\mathrm{s}$ | Seconds to minutes | Whole cell compartment |
| Gene expression | CREB-dependent transcription | Tens of minutes | Hours to days | Whole cell |

## 11.3. Neuropeptides and Volume Transmission

**Definition 11.3.1 (Neuropeptide)** A **neuropeptide** is a peptide of about 3 to 40 amino acids that is synthesized by a neuron as part of a larger precursor in the endoplasmic reticulum, processed by prohormone convertases and packaged into large dense-core vesicles in the Golgi apparatus, transported to the axon (or released from dendrites), and released to act on G protein–coupled receptors of nearby or distant cells.

More than a hundred neuropeptides are known, including the opioid peptides (enkephalins, endorphins, dynorphins), substance P, neuropeptide Y, cholecystokinin, vasoactive intestinal peptide, somatostatin, **oxytocin** and vasopressin, orexins (hypocretins) and corticotropin-releasing hormone. Their mode of action differs from that of small-molecule transmitters in four quantitative ways. Dense-core vesicles are not docked at the **active zone**, and their release requires sustained or burst firing that raises $\ce{Ca^2+}$ throughout the terminal; a single action potential releases almost none. Peptides are not recycled: every released molecule must be replaced by synthesis in the soma and axonal transport over hours. Their receptors have nanomolar affinity, a thousand times higher than those of fast transmitter receptors, so very few molecules suffice. And because they are cleared slowly (by peptidases, not transporters), they diffuse micrometers to hundreds of micrometers from the release site.

**Definition 11.3.2 (Volume Transmission)** **Volume transmission** is signaling in which a transmitter released into the extracellular space diffuses beyond the synaptic cleft and acts on receptors of cells that are not synaptically apposed to the release site; it contrasts with wiring transmission, in which transmitter acts within the cleft of one synapse.

The reach of volume transmission follows from diffusion in the extracellular space, where the effective diffusion coefficient is reduced by tortuosity to about $D^* \approx D/2.5$, roughly $0.3\,\mu\mathrm{m^2\,ms^{-1}}$ for a small molecule. The time to diffuse a distance $x$ in three dimensions is of order $x^2/(6D^*)$: about $50\,\mathrm{ms}$ for $10\,\mu\mathrm{m}$, $5\,\mathrm{s}$ for $100\,\mu\mathrm{m}$. Dopamine released from striatal varicosities, many of which have no postsynaptic partner, is taken up by the dopamine transporter within tens of milliseconds and acts within about $5$–$10\,\mu\mathrm{m}$, so that each release site influences thousands of synapses around it; peptides, cleared more slowly, reach further. Volume transmission is therefore broadcast signaling: spatially coarse, slow, but able to set the state of a whole region at once.

## 11.4. The Diffuse Neuromodulatory Systems

**Definition 11.4.1 (Neuromodulator)** A **neuromodulator** is a substance released by neurons that does not itself carry fast point-to-point signals but changes how neurons respond to other inputs, by altering their intrinsic excitability, the strength and plasticity of their synapses, or both; it acts mainly through metabotropic receptors, often by volume transmission.

The term describes an action, not a molecule: acetylcholine is a fast transmitter at the end plate and a modulator in the cortex, glutamate acting on mGluRs is modulatory, and most neuropeptides are modulators. The classical neuromodulatory systems of vertebrates share an anatomical plan: a few thousand to a few hundred thousand neurons in small nuclei of the brainstem, hypothalamus and basal forebrain, with highly branched axons that reach large parts of the brain. A single neuron of the rat locus coeruleus or of the substantia nigra has an axon whose total length in the target region can exceed tens of centimeters, with hundreds of thousands of release sites (Matsuda and colleagues, 2009, for nigrostriatal neurons).

**Definition 11.4.2 (The Four Classical Neuromodulators)** **Dopamine** is a catecholamine released by neurons of the substantia nigra pars compacta (SNc), the ventral tegmental area (VTA) and the hypothalamus, acting on $\mathrm{D_1}$-like ($\mathrm{G_s}$) and $\mathrm{D_2}$-like ($\mathrm{G_{i/o}}$) receptors; **norepinephrine** (noradrenaline) is a catecholamine released in the brain mainly by the locus coeruleus (LC) of the pons, acting on $\alpha_1$, $\alpha_2$ and $\beta$ adrenergic receptors; **serotonin** (5-hydroxytryptamine, 5-HT) is an indolamine released by neurons of the raphe nuclei along the midline of the brainstem, acting on 14 receptor subtypes; **acetylcholine** in the brain is released by projection neurons of the basal forebrain and of the pedunculopontine and laterodorsal tegmental nuclei (and by striatal interneurons), acting on nicotinic and muscarinic ($\mathrm{M_1}$–$\mathrm{M_5}$) receptors.

| System | Source nuclei | Neurons (rat, approximate) | Main targets | Functions associated with activity |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Dopamine | SNc, VTA | SNc $\approx 1.2\times10^4$, VTA $\approx 2.5\times10^4$ per side | Dorsal striatum (from SNc); ventral striatum, prefrontal cortex, amygdala (from VTA) | Movement vigor; reward prediction error and learning ([7.16.39](/7.16.39)); degeneration of SNc in **Parkinson's disease** |
| Norepinephrine | Locus coeruleus | $\approx 1600$ per side ($\approx 2\times10^4$ in humans) | Entire forebrain, cerebellum, spinal cord | Arousal, vigilance, response to salient and unexpected events; sleep–wake state |
| Serotonin | Rostral (dorsal, median) and caudal raphe | $\approx 2\times10^4$ | Forebrain (rostral group), brainstem and spinal cord (caudal group) | Mood, patience in waiting for reward, motor tone; target of antidepressants (SSRIs) |
| Acetylcholine | Basal forebrain (medial septum, diagonal band, nucleus basalis); PPT/LDT | Basal forebrain $\approx 2\times10^4$ | Cortex and hippocampus (basal forebrain); thalamus, midbrain (PPT/LDT) | Attention, cortical activation, plasticity; hippocampal theta rhythm |

The breadth of these projections once suggested that modulatory systems broadcast a single scalar signal, "arousal" or "reward", to the whole brain. Two lines of evidence have refined this picture. First, the signals are temporally precise: dopamine neurons fire brief bursts that encode reward prediction errors with a latency of about $100\,\mathrm{ms}$ ([7.16.39](/7.16.39)), and LC neurons fire phasically to salient stimuli. Second, with genetic tracing (projection-specific labeling, single-axon reconstruction and intersectional genetics, [7.16.3](/7.16.3)) the systems turn out to be modular: dopamine neurons that project to the tail of the striatum respond to novel and threatening stimuli rather than to reward, serotonin neurons projecting to different targets have different inputs and functions, and LC neurons are organized in partially separate output modules. The diversity of receptors adds another layer: the same dopamine signal excites striatal neurons with $\mathrm{D_1}$ receptors and inhibits those with $\mathrm{D_2}$ receptors, the substrate of the direct and indirect pathways of the **basal ganglia** ([7.16.40](/7.16.40)). Because $\mathrm{D_2}$ receptors have about a hundredfold higher affinity for dopamine than $\mathrm{D_1}$ receptors, tonic dopamine levels act mainly through $\mathrm{D_2}$, phasic bursts through $\mathrm{D_1}$, a dose-dependent division that follows directly from the binding curves of Formula 11.1.1.

## 11.5. How Modulators Reconfigure Circuits

### 11.5.1. One Anatomical Circuit, Many Outputs

The clearest demonstration that a circuit's wiring diagram does not determine its output comes from the stomatogastric ganglion (STG) of crabs and lobsters, studied by Marder, Selverston, Nusbaum and colleagues. Its roughly 30 identified neurons, connected mostly by inhibitory chemical synapses and **electrical synapses**, produce two rhythms: the pyloric rhythm (about $1\,\mathrm{Hz}$, a triphasic pattern of the pyloric dilator, lateral pyloric and pyloric neurons) and the slower gastric mill rhythm (about $0.1\,\mathrm{Hz}$). The ganglion is a **central pattern generator** ([7.14.6](/7.14.6#sec-6.4.2)): isolated from the animal, it continues to produce the rhythms, but only if modulatory input from other ganglia is intact.

More than twenty substances modulate the STG: amines (dopamine, serotonin, octopamine, histamine), peptides (proctolin, red pigment–concentrating hormone, crustacean cardioactive peptide, several FMRFamide-like peptides) and others, released by descending projection neurons and as hormones. Each modulator acts on a different subset of neurons and of conductances (the **hyperpolarization-activated current** $I_{\mathrm{h}}$, the transient $\ce{K+}$ current $I_{\mathrm{A}}$, a modulator-activated inward current, the strength of particular synapses), and each produces a distinct rhythm from the same neurons: dopamine alone changes the sign or strength of several synapses and the intrinsic properties of most pyloric neurons, and can turn a neuron from one functional group into a member of another. The anatomical **connectome** is thus a set of possibilities, and the modulatory state selects among them.

A second lesson is the converse. Measured in many animals, the maximal conductances of a given ion channel in identified STG neurons vary over two- to sixfold, and the mRNA levels of the channels vary similarly, yet the rhythm is nearly the same. In a database of about 20 million model pyloric networks built from all combinations of a few values of eight conductances per neuron and seven synapses, a few percent produced pyloric-like activity, from widely different parameter combinations (Prinz, Bucher and Marder, 2004). Similar output from different underlying parameters is a general property of biological circuits; it implies that a model with "the" correct parameters may not exist, that homeostatic regulation of conductances (correlated rather than independent channel expression) maintains function, and that two animals with the same behavior may respond differently to the same perturbation, such as a drug or a temperature change.

### 11.5.2. A Model of Modulation

The core of many rhythmic circuits, including parts of the STG and the spinal locomotor circuits, is a pair of neurons that inhibit each other, the **half-center oscillator** ([7.14.6](/7.14.6#sec-6.4.3)). A rate model shows how modulators can change its period or abolish its rhythm without any change in wiring.

**Example 11.5.1 (Half-Center Oscillator with Adaptation)** Let two units with rates $r_1, r_2$ inhibit each other with weight $w$ and each have a slow adaptation variable $a_i$:
$$
\tau\frac{\mathrm{d}r_i}{\mathrm{d}t} = -r_i + f\left(I_i - w\,r_j - g_i a_i\right), \qquad \tau_a\frac{\mathrm{d}a_i}{\mathrm{d}t} = -a_i + r_i, \qquad (i, j) = (1, 2), (2, 1),
$$
where $f(x) = \min(\max(x, 0), 1)$ is a threshold-linear gain function with saturation, $I_i$ the tonic drive and $g_i$ the strength of adaptation (a spike-frequency adaptation current such as a $\ce{Ca^2+}$-activated $\ce{K+}$ current). The active unit suppresses the other; as its adaptation builds up, its rate falls until the inhibition it exerts is too weak to hold the other unit below threshold, and the other unit escapes and takes over. Each phase therefore lasts roughly the time needed for $a_i$ to rise to the escape level, a fraction of $\tau_a$.

With $\tau = 20\,\mathrm{ms}$, $w = 2$, $I_i = 1$, $g_i = 2$ and $\tau_a = 400\,\mathrm{ms}$, the units alternate at about $1.2\,\mathrm{Hz}$ (figure below, top). A modulator that speeds the adaptation current ($\tau_a = 150\,\mathrm{ms}$) more than doubles the frequency with the same connections (middle). A modulator that suppresses adaptation in unit 1 and increases its drive ($g_1 = 0.3$, $I_1 = 1.2$) abolishes the rhythm: unit 1 fires tonically and holds unit 2 silent (bottom), a switch from rhythmic to tonic output of the kind seen when modulatory input to the STG is removed or replaced. Without adaptation ($g_i = 0$) the pair is a bistable switch, the neuronal version of the genetic toggle switch analyzed in [7.6.33](/7.6.33#sec-33.3).

In mammals the same principle operates on a larger scale. Acetylcholine and norepinephrine switch the thalamocortical network from the synchronized slow oscillations of non-REM sleep to the desynchronized activity of waking, largely by closing $\ce{K+}$ conductances in thalamic and cortical neurons; acetylcholine reduces excitatory synaptic transmission between cortical pyramidal neurons while sparing thalamocortical input, which shifts the balance from internally generated activity to sensory input (Hasselmo); and neuromodulators change the gain of neuronal input–output functions, which in network models is equivalent to changing the effective strength of all connections at once ([7.16.15](/7.16.15)).

## 11.6. Retrograde Signaling

Not all signaling at a synapse runs from pre- to postsynaptic cell. **Retrograde signaling** carries information backward, from the postsynaptic cell to the presynaptic terminal, and its best-understood messengers in the brain are lipids.

**Definition 11.6.1 (Endocannabinoid)** An **endocannabinoid** is a lipid messenger, principally 2-arachidonoylglycerol (2-AG) and anandamide, that is synthesized on demand in the postsynaptic membrane, diffuses retrogradely across the synaptic cleft and activates the presynaptic $\mathrm{G_{i/o}}$-coupled cannabinoid receptor $\mathrm{CB_1}$, which inhibits transmitter release; $\mathrm{CB_1}$ is also the receptor through which Δ⁹-tetrahydrocannabinol, the active compound of cannabis, acts.

The phenomenon that revealed the system is depolarization-induced suppression of inhibition (DSI): a brief depolarization of a hippocampal or cerebellar neuron reduces the inhibitory synaptic input it receives for tens of seconds, although the depolarization is purely postsynaptic. The suppression is presynaptic (the **paired-pulse ratio** rises, [7.16.9](/7.16.9#sec-9.6)), needs postsynaptic $\ce{Ca^2+}$, and is abolished by $\mathrm{CB_1}$ antagonists and in $\mathrm{CB_1}$-knockout mice (Wilson and Nicoll, 2001; Ohno-Shosaku, Maejima and Kano, 2001; Kreitzer and Regehr, 2001). 2-AG is produced from diacylglycerol by diacylglycerol lipase-α, which is activated by postsynaptic $\ce{Ca^2+}$ and by $\mathrm{G_q}$-coupled receptors such as $\mathrm{mGluR_5}$; the coincidence of the two strongly enhances its production, and repeated release produces a lasting presynaptic **long-term depression** at many synapses, including corticostriatal synapses. $\mathrm{CB_1}$ is among the most abundant GPCRs in the brain and is concentrated on the terminals of cholecystokinin-expressing basket cells and of many glutamatergic neurons. Other retrograde messengers include nitric oxide, produced by **nitric oxide synthase** and acting on presynaptic guanylyl cyclase, and peptides and neurotrophins released from dendrites.

Retrograde signals allow a neuron to regulate its own inputs: the more active it is, the less input it receives, a negative feedback at the level of the synapse. They also let the postsynaptic cell report to the presynaptic terminal that the synapse has been used successfully, a requirement for some forms of plasticity in which the change is expressed presynaptically ([7.16.14](/7.16.14)).

## Summary

- GPCRs act through heterotrimeric G proteins: $\mathrm{G_s}$ stimulates and $\mathrm{G_{i/o}}$ inhibits adenylyl cyclase, $\mathrm{G_q}$ activates phospholipase C (11.1.1).
- A cascade amplifies and slows (Formula 11.1.1): each stage is a low-pass filter, and saturation of early stages shifts later stages to lower agonist concentrations ("spare receptors").
- Metabotropic receptors act on channels in three ways: membrane-delimited $\mathrm{G}\beta\gamma$ (GIRK opening, $\mathrm{Ca_V2}$ inhibition; tens of milliseconds), kinase phosphorylation (seconds to minutes) and $\mathrm{PIP_2}$ depletion (closure of the M current) (11.2).
- Neuropeptides (Definition 11.3.1) are released by bursts from dense-core vesicles, act on high-affinity receptors and spread by volume transmission (Definition 11.3.2), reaching $10\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ in about $50\,\mathrm{ms}$ and $100\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ in seconds.
- Dopamine, norepinephrine, serotonin and acetylcholine (Definition 11.4.2) come from small nuclei with brain-wide projections; they are neuromodulators (Definition 11.4.1) whose signals are temporally precise and modular.
- In the stomatogastric ganglion each modulator produces a different output from the same neurons, and similar outputs arise from widely different conductance combinations; in a half-center model modulators change the period or abolish the rhythm without rewiring (Example 11.5.1).
- Endocannabinoids (Definition 11.6.1) are retrograde messengers that suppress release through presynaptic $\mathrm{CB_1}$ receptors.

## Questions

1. In Formula 11.1.1 at steady state, at what receptor occupancy $R$ does the G protein reach half of its value at full occupancy when $k_{\mathrm{G}}\tau_{\mathrm{G}} = 2$? If cAMP is proportional to $G$ and PKA follows a Hill function with $n = 2$ that is half-maximal at 25% of the maximal cAMP, at what receptor occupancy is PKA half-activated?

   **Answer** Normalize each stage to its value at full occupancy ($R = 1$). $G_\infty(R) = 2R/(1 + 2R)$, so $G_\infty(R)/G_\infty(1) = 3R/(1 + 2R)$, which equals $1/2$ at $R = 0.25$: the G protein is half-activated when a quarter of the receptors are occupied. cAMP, proportional to $G$, reaches 25% of its maximum when $3R/(1 + 2R) = 0.25$, i.e. $R = 0.1$, and there PKA is half-activated. Each stage that saturates shifts the dose–response curve to the left: PKA is half-active when only 10% of the receptors are occupied.

2. Why does a presynaptic $\mathrm{G_{i/o}}$-coupled receptor that reduces the $\ce{Ca^2+}$ current by 20% reduce transmitter release by about 60%? Which experimental signatures would show that the effect is presynaptic?

   **Answer** Release depends on about the fourth power of $\ce{Ca^2+}$ influx (Formula 9.3.1 and the Dodge–Rahamimoff relation, [7.16.9](/7.16.9#sec-9.3)), so a reduction to 80% of the influx reduces release to $0.8^4 \approx 0.41$, a reduction of about 60%. Presynaptic signatures: an increase of the paired-pulse ratio and of the coefficient of variation and failure rate of evoked responses, no change in the amplitude of miniature (spontaneous) postsynaptic currents, and no change in the response to exogenously applied transmitter.

3. In Example 11.5.1, argue qualitatively why the oscillation stops if the mutual inhibition $w$ is too weak, and why it also stops (in a different way) if $g_i = 0$.

   **Answer** If $w$ is small, the active unit cannot suppress the other: both units receive enough drive to be active together, the system settles into a stable fixed point with $r_1 = r_2 > 0$, and there is nothing to alternate. If $g_i = 0$ there is no slow process to terminate a phase: with strong inhibition the system has two stable states (unit 1 on and unit 2 off, or the reverse) and stays in whichever it starts in, a bistable switch. Oscillation requires both strong mutual inhibition, which creates the two quasi-stable states, and a slow negative feedback (adaptation, or synaptic depression of the inhibitory synapses), which destabilizes each in turn: the same combination of positive feedback and delayed negative feedback that underlies the relaxation oscillators of [7.6.33](/7.6.33#sec-33.5.4).

4. (Open question) Model pyloric networks with very different conductances produce the same rhythm. How would you test whether a real STG neuron maintains its activity by regulating its conductances homeostatically, and what does such degeneracy imply for the use of drugs that target one channel?

   **Answer** Approach: measure several conductances and their mRNAs in the same identified neuron across animals and look for correlations between them (correlated expression, such as between $I_{\mathrm{A}}$ and $I_{\mathrm{h}}$, suggests coregulation that preserves activity); perturb the neuron chronically (isolate it from modulatory input, or block one channel for hours) and track whether activity first changes and then recovers while other conductances change; model the regulation as activity-dependent control of conductance densities toward a target $\ce{Ca^2+}$ level. Implication: because many parameter combinations give the same output, the same drug blocking one channel can have very different effects in different individuals, from no change to loss of rhythm, depending on how close each individual's parameters are to a boundary of the functional region; average effects across individuals may hide this variability.
