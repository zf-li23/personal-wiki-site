Recordings of hundreds or thousands of neurons at once have become routine, and the central question has changed from what a single neuron encodes to what the population as a whole does. A population of $N$ neurons has an $N$-dimensional state space, with one axis for the rate of each neuron, and its activity over time is a trajectory in that space. If the trajectory fills the space, nothing can be learned beyond listing the neurons; but in practice the activity of populations is confined to a small number of dimensions, often a handful, and the shape of the surface on which it lies reflects the computation the circuit performs. This chapter treats the statistical tools for finding that structure: linear methods (principal component analysis, factor analysis, discriminant analysis, independent component analysis), nonlinear methods for visualizing clusters and manifolds, and the interpretation of the low-dimensional structures that have been found. The underlying linear algebra is that of [2.1](/2.1) and [3.4](/3.4); the focus here is on what the methods mean for neural data, what they can and cannot show, and how the dimensions found relate to the dynamics of [7.16.15](/7.16.15) and [7.16.44](/7.16.44) and to the decoding of [7.16.26](/7.16.26). The motor-cortex applications are developed in [7.16.32](/7.16.32).

Throughout the chapter the activity of $N$ neurons at time $t$ (or in trial $k$) is the vector $\boldsymbol{r}(t) \in \mathbb{R}^N$ of rates or counts, $R$ the $N \times T$ matrix with these vectors as columns (after subtracting the mean of each row, unless stated otherwise), and $C = RR^{\mathsf T}/(T - 1)$ the sample covariance matrix with eigenvalues $\lambda_1 \ge \dots \ge \lambda_N$. Eigenvalues of covariance matrices are written $\lambda$ as in the other network chapters, and are not to be confused with the length constant of the cable.

## 28.1. High-Dimensional Recordings, Low-Dimensional Activity

If every neuron were binary, a brain area with $N$ neurons ($10^3$–$10^6$) could in principle take $2^N$ different activity patterns, a number so large that it has no meaning. Real populations visit a vanishingly small part of this space. The reason is that the *dimension of activity is determined by the dynamics and by the task*, not by the number of neurons. A ring-attractor network of 100 neurons ([7.16.44](/7.16.44)) has a one-dimensional family of stable activity profiles, the bump at different angles, and its activity lies on a closed curve however many neurons it contains. A motor-cortex population preparing and executing a reach varies in a few dimensions that correspond to the few degrees of freedom of the movement, though hundreds of neurons contribute.

The number of simultaneously recorded neurons has doubled roughly every seven years (Stevenson and Kording, 2011), reaching thousands with silicon probes and imaging, but the dimension found does not grow in proportion: Gao and Ganguli (2015) showed that it is bounded by the complexity of the task, the number of independent conditions, and by the smoothness of the neural responses over the stimulus space.

**Definition 28.1.1 (Population State Space)** The **population state space** is the space $\mathbb{R}^N$ of activity vectors of $N$ recorded neurons. At each time the population occupies a point, the *population state*, and the sequence of states through time is the *population trajectory*.

"Redundancy" in neural populations, that many neurons carry similar information, is often described as waste. It is better seen as a feature: redundancy gives robustness to noise (averaging over many neurons, as in Section 26.5), robustness to the loss of cells, the ability to generalize, and a representation in which relations among stimuli can be read by linear readouts. Dimensionality reduction does not remove the redundancy but describes it: it finds the few combinations of neurons that carry the variation, and the coordinates in which the trajectory is easiest to interpret.

## 28.2. Principal Component Analysis of Population Activity

### 28.2.1. PCA and SVD

For a centered data matrix $R$ ($N$ neurons, $T$ samples), **principal component analysis** ([3.4.5](/3.4.5)) seeks the unit vector $\boldsymbol{w}$ (a pattern of weights over the neurons) such that the projection $x_t = \boldsymbol{w}^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol{r}(t)$ has maximal variance, which is the Rayleigh quotient problem

$$
\boldsymbol{w}_1 = \arg\max_{\lVert\boldsymbol{w}\rVert = 1}\ \boldsymbol{w}^{\mathsf T}C\boldsymbol{w}. \tag{28.1}
$$
Its solution is the eigenvector of $C$ with the largest eigenvalue $\lambda_1$, which is the variance along it; the next components are the eigenvectors orthogonal to the earlier ones, found equivalently by subtracting the first component and repeating. Because $C$ is symmetric, its eigenvectors form an orthogonal matrix $W$ and the variance along component $k$ is $\lambda_k$. The **singular value decomposition** ([2.1.7](/2.1.7#thm-7.4.1)) $R = U\Sigma V^{\mathsf T}$ ($U$ is $N \times N$, $V$ is $T \times T$ orthogonal) gives the same result in one step: $RR^{\mathsf T} = U\Sigma\Sigma^{\mathsf T}U^{\mathsf T}$, so the columns of $U$ are the principal axes (patterns over neurons), the singular values satisfy $\sigma_k^2 = (T - 1)\lambda_k$, and the columns of $V$ are the time courses of the components. Truncating the SVD after $d$ terms gives the best rank-$d$ approximation of $R$ in the least-squares sense, so the fraction of variance retained by $d$ components is $\sum_{k\le d}\lambda_k/\sum_{k}\lambda_k$.

Three practical points affect the result of PCA on neural data. First, *centering*: the mean over time of each neuron is subtracted, so the first PC does not capture the mean rate. Second, *normalization*: neurons with high rates and large variance dominate the covariance, and for spike counts the variance grows with the mean (Section 16.4 of [7.16.16](/7.16.16#sec-16.4)); the usual remedies are to divide each neuron by its standard deviation (analysis of the correlation matrix) or to apply a variance-stabilizing square-root transform to the counts, $\sqrt{r}$, for which the Poisson variance is approximately constant. Third, *time*: rows of $R$ may be samples in time (a continuous recording, as in the figure), trial-averaged responses for each condition, or single trials, and the answer differs in what the PCs mean (see the next section).

### 28.2.2. How Many Dimensions?

To say how many dimensions the activity has, one counts the components that explain more than the noise. A single number, independent of any threshold, is the participation ratio.

**Definition 28.2.1 (Participation Ratio)** The **participation ratio** of a covariance matrix with eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_N$ is

$$
\mathrm{PR} = \frac{\bigl(\sum_k\lambda_k\bigr)^2}{\sum_k\lambda_k^2}. \tag{28.2}
$$

It is $N$ when all $N$ eigenvalues are equal (isotropic activity), $1$ when a single eigenvalue is nonzero, and in general is the number of components that "participate" substantially, so that a spectrum with $d$ equal eigenvalues has $\mathrm{PR} = d$. It weights the large eigenvalues, and so changes less than a variance threshold when small, noisy components are included; a noise floor of many small eigenvalues still raises it somewhat (see below).

For a ring population the spectrum can be computed exactly.

**Theorem 28.2.1 (PCA of a Translation-Invariant Ring Population)** Let $N$ neurons have preferred directions $\theta_i = 2\pi i/N$ and responses $r_i = f(\theta - \theta_i)$ to a direction $\theta$ distributed uniformly on the circle, where $f$ is an even, $2\pi$-periodic tuning function with Fourier coefficients $\hat f_k = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int f(\phi)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}k\phi}\,\mathrm{d}\phi$. For $N$ large the principal axes are the Fourier modes $\cos k\theta_i$ and $\sin k\theta_i$, each pair $\pm k$ having the eigenvalue $\lambda_k = N\lvert\hat f_k\rvert^2$ $(k \ge 1)$, and the population state projected on the first pair traces a circle in the order of the direction $\theta$.

**Proof** The covariance $C_{ij} = \mathbb{E}_\theta[f(\theta - \theta_i)f(\theta - \theta_j)] - \bar f^2$ depends only on $\theta_i - \theta_j$, so $C$ is a circulant matrix, which the discrete Fourier modes diagonalize. Its eigenvalue for mode $k$ is $\sum_jc(\theta_j)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}k\theta_j} \approx N\hat c_k$, where $c(\phi)$ is the autocorrelation of $f$ minus $\bar f^2$; by the correlation theorem $\hat c_k = \lvert\hat f_k\rvert^2$ for $k \ne 0$, and the mode $k = 0$ (the mean) has no variance after centering. Modes $\pm k$ are degenerate (real $\cos$ and $\sin$). The projection of the state on $\cos k\theta_i$ is $\sum_if(\theta - \theta_i)\cos k\theta_i \approx N\hat f_k\cos k\theta$ and on $\sin k\theta_i$ it is $N\hat f_k\sin k\theta$, which for $k = 1$ is a circle of radius $N\hat f_1$ traversed once as $\theta$ goes around. $\square$

For a Gaussian tuning bump of angular width $w \ll 1$, $\lvert\hat f_k\rvert^2 \propto \mathrm{e}^{-k^2w^2}$, and replacing the sums over the pairs by integrals gives $\mathrm{PR} \approx \sqrt{2\pi}/w$: narrower tuning means a higher dimension, because more Fourier modes are needed to describe the bump. For broad tuning the integral approximation fails and the coefficients must be summed exactly; for von Mises tuning $\hat f_k \propto I_k(\kappa)$ (modified Bessel functions), and $\kappa = 2$ gives $\mathrm{PR} = 2.8$ without noise, though the population encodes a *one-dimensional* variable. In the simulation below the noise adds a floor of about 94 small eigenvalues and raises the measured value to $3.5$. Dimension counted linearly (the number of PCs) differs from the intrinsic dimension of the manifold on which the activity lies, one here, and we return to the distinction in Section 28.5. The figure shows the simulation: the spectrum has pairs of equal eigenvalues (the Fourier modes, 1–2, 3–4, 5–6) and a noise floor, and the first two PCs trace a ring ordered by the represented direction.

### 28.2.3. The Noise Floor

Eigenvalues of a sample covariance matrix are biased: even if the true covariance is the identity, the sample eigenvalues spread out when the number of samples is not much larger than the number of neurons. For $N$ neurons and $T$ independent samples of pure noise of variance $\sigma^2$, in the limit $N, T \to \infty$ with $\gamma = N/T$ fixed, the eigenvalues lie in the interval (Marchenko and Pastur, 1967)

$$
\sigma^2\bigl(1 - \sqrt\gamma\bigr)^2 \le \lambda \le \sigma^2\bigl(1 + \sqrt\gamma\bigr)^2. \tag{28.3}
$$
Components with an eigenvalue inside this band cannot be distinguished from noise, and the participation ratio of noise is far from $N$ unless $T \gg N$. The practical consequences are that the number of dimensions must be judged against this floor or, better, by **cross-validation**: fit the PCs on one part of the data (trials or neurons) and measure how much variance they explain in held-out data, so that only components that generalize are counted ([3.4.10](/3.4.10)).

## 28.3. Trial Averaging and Single Trials

### 28.3.1. Demixed PCA

Most experiments vary several task parameters: the stimulus, the decision, the time within the trial. Ordinary PCA of trial-averaged data finds the directions of largest variance, in which the effects of different parameters are mixed: a single PC often combines stimulus and decision signals because single neurons do ([7.16.47](/7.16.47)). Demixed PCA decomposes the data first, then reduces each part.

**Definition 28.3.1 (Demixed PCA)** Let $X$ be the matrix of trial-averaged activity (rows: neurons; columns: combinations of time $t$, stimulus $s$ and decision $d$). It is split by marginalization, averaging over some parameters and subtracting the lower-order terms, into condition-independent, stimulus, decision and interaction parts, $X = X_t + X_s + X_d + X_{sd} + \text{noise}$. **Demixed PCA** (dPCA; Kobak et al., 2016) finds for each part $\varphi$ a decoder $D_\varphi$ and encoder $F_\varphi$ (each of low rank) that minimize

$$
L = \sum_\varphi\bigl\lVert X_\varphi - F_\varphi D_\varphi X\bigr\rVert^2, \tag{28.4}
$$
so that a few components capture, say, the stimulus-dependent variance alone, from linear combinations of the neurons, while others capture the decision or the condition-independent time course.

Equation (28.4) is a reduced-rank regression with a closed solution, and combines the objective of PCA (explain variance) with that of a linear discriminant (separate parameters). It showed, in recordings from prefrontal cortex during a working-memory task, that a population which seems to mix stimulus and decision at the single-neuron level has well-separated low-dimensional components for each, with time courses that differ in the delay.

### 28.3.2. Single-Trial Latent Variables

Trial averaging removes the variability that may be of interest: if a neural trajectory runs faster on one trial than on another, the average blurs it. To analyze single trials the noise in the spike counts must be separated from the signal. A *latent variable model* assumes that a few smooth latent variables $\boldsymbol{z}(t)$ drive all neurons and the rest is noise private to each neuron.

**Definition 28.3.2 (Factor Analysis and GPFA)** In **factor analysis** the activity is $\boldsymbol{r} = L\boldsymbol{z} + \boldsymbol{\mu} + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}$, with latent factors $\boldsymbol{z} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I_d)$, loading matrix $L$ ($N \times d$) and private noise $\boldsymbol{\varepsilon} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \Psi)$ with a *diagonal* covariance $\Psi$ (different for each neuron); the covariance of the data is $LL^{\mathsf T} + \Psi$. In **Gaussian-process factor analysis** (GPFA; Yu et al., 2009) each latent coordinate also has a Gaussian-process prior over time, $z_j(\cdot) \sim \mathcal{GP}(0, \kappa_j)$ with, for example, $\kappa_j(t, t') = \exp[-(t - t')^2/2\tau_j^2]$, so that the latent trajectories are smooth with a time scale $\tau_j$ fitted to the data.

Factor analysis ([3.4.8](/3.4.8)) differs from PCA in separating the *shared* variance, described by $L$, from the *private* variance $\Psi$ of each neuron; With equal private variances, $\Psi = \psi I$, the model is probabilistic PCA, whose maximum-likelihood loadings span the principal subspace; PCA is its limit $\psi \to 0$, in which noisy neurons are not downweighted. GPFA uses temporal smoothness to separate signal from noise: the posterior mean of the latent trajectory is a smoothed version of the data, weighted by the noise level of each neuron. Fitting is by the **EM algorithm** ([3.5.6](/3.5.6)). Extensions have replaced the linear Gaussian observation model by Poisson counts and the GP by nonlinear recurrent dynamics (e.g. LFADS; Pandarinath et al., 2018). The figure compares methods on simulated data with a two-dimensional smooth latent and 40 Poisson neurons at about $0.4$ spikes per bin: smoothing in time (before PCA) or in the latent space (after factor analysis) recovers the latent trajectory of a single trial with $R^2$ of about $0.9$, against $0.8$ for PCA of the square-root counts in which each bin is an independent sample.

## 28.4. Nonlinear Dimensionality Reduction

Linear methods find a subspace, but the activity may lie on a curved surface or in clusters (for instance the states of a network visiting several attractors, or the responses of cell types). A ring is not captured by one linear dimension and needs at least two, and a Swiss-roll shaped manifold needs more, though its intrinsic dimension is low. Nonlinear methods embed the data in two or three dimensions so that *neighbors in the original space remain neighbors*, mostly for visualization.

### 28.4.1. t-SNE

The **t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding** (t-SNE; van der Maaten and Hinton, 2008; [3.4.6](/3.4.6)) turns distances into probabilities. For high-dimensional points $\boldsymbol{x}_i$, the probability that $i$ picks $j$ as a neighbor is Gaussian in the distance,

$$
p_{j\mid i} = \frac{\exp(-\lVert\boldsymbol{x}_i - \boldsymbol{x}_j\rVert^2/2\sigma_i^2)}{\sum_{l\ne i}\exp(-\lVert\boldsymbol{x}_i - \boldsymbol{x}_l\rVert^2/2\sigma_i^2)},\qquad p_{ij} = \frac{p_{j\mid i} + p_{i\mid j}}{2n}, \tag{28.5}
$$
where the bandwidth $\sigma_i$ of each point is chosen so that the **perplexity** $2^{H(P_i)}$ of the distribution $P_i = (p_{j\mid i})_j$ ($H$ is its entropy in bits) equals a value set by the user, which can be read as the effective number of neighbors. The points $\boldsymbol{y}_i$ in the low-dimensional map have a heavy-tailed similarity, a Student $t$ distribution with one degree of freedom (a Cauchy distribution),

$$
q_{ij} = \frac{(1 + \lVert\boldsymbol{y}_i - \boldsymbol{y}_j\rVert^2)^{-1}}{\sum_{k\ne l}(1 + \lVert\boldsymbol{y}_k - \boldsymbol{y}_l\rVert^2)^{-1}}, \tag{28.6}
$$
and the map is found by gradient descent on the Kullback–Leibler divergence $\mathrm{KL}(P\Vert Q) = \sum_{i\ne j}p_{ij}\ln(p_{ij}/q_{ij})$, with gradient $\partial\mathrm{KL}/\partial\boldsymbol{y}_i = 4\sum_j(p_{ij} - q_{ij})(\boldsymbol{y}_i - \boldsymbol{y}_j)(1 + \lVert\boldsymbol{y}_i - \boldsymbol{y}_j\rVert^2)^{-1}$. Pairs with $p_{ij} > q_{ij}$ (close in the data but far in the map) attract; pairs with $p_{ij} < q_{ij}$ repel. The heavy tail of $q$ solves the *crowding problem*: in two dimensions there is little room for the many moderately distant neighbors of a point in a high-dimensional space, and the heavy tail allows them to sit farther apart than a Gaussian would.

The method makes clusters very visible, and is therefore easy to over-interpret. Its output must be read with these cautions (Wattenberg et al., 2016; Kobak and Berens, 2019):

- *Cluster sizes mean nothing*: $\sigma_i$ adapts to the local density, so that a tight and a diffuse cluster come out equally wide (figure).
- *Distances between clusters mean little*: the global arrangement depends on the initialization and the perplexity; initializing with the first two PCs preserves more of the global structure than random initialization.
- *The perplexity matters*: small values (5) split the data into many small groups, large values (comparable with the number of points per cluster) shrink the clusters and can merge them; several values should be tried, and structures that persist are more trustworthy.
- *Structure can be created*: random noise can produce apparent clusters at low perplexity.

### 28.4.2. UMAP

*Uniform manifold approximation and projection* (UMAP; McInnes et al., 2018) is built on the same idea, with a different construction that is faster and in practice tends to give more compact clusters. Each point $i$ is connected to its $k$ nearest neighbors with the weights $v_{j\mid i} = \exp[-(d(\boldsymbol{x}_i, \boldsymbol{x}_j) - \rho_i)/\sigma_i]$, where $\rho_i$ is the distance to the nearest neighbor (so every point is fully connected to at least one other, which ensures the local connectivity of the manifold) and $\sigma_i$ is set so that the weights sum to $\log_2k$; the directed weights are symmetrized by a fuzzy union, $v_{ij} = v_{j\mid i} + v_{i\mid j} - v_{j\mid i}v_{i\mid j}$. In the map the similarity is $w_{ij} = (1 + a\lVert\boldsymbol{y}_i - \boldsymbol{y}_j\rVert^{2b})^{-1}$, and the cross-entropy between the two weighted graphs,

$$
\sum_{i\ne j}\Bigl[v_{ij}\ln\frac{v_{ij}}{w_{ij}} + (1 - v_{ij})\ln\frac{1 - v_{ij}}{1 - w_{ij}}\Bigr],
$$
is minimized by stochastic gradient descent with sampled repulsion of nonneighbors. The first term pulls neighbors together as in t-SNE, and the second term, which t-SNE lacks in this form, pushes non-neighbors apart. UMAP is claimed to preserve more global structure than t-SNE; but much of the difference disappears when both are initialized in the same way, from a spectral embedding or from PCA (Kobak and Linderman, 2021). Both methods are tools for *looking at* data: the embeddings are not coordinates in which distances or axes can be interpreted, and conclusions should be tested in the original space (clustering, decoding, shuffled controls).

## 28.5. Neural Manifolds

**Definition 28.5.1 (Neural Manifold)** A **neural manifold** is a low-dimensional (generally curved) surface in the population state space to which the activity of a neural population is confined. Its *intrinsic dimension* is the number of coordinates needed to specify a point on it; its *linear dimension* is the dimension of the smallest linear subspace that contains it, which is at least as large.

A ring has intrinsic dimension 1 and linear dimension 2 (a circle) or more. Several manifolds have been found in neural data, and they are informative because their *topology* matches the topology of the variable that the circuit represents.

*Head-direction cells.* In the anterodorsal nucleus of the thalamus of mice, the population activity of head-direction cells lies on a ring while the animal moves, the position on the ring being the heading, as predicted by the ring attractor model ([7.16.44](/7.16.44)). Chaudhuri et al. (2019) showed that the ring persists, with the same correlations among cells, during sleep (in REM and non-REM sleep) with no sensory input, so it is generated by the internal connectivity, as an attractor, and not by the input; this had been indicated by Peyrache et al. (2015), who showed that the pairwise correlations among head-direction cells are preserved in sleep.

*Grid cells.* Grid cells of the medial entorhinal cortex have hexagonal firing fields in space, and within a module (cells with the same grid spacing and orientation) the spatial phase is a point in a periodic two-dimensional rhombus, a torus $\mathbb{T}^2$: the state of a module is determined by two phases, each periodic. Gardner et al. (2022) recorded hundreds of grid cells of a module in rats with Neuropixels probes and showed, with topological data analysis (persistent homology, with the Betti numbers $b_0, b_1, b_2 = 1, 2, 1$ of the torus, against $1, 1$ for a ring), that the population activity lies on a torus, also in sleep and in the dark. The model of [7.16.44](/7.16.44) on the toroidal attractor predicts it. Because each phase enters the tuning of the cells through its cosine and sine, the population torus spans at least four linear dimensions, twice its intrinsic dimension of 2.

*Motor cortex.* The population activity of motor cortex in a reaching movement does not simply follow the direction or force: Churchland et al. (2012) found that the population state rotates, as described next. More generally, Gallego et al. (2017, 2018) found that the activity during different motor behaviors (reaching, grasping, wrist movements) lies in the same low-dimensional manifold (about 10–12 dimensions in a 100-neuron recording), and that across days, when different neurons are recorded, the dynamics within the manifold are stable and can be aligned by canonical correlation analysis ([3.4.9](/3.4.9)) (Gallego et al., 2020); this is the basis of stable brain–machine interfaces ([7.16.32](/7.16.32)).

### 28.5.1. Rotational Dynamics and jPCA

PCA finds directions of large variance, but the dynamics of the population need not be best described in them. If the population state obeys approximately a linear dynamical system $\dot{\boldsymbol{x}} = \boldsymbol{x}M$ (written for row vectors) in a low-dimensional space, the structure is in the matrix $M$. An unconstrained $M$ can describe expansion, decay or rotation; the experiments of Churchland et al. showed that a particular part of it, rotation, dominates.

**Definition 28.5.2 (jPCA)** **jPCA** (Churchland et al., 2012) first reduces the population activity of all conditions to a low-dimensional subspace by PCA, then fits the linear dynamics $\dot{\boldsymbol{x}} = \boldsymbol{x}M$ by least squares subject to the constraint that $M$ is *skew-symmetric*, $M = -M^{\mathsf T}$, and finally projects the data onto the planes spanned by the real and imaginary parts of the eigenvectors of $M$, the jPC planes, ordered by the imaginary part of the eigenvalue (the rotation frequency).

**Theorem 28.5.1 (Skew-Symmetric Dynamics Are Rotations)** If $M = -M^{\mathsf T}$, then $\lVert\boldsymbol{x}(t)\rVert$ is constant along solutions of $\dot{\boldsymbol{x}} = \boldsymbol{x}M$, the eigenvalues of $M$ are purely imaginary, $\pm\mathrm{i}\omega_k$ (and $0$ if the dimension is odd), and in the real invariant plane of each pair the solution rotates at the angular frequency $\omega_k$.

**Proof** $\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\boldsymbol{x}\boldsymbol{x}^{\mathsf T} = \boldsymbol{x}M\boldsymbol{x}^{\mathsf T} + \boldsymbol{x}M^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol{x}^{\mathsf T} = \boldsymbol{x}(M + M^{\mathsf T})\boldsymbol{x}^{\mathsf T} = 0$. If $M\boldsymbol{v} = \lambda\boldsymbol{v}$ then $\bar{\boldsymbol{v}}^{\mathsf T}M\boldsymbol{v} = \lambda\lvert\boldsymbol{v}\rvert^2$ is purely imaginary since $M$ is real skew-symmetric, so $\lambda \in \mathrm{i}\mathbb{R}$. In the plane spanned by the real and imaginary parts of the eigenvector for $\pm\mathrm{i}\omega$, $M$ acts as the $2\times2$ matrix $\begin{pmatrix}0 & -\omega\\ \omega & 0\end{pmatrix}$ (in a suitable orthonormal basis), whose exponential is a rotation by the angle $\omega t$ ([2.4](/2.4)). $\square$

The constraint is the hypothesis being tested: how much of the variance of $\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}$ is explained by a skew-symmetric $M$ compared with the unconstrained fit? For the motor cortex of monkeys reaching to targets, the skew-symmetric model explained most of the variance explained by the unconstrained one, the trajectories rotated in the same sense in all conditions, with the phase and amplitude of the rotation dependent on the reach, and the rotation was much stronger than in comparable data of the same smoothness without such structure; the rotations were also found in the activity of recurrent networks trained to produce the muscle activity of the reach ([7.16.31](/7.16.31), [7.16.32](/7.16.32)). The figure shows the analysis on synthetic data: the dynamics fits are almost as good with the skew-symmetric constraint as without it for the rotational data and poor for smooth random trajectories, and in the jPC plane the trajectories of all conditions spiral in the same direction.

![jPCA on synthetic population data (8 conditions, 80 neurons, a six-dimensional latent state with two decaying rotation planes of 2.0 and 3.2 Hz observed for 0.6 s; noise s.d. 0.05). A: condition trajectories (dots: start) in the first jPC plane (the plane of the fastest rotation, 3.2 Hz), after PCA to six dimensions and a skew-symmetric fit of the dynamics: all conditions rotate in the same direction. A caution applies to all manifold claims. Low-dimensional, smooth, even rotational structure arises as a by-product of simple properties of the data, the smoothness of the neural responses in time and the correlations across conditions and neurons; to claim that a structure reflects the computation of the circuit, it must be significant against surrogate data that preserve those properties while destroying the structure in question (Elsayed and Cunningham, 2017). The control in panel C is of that kind.

## 28.6. Discrimination Instead of Variance: Fisher's Discriminant

PCA ignores the labels: it finds the directions of largest variance, which are determined by the noise as much as by the signal. When the aim is to read out a stimulus or a choice, a better criterion is how well the classes are *separated*. For two classes with means $\boldsymbol{\mu}_1, \boldsymbol{\mu}_2$ and within-class covariance $S_{\mathrm{W}}$, **linear discriminant analysis** ([3.4.10](/3.4.10)) maximizes the ratio of the squared distance between the projected means to the projected within-class variance,

$$
J(\boldsymbol{w}) = \frac{\bigl(\boldsymbol{w}^{\mathsf T}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_1 - \boldsymbol{\mu}_2)\bigr)^2}{\boldsymbol{w}^{\mathsf T}S_{\mathrm{W}}\boldsymbol{w}}. \tag{28.7}
$$

**Theorem 28.6.1 (Fisher's Discriminant Direction)** The maximum of $J$ is attained for $\boldsymbol{w} \propto S_{\mathrm{W}}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_1 - \boldsymbol{\mu}_2)$ and equals the squared sensitivity $d'^2 = (\boldsymbol{\mu}_1 - \boldsymbol{\mu}_2)^{\mathsf T}S_{\mathrm{W}}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_1 - \boldsymbol{\mu}_2)$.

**Proof** Setting the derivative of $J$ to zero gives the generalized eigenvalue problem $S_{\mathrm{B}}\boldsymbol{w} = JS_{\mathrm{W}}\boldsymbol{w}$ with the between-class matrix $S_{\mathrm{B}} = (\boldsymbol{\mu}_1 - \boldsymbol{\mu}_2)(\boldsymbol{\mu}_1 - \boldsymbol{\mu}_2)^{\mathsf T}$. Since $S_{\mathrm{B}}\boldsymbol{w}$ is always proportional to $\boldsymbol{\mu}_1 - \boldsymbol{\mu}_2$, the solution is $\boldsymbol{w} \propto S_{\mathrm{W}}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_1 - \boldsymbol{\mu}_2)$; substituting it in $J$ gives $d'^2$. $\square$

The discriminant direction is the *decoding direction* of Section 26.5: for two nearby stimuli, $\boldsymbol{\mu}_1 - \boldsymbol{\mu}_2 = \boldsymbol{f}'\Delta s$ and $d'^2 = \Delta s^2\,\boldsymbol{f}'^{\mathsf T}\Sigma^{-1}\boldsymbol{f}' = \Delta s^2I_{\mathrm{F}}$, the linear Fisher information. Noise correlations enter as $\Sigma^{-1}$: the optimal axis is not the signal direction but the signal direction rotated away from the directions of large noise. The figure shows a pair of neurons with strongly correlated noise (the situation of Section 26.5 with equal slopes): the first PC lies along the noise, and projecting on it gives almost no discrimination (AUC about $0.5$), while the Fisher axis, perpendicular to the noise, separates the classes perfectly.

The sample version fits $S_{\mathrm{W}}$ from data; with many neurons and few trials it needs regularization ([3.4.10](/3.4.10)). The generalization to more than two classes, multiple discriminant analysis, finds a low-dimensional subspace in which the classes are best separated, and has been used to find, in the population activity of hippocampal neurons, the few axes along which positions or contexts are separated ([7.16.42](/7.16.42)). The comparison of PCA and discriminant axes is a general lesson: variance is not information.

## 28.7. Independent Component Analysis

PCA finds orthogonal directions that decorrelate the data, which constrains only second-order statistics. Many problems are instead *blind source separation*: the recorded signals $\boldsymbol{x}$ are linear mixtures of unknown sources, as in the extracellular recording in which each electrode picks up several neurons, or the EEG in which each scalp electrode sums many cortical generators and eye-movement artifacts.

**Definition 28.7.1 (Independent Component Analysis)** In the model $\boldsymbol{x} = A\boldsymbol{s}$ with unknown mixing matrix $A$ and *statistically independent*, non-Gaussian sources $s_1, \dots, s_d$, **independent component analysis** (ICA) estimates an unmixing matrix $B$ such that the components $\boldsymbol{y} = B\boldsymbol{x}$ are as independent as possible.

Independence is a far stronger property than uncorrelatedness, and fixes what PCA leaves open. Whitening the data (a PCA step that makes them uncorrelated with unit variance) leaves an unknown rotation, because any rotation of white data is again white; ICA chooses the rotation under which the components are least Gaussian, measured by the kurtosis, the negentropy or the likelihood under a sparse source model. The reason is the central limit theorem: a sum of independent variables is more Gaussian than its terms, so the most non-Gaussian directions are the ones that recover single sources. The FastICA algorithm of Hyvärinen and Oja (2000) iterates the fixed-point update $\boldsymbol{w} \leftarrow \mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{z}\,g(\boldsymbol{w}^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol{z})] - \mathbb{E}[g'(\boldsymbol{w}^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol{z})]\boldsymbol{w}$ with $g = \tanh$ on the whitened data $\boldsymbol{z}$, followed by normalization. In the figure two sparse sources mixed linearly produce a cloud with two long arms: the PCA axes (orthogonal) bisect the arms, whereas the ICA axes align with the arms, the columns of $A$.

C: 2000 samples of two independent Laplace (sparse) sources mixed by a fixed 2 × 2 matrix; PCA axes (dashed, orthogonal) and ICA axes (orange, FastICA), which coincide with the mixing directions](./plots/lda-ica.py)

ICA has been used in neuroscience for the removal of eye-blink and muscle artifacts from EEG and magnetoencephalography (Makeig et al., 1996), for the extraction of independent spatial networks from fMRI, and for the separation of cell bodies and overlapping signals in calcium imaging (Mukamel et al., 2009); in spike sorting, where the waveforms of neurons recorded on the same electrode are separated, PCA of the waveform shapes followed by clustering is the standard and ICA is used to separate overlapping spikes ([7.16.3](/7.16.3)). The assumptions are strong: the sources must be independent and non-Gaussian, the number of sources cannot exceed the number of channels, and the sources are determined only up to permutation and scale. When the "sources" of neural activity are not independent, as for the components of a smooth trajectory, ICA gives no meaningful decomposition and the structured methods of Section 28.3 are more appropriate.

## Summary

- The dimension of population activity is set by the dynamics and task, not by the number of neurons (Section 28.1); redundancy is a feature that dimensionality reduction describes.
- PCA finds the directions of largest variance (Eq. (28.1)), computed by the SVD; the participation ratio (Eq. (28.2)) measures dimension without a threshold. For a translation-invariant ring population PCA yields the Fourier modes, with pairs of equal eigenvalues, and the first two PCs trace a circle (Theorem 28.2.1), with $\mathrm{PR} \approx \sqrt{2\pi}/w$ for a bump of width $w$.
- Sample eigenvalues of noise fill the Marchenko–Pastur band (Eq. (28.3)); components must be validated by cross-validation.
- Demixed PCA (Eq. (28.4)) separates the task parameters; factor analysis and GPFA separate shared latent variables from private noise and use temporal smoothness to recover single-trial trajectories.
- t-SNE (Eqs. (28.5)–(28.6)) and UMAP visualize clusters but their sizes, distances and sometimes even clusters are artifacts of the settings; conclusions need controls in the original space.
- Neural manifolds (Definition 28.5.1) match the topology of the represented variable: a ring for head direction, a torus for grid cells, rotational dynamics in motor cortex, found by jPCA (Definition 28.5.2, Theorem 28.5.1); claims need surrogate-data controls.
- Variance is not information: the Fisher discriminant $\boldsymbol{w} \propto S_{\mathrm{W}}^{-1}\Delta\boldsymbol{\mu}$ (Theorem 28.6.1) is the optimal linear decoding direction and is linked to the Fisher information of Chapter 26; ICA separates independent non-Gaussian sources (Definition 28.7.1).

## Questions

1. Compute the participation ratio of a population whose covariance matrix has the eigenvalues $4, 2, 1, 1$ (all others zero), and of one with $N$ equal nonzero eigenvalues. Why is the participation ratio less sensitive than "the number of components explaining $90\,\%$ of the variance" to the choice of a threshold?

   **Answer** For $(4, 2, 1, 1)$: $\sum\lambda = 8$, $\sum\lambda^2 = 16 + 4 + 1 + 1 = 22$, so $\mathrm{PR} = 64/22 = 2.9$. For $N$ equal eigenvalues $\lambda$: $(N\lambda)^2/(N\lambda^2) = N$. The participation ratio is a smooth function of all eigenvalues, with the large ones weighted most, so adding many small noisy eigenvalues changes it little; the $90\,\%$ criterion depends on where the cumulative sum crosses a threshold, and can jump by many components when the spectrum has a long tail.
2. A skew-symmetric $2\times2$ matrix $M = \begin{pmatrix}0 & -\omega\\ \omega & 0\end{pmatrix}$ describes the dynamics $\dot{\boldsymbol{x}} = \boldsymbol{x}M$ of a population state. Find the eigenvalues, the period of the rotation for a movement with a rotation frequency of $2\,\mathrm{Hz}$, and the angle covered in $100\,\mathrm{ms}$.

   **Answer** The characteristic polynomial is $\lambda^2 + \omega^2$, so $\lambda = \pm\mathrm{i}\omega$. A frequency of $2\,\mathrm{Hz}$ means $\omega = 2\pi\times2 = 12.6\,\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}$ and period $1/2\,\mathrm{s} = 500\,\mathrm{ms}$. In $100\,\mathrm{ms}$ the angle is $\omega t = 1.26\,\mathrm{rad} = 72^\circ$, one fifth of a turn. Motor-cortex rotations in the experiments of Churchland et al. are of this order, completing about one cycle during a reach of a few hundred milliseconds.
3. A recording of 100 neurons has $T = 400$ time bins. What eigenvalue band does Eq. (28.3) give for noise of unit variance, and what does it imply for a component with an eigenvalue of $2.0$?

   **Answer** $\gamma = N/T = 0.25$ and $\sqrt\gamma = 0.5$, so the noise eigenvalues lie between $(1 - 0.5)^2 = 0.25$ and $(1 + 0.5)^2 = 2.25$. A component with an eigenvalue of $2.0$ is inside the band and cannot be distinguished from noise on this evidence alone; only components well above $2.25$ (or components that pass cross-validation) are reliable. For the same $N$, a recording with $T = 4000$ gives an upper edge of $(1 + 0.158)^2 = 1.34$.
4. Two clusters of equal size, one tight and one diffuse, appear equally large in a t-SNE map. Explain why, using Eq. (28.5), and say what can still be learned from the map.

   **Answer** The bandwidth $\sigma_i$ of each point is adjusted so that its perplexity (effective number of neighbors) has a fixed value. In a tight cluster $\sigma_i$ is small and in a diffuse one large, so that every point has the same effective number of neighbors, and the probabilities $p_{j\mid i}$ within each cluster have the same form. The low-dimensional map has no scale on its own, so both clusters are drawn with the same size, and the density information is lost. What remains is the neighborhood structure: points that are neighbors in the map are usually neighbors in the data, so cluster membership and the presence of substructure can be assessed (with several perplexities), though not size, density or the distance between clusters.
5. (Open question) A population recorded during head turns appears to lie on a ring. Describe an analysis that tests whether it is really a ring (intrinsic dimension 1, circular topology) and not a bump of a two-dimensional manifold or an artifact of smoothness.

   **Answer** First, decode the head direction from the population in held-out data: if the activity lies on a ring ordered by the angle, the decoded angle should follow the true angle with small circular error, and the PC1–PC2 projection should be a closed curve ordered by heading (Figure of Section 28.2). Second, compute the persistent homology of the point cloud (after a nonlinear reduction or directly in the PC space): a ring has one long-lived one-dimensional feature (Betti numbers $1, 1$) and no long-lived two-dimensional hole, whereas a torus has two ($1, 2, 1$) and a filled disk none. Third, compare with surrogate data that keep the single-neuron tuning curves and the autocorrelation in time but destroy the relative timing between neurons (circularly shifting each neuron's spike train by independent random lags): a ring that survives only in the real data reflects population structure, not merely the smoothness of the tuning (Elsayed and Cunningham, 2017). Finally, test that the structure is intrinsic by checking that it persists when the animal is not moving or is asleep, as for head-direction cells (Chaudhuri et al., 2019).
