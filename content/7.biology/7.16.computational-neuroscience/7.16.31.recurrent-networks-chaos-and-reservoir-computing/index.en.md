The networks of [7.16.15](/7.16.15) were analyzed near fixed points: they amplify, integrate or oscillate, and their behavior follows from a few eigenvalues. Cortical circuits are not like that. Their neurons fire irregularly, their connections are numerous, strong and to a large extent random, and their activity is a high-dimensional, ever-changing trajectory. This chapter develops the theory of such networks. We first ask why firing is irregular and find the answer in the dynamic balance of excitation and inhibition; we then introduce the mathematics of chaos and show that random recurrent networks become chaotic when their connections are strong enough. Far from being a defect, rich and even chaotic dynamics can be a resource: reservoir computing and FORCE learning extract arbitrary computations from them by training only a readout. Finally, we show how trained networks can be reverse-engineered by finding their fixed points, which leads to the dynamical description of motor cortex in the next chapter ([7.16.32](/7.16.32)) and of working memory and decision-making later ([7.16.45](/7.16.45), [7.16.47](/7.16.47)). The general theory of nonlinear flows is in [2.4.11](/2.4.11), and the machine-learning view of recurrent networks in [4.7.5](/4.7.5).

## 31.1. The Puzzle of Irregular Firing

Cortical neurons in vivo fire with interspike intervals as variable as those of a Poisson process: the **coefficient of variation** of the intervals is close to 1, and the **Fano factor** of spike counts across repeated trials is near 1 ([7.16.16](/7.16.16)). Softky and Koch (1993) pointed out that this is surprising. A neuron that integrates many independent excitatory inputs averages out their randomness, as a sum of many random variables does, and should fire nearly periodically; irregular firing therefore requires either a coincidence-detecting neuron or an input whose fluctuations, not its mean, drive the neuron to threshold. Shadlen and Newsome (1998) proposed the second, with excitation and inhibition nearly cancelling, and van Vreeswijk and Sompolinsky (1996) showed that such cancellation arises automatically in strongly coupled networks.

**Proposition 31.1.1 (Why Inputs Must Balance)** Let a neuron receive $K$ independent Poisson inputs of rate $r$, each producing a postsynaptic current pulse of integrated charge $J/\sqrt K$, and integrate them over a time $\tau$. The mean input is $\sqrt K\,Jr\tau$ and its standard deviation is $J\sqrt{r\tau}$. As $K$ grows the mean diverges while the fluctuations stay of order 1, so a neuron with $K$ in the thousands is either saturated or silent unless an inhibitory input of the same order cancels the mean.

**Proof** The number of spikes from each input in a window $\tau$ is Poisson with mean and variance $r\tau$. The total charge is $(J/\sqrt K)$ times a sum of $K$ independent such counts, with mean $(J/\sqrt K)\,Kr\tau = \sqrt K\,Jr\tau$ and variance $(J^2/K)\,Kr\tau = J^2r\tau$. $\square$

In the **balanced state** ([Definition 15.7.1](/7.16.15#def-15.7.1)) the excitatory and inhibitory inputs, each of order $\sqrt K$, cancel to leading order, which fixes the population rates by the linear equations (15.11) regardless of the single-neuron nonlinearity; the neurons are driven by the remaining fluctuations of order 1 and fire irregularly. The cancellation is dynamic: if excitation momentarily exceeds inhibition, the inhibitory neurons, which receive the same excess, fire more within a synaptic time constant and restore the balance. Because the net feedback is strong, a balanced network tracks its inputs quickly, and because the excitation alone would be unstable, it is an **inhibition-stabilized network** ([7.16.15](/7.16.15#sec-15.4.2)).

The microscopic dynamics of the balanced state are chaotic. In the binary-neuron model of van Vreeswijk and Sompolinsky, the separation between two network states that differ in a single spike grows at a rate that diverges in the limit of large $K$; London and colleagues (2010) tested this in rat barrel cortex by adding a single extra spike to one neuron and found that it caused, on average, about 28 additional spikes in its neighbors within $20\,\mathrm{ms}$, with no lasting change in the mean rate. The network is sensitive to individual spikes, but its population rates are stable and track the input. To understand what such chaos means, we first study it in its simplest form.

## 31.2. Chaos

### 31.2.1. One-Dimensional Maps

A map $x_{n+1} = f(x_n)$ is the simplest dynamical system with discrete time. Its fixed points solve $x^* = f(x^*)$, and their stability follows from linearization, the discrete analogue of the eigenvalue criterion for flows ([2.4.11](/2.4.11#sec-11.3)).

**Proposition 31.2.1 (Stability of a Fixed Point of a Map)** Let $f$ be continuously differentiable with fixed point $x^*$. If $\lvert f'(x^*)\rvert < 1$, then $x^*$ is asymptotically stable; if $\lvert f'(x^*)\rvert > 1$, it is unstable. A periodic orbit $x_1^*, \dots, x_p^*$ of period $p$ is stable if $\bigl\lvert\prod_{k=1}^pf'(x_k^*)\bigr\rvert < 1$.

**Proof** For $x_n = x^* + \varepsilon_n$, Taylor's theorem gives $\varepsilon_{n+1} = f'(x^*)\varepsilon_n + o(\varepsilon_n)$, so small deviations shrink geometrically if $\lvert f'(x^*)\rvert < 1$ and grow if $\lvert f'(x^*)\rvert > 1$. A $p$-periodic orbit consists of fixed points of the $p$-fold composition $f^{(p)}$, whose derivative by the chain rule is $\prod_kf'(x_k^*)$. $\square$

**Definition 31.2.1 (Logistic Map)** The **logistic map** is $x_{n+1} = rx_n(1 - x_n)$ on $[0, 1]$, with parameter $0 \le r \le 4$; it is the discrete-time version of logistic population growth with nonoverlapping generations (May, 1976).

For $1 < r < 3$ the map has the stable fixed point $x^* = 1 - 1/r$, with $f'(x^*) = 2 - r$. At $r = 3$, $f'(x^*) = -1$ and the fixed point loses stability to a stable orbit of period 2: a *period-doubling bifurcation*, in which the deviation flips sign at each step and its magnitude no longer decays. The 2-cycle loses stability in turn at $r = 1 + \sqrt6 \approx 3.449$, giving a 4-cycle, and the bifurcations accumulate at $r_\infty \approx 3.5699$, the intervals between them shrinking by the universal Feigenbaum ratio $\delta \approx 4.669$, the same for all smooth unimodal maps. Beyond $r_\infty$ the orbits of most parameters never settle on a cycle: they are chaotic, interrupted by windows of periodic behavior, the largest of which, with period 3, opens at $r = 1 + \sqrt8 \approx 3.828$ (figure below, A).

**Definition 31.2.2 (Lyapunov Exponent)** The **Lyapunov exponent** of an orbit $x_0, x_1, \dots$ of a map $f$ is
$$
\lambda = \lim_{n\to\infty}\frac1n\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\ln\lvert f'(x_k)\rvert, \tag{31.1}
$$
the mean exponential rate at which nearby orbits separate: an infinitesimal separation $\delta_0$ grows as $\delta_n \approx \delta_0\,\mathrm{e}^{\lambda n}$. For a flow $\dot{\boldsymbol{x}} = \boldsymbol{F}(\boldsymbol{x})$ in $\mathbb{R}^N$ there are $N$ exponents; the largest is the growth rate of a typical tangent vector under the linearized flow $\dot{\boldsymbol{v}} = J(\boldsymbol{x}(t))\boldsymbol{v}$.

**Definition 31.2.3 (Chaos)** A bounded orbit of a deterministic dynamical system is **chaotic** when it is aperiodic (not converging to a fixed point, a periodic orbit or a quasiperiodic orbit) and has a positive largest Lyapunov exponent, that is, sensitive dependence on initial conditions.

A positive exponent means that any uncertainty in the initial state, however small, grows to the size of the attractor after a time of order $\lambda^{-1}\ln(L/\delta_0)$: prediction is possible for a few Lyapunov times and then fails, although the system is deterministic. Flows need at least three dimensions to be chaotic (the Poincaré–Bendixson theorem forbids chaos in the plane; [2.4.11](/2.4.11#sec-11.6.2)); the Lorenz equations of atmospheric convection are the classic example, with largest exponent $\lambda \approx 0.906$ in their standard parameterization.

### 31.2.2. The Transition to Chaos in Random Networks

Sompolinsky, Crisanti and Sommers (1988) asked what happens in a large rate network whose connections are random.

**Formula 31.2.1 (Random Rate Network)** The currents $x_i$ of $N$ units obey
$$
\tau\frac{\mathrm{d}x_i}{\mathrm{d}t} = -x_i + g\sum_{j=1}^Nw_{ij}\tanh(x_j), \qquad w_{ij} \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \mathcal{N}(0, 1/N), \tag{31.2}
$$
where the gain $g$ sets the strength of the connections (here $g$ is a dimensionless gain, not a conductance) and $r_j = \tanh(x_j)$ plays the role of a rate measured from baseline.

**Theorem 31.2.1 (Stability of the Silent State)** For $N \to \infty$ the fixed point $\boldsymbol{x} = \boldsymbol{0}$ of Eq. (31.2) is stable if $g < 1$ and unstable if $g > 1$.

**Proof** The Jacobian at $\boldsymbol{0}$ is $(-I + gW)/\tau$, since $\tanh'(0) = 1$. By the circular law (Girko; Bai), the eigenvalues of a random $N\times N$ matrix with independent entries of variance $1/N$ fill the unit disk in the complex plane uniformly as $N \to \infty$, so those of $gW$ fill the disk of radius $g$ and those of the Jacobian the disk of radius $g/\tau$ centered at $-1/\tau$. All real parts are negative if and only if $g < 1$. $\square$

For $g < 1$ every initial condition decays to silence, with largest Lyapunov exponent $(g - 1)/\tau$. For $g > 1$ the silent state is unstable, and Sompolinsky and colleagues showed with dynamic mean-field theory that the network settles into a state of irregular, self-sustained fluctuations that is chaotic for any $g > 1$ in the limit $N \to \infty$. The theory gives a self-consistent equation for the autocorrelation of $x_i(t)$; its correlation time diverges as $g \to 1^+$, so near the transition the network generates slow fluctuations from fast units, and the largest Lyapunov exponent rises continuously from zero (figure below). Finite networks behave the same way, with the transition smeared over a range of $g$ around 1. The same transition occurs in networks that respect Dale's law and in spiking networks, where rate chaos coexists with the spike-level chaos of the balanced state (Ostojic, 2014; Kadmon and Sompolinsky, 2015).

A: rates of five units for $g = 0.8$: activity decays to the silent fixed point within a few time constants. ## 31.3. Reservoir Computing

### 31.3.1. Computing with Transients

A chaotic network is useless as a memory of fixed states but rich as a source of dynamics: its units respond to an input with a large variety of nonlinear, history-dependent time courses. Two proposals, made independently in 2001 and 2002, exploited this.

**Definition 31.3.1 (Reservoir Computing)** In **reservoir computing** a fixed, randomly connected recurrent network (the reservoir) is driven by the input, and only a readout, usually a linear combination of the activities of the reservoir units, is trained to produce the desired output. The **echo state network** (Jaeger, 2001) uses a reservoir of rate units in discrete time, $\boldsymbol{x}(n + 1) = \tanh\bigl(W\boldsymbol{x}(n) + W_{\mathrm{in}}\boldsymbol{u}(n + 1)\bigr)$, $\boldsymbol{y}(n) = W_{\mathrm{out}}\boldsymbol{x}(n)$; the **liquid state machine** (Maass, Natschläger and Markram, 2002) uses a reservoir of spiking neurons with dynamic synapses, like the surface of a liquid that retains ripples from all recent perturbations.

The idea separates the two jobs of a recurrent network. The reservoir projects the recent history of the input into a high-dimensional state, where nonlinear functions of the history become linearly separable, the same advantage of expansion that **support vector machines** obtain with a kernel ([4.5.5](/4.5.5)) and that the granule cells of the cerebellum provide ([7.16.33](/7.16.33)). The readout performs the task. For this to work, the reservoir state must depend on the input history and not on its own initial condition.

**Definition 31.3.2 (Echo State Property)** A reservoir has the **echo state property** if, for every input sequence, any two state sequences generated from the same input converge to each other: $\lVert\boldsymbol{x}(n) - \boldsymbol{x}'(n)\rVert \to 0$ as $n \to \infty$ for all initial states. The state is then an "echo" of the input history, a function of $\boldsymbol{u}(n), \boldsymbol{u}(n - 1), \dots$ alone.

**Proposition 31.3.1 (A Sufficient Condition)** If the largest singular value of $W$ satisfies $\sigma_{\max}(W) < 1$, a $\tanh$ reservoir has the echo state property. If the **spectral radius** of $W$ exceeds 1, it does not have the echo state property for zero input.

**Proof** Since $\lvert\tanh a - \tanh b\rvert \le \lvert a - b\rvert$ componentwise, two states driven by the same input satisfy $\lVert\boldsymbol{x}(n + 1) - \boldsymbol{x}'(n + 1)\rVert \le \lVert W(\boldsymbol{x}(n) - \boldsymbol{x}'(n))\rVert \le \sigma_{\max}\lVert\boldsymbol{x}(n) - \boldsymbol{x}'(n)\rVert$, a contraction. For zero input, $\boldsymbol{x} = \boldsymbol{0}$ is a fixed point with Jacobian $W$; if the spectral radius exceeds 1 it is unstable (Proposition 31.2.1 in many dimensions), so the zero state sequence and a nearby one do not converge. $\square$

In practice reservoirs are scaled to a spectral radius just below 1, which is usually sufficient for the echo state property when the input is strong enough, although not guaranteed by the proposition (Yildiz, Jaeger and Kiebel, 2012). The readout is fitted by **ridge regression**: collecting the states of a training run as the columns of $X$ and the targets as those of $Y$,
$$
W_{\mathrm{out}} = YX^{\mathsf T}\bigl(XX^{\mathsf T} + \lambda I\bigr)^{-1}, \tag{31.3}
$$
a single linear solve that replaces the gradient training of a recurrent network.

### 31.3.2. Memory, the Edge of Chaos and Prediction

How much of its input history does a reservoir hold? Jaeger (2002) defined the short-term memory capacity as the sum over delays $k$ of the squared correlation $r^2(u(n - k), y_k(n))$ between the input $k$ steps ago and the best linear readout $y_k$ trained to reproduce it, and proved that it cannot exceed the number of units $N$ for i.i.d. input. The capacity is low in a reservoir with small spectral radius, whose activity forgets quickly, and in one with spectral radius much above 1, whose own dynamics overwrite the input; it is maximal just below 1, near the transition to chaos (figure below, B). This is one instance of the hypothesis that recurrent networks compute best near the "edge of chaos", where perturbations neither die out at once nor grow (Bertschinger and Natschläger, 2004). Reservoirs can also learn to imitate a dynamical system: trained to predict the next state of the Lorenz equations and then fed with their own predictions, they continue the chaotic trajectory accurately for several Lyapunov times and then remain on an attractor with the same statistics (Pathak and colleagues, 2018).

A: echo state property (Definition 31.3.2): two copies of a 200-unit reservoir, started from different states and driven by the same random input, converge for $\rho = 0.9$, converge slowly for $\rho = 1.0$ and do not converge for $\rho = 1.5$. B: short-term memory capacity of a 100-unit reservoir with i.i.d. input against $\rho$: maximal just below 1. C: a 400-unit reservoir with a ridge-regression readout (Eq. Reservoir computing has three appeals for neuroscience. Cortical circuits are recurrent with connections that are partly random, and many of their computations could be read out by downstream neurons that learn, without changing the recurrent circuit; neurons in prefrontal cortex have mixed selectivity to many task variables, the high-dimensional representation that a reservoir produces and that makes flexible linear readout possible (Rigotti and colleagues, 2013). Learning only the readout is easier to implement with local plasticity than training the recurrent connections. And any physical system with rich, fading dynamics can serve as a reservoir, which makes reservoir computing a leading approach in neuromorphic engineering with photonic, spintronic and memristive devices.

## 31.4. FORCE Learning

A reservoir with a linear readout produces an output that depends on the input history. To generate patterns autonomously, such as the commands for a rhythmic movement, the output must be fed back into the reservoir. Feedback makes training hard: the reservoir now receives its own, initially wrong, output, and the errors propagate around the loop and change the states on which the readout is trained. Sussillo and Abbott (2009) solved the problem by keeping the error small from the first moment of training.

**Algorithm 31.4.1 (FORCE Learning)** Consider the network $\tau\dot{\boldsymbol{x}} = -\boldsymbol{x} + gW\boldsymbol{r} + \boldsymbol{w}_{\mathrm{f}}z$, $\boldsymbol{r} = \tanh(\boldsymbol{x})$, with readout $z = \boldsymbol{w}^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol{r}$ fed back through fixed weights $\boldsymbol{w}_{\mathrm{f}}$, and a target $f(t)$. Initialize $\boldsymbol{w} = \boldsymbol{0}$ and $P = I/\alpha$. At each update time:

1. compute the error $e = z - f(t)$ and the gain vector $\boldsymbol{k} = P\boldsymbol{r}/(1 + \boldsymbol{r}^{\mathsf T}P\boldsymbol{r})$;
2. update $P \leftarrow P - \boldsymbol{k}\boldsymbol{r}^{\mathsf T}P$ and $\boldsymbol{w} \leftarrow \boldsymbol{w} - e\,\boldsymbol{k}$.

$P$ is the running estimate of the inverse of the correlation matrix of the rates plus $\alpha I$, and the update is recursive least squares: at each step $\boldsymbol{w}$ is the ridge-regression solution (Eq. (31.3)) for all data seen so far. Training stops when the weight changes have become negligible.

The name stands for first-order reduced and controlled error. Because recursive least squares makes the output match the target almost immediately, the network is always driven by nearly the correct feedback, and the weights then only need to be refined until the output remains correct without further changes. Training works best when the network is initially chaotic ($g \approx 1.5$): the strong feedback of the trained output suppresses the chaos and entrains the network into a stable, periodic or more complex trajectory, and the initial richness of the dynamics provides the basis functions from which the output is built (figure below). Networks trained in this way produce periodic patterns, motor-like time series, the dynamics of a chaotic attractor, and, with several readouts, switching between patterns on command.

![FORCE learning (Algorithm 31.4.1; $N = 500$, $g = 1.5$, $\tau = 10\,\mathrm{ms}$, updates every $2\,\mathrm{ms}$, $\alpha = 1$). A: before training (first second) the output of the spontaneously chaotic network is near zero; when recursive least squares is switched on the output matches the target, a sum of four harmonics with a period of $600\,\mathrm{ms}$, within a few updates; after $10\,\mathrm{s}$ the weights are frozen, and the network continues to generate the pattern by itself. B: the size of the weight changes decreases by orders of magnitude during training. C: rates of four network units: irregular before training, periodic and locked to the output after.](./plots/force.py)

FORCE learning is not a biologically plausible learning rule, because the update of each weight requires the matrix $P$, which depends on the activities of all units. Its role in neuroscience is that of an existence proof and a model generator: it shows that a recurrent network of a given size and type can produce a given behavior, and it provides networks whose activity can be compared with recordings. Variants train the recurrent weights themselves (full-FORCE; DePasquale and colleagues, 2018) or spiking networks (Nicola and Clopath, 2017), and local approximations to the rule have been proposed in which a reward-modulated Hebbian term replaces recursive least squares (Hoerzer, Legenstein and Maass, 2014; the **three-factor learning rule** of [7.16.14](/7.16.14#sec-14.9)).

## 31.5. Opening the Black Box

Recurrent networks trained by **backpropagation through time** ([4.7.5](/4.7.5#sec-5.2.2)) or by FORCE solve tasks that neuroscientists study in animals: holding items in memory, integrating evidence, producing timed movements. The trained network is as opaque as the brain, but unlike the brain it can be analyzed completely, and Sussillo and Barak (2013) proposed a general method.

**Definition 31.5.1 (Fixed and Slow Points)** For an autonomous network $\dot{\boldsymbol{x}} = \boldsymbol{F}(\boldsymbol{x})$ (the trained network with its input held constant), let $q(\boldsymbol{x}) = \tfrac12\lVert\boldsymbol{F}(\boldsymbol{x})\rVert^2$, which is proportional to the squared speed of the state. Its global minima $q = 0$ are the fixed points; local minima with small positive $q$ are **slow points**, regions where the state moves so slowly that it behaves as near a fixed point on the time scale of the task.

The method minimizes $q$ by gradient descent from many initial states, usually sampled from the trajectories of the network during the task, and linearizes the dynamics at each minimum. The eigenvalues of the Jacobian classify the points: attractors, saddles with a given number of unstable directions, line attractors with an eigenvalue near zero. Together with the eigenvectors, the fixed points form a "dynamical skeleton" that explains how the network solves the task. In the 3-bit flip-flop task, three input channels deliver brief pulses of $+1$ or $-1$, and three outputs must hold the sign of the last pulse on their channel. The trained network has eight attractors, one for each of the $2^3$ memories, and saddles with a single unstable direction between attractors that differ in one bit; an input pulse pushes the state across the stable manifold of a saddle, and the unstable direction of the saddle channels it to the new attractor (figure below, A and B).

The approach has explained how prefrontal circuits could implement context-dependent decisions (Mante, Sussillo, Shenoy and Newsome, 2013). Monkeys reported either the color or the direction of motion of a random-dot stimulus, depending on a contextual cue. Neurons in prefrontal cortex carried both kinds of sensory evidence in both contexts, so the irrelevant evidence was not filtered out early. A network trained on the same task integrated the relevant evidence along an approximate line attractor whose orientation was the same in both contexts, while the context changed the *selection vector*, the left eigenvector of the slow mode, which determines which input direction is integrated. The same analysis applies to networks that reproduce the population activity of motor cortex ([7.16.32](/7.16.32)).

## 31.6. Metastability and Stable Heteroclinic Channels

Fixed points and the transitions between them can also generate sequences without input. If a network has saddles, each of which has a single unstable direction that leads to the next, the state can travel from saddle to saddle in a fixed order.

**Definition 31.6.1 (Stable Heteroclinic Channel)** A **stable heteroclinic channel** is a sequence of saddle states, each connected to the next by a heteroclinic orbit along its unstable manifold, together with a neighborhood of these orbits that attracts nearby trajectories. A trajectory in the channel dwells near each saddle, a metastable state, for a time set by the strength of instability and by noise, and then moves to the next, in a reproducible order (Rabinovich, Huerta and Laurent, 2008).

**Formula 31.6.1 (Winnerless Competition)** In the generalized Lotka–Volterra network $\dot a_i = a_i\bigl(1 - \sum_j\rho_{ij}a_j\bigr)$, $a_i \ge 0$, with three units and cyclic competition $\rho = \begin{bmatrix}1 & \alpha & \beta\\ \beta & 1 & \alpha\\ \alpha & \beta & 1\end{bmatrix}$, $0 < \alpha < 1 < \beta$ and $\alpha + \beta > 2$, the states in which one unit is active and the others silent are saddles, connected in a cycle $1 \to 3 \to 2 \to 1$ by heteroclinic orbits, and the cycle attracts all interior trajectories (May and Leonard, 1975).

Without noise the dwell times grow without bound as the trajectory approaches the cycle; small noise keeps it at a fixed distance from the saddles and makes the switching regular (figure below, C). Such winnerless competition was proposed for the odor-specific sequences of activity in the insect antennal lobe and for sequences of metastable states in sensory and frontal cortex. In the gustatory cortex of rats, hidden Markov model analysis of ensemble recordings shows that a taste is processed through a sequence of discrete states, each lasting hundreds of milliseconds, whose transition times vary from trial to trial (Jones, Fontanini, Sadacca, Miller and Katz, 2007); these are naturally explained by metastable attractors visited in turn. Metastability reconciles two demands on brain dynamics, the stability of states that hold information and the flexibility to move between them (Tognoli and Kelso, 2014).

A: a 3-bit flip-flop built from three bistable self-exciting units ($\dot x_k = -x_k + 2\tanh x_k + u_k$), embedded by a random rotation in a ten-dimensional state space: pulses on each input (gray) set the corresponding output to $+1$ or $-1$, and the other bits are unaffected. ## 31.7. Relation to Machine-Learning Recurrent Networks

The networks of this chapter are close relatives of the **recurrent neural networks** of machine learning ([4.7.5](/4.7.5)). A simple recurrent network is an echo state network whose recurrent weights are also trained, and the difficulty of training it, the vanishing and exploding gradients through time, is the discrete-time version of the dichotomy between decaying ($g < 1$) and chaotic ($g > 1$) dynamics of Theorem 31.2.1: gradients propagate stably only near the edge of chaos, which is one reason why recurrent weights are initialized with spectral radius near 1. **Long short-term memory** networks avoid the problem with gates that create near-integrator dynamics, the line attractors of [7.16.15](/7.16.15#sec-15.2.1) in engineered form. Modern state-space models, which use linear recurrences with carefully parameterized eigenvalues, return to the reservoir idea of a fixed or structured recurrent part with fast parallel training ([4.7.9](/4.7.9)).

For neuroscience the main use of trained recurrent networks is as hypothesis generators. Networks trained on the same tasks as animals reproduce features of neural recordings that were not built into them, such as the mixed selectivity of prefrontal neurons, the rotational dynamics of motor cortex and the low-dimensional geometry of working memory, and a single network trained on many cognitive tasks develops clusters of units specialized for families of tasks and reuses dynamical motifs across tasks (Yang and colleagues, 2019; Driscoll, Shenoy and Sussillo, 2024). The question for each such model is which of its features are dictated by the task and which by arbitrary choices of architecture and training, and comparing many networks with the same data is the current method for answering it.

## Summary

|---|---|---|
| Balance | Mean input $\sim \sqrt K$, fluctuations $\sim 1$: E and I must cancel | Proposition 31.1.1 |

| Echo state property | Sufficient: $\sigma_{\max}(W) < 1$ | Proposition 31.3.1 |

| Reverse engineering | Minima of $q = \frac12\lVert\boldsymbol{F}\rVert^2$, linearization | Definition 31.5.1 |

- Irregular cortical firing is explained by the balanced state, in which large excitation and inhibition cancel and fluctuations drive the neurons; such networks are chaotic at the level of spikes and sensitive to single spikes, but their rates are stable.
- The logistic map shows the route to chaos by period doubling and the meaning of the Lyapunov exponent; random rate networks become chaotic when the gain exceeds 1, the edge where the spectrum of the connectivity leaves the unit disk.
- Rich dynamics are a computational resource: reservoirs with fading memory, read out linearly, hold up to $N$ steps of input history and can predict chaotic systems; FORCE learning tames chaotic networks to produce arbitrary outputs.
- Trained networks can be understood through their fixed and slow points; metastable saddles connected in sequence generate reproducible sequences of states.

## Questions

1. Show that the fixed point $x^* = 1 - 1/r$ of the logistic map is stable for $1 < r < 3$, and compute the 2-cycle that appears at $r = 3$ for $r = 3.2$. Verify its stability with Proposition 31.2.1.

   **Answer** $f'(x) = r(1 - 2x)$, so $f'(x^*) = r(1 - 2 + 2/r) = 2 - r$, and $\lvert 2 - r\rvert < 1$ iff $1 < r < 3$. The 2-cycle solves $f(f(x)) = x$ without the fixed points; dividing out the fixed-point factor gives $r^2x^2 - r(r + 1)x + (r + 1) = 0$, so $x_\pm = \bigl(r + 1 \pm \sqrt{(r + 1)(r - 3)}\bigr)/(2r)$. For $r = 3.2$: $\sqrt{4.2\cdot0.2} = 0.9165$, $x_\pm = (4.2 \pm 0.9165)/6.4 = 0.7995$ and $0.5130$. The product of derivatives is $f'(x_+)f'(x_-) = r^2(1 - 2x_+)(1 - 2x_-) = 10.24\cdot(-0.599)(-0.026) \approx 0.16$, of absolute value below 1: the cycle is stable. (In general the product equals $-r^2 + 2r + 4$, which leaves $(-1, 1)$ at $r = 1 + \sqrt6$.)

2. In the random network (Formula 31.2.1) with $N = 1000$ and $g = 0.9$, how long does a perturbation of the silent state take to decay by a factor of $\mathrm{e}$, with $\tau = 10\,\mathrm{ms}$? What happens to this time as $g \to 1$, and why might a network operate near this point?

   **Answer** The slowest mode decays at the rate $(1 - g)/\tau$, so the time is $\tau/(1 - g) = 100\,\mathrm{ms}$, ten times the single-unit time constant (finite $N$ blurs the edge of the disk slightly). As $g \to 1$ the time diverges: the network integrates and remembers its inputs over long times, as for the selective amplification of Proposition 15.2.1, and just above 1 it generates slow, rich spontaneous fluctuations. Operating near the transition maximizes memory capacity and the variety of responses available to a readout (Section 31.3.2), at the price of sensitivity to noise.

3. Why does training a reservoir with feedback by batch ridge regression on recorded states (teacher forcing) often fail when the trained network is then run with its own output fed back? How does FORCE avoid the problem?

   **Answer** With teacher forcing the reservoir is driven by the exact target during training, and the readout is fitted to the states that the exact feedback produces. When the network runs freely, small readout errors change the feedback, which changes the states, which the readout was not trained on; the errors compound around the loop, and the trajectory can drift off or become unstable. FORCE trains online while the network is driven by its own output: recursive least squares keeps the output error small from the first steps, so the states visited during training are those of the closed loop, and the weights converge to a solution that is stable under self-generated feedback.

4. A network trained on a 1-bit flip-flop has two attractors. What other fixed point must exist between them, what is its type, and why would the method of Definition 31.5.1 find it?

   **Answer** Two attractors in a connected state space are separated by the boundary between their basins, the stable manifold of a saddle with one unstable direction (a saddle of index 1), which typically lies between them; along the unstable direction trajectories flow to either attractor. Minimizing $q$ finds all zeros of $\boldsymbol{F}$, saddles included, because $q$ measures the speed and not the stability; starting points sampled from the transitions between the memories pass near the saddle, where the state moves slowly, so the minimization converges to it. Its unstable eigenvector points along the transition, and the size of the input pulse needed to flip the bit is the distance to its stable manifold.

5. (Open question) Is the chaos of cortical networks a problem for coding, or a feature? Discuss with reference to the experiment of London and colleagues (2010).

   **Answer** The experiment shows that a single extra spike changes the subsequent spike pattern of the local network without changing the population rate. This argues against codes based on precise spike timing generated by recurrent cortical circuits, since such timing is not reproducible, and in favor of rate or population codes that are robust to microscopic chaos; information carried by timing would have to be imposed by strong, synchronous input. At the same time, chaotic or near-chaotic dynamics produce the variability that allows exploration in learning (for example, in songbird motor learning), the rich basis of responses that reservoir readouts exploit, and fast responses to inputs in the balanced state. A middle view is that cortex operates near the edge of chaos, where population dynamics are stable and controllable by input while microscopic dynamics are chaotic; testing it requires measuring Lyapunov exponents or perturbation responses of real networks at the population level, for example with holographic optogenetic stimulation of chosen neurons.
