Social behaviors (courtship, aggression, parenting, affiliation) are among the most stereotyped behaviors of animals, and many are innate: a male mouse raised in isolation attacks an intruder the first time it meets one. They offer a route to the question of how genes build circuits that produce behavior. Two features make them informative. They differ between the sexes, so the genetic switches of **sex determination** ([7.5.26](/7.5.26)) give a handle on the neurons involved; and they are controlled by a small number of genetically identifiable neuron types in the hypothalamus and its insect counterparts, whose activity can be recorded and manipulated ([7.16.3](/7.16.3)). This chapter treats these circuits at the level of cell types, development and dynamics. The regulatory logic of the hypothalamus was introduced in [7.16.34](/7.16.34); the ecology and evolution of social behavior belong to animal behavior and are not treated here.

## 36.1. A Genetic Switch for a Behavior in *Drosophila*

### 36.1.1. fruitless and doublesex

Male fruit flies court females through a stereotyped sequence of orienting, following, tasting contact pheromones with the forelegs and producing a wing-vibration song; each step depends on cues from the partner and raises the probability of the next. Sex in *Drosophila* is determined cell-autonomously by the number of X chromosomes, through a cascade of **alternative splicing**: in females the protein Sex-lethal directs female-specific splicing of *tra* mRNA, and the Tra protein in turn directs the splicing of two transcription factor genes, *fruitless* (*fru*) and *doublesex* (*dsx*) ([7.5.26](/7.5.26#sec-26.5)).

**Definition 36.1.1 (Fruitless and Doublesex)** **Fruitless** (FruM) is a BTB–zinc finger transcription factor produced only in males, by default splicing of transcripts from one promoter of the *fru* gene; it is expressed in about 2000 neurons (2% of the nervous system) and is required for male courtship. **Doublesex** is a transcription factor whose male (DsxM) and female (DsxF) isoforms, produced by sex-specific splicing, control sexual differentiation of the body and of a subset of neurons.

Genetic experiments established the role of FruM. Males lacking it do not court females; females engineered to splice *fru* in the male mode, and so to express FruM, perform the male courtship sequence toward other females (Demir and Dickson, 2005). A single splicing decision in one gene thus specifies much of a complex behavior. FruM acts during development by specifying the number, arbors and connections of the Fru⁺ neurons. In males a cluster of interneurons called P1 survives and branches widely because of FruM and DsxM; in females the homologous cells die by apoptosis. In the olfactory system, male-specific arbors of Fru⁺ neurons route the male pheromone cis-vaccenyl acetate, detected by the odorant receptor Or67d, to circuits that suppress courtship of other males.

### 36.1.2. P1 Neurons and a Persistent State

The Fru⁺ neurons form a circuit from sensory neurons (taste neurons of the forelegs that detect female pheromones, olfactory and visual neurons) through integrating interneurons to descending neurons and motor circuits of the ventral nerve cord. P1 neurons, about 20 per hemisphere, are its decision node: they are excited by female cues and inhibited by male cues, and activating them makes a solitary male sing toward nothing. Their output reaches, through a descending neuron, the thoracic circuits that pattern the song, which act as a **central pattern generator**. A brief activation of P1 neurons also leaves the male in a state of enhanced courtship and aggression that persists for minutes after the stimulation (Hoopfer and colleagues, 2015), much longer than the P1 response itself. P1 neurons thus not only command an action but set an internal state: a scalable, persistent variable that biases later behavior, a property that recurs in mammalian social circuits (Section 36.3.2).

## 36.2. Sexual Differentiation of the Mammalian Brain

### 36.2.1. Hormones Organize and Activate

In mammals the gonads are determined by *Sry* on the Y chromosome ([7.5.26](/7.5.26#sec-26.2)), and the brain differentiates mainly under the influence of gonadal hormones rather than of its own sex chromosomes. Phoenix, Goy, Gerall and Young (1959) found that female guinea pigs exposed to testosterone before birth showed male-typical rather than female-typical behavior as adults, even when given the hormones appropriate to adult females, whereas testosterone given to adult females had no lasting effect.

**Definition 36.2.1 (Organizational–Activational Hypothesis)** The **organizational–activational hypothesis** states that gonadal steroids act on the developing brain during a sensitive period to organize sexually dimorphic neural circuits permanently (organizational effects), and act on the adult brain to activate the behaviors these circuits support, transiently and reversibly (activational effects).

In rodents the organizational period spans late gestation and the first postnatal week, when the testes of males produce a surge of testosterone. Most masculinizing effects in the rodent brain are produced by estradiol: testosterone entering neurons is converted to estradiol by the enzyme aromatase, and estradiol acts through estrogen receptor α (Esr1). Female fetuses are protected from maternal estrogen because α-fetoprotein in their plasma binds estradiol but not testosterone. In primates, including humans, androgen receptors play a larger role: people with complete **androgen insensitivity syndrome** (XY, with testes but nonfunctional androgen receptors) develop a female-typical gender identity.

### 36.2.2. Sexually Dimorphic Nuclei

**Definition 36.2.2 (Sexually Dimorphic Nucleus)** A **sexually dimorphic nucleus** is a brain nucleus whose size, cell number or cellular composition differs consistently between the sexes; the classical example is the sexually dimorphic nucleus of the preoptic area (SDN-POA) of the rat, several times larger in males (Gorski and colleagues, 1978).

The SDN-POA illustrates the mechanism of organization: perinatal estradiol prevents the apoptosis of its neurons, so females treated with testosterone at birth develop a male-sized nucleus and males castrated at birth a female-sized one. Dimorphism can go either way: the anteroventral periventricular nucleus is larger in females and contains the kisspeptin neurons that trigger the preovulatory surge of luteinizing hormone. Many dimorphisms are not in size but in gene expression and connectivity: the same nucleus contains neuron types in different proportions, or with sex-specific receptor expression. Social behavior is therefore built mostly from shared circuits tuned differently in the two sexes, and sex-typical behaviors can often be evoked in the other sex by stimulating the right neurons. Pheromone sensing through the **vomeronasal organ** shows this: female mice lacking the vomeronasal channel TRPC2 display male-typical social behaviors (Kimchi, Xu and Dulac, 2007), so circuits for these behaviors exist in females and are normally suppressed by sensory input.

## 36.3. Aggression and the Ventromedial Hypothalamus

### 36.3.1. Graded Activity Selects Behavior

The ventrolateral part of the ventromedial hypothalamus (VMHvl) of the mouse contains neurons that express Esr1 and the progesterone receptor. In males these neurons are active during social encounters with both sexes, and optogenetic activation of the VMHvl evokes immediate attack, even toward inappropriate targets (Lin and colleagues, 2011). Graded activation of VMHvl Esr1⁺ neurons revealed a continuum: weak stimulation evokes social investigation and mating-related behavior, strong stimulation evokes attack (Lee and colleagues, 2014), and silencing the population reduces aggression. The same population thus supports behaviors of opposite character, and which one is expressed depends on the level of activity and on sensory context, as if the behaviors were different thresholds on one variable. In females, VMHvl Esr1⁺ neurons fall into a medial subpopulation involved in reproductive behavior and a lateral one that promotes aggression, which is high in lactating females (Hashikawa and colleagues, 2017).

### 36.3.2. An Approximate Line Attractor

Aggression has the properties of an internal state: it escalates over repeated encounters, persists for tens of seconds to minutes after the opponent leaves, and generalizes to new targets. Calcium imaging of hundreds of VMHvl Esr1⁺ neurons during encounters, analyzed with dynamical-systems models fitted to the population activity, found one slowly decaying dimension that integrates encounters and predicts the probability of attack (Nair and colleagues, 2023). The population behaves like an approximate line attractor ([7.16.33](/7.16.33)): a direction in state space along which activity neither grows nor decays quickly, so that the network stores a graded value.

**Example 36.3.1 (A Persistent Aggressive State)** A linear rate network $\tau\,\dot{\boldsymbol{r}} = -\boldsymbol{r} + W\boldsymbol{r} + \boldsymbol{b}\,u(t)$ of $N = 50$ units, with $\tau = 1\,\mathrm{s}$, has a symmetric weight matrix $W$ whose leading eigenvector $\boldsymbol{q}$ has eigenvalue $\lambda$ and whose other eigenvalues lie between 0 and 0.3. Activity along $\boldsymbol{q}$, $x = \boldsymbol{q}^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol{r}$, obeys $\tau\,\dot x = -(1 - \lambda)x + (\boldsymbol{q}^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol{b})\,u$, so its time constant is $\tau/(1 - \lambda)$: $100\,\mathrm{s}$ for $\lambda = 0.99$ and $5\,\mathrm{s}$ for $\lambda = 0.8$, while all other directions decay within about $1.4\,\mathrm{s}$. Brief inputs $u(t)$ (encounters with an intruder) accumulate along $\boldsymbol{q}$ only if $\lambda$ is close to 1. If the probability of attack is a sigmoid function of $x$, the near-integrator network escalates over three encounters and stays aggressive for minutes; the control network returns to baseline between encounters.

The model makes testable predictions: perturbations along the slow direction should shift the state persistently, whereas perturbations along other directions should decay quickly; and the slow time constant should depend on whatever provides the effective recurrent excitation. Optogenetic perturbations of VMHvl neurons do produce persistent shifts along the integration dimension, and its time constant depends on neuropeptide signaling; whether the slow dynamics arise from recurrent connections, slow peptide actions or intrinsic properties is not settled.

## 36.4. Parental Behavior

Virgin male mice often attack pups, whereas fathers and females care for them: they build nests, retrieve pups and groom them. Wu, Autry, Bergan, Watabe-Uchida and Dulac (2014) identified galanin-expressing neurons of the medial preoptic area (MPOA) as a key node. These neurons are active during parenting in both sexes; their ablation impairs parenting, and their optogenetic activation makes virgin males stop attacking pups and groom them. Subsets of the galanin neurons with different targets control different components of parenting: projections to the periaqueductal gray control pup grooming, to the VTA the motivation to approach pups, and to the medial amygdala the suppression of competing social interactions (Kohl and colleagues, 2018). As in the fly circuit, a behavior is assembled from modules by a central node that dispatches to motor and motivational targets. Parental and pup-directed aggressive behaviors compete, and their balance shifts with social experience and hormonal state over a period of weeks.

## 36.5. Oxytocin, Vasopressin and Social Bonds

**Oxytocin** and **vasopressin** are nine-amino-acid neuropeptides of the magnocellular neurons of the PVH and SON ([7.14.34](/7.14.34#sec-34.4)); besides their peripheral hormonal actions they act in the brain as **neuromodulators** released from dendrites and axon collaterals.

The clearest evidence for their role in social bonds comes from voles. Prairie voles form lasting pair bonds and both parents care for the young; closely related montane and meadow voles do not. The species differ not in the peptides but in the distribution of their receptors: prairie voles have dense oxytocin receptors in the nucleus accumbens and vasopressin V1a receptors in the ventral pallidum, regions of the reward system ([7.16.37](/7.16.37)). Receptor antagonists in these regions prevent the formation of a partner preference, and viral overexpression of the V1a receptor in the ventral pallidum of male meadow voles induced one (Lim and colleagues, 2004). A bond appears to form when cues of the partner are associated with activation of the reward system through these receptors, a form of reward learning ([7.16.39](/7.16.39)).

Oxytocin also modulates sensory processing for social behavior. In mice it enhances the responses of the left auditory cortex to pup distress calls, by transiently reducing inhibition, and enables pup retrieval by virgin females (Marlin and colleagues, 2015). Reported effects of intranasal oxytocin on human trust and social cognition have replicated poorly and should be treated with caution.

## 36.6. Birdsong

Songbirds learn their songs, and in many species only males sing. Learning proceeds in two phases: a sensory phase, in which a juvenile memorizes the song of an adult tutor during a **critical period**, and a sensorimotor phase, in which it practices and gradually matches its own song to the memorized template using auditory feedback. Birds deafened after the sensory phase but before practicing never produce normal song, and birds raised in isolation produce an abnormal song that still has species-typical features, so learning is constrained by an innate template.

The song system is a set of discrete forebrain nuclei. A motor pathway from HVC to the robust nucleus of the arcopallium (RA) to brainstem motor neurons of the vocal organ produces the song: HVC neurons that project to RA fire one brief burst at one moment of each song, so the population tiles the song in time like a clock, while RA neurons encode the acoustic features of syllables. An anterior forebrain pathway, homologous to a cortex–basal ganglia–thalamus loop (Area X, DLM, LMAN), is required for learning but not for singing a learned song. LMAN injects variability into RA that serves as exploration, and dopamine signals to Area X report whether a rendition was better or worse than expected, so song learning is a form of reinforcement learning in a **basal ganglia** circuit ([7.16.40](/7.16.40)). HVC and RA are several times larger in male than in female zebra finches (Nottebohm and Arnold, 1976), the first sexual dimorphism of brain nuclei discovered. Estradiol given to female hatchlings partially masculinizes them, but a zebra finch that was genetically male on one side of the body and female on the other had a more masculine song system on its male side in the same hormonal environment (Agate and colleagues, 2003): in birds, sex chromosome genes in the neurons themselves also contribute.

## Summary

- In *Drosophila*, sex-specific splicing produces FruM in males (Definition 36.1.1); FruM is necessary for courtship and sufficient to make females court, and FruM and DsxM specify the survival and wiring of P1 neurons, which command courtship and set a persistent internal state.
- In mammals, gonadal steroids organize the developing brain in a sensitive window and activate behavior in adults (Definition 36.2.1); in rodents masculinization is largely by estradiol made from testosterone by aromatase, in primates more by androgen receptors. Sexually dimorphic nuclei (Definition 36.2.2) arise by hormone-regulated cell survival, but most dimorphisms are differences of tuning within shared circuits.
- Graded activity of VMHvl Esr1⁺ neurons selects among social behaviors, from investigation to attack; their population activity contains a slow, integrating dimension, an approximate line attractor for an aggressive state (Example 36.3.1).
- MPOA galanin neurons are central to parental behavior, with projection-defined subsets controlling its components.
- Oxytocin and vasopressin receptors in reward areas are required for pair bonding in prairie voles; species differ in receptor distribution, not in the peptides.
- Birdsong is learned during a critical period through a motor pathway (HVC–RA) and a basal ganglia loop that implements reinforcement learning; the song nuclei are sexually dimorphic through hormones and cell-autonomous genetic factors.

| System | Key neurons | Developmental switch | Principle |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fly courtship | P1, Fru⁺ circuit | *fru*, *dsx* splicing | One gene specifies a circuit for an innate behavior; command neurons set states |
| Rodent aggression | VMHvl Esr1⁺ | Perinatal estradiol, adult steroids | Graded activity selects behaviors; slow integration |
| Rodent parenting | MPOA galanin | Social experience, hormones | A central node dispatches to modular outputs |
| Social bonds | Oxytocin and V1a receptors in reward areas | Receptor distribution | Social cues associated with reward |
| Birdsong | HVC, RA, Area X | Estradiol and sex chromosomes | Critical-period learning with a basal ganglia loop |

## Questions

1. Females that express FruM court other females, and males lacking FruM have male bodies but do not court. What do these results show about the relation between body and brain in sexual differentiation in flies, and how does it contrast with mammals?

   **Answer** In flies sexual identity is cell-autonomous: each cell reads its own chromosomal sex, and neurons acquire male or female identity through their own splicing of *fru* and *dsx*, independently of the rest of the body. Behavior follows the genotype of the neurons, not of the body. In mammals the brain is differentiated mainly by hormones from the gonads, so it develops according to its hormonal exposure; cell-autonomous effects of sex chromosome genes exist (and are more prominent in birds) but are secondary.

2. In Example 36.3.1, derive the slow-mode activity just after the $n$-th encounter when encounters are separated by an interval $T$. How do $\lambda$ and $T$ determine whether a threshold $\theta$ is ever reached?

   **Answer** Each short encounter adds an increment $\Delta$ (about $\boldsymbol{q}^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol{b}$ times the pulse duration divided by $\tau$), and between encounters $x$ decays by the factor $\rho = \mathrm{e}^{-(1-\lambda)T/\tau}$. After $n$ encounters $x_n = \Delta(1 + \rho + \dots + \rho^{n-1}) = \Delta(1 - \rho^n)/(1 - \rho)$, which approaches $\Delta/(1 - \rho)$. The threshold can be reached only if $\Delta/(1 - \rho) > \theta$, after $n = \lceil \ln(1 - \theta(1 - \rho)/\Delta)/\ln\rho \rceil$ encounters. With $\lambda = 0.99$ and $T = 30\,\mathrm{s}$, $\rho = \mathrm{e}^{-0.3} \approx 0.74$ and the asymptote is about $3.9\Delta$; with $\lambda = 0.8$, $\rho = \mathrm{e}^{-6} \approx 0$ and activity never exceeds about $\Delta$. Larger $\lambda$ and more frequent encounters both speed escalation.

3. Testosterone given to a newborn female rat produces a male-sized SDN-POA, and so does estradiol; testosterone given to a newborn female mouse lacking aromatase does not. What does each result show, and why are female fetuses not masculinized by maternal estradiol?

   **Answer** The first two results show that the masculinizing signal acts during the neonatal sensitive period and that estradiol is sufficient. The aromatase-knockout result shows that testosterone acts through its conversion to estradiol in the brain. Female fetuses are protected because α-fetoprotein in fetal plasma binds estradiol and keeps it out of the brain, whereas testosterone is not bound, reaches the brain and is aromatized locally; the masculinizing signal is thus confined to males although the active molecule is an estrogen.

4. (Open question) Pair bonding in prairie voles requires oxytocin and vasopressin receptors in reward areas. Propose a model in which a social bond is a learned association, and an experiment that distinguishes it from a model in which the peptides create a preference without learning.

   **Answer** Learning model: during social interaction with the partner, dopamine release in the nucleus accumbens coincides with oxytocin and vasopressin receptor activation and with the sensory signature of the partner (its odors); a three-factor plasticity rule strengthens the synapses that carry this signature onto reward circuits, so the partner becomes a conditioned rewarding stimulus. Predictions: the preference should be specific to the cues present during the interaction, should require dopamine receptor activation at the same time, and should be blocked by preventing plasticity in the accumbens during but not after the interaction. A test: pair peptide receptor activation (for example by local agonist infusion) with the odor of one individual and not another, without any social interaction, and test whether a preference forms specifically for the paired odor; then repeat with dopamine receptor antagonists during pairing. A learning model predicts an odor-specific preference that depends on dopamine; a direct, non-associative model predicts a general increase in sociability toward any individual.
