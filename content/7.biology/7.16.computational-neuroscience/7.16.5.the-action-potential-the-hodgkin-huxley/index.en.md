The **action potential** is the one signal that a neuron sends over a distance. It is produced by a handful of voltage-gated channel types acting on the passive membrane of [7.16.4](/7.16.4), and since Hodgkin and Huxley (1952) it has been described by a model in which each channel type is a conductance controlled by first-order gates. This chapter writes that model out in full, asks which of its assumptions are physics and which are curve fitting, uses it as a numerical laboratory for the classic phenomena of excitability, and then extends it with the other voltage- and $\ce{Ca^2+}$-gated currents that give real neurons their characteristic firing patterns. It ends with conduction along the axon.

The physiology of the action potential (phases, ionic basis, refractory periods, compound action potentials) is described in [7.14.4](/7.14.4#sec-4.6), and the biophysics in [7.6.30](/7.6.30#sec-30.6): the voltage clamp, the **Hodgkin–Huxley model** with its rate constants, the integration algorithm and the propagating wave. Here we do not repeat those derivations. The model built here is the object that the next three chapters reduce to two dimensions ([7.16.6](/7.16.6)), classify by bifurcations ([7.16.7](/7.16.7)) and replace by integrate-and-fire caricatures ([7.16.8](/7.16.8)).

## 5.1. Regenerative Excitation and the Logic of the Voltage Clamp

The defining property of the action potential is that it is *regenerative*: depolarization opens $\ce{Na+}$ channels, the inward $\ce{Na+}$ current depolarizes further, and the loop runs away until the membrane potential approaches $E_{\mathrm{Na}}$. A positive feedback of this kind has a threshold: below it the leak and $\ce{K+}$ currents win and the membrane returns to rest, above it the $\ce{Na+}$ current wins. Two slower negative feedbacks then end the event, $\ce{Na+}$ channel **inactivation** and the delayed opening of $\ce{K+}$ channels, and both remain in force for several milliseconds, which is the origin of refractoriness.

Positive feedback also makes the action potential impossible to analyze while it happens, because the current through each channel depends on a voltage that the current itself is changing. The **voltage clamp** of Cole, Marmont, Hodgkin and Huxley breaks the loop: a feedback amplifier holds $V$ at a command value, so that the capacitive current vanishes after a brief transient and the measured current is the ionic current at a fixed voltage ([7.6.30](/7.6.30#sec-30.6.1)). Replacing external $\ce{Na+}$ by choline (later, blocking with tetrodotoxin and tetraethylammonium) separates $I_{\mathrm{Na}}$ and $I_{\mathrm{K}}$; dividing each by its driving force gives conductances that depend on voltage and time but not on the current flowing. The program of Hodgkin and Huxley was therefore to measure $g_{\mathrm{Na}}(V, t)$ and $g_{\mathrm{K}}(V, t)$ under clamp, describe them by differential equations, and then *predict* the unclamped action potential, its shape, threshold and conduction velocity, with no further fitting. The prediction succeeded to within about 10%.

## 5.2. The Gating Equations

### 5.2.1. The Model

With outward current positive and per unit area of membrane, the Hodgkin–Huxley equations are

$$
c_{\mathrm{m}}\frac{\mathrm{d}V}{\mathrm{d}t} = -\bar g_{\mathrm{Na}}\,m^3h\,(V - E_{\mathrm{Na}}) - \bar g_{\mathrm{K}}\,n^4(V - E_{\mathrm{K}}) - g_{\mathrm{L}}(V - E_{\mathrm{L}}) + I_{\mathrm{ext}}, \tag{5.1}
$$

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}t} = \alpha_x(V)(1 - x) - \beta_x(V)\,x = \frac{x_\infty(V) - x}{\tau_x(V)}, \qquad x \in \{m, h, n\}, \tag{5.2}
$$

with $x_\infty = \alpha_x/(\alpha_x + \beta_x)$ and $\tau_x = 1/(\alpha_x + \beta_x)$. The rate functions, fitted to the squid giant axon at $6.3\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$, and the parameters ($\bar g_{\mathrm{Na}} = 120$, $\bar g_{\mathrm{K}} = 36$, $g_{\mathrm{L}} = 0.3\,\mathrm{mS\,cm^{-2}}$; $E_{\mathrm{Na}} = 50$, $E_{\mathrm{K}} = -77$, $E_{\mathrm{L}} = -54.4\,\mathrm{mV}$; $c_{\mathrm{m}} = 1\,\mu\mathrm{F\,cm^{-2}}$; rest at $-65\,\mathrm{mV}$) are listed in [7.6.30](/7.6.30#sec-30.6.2) and used in all simulations of this chapter. The rates scale with temperature by $Q_{10} \approx 3$: at $20\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ they are 4.5 times faster, at $37\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ about 30 times.

**Definition 5.2.1 (Gating Variable)** A **gating variable** $x(t) \in [0, 1]$ is the probability that a gate of a given type is in its permissive state, equivalently the fraction of such gates that are permissive in a large population of channels; it obeys the first-order kinetics (5.2) with voltage-dependent opening rate $\alpha_x$ and closing rate $\beta_x$. A gate whose permissive probability increases with depolarization is an *activation* gate ($m$, $n$); one whose permissive probability decreases with depolarization is an **inactivation** gate ($h$).

The three gates of the $\ce{Na+}$ conductance differ by an order of magnitude in speed: near threshold $\tau_m \approx 0.2$–$0.5\,\mathrm{ms}$, whereas $\tau_h$ and $\tau_n$ are 1–8 ms. This separation of time scales is what makes the spike possible, since $m$ follows $V$ almost instantly and opens the positive feedback before $h$ and $n$ can close it, and it is also what allows the reduction to two dimensions in [7.16.6](/7.16.6#sec-6.3).

### 5.2.2. Why the Rates Have the Form They Have

Hodgkin and Huxley chose their rate functions empirically, and some of the forms ($\alpha_m$ of the shape $x/(1 - \mathrm{e}^{-x})$) have no physical basis. Two features, however, do follow from physics. In a two-state gate $\mathrm{C} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{O}$ at equilibrium, the ratio of occupancies is a **Boltzmann factor**,

$$
\frac{x_\infty}{1 - x_\infty} = \frac{\alpha_x}{\beta_x} = \exp\!\left(-\frac{\Delta G_0 - z_{\mathrm{g}}eV}{k_{\mathrm{B}}T}\right),
$$

where $\Delta G_0$ is the free-energy difference between the states at $V = 0$ and $z_{\mathrm{g}}e$ is the **gating charge** moved through the membrane field when the gate opens. Hence $x_\infty(V) = 1/(1 + \mathrm{e}^{-(V - V_{1/2})/k})$ with slope factor $k = k_{\mathrm{B}}T/(z_{\mathrm{g}}e)$: the steeper the activation curve, the more charge each gate carries. In transition-state theory each rate is itself exponential in $V$, $\alpha \propto \mathrm{e}^{\delta z_{\mathrm{g}}eV/k_{\mathrm{B}}T}$ and $\beta \propto \mathrm{e}^{-(1-\delta)z_{\mathrm{g}}eV/k_{\mathrm{B}}T}$, which is the form of HH's $\beta_m$, $\alpha_h$, $\beta_n$.

**Example 5.2.1 (Gating Charge of the Hodgkin–Huxley Gates)** Far from the midpoint, $\ln(\alpha_n/\beta_n)$ grows with slope $1/10 + 1/80 = 0.1125\,\mathrm{mV^{-1}}$. With $k_{\mathrm{B}}T/e = 24.1\,\mathrm{mV}$ at $6.3\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$, each $n$ gate carries $z_{\mathrm{g}} \approx 0.1125 \times 24.1 = 2.7$ elementary charges, and a $\ce{K+}$ channel with four gates about 11. For the $m$ gate the slope is $1/10 + 1/18 = 0.156\,\mathrm{mV^{-1}}$, so $z_{\mathrm{g}} \approx 3.8$, and about 11 charges for three gates. Measurements of the gating current of the Shaker $\ce{K+}$ channel give 12–14 elementary charges per channel, carried mainly by the four arginines of each S4 helix: the phenomenological model predicted the charge of the voltage sensor to within 20%.

### 5.2.3. Why $m^3h$ and $n^4$, and Is It Really Exponential?

A single first-order variable relaxes exponentially after a voltage step. The measured $\ce{K+}$ conductance after a depolarizing step does not: it rises along an S-shaped curve with a delay. Raising $n$ to a power reproduces the delay, since $n^4 = [n_\infty(1 - \mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau_n})]^4$ starts with zero slope (Figure 1A). The fourth power was the smallest integer that fitted the onset; Hodgkin and Huxley noted that $n^5$ or $n^6$ would do almost as well. The strongest evidence for the power law is the asymmetry between onset and offset. On repolarization to a voltage where $n_\infty \approx 0$, $n^4 = n_0^4\,\mathrm{e}^{-4t/\tau_n}$ decays as a *single* exponential with time constant $\tau_n/4$, as observed, whereas a sigmoidal rise followed by an exponential fall is impossible for a single first-order variable.

The product $m^3h$ gives the $\ce{Na+}$ conductance its delayed rise and its decay at a constant voltage (Figure 1B). The exponents have a structural meaning ([7.6.30](/7.6.30#sec-30.6.2)): $\mathrm{K_V}$ channels are tetramers with four voltage sensors, and $\mathrm{Na_V}$ channels have four homologous domains, three of whose sensors drive activation while the fourth is coupled to the inactivation particle (the IFM motif of the III–IV linker). The model's interpretation of $n^4$ as four *independent* gates is the simplest stochastic reading: if each of four subunits flips independently between two states, the channel is open with probability $n^4$, and averaging 400 such channels reproduces the deterministic curve (Figure 1C).

![Why the gating variables are raised to powers (Hodgkin–Huxley model, voltage steps from $-65\,\mathrm{mV}$). A: the $\ce{K+}$ conductance $\propto n^p$ after a step to 0 mV, normalized: with $p = 1$ it rises exponentially at full speed from the start, with $p = 4$ it has the sigmoidal delay seen in the voltage clamp. B: the $\ce{Na+}$ conductance $\propto m^p h$ after a step to $-20\,\mathrm{mV}$: the exponent sets the onset and the peak, $h$ the decay. C: a Markov model of a channel with four independent two-state gates: three single channels open after random latencies (bottom, grey), and the open fraction of 400 channels follows $n^4$](./plots/gating-exponents.py)

The independence assumption is only approximately right. Real $\mathrm{K_V}$ channels show the *Cole–Moore shift*: after a hyperpolarizing prepulse the delay of activation increases more than $n^4$ predicts, and the opening of the pore after all four sensors have moved is a final, cooperative step. $\mathrm{Na_V}$ inactivation is coupled to activation (channels inactivate mostly from the open or pre-open states), not independent of it as the product $m^3h$ assumes. Modern channel models are therefore Markov chains with several closed, open and inactivated states, fitted to single-channel and gating-current data; the HH form is the special case in which the chain factorizes into independent gates. For the macroscopic currents of a neuron the factorized form is usually accurate enough, and it is the form used in almost all conductance-based models.

## 5.3. Phenomena of the Model

The model (5.1)–(5.2) can be used as a numerical preparation: one applies the stimuli that physiologists apply to axons and compares the responses with experiment (Figure 2). The simulations integrate the equations with the fourth-order **Runge–Kutta method** and a step of $0.01\,\mathrm{ms}$ ([7.6.30](/7.6.30#sec-30.6.3)).

1. *Threshold and all-or-none response.* A $0.5\,\mathrm{ms}$ current pulse of $13.2\,\mu\mathrm{A\,cm^{-2}}$ (a depolarization of 6.6 mV) decays back to rest; one of $13.6\,\mu\mathrm{A\,cm^{-2}}$ triggers a full spike, delayed by a few milliseconds because the trajectory lingers near threshold. Above threshold the spike amplitude is almost independent of the stimulus (Figure 2A). The threshold is not a fixed voltage: it depends on the duration of the stimulus (the strength–duration curve of [7.14.4](/7.14.4#sec-4.5.3)) and on the recent history of the membrane, and its geometric nature is the subject of [7.16.6](/7.16.6#sec-6.4).
2. *Refractoriness.* After a spike, a second stimulus of the same strength ($40\,\mu\mathrm{A\,cm^{-2}}$, 0.5 ms) fails at intervals up to 10 ms and evokes a slightly smaller spike at 11–13 ms (Figure 2B). During the first 2–3 ms no stimulus of any strength succeeds (the **absolute refractory period**); afterwards the threshold is elevated and returns to normal over about 15 ms (the **relative refractory period**). Both phases are visible in the gates: $h$ is near zero and recovers with $\tau_h \approx 5$–$8\,\mathrm{ms}$, and $n$ remains elevated, so that the extra $\ce{K+}$ conductance opposes depolarization.
3. *Anode-break excitation.* A prolonged hyperpolarizing current, when switched off, fires a spike without any depolarizing stimulus (Figure 2C). During hyperpolarization $h$ increases (inactivation is removed) and $n$ decreases; on release, the membrane returns toward rest with more $\ce{Na+}$ channels available and fewer $\ce{K+}$ channels open than at rest, and the effective threshold has dropped below the resting potential. The same "rebound" principle, with dedicated low-threshold channels, underlies postinhibitory rebound bursts in thalamic and other neurons (Section 5.4).
4. *Repetitive firing.* A current step above about $6.3\,\mu\mathrm{A\,cm^{-2}}$ produces periodic firing, which starts abruptly at about 55–60 Hz rather than at an arbitrarily low rate, and the rate then increases only to about 90 Hz at $20\,\mu\mathrm{A\,cm^{-2}}$ (Figure 2D). The squid axon is thus a poor rate encoder, in contrast to most cortical neurons. The rest state itself remains stable up to about $9.8\,\mu\mathrm{A\,cm^{-2}}$: between 6.3 and 9.8 rest and repetitive firing coexist, and it is the onset of the step that kicks the membrane into firing (a slow ramp of current would start firing only at 9.8). The reason for both features, a subcritical **Hopf bifurcation** of the resting state, is explained in [7.16.7](/7.16.7#sec-7.1).

A: responses to 0.5 ms current pulses of increasing amplitude; the threshold lies between 13.2 and 13.6 µA cm⁻², and above it the response is all-or-none. B: refractoriness: a second pulse of the same size (40 µA cm⁻², 0.5 ms) produces a full spike after 20 ms, a slightly reduced spike after 12 ms, and only small subthreshold responses after 8 and 5 ms. C: anode-break excitation: at the end of a 20 ms hyperpolarizing current (grey, right axis) the membrane fires a spike. D: repetitive firing under steady current: the firing rate jumps from zero to about 58 Hz at the onset, a signature of class 2 excitability](./plots/hh-excitability.py)

**Example 5.3.1 (The Membrane Does Not Reach $E_{\mathrm{Na}}$)** At the peak of the simulated spike $V = +39.3\,\mathrm{mV}$, whereas $E_{\mathrm{Na}} = +50\,\mathrm{mV}$. At the peak $\mathrm{d}V/\mathrm{d}t = 0$, so the net ionic current vanishes and, by Proposition 4.3.1, $V$ equals the conductance-weighted mean of the **reversal potentials**. The simulation gives $g_{\mathrm{Na}} = 29.9$ and $g_{\mathrm{K}} = 2.5\,\mathrm{mS\,cm^{-2}}$ at that moment (with $g_{\mathrm{L}} = 0.3$), and indeed $(29.9 \cdot 50 - 2.5 \cdot 77 - 0.3 \cdot 54.4)/32.7 = 39.3\,\mathrm{mV}$. The peak $g_{\mathrm{Na}}$ is only a quarter of $\bar g_{\mathrm{Na}} = 120\,\mathrm{mS\,cm^{-2}}$, because $h$ has fallen from 0.60 at rest to 0.33 and $m^3$ is only 0.75, and the $\ce{K+}$ conductance has begun to rise: the spike falls short of $E_{\mathrm{Na}}$ because inactivation and $\ce{K+}$ activation are already under way.

## 5.4. Channel Diversity and Firing Patterns

The squid axon has essentially two voltage-gated currents, because its job is to conduct. Neuronal somata and dendrites, whose job is to transform inputs into spike patterns, express ten or more. The genome supplies the repertoire (about 40 $\mathrm{K_V}$ genes, 10 $\mathrm{Na_V}$, 10 $\mathrm{Ca_V}$, 4 HCN, 8 Ca²⁺-activated $\ce{K+}$ channel genes in mammals), and each **cell type** expresses its own subset. The diversity matters because currents that are small compared with the spike currents, but active below threshold, dominate the time between spikes and therefore the firing pattern.

**Definition 5.4.1 (Subthreshold and Modulatory Currents)** The main voltage- and $\ce{Ca^2+}$-gated currents beyond the spike-generating $I_{\mathrm{Na}}$ and delayed-rectifier $I_{\mathrm{K}}$ are:

- the **A-type current** $I_{\mathrm{A}}$, a transient $\ce{K+}$ current that activates rapidly at subthreshold voltages and inactivates within tens of milliseconds ($\mathrm{K_V}4$, and Shaker, the first cloned $\ce{K+}$ channel, in *Drosophila*);
- the **M-current** $I_{\mathrm{M}}$, a slowly activating ($\tau \approx 50$–$200\,\mathrm{ms}$), noninactivating $\ce{K+}$ current carried by $\mathrm{K_V}7$ (KCNQ2/3) channels and suppressed by muscarinic acetylcholine receptors, after which it is named;
- the **hyperpolarization-activated current** $I_{\mathrm{h}}$, a mixed $\ce{Na+}$/$\ce{K+}$ current ($E_{\mathrm{h}} \approx -30\,\mathrm{mV}$) through HCN channels that opens on hyperpolarization with time constants of 100 ms to seconds and is facilitated by cAMP;
- the **T-type calcium current** $I_{\mathrm{T}}$, a low-threshold, transient $\ce{Ca^2+}$ current ($\mathrm{Ca_V}3$) that is inactivated at rest and becomes available only after hyperpolarization, in contrast to the high-threshold, slowly inactivating L-type current ($\mathrm{Ca_V}1$) and the N-, P/Q- and R-type currents of presynaptic terminals ($\mathrm{Ca_V}2$);
- the **calcium-activated potassium currents**, gated by intracellular $\ce{Ca^2+}$: the large-conductance BK current, which is also voltage-gated and speeds spike repolarization, and the small-conductance SK current, which produces the medium afterhyperpolarization;
- the **persistent sodium current** $I_{\mathrm{Na,p}}$, a small (about 1% of the peak), noninactivating component of the $\ce{Na+}$ current that activates 10 mV below the transient current and amplifies subthreshold depolarization.

The effect of each current on firing follows from when it is active. The examples in Figure 3 were computed by adding one current at a time to a conductance-based model.

*A-current: low rates and long latencies.* In the Connor–Stevens model of a crab motor neuron (Dayan and Abbott, Section 6.2), $I_{\mathrm{A}}$ is partly active between spikes. As the membrane depolarizes after a spike, $I_{\mathrm{A}}$ first opposes the depolarization and then slowly inactivates, so that $V$ creeps toward threshold along a slow ramp. The ramp can be arbitrarily long: firing starts at an arbitrarily low rate and the f–I curve is continuous (Figure 3A), whereas without $I_{\mathrm{A}}$ the same model, with the leak adjusted to keep rest and input conductance, fires only above $40\,\mu\mathrm{A\,cm^{-2}}$ and starts at 150 Hz. The same slow ramp delays the first spike after a current step by up to 100 ms near threshold (Figure 3B). The A-current is the classical way to convert a squid-type class 2 membrane into a class 1 rate encoder ([7.16.6](/7.16.6#sec-6.4)).

*M-current and $\ce{Ca^2+}$-activated $\ce{K+}$ current: spike-frequency adaptation.* A slow outward current that accumulates with each spike makes the **interspike intervals** grow during a maintained stimulus (Figure 3C). Most pyramidal neurons adapt in this way through $I_{\mathrm{M}}$ and SK currents; **acetylcholine** and **noradrenaline** suppress these currents and switch neurons from adapting to tonic firing, one of the main mechanisms of neuromodulation ([7.16.11](/7.16.11)). Mutations in KCNQ2/3 cause benign familial neonatal epilepsy, and the anticonvulsant retigabine acts by opening these channels.

*$I_{\mathrm{h}}$: sag and rebound.* When a hyperpolarizing current is injected, $I_{\mathrm{h}}$ slowly activates and its inward current pulls the voltage back toward rest (the "sag"); when the current ends, the still-open HCN channels depolarize the cell above rest (Figure 3D). $I_{\mathrm{h}}$ stabilizes the resting potential, shortens the effective **membrane time constant** in dendrites, makes neurons resonate at 2–10 Hz, and together with $I_{\mathrm{T}}$ drives pacemaking in the sinoatrial node (the "funny current" $I_{\mathrm{f}}$, [7.14.13](/7.14.13#sec-13.1.2)) and in thalamic relay neurons.

*$I_{\mathrm{T}}$: rebound bursts.* At rest $I_{\mathrm{T}}$ is inactivated. After a period of hyperpolarization (for instance, by inhibitory input) its inactivation is removed, and the return to rest triggers a low-threshold $\ce{Ca^2+}$ spike lasting 20–50 ms, on top of which ride several $\ce{Na+}$ spikes. Thalamic relay neurons therefore have two modes: a *tonic mode* when depolarized, in which they relay input faithfully, and a *burst mode* when hyperpolarized, as in slow-wave sleep, in which they fire rhythmic rebound bursts. The dynamics of bursting are analyzed in [7.16.7](/7.16.7#sec-7.4).

*$I_{\mathrm{Na,p}}$: amplification and bistability.* A persistent inward current adds a region of negative slope to the steady-state current–voltage relation, which amplifies subthreshold inputs and, if strong enough, makes the membrane bistable. The simplest models of [7.16.6](/7.16.6) are built from $I_{\mathrm{Na,p}}$ and a $\ce{K+}$ current alone.

The cortical electrophysiological classes of [7.16.2](/7.16.2#sec-2.1) map onto these currents. Fast-spiking PV interneurons express $\mathrm{K_V}3$ channels, which activate only at depolarized voltages and deactivate very fast, so that spikes are brief (0.3 ms at half-width) and the rate can exceed 300 Hz with little adaptation; regular-spiking pyramidal cells adapt through $I_{\mathrm{M}}$ and SK; intrinsically bursting layer-5 pyramidal cells have dendritic $\ce{Ca^2+}$ currents; low-threshold-spiking SST cells have $I_{\mathrm{T}}$. These patterns can be reproduced with two-variable models ([7.16.8](/7.16.8#sec-8.4)).

**Remark 5.4.1 (Degeneracy)** The same firing pattern can be produced by many combinations of conductances. In the stomatogastric ganglion of the crab, neurons of one identified type vary two- to fourfold in the densities of their channels from animal to animal, and models with randomly sampled conductances show that thousands of combinations produce the same bursting rhythm (Prinz, Bucher and Marder 2004). What is conserved are correlations between conductances, maintained by activity-dependent regulation of channel expression. A fitted conductance-based model is therefore one member of a family of equally good models, not a unique description of a neuron.

## 5.5. Conduction

### 5.5.1. Unmyelinated Axons

An action potential propagates because the depolarized region drives axial current into its neighbors, bringing them to threshold. Combining (5.1) with the **cable equation** of [7.6.30](/7.6.30#sec-30.5) gives a reaction–diffusion equation for $V(x, t)$,

$$
\frac{a}{2R_{\mathrm{i}}}\,\frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial x^2} = c_{\mathrm{m}}\frac{\partial V}{\partial t} + i_{\mathrm{ion}}(V, m, h, n) - i_{\mathrm{ext}}, \tag{5.3}
$$

with axon radius $a$ and axial resistivity $R_{\mathrm{i}}$, which has traveling-pulse solutions ([7.6.30](/7.6.30#sec-30.6.4)). For simulation the axon is divided into compartments of length $\Delta x$ much smaller than the **length constant** $\lambda$, each an HH membrane coupled to its neighbors by the axial conductance $\pi a^2/(R_{\mathrm{i}}\Delta x)$; the stiff coupling is best integrated implicitly (one tridiagonal solve per time step, as in the NEURON simulator). Figure 4A shows a pulse launched at one end of a $2\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ axon at $20\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$, traveling at $0.75\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ with constant shape.

Dimensional analysis gives the scaling of the velocity $v$. Rescaling $x$ by $\sqrt{a}$ leaves (5.3) invariant, so a pulse of the same shape travels with $v \propto \sqrt{a/(R_{\mathrm{i}}c_{\mathrm{m}}^2)}$ times a factor that depends only on the membrane kinetics. The simulated velocities follow the square-root law (Figure 4C, slope 1/2): doubling the speed costs four times the diameter and four times the cross section, which is why invertebrates that need fast escape conduction evolved giant axons.

### 5.5.2. Myelinated Axons

In a myelinated axon the internodes are covered by $N$ wraps of glial membrane ([7.16.2](/7.16.2#sec-2.2)), which divide the internodal capacitance and leak by about $2N$, and the voltage-gated $\ce{Na+}$ channels are concentrated at the nodes. Modeling this requires only a change of parameters in the compartmental model: nodes of $1\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ with a high $\ce{Na+}$ conductance, internodes of length $100\,d$ with capacitance $c_{\mathrm{m}}/(2N)$ and $N \propto d$, as observed. The pulse now jumps from node to node, each node reaching threshold some tens of microseconds after its predecessor (Figure 4B): **saltatory conduction**.

The internodal length and the number of wraps both scale with diameter, while the time for a node to reach threshold is nearly independent of it. The velocity, internode length divided by nodal delay, is therefore proportional to $d$ (Figure 4C, slope 1): myelinated fibers win increasingly with size. In the simulation, at $20\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ and with squid kinetics, a $10\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ myelinated axon conducts at about 27 m/s, against 1.7 m/s for an unmyelinated one; at mammalian body temperature the rates are faster and the empirical rule is about $6\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ per micrometer of fiber diameter (Hursh 1939; [7.6.30](/7.6.30#sec-30.7), [7.14.4](/7.14.4#sec-4.9.3)). Below some diameter the advantage reverses, because the nodal delay and the cost of the node outweigh the gain: Rushton (1951) estimated the crossover at about 1 µm, close to the observed lower limit of myelinated axons (about 0.2–0.3 µm in the central nervous system). The idealized model used here, with nodes that are equally effective at every diameter, does not reach the crossover in the range shown.

Demyelinating diseases such as **multiple sclerosis** expose internodal membrane that has few $\ce{Na+}$ channels and a large capacitance; in the model, increasing the internodal capacitance and leak slows conduction and, beyond a critical point, the current from a node no longer brings the next node to threshold and conduction fails. Because the safety factor is lowest at branch points and where the diameter changes, these are the first sites of failure.

| Time scales | $\tau_m \ll \tau_h, \tau_n$ | regenerative upstroke, refractoriness, anode-break excitation |
| Repetitive firing | onset at about 58 Hz in squid axon | class 2 excitability (Hopf bifurcation) |
| Extra currents | $I_{\mathrm{A}}$, $I_{\mathrm{M}}$, $I_{\mathrm{h}}$, $I_{\mathrm{T}}$, $I_{\mathrm{K(Ca)}}$, $I_{\mathrm{Na,p}}$ (Definition 5.4.1) | low-rate firing and latency, adaptation, sag and rebound, bursts, amplification |
| Conduction | cable plus HH (Eq. (5.3)) | $v \propto \sqrt d$ unmyelinated, $v \propto d$ myelinated |

- The Hodgkin–Huxley model is phenomenological in its rate functions but physical in two essential respects, the Boltzmann voltage dependence and the independent-gate interpretation of the exponents, and both predicted features of channel structure.
- The same model accounts for threshold, refractoriness and rebound excitation; it does not account for low-rate firing, which requires additional subthreshold currents.
- Firing patterns are set by currents that are active between spikes, and the same pattern can be built from many combinations of conductances (Remark 5.4.1).

## Questions

1. At $20\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ the HH rates are multiplied by $3^{(20 - 6.3)/10}$. Compute this factor, and estimate how the duration of the spike and the conduction velocity change. Why do the passive parameters not change by the same factor?

   **Answer** $3^{1.37} = 4.5$. All gating time constants divide by 4.5, so the spike becomes about 4.5 times shorter (but somewhat smaller, because the membrane capacitance does not speed up and the upstroke has less time to charge it). The velocity scales roughly as the square root of the rate at which the membrane at the front reaches threshold, so it increases by roughly $\sqrt{4.5} \approx 2$. The capacitance and the axial and membrane resistances are determined by geometry and ionic mobilities, whose temperature coefficients are small ($Q_{10} \approx 1.2$–1.4).

2. A $\ce{K+}$ channel has an activation curve $n_\infty^4(V)$ that rises e-fold per 4 mV at very negative voltages. Estimate the total gating charge per channel at $22\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$.

   **Answer** At very negative voltages $n_\infty \approx \mathrm{e}^{z_{\mathrm{g}}e(V - V_{1/2})/k_{\mathrm{B}}T}$ for each gate, so $n_\infty^4$ rises e-fold per $k_{\mathrm{B}}T/(4z_{\mathrm{g}}e)$. The total charge is $4z_{\mathrm{g}} = (k_{\mathrm{B}}T/e)/4\,\mathrm{mV} = 25.4/4 \approx 6.4$ elementary charges. This "limiting slope" method gives a lower bound on the charge when the transitions are not all equally voltage-dependent; for Shaker channels it yields 12–14.

3. Explain, using the gating variables, why a long hyperpolarizing current makes the HH model fire on release, and predict whether a short hyperpolarizing pulse of 0.5 ms would do the same.

   **Answer** During the hyperpolarization $h$ rises above its resting value (removal of inactivation) and $n$ falls, both with time constants of several milliseconds. At the release $m$ quickly returns to its resting value, but there are more available $\ce{Na+}$ channels and fewer open $\ce{K+}$ channels, so the inward current wins and a spike results. A 0.5 ms pulse is much shorter than $\tau_h$ and $\tau_n$ and changes them too little; it produces only a small passive hyperpolarization and no rebound spike.

4. (Open question) Design a numerical experiment with the Connor–Stevens model that distinguishes the effect of the A-current on the f–I curve from its effect on the resting conductance.

   **Answer** Remove the A-current but add an ohmic conductance equal to the slope conductance of $I_{\mathrm{A}}$ at rest, with a reversal potential chosen so that the resting potential is unchanged (this is how Figure 3A was computed). Any remaining difference in the f–I curve is then due to the dynamics of $I_{\mathrm{A}}$ (its activation and slow inactivation between spikes), not to the change of input conductance. A second control is to freeze the A-current gates at their resting values, which keeps the conductance but removes the dynamics; the f–I curve then has the discontinuous onset of the model without $I_{\mathrm{A}}$.
