Learning is a change of behavior produced by experience, and memory is the retention of that change. The simplest forms (habituation to a repeated stimulus, sensitization after a noxious one, association of a cue with an outcome) are shared by all animals with nervous systems and do not require awareness. They were the first forms of memory whose cellular and molecular mechanisms were worked out, in two invertebrates chosen for their accessibility: the sea slug *Aplysia*, with large identified neurons, and the fruit fly, with its genetics. This chapter follows those studies and the computational principles they revealed: synapse-specific changes of strength, a molecular **coincidence detector**, a cascade from short-lived modification of proteins to long-lived growth, and a dopamine-gated three-factor rule ([7.16.14](/7.16.14#sec-14.9)). **Declarative memory** and the hippocampus follow in [7.16.42](/7.16.42); the **reinforcement learning** theory that formalizes conditioning in [7.16.39](/7.16.39). The physiological classification of memory and its stages is summarized in [7.14.46](/7.14.46#sec-46.5).

## 38.1. Kinds of Memory

Memories differ in content and in duration. By content, declarative (explicit) memory of facts and events, which depends on the hippocampus and medial temporal lobe, is distinguished from nondeclarative (implicit) memory, expressed in performance rather than recollection: skills and habits (striatum), priming (neocortex), simple **classical conditioning** (**amygdala** for emotional responses, cerebellum for motor responses) and nonassociative learning (reflex pathways) ([7.14.46](/7.14.46#sec-46.5.2)). The distinction comes from patients such as H.M., who after bilateral medial temporal lobe resection learned the mirror-drawing task over days with normal improvement while denying each time that he had done it before.

By duration, short-term memory lasts seconds to hours and long-term memory days to a lifetime. Two operational differences separate them in every system studied: long-term memory requires new gene expression and protein synthesis during or shortly after training, and it is usually associated with structural changes of synapses; short-term memory requires neither. Long-term memory is also favored by spaced rather than massed training (the spacing effect, first described by Ebbinghaus for human verbal learning and found in *Aplysia*, flies and mice), a property that has a molecular explanation (Section 38.3.3).

## 38.2. Forms of Learning

Nonassociative learning involves a single kind of stimulus. **Habituation** is the decline of a response to a repeated, harmless stimulus; it is stimulus specific (a different stimulus still evokes the full response), recovers spontaneously with rest, and is reversed by a strong stimulus of another kind (dishabituation). Sensitization is the enhancement of responses to many stimuli after a strong or noxious stimulus.

Associative learning relates two events. Two paradigms define it.

**Definition 38.2.1 (Classical Conditioning)** **Classical conditioning** (Pavlovian conditioning) is learning in which a neutral conditioned stimulus (CS) is repeatedly followed by an unconditioned stimulus (US) that elicits a response without learning, so that the CS comes to elicit a conditioned response; the outcome is delivered regardless of the animal's behavior.

**Definition 38.2.2 (Operant Conditioning)** **Operant conditioning** (instrumental conditioning) is learning in which the consequence of an action (a reward or a punishment) changes the future frequency of that action; the outcome depends on the animal's behavior.

Thorndike's cats escaping from puzzle boxes (the law of effect) and Skinner's rats pressing levers are the classical examples of operant conditioning. The two forms differ in what is learned: classical conditioning establishes a prediction (the CS predicts the US), operant conditioning a **policy** (which action to take in which situation). Both are error-driven: learning is fast when the outcome is surprising and stops when it is predicted, as the phenomenon of blocking shows (Kamin, 1969). This shared logic is what **reinforcement learning** formalizes: prediction learning in [7.16.39](/7.16.39), action learning in [7.16.40](/7.16.40) and [7.16.41](/7.16.41).

| Form | Stimuli | What changes | Example | Model system |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Habituation | One, repeated | Response to that stimulus decreases | Ignoring a ticking clock | *Aplysia* gill withdrawal |
| Sensitization | Strong stimulus, then others | Responses to many stimuli increase | Startle after a loud noise | *Aplysia* tail shock |
| Classical conditioning | CS paired with US | CS predicts US | Salivation to a bell; fear to a tone | *Aplysia* siphon, fly olfaction, rodent amygdala |
| Operant conditioning | Action followed by outcome | Action frequency changes | Lever pressing for food | Rodent striatum, fly operant tasks |

## 38.3. Aplysia: From Short- to Long-Term Memory

### 38.3.1. Habituation

A light touch to the siphon of *Aplysia* evokes withdrawal of the siphon and gill, a defensive reflex mediated in part by a monosynaptic pathway: about 24 mechanosensory neurons of the abdominal ganglion excite gill and siphon motor neurons directly and through interneurons. Repeated touches habituate the reflex. Kandel and colleagues recorded the identified sensory and motor neurons during habituation and found that the excitatory postsynaptic potential (EPSP) of the sensory-to-motor synapse declines with each stimulus, whereas the excitability of the motor neuron and its response to applied glutamate do not change: short-term habituation is a homosynaptic depression of transmitter release, of the kind analyzed in [7.16.12](/7.16.12). Quantal analysis showed a decrease in the number of quanta released per action potential, with unchanged quantal size.

Habituation has two properties that a single depleting resource cannot explain. It is faster and deeper at short interstimulus intervals, yet recovers faster after training at short intervals than after training at long ones (rate sensitivity), and repeated sessions of training produce habituation that lasts days. A cascade of processes with increasing time constants, in which each stage is driven by the output of the previous one (Staddon and Higa, 1996), reproduces rate sensitivity: at short intervals the fast stage suppresses the response almost completely, so little drive reaches the slow stage and recovery is quick.

### 38.3.2. Sensitization and Its Coincidence Detector

A shock to the tail sensitizes the reflex. The shock excites facilitating interneurons that release **serotonin** onto the terminals of the sensory neurons. Serotonin acts on G protein–coupled receptors to raise cAMP and activate protein kinase A (PKA), which phosphorylates potassium channels (closing a background $\ce{K+}$ conductance and slowing repolarization), broadens the presynaptic action potential, increases $\ce{Ca^2+}$ entry and enhances release; PKA and protein kinase C also act on the release machinery, mobilizing vesicles at depressed synapses. Because release depends steeply on $\ce{Ca^2+}$ entry ([7.16.9](/7.16.9#sec-9.3)), a modest broadening produces a large facilitation. The cascade is the canonical metabotropic pathway of [7.16.11](/7.16.11#sec-11.1), the same that carries other neuromodulatory signals.

The reflex can also be classically conditioned: a weak touch to the siphon (CS) that shortly precedes a tail shock (US) comes to produce a larger withdrawal than a touch unpaired with the shock. At the cellular level, facilitation by serotonin is larger in sensory neurons that fired just before serotonin arrived (activity-dependent presynaptic facilitation; Hawkins, Abrams, Carew and Kandel, 1983). The coincidence detector is the adenylyl cyclase itself: it is stimulated by the G protein activated by serotonin and also by $\ce{Ca^2+}$/calmodulin, which enters with the action potentials of the CS, and the two together activate it supra-additively. In addition, the postsynaptic motor neuron contributes a Hebbian component through NMDA-like receptors and retrograde signals. Classical conditioning in *Aplysia* thus combines a presynaptic "third factor" (serotonin as the US signal) with the activity of the synapse, a three-factor rule in the sense of [7.16.14](/7.16.14#sec-14.9).

### 38.3.3. Long-Term Memory: CREB and Growth

One tail shock produces sensitization for minutes; five shocks spaced over an hour produce sensitization lasting days. The same holds in a culture of one sensory and one motor neuron: one pulse of serotonin produces short-term facilitation of the EPSP that lasts minutes, five spaced pulses produce long-term facilitation that lasts more than a day and is blocked by inhibitors of transcription and translation applied during the pulses (Montarolo and colleagues, 1986). Repeated pulses cause the catalytic subunits of PKA, together with the kinase MAPK, to translocate into the nucleus of the sensory neuron, where they activate the transcription factor CREB-1 and relieve the repression by CREB-2. CREB-1 induces immediate-early genes, among them the transcription factor C/EBP and a ubiquitin hydrolase that degrades the regulatory subunits of PKA and makes the kinase persistently active. The end result is structural: sensory neurons of long-term sensitized animals have about twice as many presynaptic varicosities as controls (Bailey and Chen, 1983), and habituated animals fewer.

The cascade explains the spacing effect. Nuclear translocation of PKA and activation of CREB require a sustained, high level of cAMP signaling, which accumulates over repeated pulses only if each pulse arrives while the slow nuclear signal from the previous one persists; at the same time, the inhibitory constraints (CREB-2, phosphatases) recover between pulses. A minimal model treats the two stages as a fast and a slow integrator, with a steep threshold for the second.

**Formula 38.3.1 (Two-Stage Model of Sensitization)** Let $p$ be presynaptic PKA activity, raised by 1 at each serotonin pulse and decaying with $\tau_p$; let nuclear signaling $n$ follow $p$ with a slower time constant $\tau_n$; and let synaptic growth $g$ be driven by a steep function of $n$ and decay over days:
$$
\dot p = -\frac{p}{\tau_p}, \qquad \dot n = \frac{p - n}{\tau_n}, \qquad \dot g = k\,\frac{n^4}{n^4 + n_{1/2}^4}\,(1 - g) - \frac{g}{\tau_g}, \qquad F = 1 + \frac{p}{1 + p} + c\,g,
$$
where $F$ is the EPSP amplitude relative to baseline.

![The two-stage model of sensitization (Formula 38.3.1; $\tau_p = 20\,\mathrm{min}$, $\tau_n = 30\,\mathrm{min}$, $k = 0.05\,\mathrm{min^{-1}}$, $n_{1/2} = 1.5$, $\tau_g = 10\,\mathrm{d}$, $c = 1.5$; logarithmic time axis). One pulse of serotonin (blue) produces short-term facilitation that decays within about an hour; five pulses at 20-min intervals (red) drive nuclear signaling high enough on the steep threshold function of the growth stage to leave a facilitation that lasts days.](./plots/sensitization-stages.py)

The maintenance of long-term facilitation for days to weeks requires local protein synthesis at the stimulated synapses. In *Aplysia* this depends on a neuronal isoform of the cytoplasmic polyadenylation element–binding protein (CPEB), which can adopt a self-perpetuating, prion-like aggregated state that keeps translation of local mRNAs active (Si, Lindquist and Kandel, 2003): a molecular mechanism by which a synapse could remember that it was potentiated, related to the synaptic tagging of [7.16.14](/7.16.14#sec-14.7).

## 38.4. Olfactory Conditioning in *Drosophila*

### 38.4.1. Learning Mutants

Quinn, Harris and Benzer (1974) trained populations of flies in a T-maze: one odor was paired with electric shock, a second odor was not, and the flies were then allowed to choose between the two. Normal flies avoid the shock-paired odor; the performance index (the difference between the fractions choosing the two odors) is high immediately after training and decays over hours, and spaced training produces memory that lasts a day or more and requires protein synthesis and the fly CREB, as in *Aplysia*. Screens for mutants with poor learning found *dunce*, which encodes a cAMP phosphodiesterase (so cAMP is too high), and *rutabaga*, which encodes a $\ce{Ca^2+}$/calmodulin-dependent adenylyl cyclase, the fly counterpart of the coincidence detector of Section 38.3.2. That the first two learning genes in an insect encoded the enzymes that make and break cAMP, the same pathway found independently in *Aplysia*, was one of the strongest early indications that the molecular mechanisms of memory are conserved.

### 38.4.2. The Mushroom Body Circuit

The products of *rutabaga* and *dunce* are enriched in the **mushroom body**, and its function in learning is explained by its circuit, which is now known at the level of the **connectome**. Each odor activates a sparse, nearly random set of about 5% of the roughly 2000 **Kenyon cells** in each hemisphere ([7.16.23](/7.16.23)). The Kenyon cell axons run in parallel through the mushroom body lobes, which are divided into about 15 compartments. In each compartment the axons contact the dendrites of one or two types of mushroom body output neurons (MBONs; 21 types in total) and are innervated by the axons of one or two types of **dopamine** neurons (DANs). The compartments are thus repeated units of a three-layer design: sparse sensory code, plastic synapses onto a few outputs, and a dopaminergic teaching signal confined to each compartment (Aso and colleagues, 2014).

The plasticity rule is dopamine-gated depression. Pairing odor-evoked Kenyon cell activity with activation of the DAN of a compartment depresses the synapses from the active Kenyon cells onto the MBON of that compartment, lasting for at least an hour, whereas Kenyon cell activity alone or dopamine alone has no effect (Hige and colleagues, 2015). The order matters: dopamine after odor depresses, dopamine before odor potentiates, a timing dependence on the scale of seconds that relies on different dopamine receptors (Handler and colleagues, 2019). Different compartments form a map of valence. DANs of the PPL1 cluster respond to punishment and innervate compartments whose MBONs promote approach; DANs of the PAM cluster respond to reward and innervate compartments whose MBONs promote avoidance. Aversive learning therefore depresses the approach drive to the trained odor and reward learning depresses the avoidance drive, and behavior follows the balance of MBON outputs.

**Example 38.4.1 (A Mushroom Body Model)** Let 500 Kenyon cells represent odors by random sets of 25 active cells; odor A′ shares half of the cells of A, and B is unrelated. An approach MBON and an avoidance MBON receive all Kenyon cells with initial weights 1 and respond with the mean weight of the active cells; the preference for an odor is $\tanh(2(M_+ - M_-))$. In each aversive training trial, odor A is paired with a punishment DAN that innervates the compartment of the approach MBON, and the weights from the active cells are multiplied by $1 - \eta$ with $\eta = 0.3$, a three-factor rule with the DAN as third factor. After 8 trials the approach response to A has fallen to about 6% and the fly strongly avoids A; the response to A′ falls in proportion to the shared cells, so the memory generalizes to similar odors, while B is unaffected.

The design has computational advantages. Because odor codes are sparse and nearly orthogonal, depression at the synapses of one odor barely affects the response to another, so many associations can be stored with little interference; and because each compartment has its own DAN, the same odor can be associated with several outcomes in parallel compartments with different learning rates and memory durations. The fly connectome also revealed recurrent connections from MBONs back to DANs, which let the circuit compute prediction errors (the DAN response to a predicted outcome is reduced) and implement extinction and second-order conditioning, connecting the mushroom body to the temporal-difference theory of [7.16.39](/7.16.39).

## 38.5. Procedural Memory and Habits

Skills (typing, riding a bicycle) and **habits** (actions triggered automatically by their usual context) are learned slowly, by repetition, and expressed without recollection. Motor skill learning has at least two components with different substrates: the calibration of movements by sensory prediction errors, in which the cerebellum adjusts **internal models** ([7.16.33](/7.16.33)), and the selection and sequencing of actions by reward, in which cortico-**basal ganglia** loops and **dopamine** are central ([7.16.40](/7.16.40)). Practice curves follow approximately a power law of the number of trials, $T_n \approx T_1 n^{-\beta}$ with $\beta$ of a few tenths, which can be produced by a mixture of learning processes with different rates. Habits are distinguished from goal-directed actions by their insensitivity to the current value of the outcome: after extensive training, rats continue to press a lever for a food that they have been made to dislike (outcome devaluation), whereas after limited training they stop. This dissociation and its basis in the dorsolateral and dorsomedial striatum are treated in [7.16.40](/7.16.40#sec-40.5).

Priming, the facilitated processing of a stimulus after a previous encounter with it, is a further form of nondeclarative memory. It is preserved in amnesic patients, is specific to the perceptual form of the stimulus and is associated with reduced activity (repetition suppression) in the cortical areas that process it, a signature of more efficient processing used widely in functional imaging.

## 38.6. Early Experience and Imprinting

Some learning occurs only during a restricted period of development. Lorenz described how goslings follow the first moving object they see after hatching, normally their mother but also a person or a box, and prefer it thereafter. This filial imprinting is learned rapidly, is restricted to a **critical period** of about the first two days after hatching, and is hard to reverse. In domestic chicks, imprinting depends on a region of the forebrain, the intermediate medial mesopallium: its lesion prevents imprinting, neurons there respond selectively to the imprinted object after training, and their synapses show **NMDA receptor**–dependent changes (Horn and colleagues). Song learning in songbirds ([7.16.36](/7.16.36#sec-36.6)), the development of binocular vision ([7.16.21](/7.16.21)) and of language are other examples of learning within critical periods. What opens and closes such periods (maturation of inhibitory circuits, perineuronal nets, neuromodulatory gating) is discussed in [7.16.21](/7.16.21).

## Summary

- Memory is divided by content (declarative and nondeclarative) and by duration; long-term memory needs gene expression and protein synthesis, is associated with structural change and benefits from spaced training.
- Habituation and sensitization are nonassociative; classical conditioning (Definition 38.2.1) learns predictions and operant conditioning (Definition 38.2.2) learns actions; both are error-driven.
- In *Aplysia*, habituation is homosynaptic depression of release, with a cascade of time scales producing rate sensitivity; sensitization is presynaptic facilitation by serotonin through cAMP and PKA; classical conditioning uses an adenylyl cyclase that detects the coincidence of $\ce{Ca^2+}$/calmodulin and serotonin.
- Long-term facilitation requires nuclear PKA and MAPK, CREB-1 activation and relief of CREB-2 repression, persistent PKA and new synapses; a two-stage model with a steep threshold for the slow stage explains why spaced training is needed (Formula 38.3.1).
- In *Drosophila*, *dunce* and *rutabaga* (cAMP phosphodiesterase and adenylyl cyclase) are required for olfactory learning; in the mushroom body, compartment-specific dopamine depresses Kenyon cell–MBON synapses active just before it, so punishment reduces approach drive and reward reduces avoidance drive (Example 38.4.1); sparse odor codes limit interference.
- Procedural memory includes cerebellar calibration and basal ganglia action selection; habits are insensitive to outcome devaluation. Imprinting is learning restricted to a critical period.

## Questions

1. How would you show that short-term habituation of the gill-withdrawal reflex is presynaptic rather than a change in the motor neuron? Which result of quantal analysis distinguishes the two?

   **Answer** Record the identified sensory and motor neurons during repeated stimulation: the EPSP declines while the input resistance and excitability of the motor neuron and its depolarization by iontophoretically applied transmitter are unchanged, so the postsynaptic sensitivity is intact. Quantal analysis ([7.16.9](/7.16.9)) distinguishes the sites: a presynaptic change reduces the mean number of quanta released (quantal content) and increases the proportion of failures, leaving the amplitude of single quanta (quantal size) unchanged; a postsynaptic change would reduce quantal size. The data show reduced quantal content with constant quantal size.

2. In Formula 38.3.1, explain why five pulses spaced by 20 minutes produce long-term facilitation whereas one pulse does not, and predict what happens if the five pulses are delivered within 2 minutes (massed). What additional mechanism would be needed for massed training to be less effective than spaced training, as observed?

   **Answer** One pulse raises $p$ to 1 and $n$ to a peak well below 1 because $n$ follows $p$ slowly; the steep function $n^4/(n^4 + 1.5^4)$ stays near 0 and $g$ barely grows. Spaced pulses keep $p$ elevated for over an hour, so $n$ climbs to about 0.9, where the steep function is no longer negligible, and $g$ grows. Massed pulses add almost the same total input in a shorter time: $p$ jumps to about 5 and decays, and $n$, integrating $p$, reaches a similar or even higher peak, so in this model massed training would be as effective or more effective. The observed advantage of spacing requires a mechanism that limits the response to closely repeated pulses, for example saturation or refractoriness of PKA or MAPK activation (a pulse adds little while the pathway is already active), or inhibitory constraints (CREB-2, phosphatases) that are activated by each pulse and recover between spaced pulses; with such a nonlinearity, the total nuclear signal is larger when the pulses are spread out.

3. In the mushroom body model, how many independent odor associations could be stored before learning one odor substantially affects responses to others? Assume random codes of $k$ active cells out of $N$.

   **Answer** Two random codes share on average $k^2/N$ cells, a fraction $k/N$ of each code (5% for $k = 25$, $N = 500$). After aversive training of $m$ odors to complete depression of their cells, the fraction of a new odor's cells that have been depressed is $1 - (1 - k/N)^m \approx 1 - \mathrm{e}^{-mk/N}$. For this to stay below about 20%, $m < -\ln 0.8 \cdot N/k \approx 0.22\,N/k$, about 4 odors with these numbers and the same for any code of 5% density, including the fly's roughly 2000 Kenyon cells: capacity in this simple sense depends on the inverse coding density $N/k$, not on $N$ alone. Sparser codes raise it; partial rather than complete depression, depression confined to one compartment per outcome, and readouts that compare MBONs rather than requiring complete silencing raise the practical capacity far above this estimate.

4. (Open question) Habits persist after outcome devaluation, whereas goal-directed actions do not. Design an experiment in mice that would test whether the transition from goal-directed to habitual control involves a shift of dopamine-dependent plasticity from one part of the striatum to another.

   **Answer** Approach: train mice to press a lever for a food reward with either limited training or extended training on a variable-interval schedule (which favors habits), test sensitivity to outcome devaluation (pre-feeding the reward or pairing it with illness), and record or manipulate the dorsomedial and dorsolateral striatum. Predictions: during early training, dopamine release or prediction-error signals and plasticity markers are prominent in the dorsomedial striatum, later in the dorsolateral striatum; lesions or optogenetic inhibition of the dorsolateral striatum (or its dopamine input from lateral SNc) during extended training should keep behavior goal-directed (sensitive to devaluation), and inhibition of the dorsomedial striatum should make behavior habitual earlier. Measuring corticostriatal synaptic strength ex vivo after each training stage would test whether the shift is in plasticity itself.
