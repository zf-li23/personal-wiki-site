Neurodegenerative diseases are defined by the progressive loss of particular populations of neurons: cholinergic and cortical neurons in Alzheimer's disease, dopamine neurons of the substantia nigra in Parkinson's disease, striatal projection neurons in Huntington's disease, motor neurons in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis. Most share three features that make them a subject for this course rather than only for medicine. First, each is associated with the aggregation of a specific protein into fibrils with the cross-β structure of **amyloid fibrils** (amyloid-β and tau, α-synuclein, huntingtin, TDP-43), and the kinetics of aggregation can be modeled with the physics of nucleated polymerization ([7.6.26](/7.6.26)). Second, pathology spreads through the brain along neural connections in stereotyped sequences, which the **connectome** ([7.16.49](/7.16.49)) and network models can explain. Third, human genetics has identified causal genes that both prove the role of particular molecules and point to treatments. This chapter treats the diseases as mechanisms and models; it does not describe clinical management. Protein folding and misfolding are in [7.6.16](/7.6.16) and [7.1](/7.1); the immune functions of microglia and complement in [7.15](/7.15); the basal ganglia circuit, whose failure explains the symptoms of Parkinson's and Huntington's disease, in [7.16.40](/7.16.40).

## 51.1. Alzheimer's Disease

### 51.1.1. Plaques and Tangles

**Definition 51.1.1 (Alzheimer's Disease)** **Alzheimer's disease** is a neurodegenerative disease characterized clinically by progressive loss of memory and other cognitive functions (dementia) and defined neuropathologically by two lesions: extracellular *amyloid plaques*, composed mainly of aggregated amyloid-β (Aβ) peptides, and intracellular *neurofibrillary tangles*, composed of hyperphosphorylated, aggregated tau, a microtubule-associated protein; it is accompanied by loss of synapses and neurons, beginning in the entorhinal cortex and hippocampus.

Alois Alzheimer described both lesions in 1906 in the brain of a 55-year-old woman with dementia. About 55 million people live with dementia worldwide, and Alzheimer's disease accounts for 60–70 % of cases; prevalence roughly doubles every five years of age after 65. The two lesions follow different topographies. Tangles appear first in the transentorhinal and entorhinal cortex, spread to the hippocampus and limbic cortex and then to association and finally primary neocortex, the six *Braak stages* of tau pathology (Braak and Braak, 1991). Plaques begin in the neocortex and spread inward. The density of tangles, not of plaques, correlates with the severity of cognitive impairment and with neuron loss; synapse loss correlates best of all.

### 51.1.2. APP Processing and the Amyloid Hypothesis

Aβ is a fragment of the amyloid precursor protein (APP), a transmembrane protein. In the nonamyloidogenic pathway, α-secretase cleaves APP within the Aβ sequence. In the amyloidogenic pathway, β-secretase (BACE1) cleaves at the N-terminus of Aβ, and the **γ-secretase** complex, whose catalytic subunit is presenilin, cleaves within the membrane, releasing peptides of 38 to 43 residues; Aβ40 is the most abundant, and Aβ42, with two more hydrophobic residues, aggregates much faster and is the main species in plaques.

**Definition 51.1.2 (Amyloid Hypothesis)** The **amyloid hypothesis** (amyloid cascade hypothesis; Hardy and Higgins, 1992) proposes that the accumulation of Aβ, through increased production or decreased clearance, is the initiating event of Alzheimer's disease, which triggers tau pathology, synaptic dysfunction, inflammation and neuron loss.

The strongest evidence is genetic. Early-onset familial Alzheimer's disease, inherited as an autosomal dominant trait with onset often before 60 and accounting for about 1 % of cases, is caused by mutations in *APP* and in the presenilin genes *PSEN1* and *PSEN2*; most of them increase the production of Aβ or the ratio Aβ42/Aβ40. People with Down syndrome, who have three copies of chromosome 21 and hence of *APP*, develop Alzheimer pathology by their 40s, and a duplication of the *APP* locus alone causes early-onset disease. Conversely, a coding variant near the β-secretase cleavage site (A673T), found in Iceland, reduces Aβ production by about 40 % in cell assays and protects against Alzheimer's disease and against cognitive decline in the elderly (Jonsson and colleagues, 2012). Mutations in tau (*MAPT*) cause frontotemporal dementia with tangles but without plaques, consistent with tau acting downstream of Aβ.

### 51.1.3. ApoE4, Microglia and Late-Onset Disease

Late-onset Alzheimer's disease, the common form, is not inherited in a Mendelian way, but twin studies give a heritability of about 70 %. The largest genetic risk factor is the ε4 allele of *APOE*, the gene of apolipoprotein E, a lipid carrier made mainly by astrocytes and microglia that binds Aβ.

**Example 51.1.1 (Risk from ApoE ε4)** The ε4 allele has a frequency of about 15 % in the general population but about 40 % among patients. Relative to ε3/ε3, one ε4 allele increases the risk of Alzheimer's disease about 3-fold and two alleles about 12-fold, and each allele lowers the mean age of onset; the ε2 allele, at about 8 % in the population and 4 % in patients, is protective. Because ε4 is common, it contributes far more cases than the rare dominant mutations, although it is neither necessary (about half of patients lack it) nor sufficient. If $f$ is the frequency of carriers and $R$ their relative risk, the population attributable fraction is $f(R - 1)/(1 + f(R - 1))$; with $f \approx 0.25$ carriers and an average $R \approx 3.5$, it is about $0.38$, so roughly a third of cases are attributable to the allele under this simple model.

:::

ApoE4 impairs the clearance of Aβ and promotes its aggregation, and ApoE also affects tau pathology and lipid metabolism independently of Aβ. Genome-wide association studies (methods in [7.4.25](/7.4.25)) have found dozens of further loci, many expressed in microglia, which pointed to the innate immune system. A rare variant of the microglial receptor TREM2 (R47H) increases the risk about as much as one ε4 allele; TREM2 binds lipids, ApoE and Aβ and is required for microglia to cluster around plaques, compact them and limit damage to nearby neurites, and in AD mouse models its loss produces more diffuse plaques and more neuritic dystrophy. Microglia also remove synapses through the complement cascade, a mechanism of normal development ([7.16.52](/7.16.52)) that may be reactivated by Aβ and contribute to early synapse loss (Hong and colleagues, 2016).

### 51.1.4. Models, Biomarkers and Treatment

Transgenic mice overexpressing mutant human APP (with or without mutant presenilin) develop plaques and memory deficits but few tangles and little neuron loss, which is one reason drugs that work in mice have failed in patients; knock-in mice that express humanized, mutant APP at normal levels (Saito and colleagues, 2014), and mice that also carry human tau, are closer models.

In humans, the course of the disease can now be followed before symptoms with biomarkers: Aβ42 and phosphorylated tau in the cerebrospinal fluid and, more recently, in blood, PET tracers that bind amyloid plaques and tau tangles, and MRI atrophy. In families with dominant mutations, whose age of onset is predictable, CSF Aβ42 falls about 25 years before expected onset, amyloid deposition is detectable about 15 years before, and atrophy and memory decline follow (Bateman and colleagues, 2012). These measurements support a temporal ordering of biomarkers (figure below), but the long preclinical phase and the variable rate of progression show that amyloid is not sufficient on its own: many elderly people have abundant plaques without dementia.

Treatments directed at the cholinergic deficit (cholinesterase inhibitors) and at glutamate excitotoxicity relieve symptoms modestly without changing the course. Disease-modifying treatments target Aβ. Inhibitors of γ-secretase failed and worsened cognition, partly because γ-secretase also cleaves Notch and many other substrates, and β-secretase inhibitors also failed. Monoclonal antibodies that bind aggregated Aβ and promote its removal by microglia clear plaques almost completely within 18 months; in phase 3 trials in patients with early disease, lecanemab slowed decline on a clinical scale by about 27 % (van Dyck and colleagues, 2023) and donanemab by about 35 % in patients with low to intermediate tau (Sims and colleagues, 2023). The effects are statistically clear but clinically modest, and the antibodies cause brain edema and microhemorrhages in a fraction of patients (amyloid-related imaging abnormalities), more often in ApoE4 carriers. The trials are the first direct evidence that removing Aβ changes the course of the disease, supporting the amyloid hypothesis, while the size of the effect suggests that, once symptoms appear, tau pathology and neurodegeneration proceed partly independently of Aβ.

## 51.2. Prions and the Kinetics of Aggregation

### 51.2.1. Prions and Prion-Like Propagation

**Definition 51.2.1 (Prion and Prion-Like Propagation)** A **prion** is a misfolded conformation of a protein that propagates by inducing the normally folded form of the same protein to adopt the misfolded conformation; prion diseases are caused by PrP$^{\mathrm{Sc}}$, a β-sheet-rich, protease-resistant conformer of the cellular prion protein PrP$^{\mathrm{C}}$ (Prusiner, 1982). **Prion-like propagation** is the analogous templated misfolding and cell-to-cell spread of other aggregation-prone proteins, such as tau, α-synuclein and TDP-43, within an individual, without demonstrated transmission between individuals under natural conditions.

Prion diseases (kuru, Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease, scrapie in sheep, bovine spongiform encephalopathy and its human form variant CJD) are infectious, inherited (mutations of *PRNP*) or sporadic. Mice lacking *Prnp* are resistant to infection (Büeler and colleagues, 1993), which shows that the host protein is the substrate of propagation, and prion "strains", conformationally distinct aggregates of the same sequence, produce different diseases and breed true, so that conformation carries the information that a genome carries in a virus. The same templating has been shown for other proteins. Injection of brain extracts containing aggregated tau into mice expressing human tau induces tau pathology that spreads from the injection site to connected regions (Clavaguera and colleagues, 2009); synthetic fibrils of α-synuclein injected into the striatum of wild-type mice cause Lewy body–like pathology in connected regions and loss of dopamine neurons (Luk and colleagues, 2012); and fetal dopamine neurons grafted into the striatum of patients with Parkinson's disease developed Lewy bodies 10–16 years later (Li and colleagues, 2008; Kordower and colleagues, 2008).

### 51.2.2. Nucleation, Elongation and Secondary Nucleation

The aggregation of amyloid proteins in vitro follows a sigmoidal time course: a lag phase with little detectable fibril, rapid growth and a plateau when monomer is exhausted. The lag is shortened by adding preformed fibrils (seeds). A kinetic model with three microscopic steps explains these features and has been fitted to Aβ42, α-synuclein and other proteins with high precision (Knowles and colleagues, 2009; Cohen and colleagues, 2013).

**Formula 51.2.1 (Nucleated Polymerization with Secondary Nucleation)** Let $m$ be the concentration of free monomer, $P$ the number concentration of fibrils and $M$ their mass concentration (in monomer units), with $m + M = m_{\mathrm{tot}}$. Primary nucleation from monomer (rate constant $k_n$, reaction order $n_c$), elongation by monomer addition at both ends ($k_+$) and secondary nucleation of new fibrils on the surface of existing ones ($k_2$, order $n_2$) give
$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}P}{\mathrm{d}t} = k_nm^{n_c} + k_2m^{n_2}M, \qquad \frac{\mathrm{d}M}{\mathrm{d}t} = 2k_+mP. \tag{51.1}
$$

**Proposition 51.2.1 (Early-Time Growth and Seeding)** While monomer is not yet depleted ($m \approx m_{\mathrm{tot}}$), and when secondary nucleation dominates new fibril formation, $M$ grows as $M(t) \approx A\cosh(\kappa t) + B\sinh(\kappa t) - C$ with the exponential rate
$$
\kappa = \sqrt{2k_+k_2m_{\mathrm{tot}}^{n_2 + 1}}. \tag{51.2}
$$
A seed of fibril mass $M_0$ therefore shortens the half-time by about $\kappa^{-1}\ln 10$ for every tenfold increase of $M_0$, and the half-time scales with concentration as $t_{1/2} \propto m_{\mathrm{tot}}^{-(n_2 + 1)/2}$ (up to logarithmic factors).

**Proof** Differentiating the second equation of (51.1) and inserting the first with $m = m_{\mathrm{tot}}$ gives $\ddot M = 2k_+m_{\mathrm{tot}}(k_nm_{\mathrm{tot}}^{n_c} + k_2m_{\mathrm{tot}}^{n_2}M)$, a linear equation $\ddot M = \kappa^2M + c$ with $c = 2k_+k_nm_{\mathrm{tot}}^{n_c + 1}$, whose solutions are combinations of $\cosh(\kappa t)$, $\sinh(\kappa t)$ and the constant $-c/\kappa^2$. With seeds that dominate the initial condition, $M(t) \approx M_0\cosh(\kappa t) + (2k_+m_{\mathrm{tot}}P_0/\kappa)\sinh(\kappa t) \approx \tfrac12(M_0 + 2k_+m_{\mathrm{tot}}P_0/\kappa)\mathrm{e}^{\kappa t}$ for $\kappa t \gg 1$, so the time to reach a fixed fraction of $m_{\mathrm{tot}}$ is $\kappa^{-1}\ln(\text{const}/M_0)$, decreasing by $\kappa^{-1}\ln 10$ per decade of $M_0$. Since $t_{1/2}$ is dominated by $1/\kappa$, it scales as $m_{\mathrm{tot}}^{-(n_2 + 1)/2}$. $\square$

Secondary nucleation is autocatalytic: fibrils catalyze the formation of new fibrils, and for Aβ42 at physiological concentrations it produces most of the small oligomers, the species thought to be most toxic to neurons (Cohen and colleagues, 2013). The model suggests therapeutic logic: an antibody or chaperone that binds fibril surfaces and blocks secondary nucleation reduces oligomer production more than one that only sequesters monomer, and the molecular chaperone Brichos, which binds fibril surfaces, does so in vitro. In the brain, the strong dependence on concentration explains why small increases of Aβ production, as caused by familial mutations or an extra copy of *APP*, bring forward the onset by decades, and why reducing production by the Icelandic variant protects.

### 51.2.3. Spread Along the Connectome

The Braak stages of tau in Alzheimer's disease and of α-synuclein in Parkinson's disease follow anatomical connections more closely than spatial proximity. Network models make this quantitative. Raj, Kuceyeski and Weiner (2012) modeled the spread of pathology $\boldsymbol{x}$ among brain regions as diffusion on the structural connectome, $\dot{\boldsymbol{x}} = -\rho L\boldsymbol{x}$, where $L = D - W$ is the graph Laplacian of the connection matrix $W$ ($D$ the diagonal matrix of node strengths); the slowest eigenmodes of $L$ predicted the patterns of atrophy in Alzheimer's disease and frontotemporal dementia measured with MRI. Templated misfolding adds local autocatalytic growth.

**Formula 51.2.2 (Network Spread with Local Growth)** The fraction $x_k \in [0, 1]$ of misfolded protein in region $k$ obeys
$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}}{\mathrm{d}t} = -\rho L\boldsymbol{x} + \alpha\,\boldsymbol{x} \odot (\boldsymbol{1} - \boldsymbol{x}), \tag{51.3}
$$
a Fisher–Kolmogorov equation on the graph: transport along axons at rate $\rho$ and local self-templating at rate $\alpha$.

Fitted to longitudinal tau PET data, models of this kind reproduce the order in which regions become abnormal from a seed in the entorhinal cortex, and they predict that hubs and well-connected regions are affected early (Vogel and colleagues, 2020). In the continuum limit, Eq. (51.3) has traveling fronts of speed $2\sqrt{\rho\alpha}$ (in units of the connection length), so the time at which a region becomes affected grows linearly with its path distance from the seed (figure below). The models leave open what moves along connections (aggregates released and taken up by neurons, or vulnerability that is shared by connected neurons) and whether selective vulnerability of cell types explains as much as connectivity.

## 51.3. Parkinson's Disease

**Definition 51.3.1 (Parkinson's Disease)** **Parkinson's disease** is a neurodegenerative disease defined by the motor symptoms of bradykinesia (slowness of movement), rigidity and resting tremor, caused by the loss of dopamine neurons of the substantia nigra pars compacta, and pathologically by *Lewy bodies*, intracellular inclusions of aggregated α-synuclein.

Parkinson's disease affects about 1 % of people over 60, more than ten million worldwide. Motor symptoms appear when about half of the nigral dopamine neurons, and about 70–80 % of striatal dopamine, have been lost, a compensation that reflects the plasticity of the remaining terminals and of the striatum. Nonmotor symptoms, loss of smell, constipation, REM sleep behavior disorder and depression, can precede them by years; with Braak's finding that α-synuclein pathology starts in the olfactory bulb and the dorsal motor nucleus of the vagus before reaching the substantia nigra, they suggest that the disease may begin in the periphery and spread into the brain, possibly from the gut along the vagus nerve.

Genetics has identified the molecular pathways. Mutations in *SNCA*, the gene of α-synuclein (the first, A53T, in 1997), and duplications or triplications of the locus cause dominant Parkinson's disease, with earlier onset for triplication than duplication, a dose dependence that parallels that of *APP*. Mutations in the kinase LRRK2 (G2019S the most common) are the most frequent dominant cause. Recessive early-onset forms are caused by loss of the kinase PINK1 or of the ubiquitin ligase Parkin, which act together to label damaged mitochondria for **mitophagy**, and heterozygous mutations of *GBA1*, encoding the lysosomal enzyme glucocerebrosidase, are a common risk factor. The convergence on mitochondrial quality control and the lysosome, together with the toxin MPTP, which caused acute parkinsonism in drug users in 1982 by inhibiting complex I of the respiratory chain after conversion to MPP$^+$ and uptake by the dopamine transporter, explains part of the vulnerability of nigral neurons: they are large, have long, highly branched axons with hundreds of thousands of release sites, and fire autonomously with $\ce{Ca^2+}$ entry, which imposes high metabolic and oxidative stress.

The treatments act on the circuit rather than the cause. L-dopa, the precursor of dopamine, crosses the **blood–brain barrier** and is converted to dopamine by the remaining terminals, restoring dopamine signaling in the striatum; its effect declines and motor fluctuations appear as degeneration progresses. Deep brain stimulation of the subthalamic nucleus or the internal globus pallidus at about $130\,\mathrm{Hz}$ relieves symptoms; in the model of the **basal ganglia** described in [7.16.40](/7.16.40), loss of dopamine shifts the balance between the direct pathway and the indirect pathway toward inhibition of movement, and exaggerated beta-band ($13$–$30\,\mathrm{Hz}$) synchronization in the subthalamic nucleus and cortex correlates with bradykinesia, which stimulation suppresses. Optogenetic experiments in parkinsonian mice showed that driving the cortical afferents to the subthalamic nucleus, rather than the subthalamic neurons themselves, reproduces the therapeutic effect (Gradinaru and colleagues, 2009). Closed-loop stimulation triggered by beta activity is being developed on this basis.

## 51.4. Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis, Huntington's Disease and Repeat Expansions

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) is the degeneration of upper motor neurons in the cortex and lower motor neurons in the brainstem and spinal cord, leading to paralysis and death, typically within three to five years. About 10 % of cases are familial; the first gene found was *SOD1* (1993), encoding superoxide dismutase, and the most common is a GGGGCC hexanucleotide repeat expansion in an intron of *C9orf72* (2011), responsible for about 40 % of familial and 5–10 % of sporadic cases in European populations, and also of frontotemporal dementia, revealing that the two diseases form a spectrum. In about 97 % of ALS cases, the RNA-binding protein TDP-43 leaves the nucleus and aggregates in the cytoplasm, which disrupts the splicing of many neuronal transcripts. An antisense oligonucleotide that lowers SOD1 (tofersen) reduced markers of neuron damage in patients with *SOD1* mutations, the first gene-targeted treatment for a form of ALS.

**Huntington's disease** is caused by expansion of a CAG repeat in the first exon of *HTT*, which encodes huntingtin with an expanded polyglutamine tract. Normal alleles have fewer than 27 repeats; 36–39 repeats cause disease with incomplete **penetrance**, and 40 or more cause it with complete penetrance. The disease is dominant, and onset age falls with repeat length: repeat length explains more than half of the variance of onset age, and very long repeats cause juvenile onset. Repeats are unstable and tend to expand in paternal transmission (**anticipation**). The medium spiny neurons of the indirect pathway in the striatum degenerate first, which releases movement and produces chorea; later, direct-pathway neurons and cortex are lost. Genome-wide association studies of onset age found that genes of DNA mismatch repair (*MSH3*, *FAN1*) modify onset, and that the timing depends on further expansion of the CAG repeat in somatic neurons over a lifetime (somatic expansion), which suggests that disease begins when the repeat in a vulnerable neuron passes a threshold length. **Trinucleotide repeat expansion** is also the cause of fragile X syndrome ([7.16.52](/7.16.52)), several spinocerebellar ataxias, myotonic dystrophy and Friedreich's ataxia ([7.4.12](/7.4.12)).

The diseases differ in the protein, the vulnerable cells and the time course, but share a logic (Luo): a protein prone to aggregation, a dose dependence (more protein or a longer repeat means earlier onset), prion-like spread from vulnerable populations along connections, and failure of protein quality control by chaperones, the **ubiquitin–proteasome system** and **autophagy**, which declines with age.

## 51.5. The Aging Brain

Aging is the main risk factor for all of these diseases, but normal aging is not neurodegeneration. Stereological counts show that the number of neocortical neurons declines only by about 10 % between 20 and 90 years in the absence of disease, much less than once thought; what declines more is the number of synapses and dendritic spines, the volume of white matter and the integrity of myelin, and the neurogenesis that persists in the adult hippocampus of rodents (and, contentiously, of humans, [7.5.19](/7.5.19)). Brain volume shrinks by about $0.2\,\%$ per year in middle age and by about $0.5\,\%$ per year after 70, faster in the prefrontal cortex and hippocampus. Cognitive abilities age differently: processing speed, working memory and episodic memory decline from early adulthood, while vocabulary and accumulated knowledge (crystallized intelligence) are maintained into old age. Individuals with more education and cognitively demanding occupations tolerate more pathology before dementia, the concept of *cognitive reserve*. In animals, caloric restriction and reduced insulin/IGF-1 signaling extend life span and delay age-related decline ([7.14](/7.14)), and in mice the transfer of blood from young to old animals improves hippocampal plasticity and memory, an effect attributed to circulating factors, which shows that the aging of the brain depends in part on the body.

## 51.6. Antisense Oligonucleotides and Gene Therapy

Spinal muscular atrophy illustrates how a genetic mechanism can lead to treatment. The disease is a degeneration of spinal motor neurons caused by homozygous loss of *SMN1*, which encodes the survival of motor neuron protein, an essential component of the machinery that assembles small nuclear ribonucleoproteins for splicing. Humans have a nearly identical paralog, *SMN2*, but a C→T change in its exon 7 weakens an exonic splicing enhancer, so that most *SMN2* transcripts skip exon 7 and only about 10 % yield full-length protein; the number of *SMN2* copies (one to four or more) determines the severity of the disease, from death in infancy to mild adult weakness. Three treatments act at different steps.

| Treatment | Molecule | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| Nusinersen (2016) | 18-mer antisense oligonucleotide, given intrathecally | Binds the intronic splicing silencer ISS-N1 in intron 7 of *SMN2* pre-mRNA and blocks the splicing repressor hnRNP A1, increasing exon 7 inclusion |
| Risdiplam (2020) | Orally available small molecule | Stabilizes the interaction of the U1 snRNP with the 5′ splice site of exon 7, also increasing inclusion |
| Onasemnogene abeparvovec (2019) | Adeno-associated virus 9 carrying *SMN1* cDNA, one intravenous dose | Replaces the missing gene; AAV9 crosses the blood–brain barrier in infants and transduces motor neurons |

Treated early, ideally before symptoms (newborn screening makes this possible), infants with the severe form achieve motor milestones never reached in the natural history of the disease. Antisense oligonucleotides are short, chemically modified nucleic acids that bind a target RNA by base pairing and either recruit RNase H to degrade it (as tofersen lowers *SOD1*) or block a site to modify splicing or translation (as nusinersen does); they are designed directly from the sequence, which makes them suitable for rare diseases, including "n-of-1" drugs designed for a single patient's mutation. Their limits are delivery (they do not cross the blood–brain barrier and must be injected into the cerebrospinal fluid) and the need for the target to be causal: an oligonucleotide that lowered huntingtin in patients with Huntington's disease (tominersen) did not slow, and at high doses worsened, the disease in a phase 3 trial, a reminder that lowering a protein with normal functions is not automatically beneficial.

## Summary

- Alzheimer's disease (Definition 51.1.1) is defined by Aβ plaques and tau tangles; tangles and synapse loss correlate with symptoms. The amyloid hypothesis (Definition 51.1.2) rests on genetics: dominant *APP* and presenilin mutations, *APP* dosage, and the protective A673T variant. ApoE ε4 (Example 51.1.1) and microglial TREM2 dominate late-onset risk. Anti-amyloid antibodies slow decline modestly.

| Disease | Aggregating protein | Vulnerable cells | Main genes | Mechanism-based treatment |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Alzheimer's | Aβ, tau | Entorhinal, hippocampal, cortical neurons | *APP*, *PSEN1/2*, *APOE*, *TREM2* | Anti-Aβ antibodies |
| Parkinson's | α-synuclein | Substantia nigra dopamine neurons | *SNCA*, *LRRK2*, *PINK1*, *PRKN*, *GBA1* | L-dopa, deep brain stimulation |
| Huntington's | Huntingtin (polyQ) | Striatal indirect-pathway neurons | *HTT* (CAG repeat) | (Lowering huntingtin unsuccessful so far) |
| ALS | TDP-43, SOD1 | Upper and lower motor neurons | *C9orf72*, *SOD1*, *TARDBP* | ASO against *SOD1* |
| Prion diseases | PrP$^{\mathrm{Sc}}$ | Widespread | *PRNP* | — |

- Prions (Definition 51.2.1) propagate by templated misfolding, and tau, α-synuclein and TDP-43 spread in a prion-like manner. Aggregation follows nucleated polymerization with secondary nucleation (Formula 51.2.1); the early growth rate $\kappa = \sqrt{2k_+k_2m_{\mathrm{tot}}^{n_2 + 1}}$ explains the steep concentration dependence and the logarithmic effect of seeds (Proposition 51.2.1).
- Pathology spreads along the connectome; diffusion with local growth on the graph (Formula 51.2.2) reproduces Braak-like staging.
- Parkinson's disease (Definition 51.3.1) involves α-synuclein, mitochondrial and lysosomal quality control; treatments restore dopamine or correct basal ganglia dynamics. Huntington's disease and ALS show the role of repeat expansions; normal aging involves synapse and white matter loss rather than large neuron loss. Antisense oligonucleotides and AAV gene therapy treat spinal muscular atrophy by targeting splicing and gene dosage.

## Questions

1. Using Proposition 51.2.1, by what factor does the half-time of aggregation change if the concentration of Aβ42 increases by 50 % (as the ratio change caused by some familial mutations might mimic), with $n_2 = 2$? Why might this matter more over decades in the brain than in a test tube?

   **Answer** With $t_{1/2} \propto m_{\mathrm{tot}}^{-(n_2 + 1)/2} = m_{\mathrm{tot}}^{-3/2}$, a factor $1.5$ in concentration shortens the half-time by $1.5^{-1.5} \approx 0.54$, almost by half. In vitro this is a difference between hours; in the brain, where production and clearance keep Aβ near a steady state and aggregation is very slow, the "lag phase" may last decades, so halving it brings onset forward by decades, which matches the early onset of familial disease and of Down syndrome. Conversely, a modest reduction of production, as by the Icelandic A673T variant (about 40 %), lengthens the lag more than proportionally ($0.6^{-1.5} \approx 2.2$) and could delay onset beyond the life span.

2. For the network model of Formula 51.2.2 with $\alpha = 0$, show that the total amount $\sum_kx_k$ is conserved and that the pattern of pathology approaches the eigenvectors of $L$ with the smallest eigenvalues. What does adding $\alpha > 0$ change?

   **Answer** With $\alpha = 0$, $\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\boldsymbol{1}^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol{x} = -\rho\boldsymbol{1}^{\mathsf T}L\boldsymbol{x} = 0$ because the rows (and columns) of the Laplacian $L = D - W$ sum to zero for symmetric $W$, so the total is conserved. Expanding $\boldsymbol{x}(t) = \sum_jc_j\mathrm{e}^{-\rho\lambda_jt}\boldsymbol{u}_j$ in the eigenvectors of $L$ (eigenvalues $0 = \lambda_1 \le \lambda_2 \le \dots$), the components with large $\lambda_j$ decay fastest, so after a transient the deviation from the uniform state is dominated by the eigenvectors with the smallest nonzero eigenvalues (the "persistent modes" that Raj and colleagues compared with atrophy patterns), and eventually pathology becomes uniform at the initial average. With $\alpha > 0$, total pathology is not conserved but grows autocatalytically from the seed, and the dynamics is a traveling front that invades regions in order of their path distance; the final state is $x_k = 1$ everywhere, so the model predicts staging (order of involvement) rather than a static pattern.

3. Two individuals carry ApoE alleles ε3/ε4 and ε4/ε4. Using the relative risks of Example 51.1.1 and a lifetime risk of about 10 % for ε3/ε3 carriers, estimate their lifetime risks, and explain why relative risks cannot simply be multiplied with baseline risk at high values.

   **Answer** Multiplying gives about 30 % for ε3/ε4 and 120 % for ε4/ε4, the second being impossible. Relative risks in such studies are usually odds ratios estimated from case–control data; converting with odds, $0.1/0.9 = 0.111$, times 3 gives odds $0.33$ and a risk of $25\%$, and times 12 gives odds $1.33$ and a risk of about $57\%$. Risk also depends on survival to old age (lifetime risk is censored by death from other causes) and on sex and ancestry; cohort estimates for ε4 homozygotes are on the order of $50$–$60\%$ by age 85. The general point is that a multiplicative model for odds, not for probabilities, keeps risks below one.

4. Why is the subthalamic nucleus an effective target for deep brain stimulation in Parkinson's disease, in terms of the basal ganglia circuit? What did the optogenetic experiments of Gradinaru and colleagues add?

   **Answer** In the circuit model, loss of striatal dopamine reduces activity of the direct pathway (D1 receptors) and increases that of the indirect pathway (D2 receptors); the indirect pathway disinhibits the subthalamic nucleus, whose glutamatergic output drives the internal globus pallidus and substantia nigra pars reticulata to inhibit the thalamus more strongly, reducing movement. The subthalamic nucleus is thus a node at which the abnormal excess drive, and the pathological beta synchronization, converge, and high-frequency stimulation there disrupts this activity (whether by inhibition, by depolarization block or by imposing a regular pattern that carries no information is still debated). Gradinaru and colleagues found that optogenetic inhibition or excitation of subthalamic neurons themselves did not improve symptoms in parkinsonian rodents, whereas high-frequency activation of cortical axons projecting to the subthalamic nucleus (the hyperdirect pathway) did, and also changed activity in motor cortex; therapeutic stimulation therefore acts at least partly on afferent axons and on the cortex, not only on the local neurons.
