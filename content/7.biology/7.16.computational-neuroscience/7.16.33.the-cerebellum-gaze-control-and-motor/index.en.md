The cerebellum holds most of the neurons of the brain, yet its loss causes no paralysis: movements remain possible but become clumsy, inaccurate and poorly timed, and they no longer improve with practice. This chapter asks what computation explains these deficits. The anatomy of the cerebellum, its functional zones, the two input systems of **mossy fibers** and **climbing fibers**, the symptoms of cerebellar lesions and the classical evidence for cerebellar **long-term depression** and learning are described in [7.14.45](/7.14.45#sec-45.3). Here we treat the circuit quantitatively: the granule cell layer as an expansion that makes patterns linearly separable, the Purkinje cell as a **perceptron** trained by an error signal, and the adaptive filter that learns the gain of the **vestibulo-ocular reflex**. The oculomotor system, where these ideas have been tested most precisely, also contains the clearest example of persistent neural activity, the neural integrator that holds the eyes still, whose line attractor models lead to the attractor networks of [7.16.43](/7.16.43)–[7.16.45](/7.16.45). Finally, we connect the cerebellum to the **internal models** of [7.16.29](/7.16.29).

## 33.1. The Cerebellar Circuit in Numbers

### 33.1.1. Cell Types and Connections

The cerebellar cortex has the same circuit everywhere, a "crystalline" organization in which a few cell types are repeated millions of times ([7.14.45](/7.14.45#sec-45.3.4)).

**Definition 33.1.1 (Cerebellar Granule Cells and Parallel Fibers)** **Cerebellar granule cells** are the small excitatory (glutamatergic) neurons of the granular layer of the cerebellar cortex; each has about four short dendrites ending in claws, each contacting a different mossy fiber terminal. Their axons ascend into the molecular layer and bifurcate into **parallel fibers**, which run for several millimeters along the folium and make excitatory synapses on the dendrites of the Purkinje cells and interneurons they cross.

**Definition 33.1.2 (Purkinje Cell)** A **Purkinje cell** is the GABAergic projection neuron of the cerebellar cortex and its only output, which inhibits neurons of the deep cerebellar nuclei (or, for the flocculus and part of the vermis, of the vestibular nuclei). Its planar dendritic tree, perpendicular to the parallel fibers, receives excitatory synapses from of the order of $10^5$ parallel fibers and from a single climbing fiber, which wraps its proximal dendrites with hundreds of synapses.

The two inputs produce two kinds of spikes. Parallel fiber input, together with the intrinsic pacemaking of the cell, produces *simple spikes* at $50$–$100\,\mathrm{Hz}$, modulated with movement. Each climbing fiber spike produces a *complex spike*, a large $\ce{Ca^2+}$-dependent depolarization of the whole dendritic tree crowned with a burst of small spikes, at about $1\,\mathrm{Hz}$. Golgi cells, excited by mossy and parallel fibers, provide feedback inhibition to granule cells; basket and stellate cells, excited by parallel fibers, provide **feedforward inhibition** to Purkinje cells. The cerebellar nuclei receive the inhibition of Purkinje cells and excitatory collaterals of both input systems, and send the output of the cerebellum to the brainstem and, via the thalamus, to the cortex. The inferior olive, the source of the climbing fibers, receives inhibition from the nuclei, closing an olivo-cortico-nuclear loop. These loops form parasagittal modules (microzones), marked by stripes of Purkinje cells that do or do not express the enzyme aldolase C (zebrin II), with different firing rates and plasticity (Zhou and colleagues, 2014).

| Quantity | Value |
|---|---|
| Neurons in the human cerebellum | about $7\times10^{10}$ of the $8.6\times10^{10}$ neurons of the brain, almost all granule cells (Herculano-Houzel, 2010) |
| Mossy fiber inputs per granule cell | about 4 |
| Granule cells per mossy fiber | a few hundred |
| Parallel fiber synapses per Purkinje cell | about $1.5\times10^5$ (rat) to several $10^5$ (human) |
| Climbing fibers per Purkinje cell | 1 (after developmental pruning, [7.16.21](/7.16.21)) |

| Purkinje cells converging on a nuclear neuron | 20–50 |

The table shows the two extremes of the circuit. The mossy fiber input diverges onto a huge number of granule cells, each sampling only four inputs, and converges again onto Purkinje cells, each sampling a hundred thousand granule cells: an expansion followed by a compression. The climbing fiber system, by contrast, is one-to-one: one olivary neuron, one powerful signal per Purkinje cell.

### 33.1.2. Expansion Recoding

Why expand? Marr (1969) and Albus (1971) proposed that the granule cells recode the mossy fiber input into a higher-dimensional, sparser representation in which patterns that must be distinguished become easier to separate by the readout. The mathematics is Cover's counting of linearly separable dichotomies.

**Theorem 33.1.1 (Cover's Function Counting Theorem)** Let $P$ points in $\mathbb{R}^n$ be in general position (no $n + 1$ of them on a hyperplane). The number of dichotomies (assignments of labels $\pm1$) of these points that can be realized by a hyperplane through the origin is
$$
C(P, n) = 2\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\binom{P-1}{k}. \tag{33.1}
$$
Hence all $2^P$ dichotomies are separable if $P \le n$, exactly half of them if $P = 2n$, and the fraction $C(P, n)/2^P$ falls from 1 to 0 around $P = 2n$, more sharply as $n$ grows (Cover, 1965).

**Proof** (Sketch.) Add a point $\boldsymbol{x}_{P+1}$. A dichotomy of the first $P$ points realized by some hyperplane extends to both labels of the new point if and only if it can be realized by a hyperplane through $\boldsymbol{x}_{P+1}$, which is a separation problem in one dimension fewer. This gives the recursion $C(P + 1, n) = C(P, n) + C(P, n - 1)$, with $C(1, n) = 2$, whose solution is Eq. (33.1) by Pascal's rule. The fraction at $P = 2n$ is $\frac12$ by the symmetry of the binomial coefficients. $\square$

A **perceptron** with a bias on $n$ inputs is a hyperplane through the origin in $n + 1$ dimensions, so it can classify about $2(n + 1)$ random patterns. A Purkinje cell reading $10$ mossy fibers directly could learn about $20$ arbitrary input–output associations; reading the same information expanded into $200$ granule cells, it can learn several hundred, provided that the granule cell responses are nonlinear functions of the inputs, since a linear map cannot raise the dimension (figure below). The nonlinearity is the threshold of the granule cell, set by Golgi cell inhibition, which also fixes the coding level $f$, the fraction of granule cells active for a given input. Too sparse a code leaves many inputs with no active granule cell; too dense a code is nearly linear. Without noise a broad range of coding levels works (figure below, C); theory that includes noise and generalization to similar inputs favors sparser codes, of about $0.1$, and finds the best performance for about four inputs per granule cell, the number observed (Litwin-Kumar, Harris, Axel, Sompolinsky and Abbott, 2017; Billings, Piasini, Lőrincz, Nusser and Silver, 2014). The same architecture, a random expansion with a learned readout, appears in the insect **mushroom body** ([7.16.23](/7.16.23)) and in the reservoirs of [7.16.31](/7.16.31#sec-31.3.1).

B: simulation with $10$ mossy fibers carrying random Gaussian patterns with random binary labels, read either directly (gray) or after expansion into $50$ (blue) or $200$ (red) granule cells, each summing four randomly chosen mossy fibers, minus a Golgi-cell inhibition proportional to the mean mossy fiber input, and thresholded so that $20\,\%$ of them are active; separability decided exactly by linear programming, $16$ labelings per point: the transition moves from about $20$ patterns to about $2M$, in proportion to the number $M$ of granule cells. Recent recordings complicate the sparse-coding picture. Two-photon imaging in behaving mice shows that granule cell activity is dense, that many granule cells are active during movements, and that some signal the expectation of reward (Giovannucci and colleagues, 2017; Wagner, Kim, Savall, Schnitzer and Luo, 2017); during motor learning, granule cells receiving input from the motor cortex largely transmit it faithfully rather than recoding it (Wagner and colleagues, 2019). Expansion may therefore serve different purposes in different regions, or operate on combinations of inputs that imaging of local populations does not reveal.

## 33.2. The Marr–Albus–Ito Theory

### 33.2.1. The Purkinje Cell as a Perceptron

**Definition 33.2.1 (Marr–Albus–Ito Theory)** The **Marr–Albus–Ito theory** holds that the cerebellar cortex is a supervised learning machine: granule cells represent the context of a movement (mossy fiber signals of the motor command, the sensory state and the goal) in an expanded code; each Purkinje cell reads this code through modifiable parallel fiber synapses; and the climbing fiber carries an error or teaching signal, whose coincidence with parallel fiber activity changes the efficacy of the active synapses, so that in the same context the output of the Purkinje cell is corrected. Albus (1971) predicted that the change is a depression, and Ito and colleagues found it (Ito, Sakurai and Tongroach, 1982).

The Purkinje cell is then a **perceptron**, with the parallel fibers as inputs and the climbing fiber as the teacher. The plasticity rule resembles the **perceptron learning algorithm** ([Algorithm 2.2.1](/4.7.2#alg-2.2.1)): parallel fiber activity paired with a complex spike depresses the active synapses (LTD), whereas parallel fiber activity without a complex spike potentiates them (LTP), so that weights change only in proportion to the activity of their inputs and in the direction set by the error. Two features are specific to biology. The weights of parallel fiber synapses are excitatory and therefore nonnegative, and the climbing fiber fires at about $1\,\mathrm{Hz}$, so the error signal is binary and sparse; a pause in its spontaneous firing can signal an error of the opposite sign.

The theory makes a quantitative prediction about synaptic weights. Brunel, Hakim, Isope, Nadal and Barbour (2004) computed the weight distribution of a perceptron with nonnegative weights that stores the maximal number of input–output associations. At capacity, more than half of its synapses have exactly zero weight, and the nonzero weights are distributed as a truncated Gaussian: silencing synapses is how a sign-constrained perceptron implements negative weights. Paired recordings of granule cells and Purkinje cells had found that about $85\,\%$ of parallel fiber synapses produce no detectable response (Isope and Barbour, 2002), a striking agreement between learning theory and anatomy. The storage capacity of a Purkinje cell estimated in this way is about $4\times10^4$ associations, some $5\,\mathrm{kB}$ of information.

### 33.2.2. The Adaptive Filter

Motor learning requires the Purkinje cell to produce a time course, not only a binary class. The adaptive filter model (Fujita, 1982; Dean, Porrill, Ekerot and Jörntell, 2010) generalizes the perceptron to signals that vary in time.

**Formula 33.2.1 (Cerebellar Adaptive Filter)** Let the parallel fibers carry signals $p_j(t)$, different temporal transformations (delays, low-pass and band-pass filters) of the mossy fiber input, and let the Purkinje cell output be $z(t) = \sum_jw_jp_j(t)$. If the climbing fiber signals the error $s(t)$ of the movement, the weights change according to the decorrelation (least-mean-squares) rule
$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}w_j}{\mathrm{d}t} = -\eta\,s(t)\,p_j(t), \tag{33.2}
$$
with the sign chosen so that the change reduces the error; at equilibrium the error is uncorrelated with every parallel fiber signal, $\langle s\,p_j\rangle = 0$, and $z$ is the least-squares approximation of the required correction by the available signals.

The rule is gradient descent on the mean squared error when $s$ depends linearly on $z$ (the Widrow–Hoff rule of adaptive signal processing), and it is local: each synapse needs only its own input and the climbing fiber signal. The granule cell layer supplies the basis functions, as the reservoir does in [7.16.31](/7.16.31), and the richer the temporal transformations it provides, the more accurate the correction can be; the timed eyelid response of eyeblink conditioning ([7.14.45](/7.14.45#sec-45.3.6)) requires a basis that spans the interval between the conditioned and the unconditioned stimulus. Purkinje cells can also learn the timing of a pause in their own firing by a mechanism intrinsic to the cell, which involves metabotropic glutamate receptors (Johansson, Jirenhed, Rasmussen, Zucca and Hesslow, 2014).

## 33.3. Adaptation of the Vestibulo-Ocular Reflex

### 33.3.1. Gain and Its Calibration

The **vestibulo-ocular reflex** rotates the eyes opposite to the head so that the retinal image stays still ([7.14.41](/7.14.41#sec-41.5.2), [7.14.42](/7.14.42#sec-42.5.4)). It runs through a three-neuron arc (vestibular afferents, neurons of the vestibular nuclei, ocular motor neurons) with a latency of under $10\,\mathrm{ms}$, too fast for visual feedback: it is open-loop, feedforward control (Definition 29.2.1). Its accuracy is measured by the gain, the ratio of eye velocity to head velocity (sign inverted), close to 1 for distant targets. Because the reflex cannot see its own errors in real time, it must be calibrated by learning. Magnifying spectacles of power $2$ require a gain of 2, and after wearing them the gain rises toward 2; miniaturizing spectacles lower it; reversing prisms eventually reduce it and can reverse its sign over days (Gonshor and Melvill Jones, 1976). In monkeys and mice, gain changes of $20$–$40\,\%$ are produced in an hour of training with a moving visual surround. The error signal is the retinal slip, the motion of the image on the retina, which is relayed to the flocculus by climbing fibers from the inferior olive. Lesions of the flocculus abolish adaptation without abolishing the reflex.

### 33.3.2. Two Sites of Learning

Where is the memory stored? Ito proposed the flocculus: parallel fibers carrying head velocity signals onto floccular Purkinje cells undergo LTD when paired with climbing fiber signals of retinal slip, changing the Purkinje output to the vestibular nuclei and hence the gain. Miles and Lisberger (1981) argued that the changes are in the brainstem, at the synapses of vestibular afferents onto the floccular target neurons of the vestibular nuclei, because the responses of floccular Purkinje cells after learning were not those expected if they were the site of the memory. Both turned out to be right. Learning begins in the cerebellar cortex and is transferred to the vestibular nuclei over hours: inactivating the flocculus immediately after training abolishes the newly learned gain change, whereas inactivating it a day later leaves most of it (Kassardjian and colleagues, 2005). The cortex acts as a fast teacher; the brainstem synapses, which receive both the vestibular input and the learned Purkinje signal, learn more slowly from their coincidence and retain the memory (Medina and Mauk, 2000, made the same point for eyeblink conditioning). Increases and decreases of the gain use partly different mechanisms (Boyden and Raymond, 2003), and optogenetic stimulation of climbing fibers or Purkinje cells paired with head movement is sufficient to induce learning in a direction set by the timing of the stimulation (Nguyen-Vu and colleagues, 2013).

The figure shows the two-site scheme in the adaptive filter model. The cortical weights change quickly and bring the gain to its new value within minutes; the brainstem gain then increases slowly, driven by the coincidence of vestibular input and Purkinje output, and the cerebellar contribution shrinks as the brainstem takes over. A lesion of the flocculus early in training removes most of the learned gain, a lesion late in training little. The scheme is a mechanistic version of the fast and slow processes of the two-state model of adaptation (Formula 29.6.1). It also explains a paradox noted in [7.14.45](/7.14.45#sec-45.3.6): mice lacking parallel fiber LTD can still adapt (Schonewille and colleagues, 2011), because other plastic sites, LTP of parallel fiber synapses, plasticity of interneuron synapses and of the nuclei, compensate; the cerebellum distributes its memory over several synapses.

## 33.4. Gaze Control

### 33.4.1. Saccades and the Pulse–Step Command

Eye movements are the best understood motor system: the eye has three pairs of muscles, a fixed load and no reflexes from muscle spindles, and the firing of every class of neurons involved has been recorded in monkeys. Their types (fixation, **saccades**, **smooth pursuit**, vergence, the vestibulo-ocular and optokinetic reflexes) are described in [7.14.41](/7.14.41#sec-41.5.2). Saccades are stereotyped: their duration increases linearly with amplitude, by about $2$–$3\,\mathrm{ms}$ per degree plus about $20\,\mathrm{ms}$, and their peak velocity rises with amplitude and saturates at $500$–$700^\circ\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$, the *main sequence* (Bahill, Clark and Stark, 1975). Because they are too brief for visual feedback, saccades are programmed in advance, and the program can be read in the firing of ocular motor neurons.

**Formula 33.4.1 (Eye Plant and Pulse–Step Command)** To a first approximation, the eye with its muscles and orbital tissues is a first-order viscoelastic system with time constant $\tau_{\mathrm{p}} \approx 0.2\,\mathrm{s}$: $\tau_{\mathrm{p}}\dot\theta = -\theta + u$, where $\theta$ is eye position and $u$ the motor command expressed as an equivalent position. To move the eye quickly to $\theta_1$ and hold it there, motor neurons must fire as
$$
u(t) = \theta(t) + \tau_{\mathrm{p}}\dot\theta(t), \tag{33.3}
$$
a high-frequency *pulse* proportional to eye velocity that overcomes the viscosity during the saccade, followed by a *step* proportional to the new position that overcomes the elasticity (Robinson, 1964, 1970). Measured abducens motor neurons fire at $R = R_0 + k\theta + r\dot\theta$, with $k \approx 4$ spikes per second per degree and $r \approx 1$ spike per degree.

The pulse is produced by excitatory burst neurons in the paramedian pontine reticular formation (horizontal saccades) and the rostral interstitial nucleus of the medial longitudinal fasciculus (vertical saccades), together with inhibitory burst neurons that silence the antagonist motor neurons. Between saccades the burst neurons are held silent by omnipause neurons in the pontine midline, which pause during every saccade; their inhibition gates the burst generator. The step is the time integral of the pulse, computed by a separate population, the neural integrator. Robinson (1975) proposed that the burst neurons are driven by the motor error, the difference between the desired eye position and an internal estimate of the current one obtained from the integrator, an **efference copy**: the saccade stops when the error reaches zero, which makes it accurate despite variations of the burst rate. The saturating relation between motor error and burst rate produces the main sequence (figure below, C). Upstream, the deep layers of the superior colliculus contain a motor map in which the site of activity specifies the vector of the saccade, and the saccade is the average of the vectors of the active population (Lee, Rohrer and Sparks, 1988); the colliculus is driven by the frontal eye field and the lateral intraparietal area and disinhibited by the **basal ganglia**, which release it from tonic inhibition by the substantia nigra pars reticulata ([7.16.40](/7.16.40)).

### 33.4.2. The Neural Integrator

**Definition 33.4.1 (Neural Integrator)** The **neural integrator** of the oculomotor system is a population of brainstem neurons (for horizontal movements, in the nucleus prepositus hypoglossi and the medial vestibular nucleus; for vertical and torsional ones, in the interstitial nucleus of Cajal) that converts velocity commands, the saccadic pulse and the vestibular and pursuit signals, into a persistent firing rate proportional to eye position, by mathematically integrating them over time; it holds eye position between movements.

The integrator is shared by all types of eye movement, since the vestibular canals and the pursuit system also signal velocity. Its quality is measured by its time constant: after an eccentric saccade in the dark, the eye drifts back toward the center with a time constant of $20\,\mathrm{s}$ or more in normal monkeys and humans. Lesions of the integrator nuclei reduce this to the plant time constant of about $0.2\,\mathrm{s}$: saccades still occur, but the eye slides back immediately (Cannon and Robinson, 1987). A leaky integrator produces gaze-evoked **nystagmus**, a centripetal drift interrupted by corrective saccades back to the target (figure below, B), which is a sign of cerebellar or brainstem disease; lesions of the flocculus shorten the time constant to about $1$–$2\,\mathrm{s}$, showing that the cerebellum calibrates the integrator (Zee and colleagues, 1981).

How can neurons with membrane and synaptic time constants of at most $100\,\mathrm{ms}$ integrate over tens of seconds? Positive feedback through recurrent connections can lengthen the time constant of a network (Cannon, Robinson and Shamma, 1983; Seung, 1996). For a linear rate network $\tau\dot{\boldsymbol{r}} = -\boldsymbol{r} + W\boldsymbol{r} + \boldsymbol{b}\,v(t)$ driven by the velocity command $v$, the mode along an eigenvector $\boldsymbol{e}_1$ of $W$ with eigenvalue $\lambda_1$ decays with time constant $\tau/(1 - \lambda_1)$ (Proposition 15.2.1 of [7.16.15](/7.16.15#sec-15.2.1)); for $\lambda_1 = 1$ it integrates its input exactly, and the network has a continuum of fixed points.

**Definition 33.4.2 (Line Attractor)** A **line attractor** is a one-dimensional continuum of stable fixed points of a dynamical system, attracting nearby states in all other directions but neutrally stable along the line; a state on it persists without input, and inputs along the line move it from one fixed point to another. In the oculomotor integrator model, position along the line encodes eye position.

The attraction in the other directions makes the representation robust to noise orthogonal to the line, but the line itself requires fine tuning. A time constant of $20\,\mathrm{s}$ with $\tau = 100\,\mathrm{ms}$ requires $\lvert 1 - \lambda_1\rvert < 0.005$ (figure below, D): a $1\,\%$ change of the feedback strength turns the integrator into a leaky or an unstable one. In goldfish, which have a small and accessible integrator (area I of the hindbrain), the firing of integrator neurons is proportional to eye position above a threshold, with thresholds spread across the population, and persistent firing depends on recurrent excitation within the same side and on inhibition from the other side (Aksay, Gamkrelidze, Seung, Baker and Tank, 2001; Aksay and colleagues, 2007). Seung, Lee, Reis and Tank (2000) fitted a model of the goldfish circuit and showed that its persistence could be retuned by the visual feedback that the cerebellum provides, and pharmacological manipulations that change the effective feedback produce the predicted leaky and unstable drifts. Proposed remedies for the fine-tuning problem include continuous learning from retinal slip, intrinsic cellular bistability that makes the attractor a staircase of many discrete states, and negative-derivative feedback (Lim and Goldman, 2013). The same theory of continuous attractors, extended to rings and planes, models head direction and spatial **working memory** in [7.16.44](/7.16.44) and [7.16.45](/7.16.45).

## 33.5. The Cerebellum as Forward Model and Timer

The adaptive filter learns to produce whatever signal the climbing fiber error calls for. In the VOR it acts as part of an inverse model, computing the command that cancels head motion. The same circuit can learn to predict, and many cerebellar functions are best described as those of a forward model (Definition 29.3.1) that predicts the sensory consequences of motor commands from their **efference copy**, arriving by mossy fibers from the pontine nuclei. Neurons in the rostral fastigial nucleus of monkeys respond to passive rotation of the head but much less to the same rotation produced actively, because a prediction of the vestibular consequences is subtracted; when a new relation between command and consequence is imposed, the cancellation fails at first and is relearned within tens of trials (Brooks, Carriot and Cullen, 2015). In weakly electric fish, cerebellum-like electrosensory lobes learn a "negative image" of the electrical consequences of the fish's own discharge by anti-Hebbian plasticity at parallel fiber synapses and subtract it from the input, so that external objects stand out (Bell, 1981; Kennedy and colleagues, 2014). Purkinje cell simple spikes in monkeys encode the predicted kinematics of the hand, leading the movement, while complex spikes encode errors, consistent with a forward model that is updated by its errors (Streng, Popa and Ebner, 2018).

Several symptoms of cerebellar disease follow from the loss of such prediction. Dysmetria and intention tremor ([7.14.45](/7.14.45#sec-45.3.1)) are the overshoot and oscillation of a limb controlled by delayed feedback alone, the instability of Proposition 29.2.1. Ataxia of multijoint movements reflects a failure to predict and compensate the interaction torques between joints (Bastian, Martin, Keating and Thach, 1996). Saccade adaptation, which keeps saccades accurate when the target is moved during the saccade (McLaughlin, 1967), or when eye muscles weaken, depends on the oculomotor vermis and the caudal fastigial nucleus and is abolished by their lesions (Optican and Robinson, 1980). Patients with cerebellar lesions are also impaired in judging and producing time intervals in the range of hundreds of milliseconds (Ivry and Keele, 1989), consistent with a timing function of the granule cell basis.

## 33.6. The Cerebellum and Cognition

Most of the human cerebellum is connected with nonmotor cortex. Transneuronal tracing in monkeys shows that the cerebellum forms closed loops with prefrontal as well as motor cortex: a prefrontal area projects through the pons to a part of the cerebellar cortex that projects back to the same prefrontal area through the dentate nucleus and thalamus (Kelly and Strick, 2003). **Functional connectivity** maps of the human cerebellum contain a representation of every major cortical network, with the motor representation occupying only a small fraction and most of the hemispheres coupled to association networks (Buckner, Krienen, Castellanos, Diaz and Yeo, 2011). Lesions of the posterior lobe cause the cerebellar cognitive affective syndrome, impairing executive function, spatial cognition, language and the regulation of affect (Schmahmann and Sherman, 1998). Cerebellar damage early in life is among the largest single risk factors for **autism spectrum disorder**, which has led to the proposal that the cerebellum shapes the development of the cortical circuits with which it forms loops ([7.16.52](/7.16.52); Wang, Kloth and Badura, 2014). In mice, cerebellar granule cells signal reward expectation, and a direct projection from the cerebellar nuclei to the ventral tegmental area supports reward and social behavior (Carta, Chen, Schott, Dorizan and Khodakhah, 2019). If the cerebellar circuit performs the same computation everywhere, the hypothesis of a universal cerebellar transform, then it may provide prediction and error-based calibration for thought as it does for movement, but what the "errors" are outside the motor system remains an open question.

- The cerebellum contains most neurons of the brain; mossy fiber input diverges onto granule cells, each with about four inputs, which converge by parallel fibers onto Purkinje cells; each Purkinje cell has one climbing fiber.
- Cover's theorem (Theorem 33.1.1) shows why nonlinear expansion helps a linear readout: a perceptron separates about $2n$ random patterns in $n$ dimensions; coding level and the number of inputs per granule cell set the benefit.
- The Marr–Albus–Ito theory (Definition 33.2.1) treats the Purkinje cell as a perceptron trained by climbing fiber errors; capacity analysis predicts, and recordings confirm, that most parallel fiber synapses are silent. The adaptive filter (Formula 33.2.1) extends the theory to time-varying signals.
- VOR adaptation is driven by retinal slip; learning starts in the flocculus and is consolidated in the vestibular nuclei, a two-site scheme that parallels the fast and slow processes of adaptation.
- Saccades are generated by a pulse–step command (Formula 33.4.1) from burst neurons gated by omnipause neurons and driven by motor error; the step comes from a neural integrator (Definition 33.4.1), modeled as a line attractor (Definition 33.4.2) whose time constant $\tau/\lvert1 - \lambda\rvert$ requires fine tuning, which the cerebellum provides.
- The cerebellum also acts as a forward model that cancels self-generated sensation, supports timing, and participates through closed loops in cognition and affect.

## Questions

1. Using Eq. (33.1), compute the fraction of the dichotomies of $P = 6$ points in general position in $\mathbb{R}^3$ (hyperplanes through the origin) that are linearly separable. What happens if the points are first mapped nonlinearly into $\mathbb{R}^6$?

   **Answer** $C(6, 3) = 2\bigl[\binom50 + \binom51 + \binom52\bigr] = 2(1 + 5 + 10) = 32$, out of $2^6 = 64$: exactly half, as expected for $P = 2n$. If the six points are mapped nonlinearly into $\mathbb{R}^6$ so that their images are in general position, then $P \le n$ and all 64 dichotomies are separable. A linear map would not help, because the images would lie in a three-dimensional subspace and the count would remain 32; the nonlinearity of the granule cells is essential.

2. In the VOR, why must the error signal reach the cerebellum by a pathway (climbing fibers) separate from the signals to be associated (mossy fibers)? What would go wrong if the retinal slip were simply added to the mossy fiber input?

   **Answer** A supervised learning rule needs to correlate the input signals with an error, $\mathrm{d}w_j/\mathrm{d}t \propto -s\,p_j$; if the error were just another input, the Purkinje cell could not tell which signals to change and in which direction, and Hebbian plasticity on the combined input would strengthen whatever was correlated with the cell's firing, not reduce the error. The separate climbing fiber system, which produces a distinctive complex spike and gates plasticity, provides the third factor that labels the error (compare the **three-factor learning rule** of [7.16.14](/7.16.14)). Retinal slip does also reach the cerebellum by mossy fibers, where it serves as a sensory context signal and drives visual following, but it does not by itself determine the direction of learning.

3. An integrator network has a single-neuron time constant of $50\,\mathrm{ms}$. What feedback eigenvalue gives a time constant of $10\,\mathrm{s}$? If the feedback is reduced by $2\,\%$, what is the new time constant, and what will a patient see after looking $20^\circ$ to the side in the dark?

   **Answer** $\tau/(1 - \lambda) = 10\,\mathrm{s}$ gives $1 - \lambda = 0.005$, $\lambda = 0.995$. Reducing the feedback by $2\,\%$ gives $\lambda = 0.995\times0.98 = 0.975$ and a time constant of $0.05/0.025 = 2\,\mathrm{s}$. After an eccentric saccade the eye drifts back toward the center exponentially with this time constant, at an initial speed of $20^\circ/2\,\mathrm{s} = 10^\circ\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$; in the light, corrective saccades bring it back to the target repeatedly, producing gaze-evoked nystagmus with the fast phase toward the target, the eccentric side.

4. In the two-site model of VOR learning, predict the effect of inactivating the flocculus (a) immediately after one hour of training, (b) one day later. How could one distinguish this model from one in which the memory stays in the cerebellar cortex but decays slowly?

   **Answer** (a) Most of the learned change disappears, because it is still carried by the cortical weights; (b) most of it remains, because it has been transferred to the brainstem synapses. In a cortex-only model, inactivation at either time would remove the learned change completely (or whatever had not yet decayed). Further tests: record floccular target neurons in the vestibular nuclei and look for a change of their response to vestibular input, which the two-site model predicts to develop over hours; block plasticity in the nuclei pharmacologically or genetically and expect learning without consolidation; or train with gain-up and gain-down and compare the time courses of transfer, which the experiments suggest differ.

5. (Open question) The Marr–Albus–Ito theory gives the cerebellum a single computation. What evidence supports a universal cerebellar computation across motor and cognitive functions, and what would count against it?

   **Answer** For: the uniform cytoarchitecture of the cerebellar cortex, the closed loops with nearly every cortical area, the parallel between motor and cognitive symptoms (dysmetria of movement and "dysmetria of thought" in Schmahmann's description), and the presence of prediction and error signals in many regions. Against, or at least complicating: regional differences in Purkinje cell physiology and plasticity between zebrin-positive and zebrin-negative zones, dense rather than sparse granule cell codes in some regions, the evidence that learning involves several plastic sites whose roles may vary, and the lack of a clear definition of the error signal for cognitive functions. A decisive test would identify what climbing fibers signal in a cognitive task and whether the same plasticity rule, applied to the same kind of granule cell basis, explains the learning.
