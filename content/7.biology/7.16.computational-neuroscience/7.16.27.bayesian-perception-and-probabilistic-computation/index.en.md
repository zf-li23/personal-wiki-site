Chapter [7.16.26](/7.16.26) treated decoding as inference by an experimenter who knows the stimulus and wants to know how much information the neurons carry. This chapter takes the point of view of the brain, which has only the sensory evidence and must decide what is out there. Sensory evidence is noisy and ambiguous: the same retinal image is produced by many scenes, the same sound by different sources, and each measurement is corrupted by neural noise. The proposal of Helmholtz, that perception is an unconscious inference from sensory data to their likely causes, has become a precise quantitative theory through **Bayesian inference** ([3.2.7](/3.2.7), [3.7.1](/3.7.1)). It predicts how the percept should depend on the reliability of the evidence, on previous experience and on the structure of the environment, and these predictions can be tested in psychophysics and, to some extent, in neural recordings. We follow the theory from its simplest consequences, the weighting of cues by their reliability and the bias towards the prior, to the question of how a population of spiking neurons could represent and compute with probability distributions, and end with the criticisms of the framework. The connection to the state estimation of the motor system, which combines prediction and sensation in the same way, is made in [7.16.29](/7.16.29).

Throughout the chapter the state of the world is $s$ and the sensory measurement (internal representation) of a cue is $x$, with likelihood $p(x \mid s)$, usually normal, $x \sim \mathcal{N}(s, \sigma^2)$. The prior is $p(s)$ and the posterior $p(s \mid x)$. Reliability, or precision, of a cue is $1/\sigma^2$.

## 27.1. Perception as Inference

### 27.1.1. The Bayesian Observer

The observer's problem is the one of Section 26.1: infer $s$ from $x$ using **Bayes' theorem**. Two ingredients are needed beyond the data: a model of how the measurement is generated from the world, and a prior over worlds.

**Definition 27.1.1 (Bayesian Observer)** A **Bayesian observer** holds a generative model, that is a prior $p(s)$ over states of the world and a likelihood $p(x \mid s)$ of the sensory measurement, computes the posterior $p(s \mid x) \propto p(x \mid s)\,p(s)$, and reports a percept or a decision that minimizes the expected cost under the posterior: the posterior mean for squared error, the posterior mode (the **maximum a posteriori estimate**) for a hit-or-miss cost.

Normally distributed priors and likelihoods, which are conjugate ([3.7.4](/3.7.4)), give closed-form answers that capture most of the qualitative behavior, and are used throughout this chapter.

**Theorem 27.1.1 (Gaussian Posterior)** Let the prior be $s \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_0, \sigma_0^2)$ and the measurement $x \mid s \sim \mathcal{N}(s, \sigma^2)$. Then the posterior is normal, $s \mid x \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_{\mathrm{post}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{post}}^2)$, with

$$
\mu_{\mathrm{post}} = \frac{x/\sigma^2 + \mu_0/\sigma_0^2}{1/\sigma^2 + 1/\sigma_0^2} = \mu_0 + k\,(x - \mu_0),\qquad \frac{1}{\sigma_{\mathrm{post}}^2} = \frac{1}{\sigma^2} + \frac{1}{\sigma_0^2}, \tag{27.1}
$$
where $k = \sigma_0^2/(\sigma_0^2 + \sigma^2)$.

**Proof** The log posterior is, up to a constant, $-\frac{(x - s)^2}{2\sigma^2} - \frac{(s - \mu_0)^2}{2\sigma_0^2}$, a quadratic function of $s$ with leading coefficient $-\frac12(1/\sigma^2 + 1/\sigma_0^2)$; completing the square gives a normal distribution with the stated precision and mean. $\square$

Equation (27.1) states two things. The posterior mean is a *precision-weighted average* of the measurement and the prior mean, and the precisions add: the posterior is always narrower than either the likelihood or the prior. The estimate is *biased towards the prior*: for a fixed true $s$ the expectation of $\mu_{\mathrm{post}}$ is $s + (1 - k)(\mu_0 - s)$, with a variance $k^2\sigma^2$ that is smaller than $\sigma^2$. The observer trades bias for variance, and the trade is advantageous in terms of the mean squared error (the shrinkage of [3.7.2](/3.7.2)); the stronger the noise, the larger the bias. This property is what the experiments of Sections 27.3 and 27.4 exploit: the biases of perception, which look like errors, are the signature of the prior.

## 27.2. Cue Integration

### 27.2.1. Optimal Combination of Two Cues

Most properties of the world can be sensed in more than one way: the size of an object by sight and by touch, the position of a sound by hearing and sight, the direction of self-motion by the visual flow and by the vestibular organs. Combining such measurements into a single estimate is *cue integration*. With a flat prior, two independent measurements $x_1$ and $x_2$ with variances $\sigma_1^2$ and $\sigma_2^2$ give a likelihood that is the product of two normal likelihoods.

**Theorem 27.2.1 (Optimal Cue Combination)** For independent Gaussian cues $x_i \sim \mathcal{N}(s, \sigma_i^2)$, $i = 1, 2$, and a flat prior, the posterior is normal with mean and variance

$$
\hat s = w_1x_1 + w_2x_2,\quad w_i = \frac{1/\sigma_i^2}{1/\sigma_1^2 + 1/\sigma_2^2},\qquad \sigma_{12}^2 = \frac{\sigma_1^2\sigma_2^2}{\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2}. \tag{27.2}
$$
The combined estimate is the minimum-variance unbiased linear combination of the cues, its variance is smaller than $\min(\sigma_1^2, \sigma_2^2)$, and equals $\sigma^2/2$ when the cues are equally reliable.

**Proof** Apply Theorem 27.1.1 with the first cue as the measurement and the second as the "prior" $\mathcal{N}(x_2, \sigma_2^2)$ (a flat prior with the product of Gaussians), or multiply the likelihoods directly. For the second statement, minimizing $w^2\sigma_1^2 + (1 - w)^2\sigma_2^2$ over $w$ (the unbiased combinations have weights summing to one) gives $w_1$ of Eq. (27.2). $\square$

The theory predicts the weights, the variance and the bias of the combined percept from measurements made with each cue *alone*, with no free parameter. Ernst and Banks (2002) tested it with a visual-haptic task. Subjects judged the height of a ridge, which they saw as a pattern of dots in stereo and felt with a force-feedback device; the visual cue could be degraded by adding noise to the positions of the dots. From the unimodal discrimination thresholds, the authors predicted the weight of vision in the combined condition (Eq. (27.2)) and measured it from the point of subjective equality of stimuli in which the visual and haptic heights were put in conflict. The measured weights followed the prediction as the visual noise rose from $0$ to $200\,\%$, with vision dominant when it was reliable and touch when it was not. The discrimination threshold with both cues was close to the predicted $\sigma_{12}$ and below that of the better cue (figure).

The same weighting explains classic illusions as optimal percepts. In the *ventriloquist effect* a sound appears to come from the moving mouth of a puppet because vision localizes far more precisely than hearing, so the weight of vision is large and the percept moves to the visual position. Alais and Burr (2004) showed that if the visual stimulus is blurred so that its reliability falls below that of the sound, the effect reverses: the sound captures the visual location. Weights adjust within a trial to the momentary reliability of each cue, which the system must therefore also represent (Section 27.5).

### 27.2.2. Neural Correlates

In the macaque, neurons in the dorsal medial superior temporal area (MSTd) and the ventral intraparietal area respond to both visual optic flow and vestibular signals produced by self-motion, and are tuned to heading direction. Gu, Angelaki and DeAngelis (2008) recorded from MSTd neurons while the animal discriminated heading with visual cues, with vestibular cues, and with both. Neurons whose visual and vestibular preferred headings were congruent had greater sensitivity in the combined condition, close to the optimal prediction of Eq. (27.2), while neurons with opposite preferences had less; the animals' thresholds were also close to the optimal prediction. Morgan, DeAngelis and Angelaki (2008) showed that the response of a neuron to the combined stimulus is well approximated by a weighted sum of its responses to the two cues alone, with the weights shifting from the visual to the vestibular as the visual coherence falls. These findings show that a weighted sum of **firing rates**, with weights depending on reliability, can implement cue combination; Section 27.5 shows why a linear sum is exactly right for populations of Poisson-like neurons.

### 27.2.3. Causal Inference: When to Combine

Equation (27.2) assumes that the two cues come from the same source. When the sound and the light are far apart in space the observer should not fuse them: they are probably caused by different objects, and combining them would produce a false percept between the two. The brain must therefore also infer *whether* the cues share a cause.

**Definition 27.2.1 (Causal Inference Model)** In the **causal inference model** (Körding et al., 2007) the observer receives the measurements $x_{\mathrm{v}}$ and $x_{\mathrm{a}}$ of a visual and an auditory position and considers two hypotheses: $C = 1$, one source $s \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_0, \sigma_0^2)$ produced both measurements, and $C = 2$, two independent sources $s_{\mathrm{v}}, s_{\mathrm{a}}$, both from the same prior, produced one each. The prior probability of a common cause is $p_{\mathrm{c}}$.

With cue noise $\sigma_{\mathrm{v}}, \sigma_{\mathrm{a}}$, integrating the unknown sources out gives the likelihoods

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb{P}(x_{\mathrm{v}}, x_{\mathrm{a}} \mid C = 1) &= \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{V}}\exp\Bigl[-\frac{(x_{\mathrm{v}} - x_{\mathrm{a}})^2\sigma_0^2 + (x_{\mathrm{v}} - \mu_0)^2\sigma_{\mathrm{a}}^2 + (x_{\mathrm{a}} - \mu_0)^2\sigma_{\mathrm{v}}^2}{2V}\Bigr],\\
\mathbb{P}(x_{\mathrm{v}}, x_{\mathrm{a}} \mid C = 2) &= \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{(\sigma_{\mathrm{v}}^2 + \sigma_0^2)(\sigma_{\mathrm{a}}^2 + \sigma_0^2)}}\exp\Bigl[-\frac{(x_{\mathrm{v}} - \mu_0)^2}{2(\sigma_{\mathrm{v}}^2 + \sigma_0^2)} - \frac{(x_{\mathrm{a}} - \mu_0)^2}{2(\sigma_{\mathrm{a}}^2 + \sigma_0^2)}\Bigr],
\end{aligned} \tag{27.3}
$$
with $V = \sigma_{\mathrm{v}}^2\sigma_{\mathrm{a}}^2 + \sigma_{\mathrm{v}}^2\sigma_0^2 + \sigma_{\mathrm{a}}^2\sigma_0^2$, and the posterior probability of a common cause is $\mathbb{P}(C = 1 \mid x) = p_{\mathrm{c}}\mathbb{P}(x \mid C = 1)\big/\bigl[p_{\mathrm{c}}\mathbb{P}(x \mid C = 1) + (1 - p_{\mathrm{c}})\mathbb{P}(x \mid C = 2)\bigr]$. The optimal estimate of the auditory position under squared error is the model average

$$
\hat s_{\mathrm{a}} = \mathbb{P}(C = 1 \mid x)\,\hat s_{C=1} + \mathbb{P}(C = 2 \mid x)\,\hat s_{\mathrm{a},C=2}, \tag{27.4}
$$
where $\hat s_{C=1} = \dfrac{x_{\mathrm{v}}/\sigma_{\mathrm{v}}^2 + x_{\mathrm{a}}/\sigma_{\mathrm{a}}^2 + \mu_0/\sigma_0^2}{1/\sigma_{\mathrm{v}}^2 + 1/\sigma_{\mathrm{a}}^2 + 1/\sigma_0^2}$ is the fused estimate and $\hat s_{\mathrm{a},C=2}$ the auditory-only estimate of Theorem 27.1.1.

When the two measurements are close, $\mathbb{P}(C = 1 \mid x)$ is large and the auditory estimate is drawn to the visual one; when they differ by more than the combined noise, the evidence for a common cause collapses and the sound is localized by itself. The influence of vision on the auditory percept first rises with the audiovisual disparity and then falls again (figure), as measured for the ventriloquist effect: a prediction that a model with forced fusion, whose influence grows without bound, cannot make. Körding et al. fitted the model to the localization of paired flashes and bursts of noise and found it superior to models that always or never fuse. Causal inference generalizes to any problem in which the observer must decide which sensory events belong together: the grouping of features into objects, and the binding of a sound to a visible speaker.

## 27.3. Priors and Biases

### 27.3.1. The Slow-Speed Prior

A prior is a statement about the statistics of the world, and perception should be biased towards the stimuli that occur most often. Among visual motions, slow movements are more frequent than fast ones: objects at rest in the world move slowly on the retina, and the retinal motion of a stable scene is only that of the eye. Weiss, Simoncelli and Adelson (2002) proposed that the visual system uses a prior on velocity that is centered on zero.

Equation (27.1) then predicts a *contrast dependence*: at low contrast the measurement is noisier, $\sigma$ is larger, the shrinkage factor $k = \sigma_0^2/(\sigma_0^2 + \sigma^2)$ falls, and the perceived speed is reduced (figure). This matches the observation that a low-contrast pattern appears to move more slowly than a high-contrast pattern at the same speed, and that an object seen in fog or at night seems to move more slowly (in simulated fog, drivers underestimate their own speed; Snowden et al., 1998). The same prior also accounts for the misperceived direction of low-contrast moving lines and plaids, as the prior on two-dimensional velocity combined with the aperture problem. Stocker and Simoncelli (2006) turned the argument around: from psychophysical measurements at several contrasts they estimated the shape of the prior, without assuming it, and found a prior that is approximately a power law of speed, with heavier tails than a Gaussian, which falls with increasing speed.

### 27.3.2. Central Tendency

A second, ubiquitous bias is the regression of judgments towards the mean of the stimuli presented in an experiment, the *central tendency of judgment* described by Hollingworth in 1910: small magnitudes are overestimated and large ones underestimated. In a Bayesian account the observer has learned the distribution of the stimuli in the experiment and uses it as a prior. Jazayeri and Shadlen (2010) trained participants to reproduce time intervals drawn from a short or a long range; the reproduced intervals regressed toward the middle of the range, so that the same interval was reproduced differently depending on the range in which it was embedded. The noise of the internal measurement of time scales with the interval, $\sigma = w\,t$ with a Weber fraction $w \approx 0.1$ (scalar variability, Weber's law for time; compare the **Weber–Fechner law**), so the regression is larger for long intervals, where the measurement is less reliable. The posterior mean under a uniform prior and scalar noise reproduces this pattern (panel C of the figure), with a single free parameter, the Weber fraction, that can be measured in a separate experiment.

## 27.4. Serial Dependence

Perception also depends on what was seen a moment ago. Fischer and Whitney (2014) asked observers to reproduce the orientation of a briefly presented grating on each trial by turning a response bar. The reports were pulled towards the orientation seen on the *previous* trials, up to several seconds earlier, though the stimuli were independent. This dependence of the current percept on recent stimuli is called *serial dependence*. The bias was a function of the difference $\Delta = s_{\mathrm{prev}} - s$ with the shape of the first derivative of a Gaussian: zero when the two orientations were equal, growing for differences up to about $20^\circ$, and falling to zero for large differences. Attractive serial dependence has been found for orientation, position, numerosity, faces, facial identity and attractiveness, and is stronger when the current stimulus is uncertain and when the previous stimulus was attended.

A Bayesian account follows from the continuity of the world: the visual scene changes slowly, and the stimulus of the previous moment is a useful prior for the present one, but only if the two belong to the same object or scene. Let the prior over the current stimulus be a mixture,

$$
p(s \mid s_{\mathrm{prev}}) = (1 - q)\,p_0(s) + q\,\mathcal{N}(s;\, s_{\mathrm{prev}}, \tau^2), \tag{27.5}
$$
a broad background prior $p_0$ with probability $1 - q$, and with probability $q$ a narrow distribution around the previous stimulus. For a Gaussian measurement $x$ with variance $\sigma^2$ the posterior is again a mixture, with weight on the continuity component proportional to $q\,\mathcal{N}(x;\, s_{\mathrm{prev}}, \sigma^2 + \tau^2)$, and the posterior mean is the weighted average of the estimates of the two components (as in Eq. (27.4)). If the measurement is close to $s_{\mathrm{prev}}$ the continuity component dominates and the estimate is pulled towards $s_{\mathrm{prev}}$; if it is far from it, the component has negligible weight and the prior has no effect. The bias as a function of $\Delta$ therefore rises with $\Delta$, peaks at a difference of the order of $\sqrt{\sigma^2 + \tau^2}$ and decays to zero, the derivative-of-Gaussian shape (figure). It is larger for noisier measurements, which agrees with the experimental dependence on stimulus uncertainty.

A: the error of 3000 simulated trials against the difference between the previous and the current orientation (σ = 8°), with the binned mean (orange): attraction for small differences, none for large ones. B: the mean error for σ = 4°, 8° and 16°, the derivative-of-Gaussian curves of Fischer and Whitney; the bias grows with the measurement noise](./plots/serial-dependence.py)

What mechanism produces the bias in the brain? Two kinds of proposal have been tested. In one, the previous stimulus remains in a latent memory trace: Barbosa et al. (2020) found, in monkey prefrontal cortex and in a network model, that a short-term synaptic trace of the previous item ([7.16.12](/7.16.12)) persists while spiking is silent and biases the activity evoked by the next item during working memory ([7.16.45](/7.16.45)). In the other, serial dependence is sensory: adaptation at the level of the early visual cortex produces a *repulsive* bias at short intervals, and the attractive effect is a later, higher-level process; the two effects have different time courses and stimulus dependencies and combine in the observed bias. The Bayesian account does not choose between them; it says what computation the mechanism has to implement, and predicts the dependence on uncertainty, which any mechanism must reproduce.

## 27.5. Probabilities in Neural Populations

Bayesian perception requires the brain to represent more than a single estimate: the weights of Eq. (27.2) depend on the reliability of each cue, and the posterior is a distribution. How can a population of neurons represent a probability distribution, and how can it carry out the multiplication and normalization of Bayes' theorem? Two classes of proposals exist, differing in what the variability of the neurons means.

### 27.5.1. Probabilistic Population Codes

In the probabilistic population code of Ma, Beck, Latham and Pouget (2006), the variability of the neurons is noise that the decoder must take into account, and the likelihood function of the stimulus is read out from the population: the response $\boldsymbol{r}$ determines the likelihood $\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{r} \mid s)$, and the posterior follows. Take the Poisson population of Section 26.3 with Gaussian tuning and common gain $g$, $f_i(s) = g\exp[-(s - s_i)^2/2\sigma_f^2]$, with preferred values that tile the stimulus space evenly.

**Definition 27.5.1 (Probabilistic Population Code)** A **probabilistic population code** is a **population code** in which the response $\boldsymbol{r}$ represents a whole distribution over the stimulus, the likelihood $\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{r} \mid s)$ (and with a prior the posterior), rather than a single estimate. For Poisson-like variability, $\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{r} \mid s) = \phi(\boldsymbol{r})\exp[\boldsymbol{h}(s)^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol{r} - a(s)]$, the log likelihood is a linear function of the responses; for independent Poisson neurons $h_i(s) = \ln f_i(s)$ and $a(s) = \sum_i f_i(s)$. The response is then a set of weights on the basis functions $h_i(s)$ from which the likelihood of every stimulus is computed.

**Theorem 27.5.1 (Gain Encodes Reliability)** For independent Poisson neurons with Gaussian **tuning curves** of common width $\sigma_f$ and evenly spaced preferred values, the likelihood of $s$ for the response $\boldsymbol{r}$ is a normal function of $s$ with mean $\hat s = \sum_i r_is_i/\sum_i r_i$ and variance $\sigma_f^2/\sum_i r_i$.

**Proof** This is Eq. (26.5) and the curvature of Eq. (26.4) with $\ln f_i(s) = \ln g - (s - s_i)^2/2\sigma_f^2$ and $\sum_if_i(s)$ independent of $s$: $\ln\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{r} \mid s) = -\sum_ir_i(s - s_i)^2/2\sigma_f^2 + \mathrm{const}$, a quadratic function of $s$ with maximum at $\hat s$ and curvature $\sum_ir_i/\sigma_f^2$. $\square$

The precision of the likelihood is proportional to the *total number of spikes* in the population. A stimulus of higher contrast, or more attention, which raises the gain, is automatically represented with a narrower likelihood, so the reliability of a cue is carried by the amplitude of the population response, with no separate signal (panel C of the figure below verifies that the variance of the estimate across trials equals the width of the likelihood read out from the total count). The same principle explains the multiplication of cues.

**Theorem 27.5.2 (Cue Combination by Summing Responses)** Let two populations with identical tuning curves $f_i(s)$ (up to the gain) receive two independent cues about $s$, and let their responses be $\boldsymbol{r}^{\mathrm{a}}$ and $\boldsymbol{r}^{\mathrm{b}}$. A third population whose response is the sum $\boldsymbol{r} = \boldsymbol{r}^{\mathrm{a}} + \boldsymbol{r}^{\mathrm{b}}$ has the likelihood $\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{r} \mid s) \propto \mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{r}^{\mathrm{a}} \mid s)\,\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{r}^{\mathrm{b}} \mid s)$, the product of the likelihoods of the cues.

**Proof** In the form of Eq. (26.4) the log likelihoods are linear in the responses: $\ln\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{r}^{\mathrm{a}} \mid s) + \ln\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{r}^{\mathrm{b}} \mid s) = \sum_i(r_i^{\mathrm{a}} + r_i^{\mathrm{b}})\ln f_i(s) - \sum_if^{\mathrm{a}}_i(s) - \sum_if_i^{\mathrm{b}}(s) + \mathrm{const}$, which is the log likelihood of the population with response $\boldsymbol{r}$ and gain equal to the sum of the gains (the terms $\sum f_i$ being independent of $s$ for evenly spaced tuning curves). $\square$

Combining cues therefore needs only the addition of spike counts, which a neuron does naturally by summing synaptic inputs, in agreement with the linear combination of responses found in MSTd (Section 27.2.2). The figure shows two populations of different gains and the summed population, whose likelihood coincides with the product of the two. The weights of Eq. (27.2) emerge from the unequal numbers of spikes, since the population with more spikes is more reliable and contributes more to the sum. Poisson-like variability, in which the variance is proportional to the mean, is the key assumption, and the response of cortical neurons is often well described by it ([7.16.16](/7.16.16#sec-16.4.3)). Nonlinear computations, such as marginalization over nuisance variables, require nonlinear operations on the population, for which **divisive normalization** ([7.16.23](/7.16.23#sec-23.4.2)) is a candidate.

### 27.5.2. The Sampling Hypothesis

In the alternative proposal, neural variability is not noise but the *representation* of uncertainty: the activity of the network at each moment is a sample from the posterior distribution (Hoyer and Hyvärinen, 2003; Fiser et al., 2010). The probability of a value of $s$ is the fraction of time the network spends near it, so that a narrow posterior produces little variability and a broad one a large variability; a multimodal posterior is represented by activity that wanders among several states, which a population code with a single peak cannot do. Such a code would need the dynamics of the network to implement a sampler of Markov chain Monte Carlo type ([3.5.9](/3.5.9)), which a recurrent network of stochastic neurons can do (Pecevski et al., 2011).

Evidence in favor includes the finding that the spontaneous activity of ferret visual cortex, recorded without a stimulus, increases its similarity to the average of the activity evoked by natural images during development (Berkes et al., 2011), as expected if spontaneous activity samples from the prior. The variability of V1 neurons also depends on contrast in the way predicted by a model in which the stimulus is the sum of image features with uncertain presence (Orbán et al., 2016).

| | Probabilistic population code | Sampling |
|---|---|---|
| What carries the uncertainty | The amplitude (gain) of the population response | The variability of the activity over time |
| Meaning of variability | Noise (corrupts the measurement) | Signal (the sampled distribution) |
| Combination of cues | Summing of spike counts | Sampling from the product of the cues' distributions |
| Speed | Fast: one response | Slow: many samples needed |
| Multimodal posteriors | Hard | Natural |
| Typical prediction | Higher reliability, more spikes | Lower variability at higher reliability |

The two proposals are not exclusive: the population response may encode the amplitude of the evidence on short time scales, and the variability over longer times the remaining uncertainty. A third family, the distributed distributional codes, represents the posterior by the expected value of a set of basis functions, which the neurons' **firing rates** estimate.

## 27.6. Limits and Tests of the Bayesian Framework

The Bayesian framework is the most successful quantitative theory of perception, but it has also been criticized, and the criticisms define the standard of evidence.

First, a model with freely chosen priors, likelihoods and cost functions can be fitted to any behavior; if these components are adjusted after the fact, the explanation is a "just-so story" (Bowers and Davis, 2012; Jones and Love, 2011). The remedy is that the components should be *measured independently*: Ernst and Banks derived the combined thresholds and weights from unimodal data, with no free parameters; Stocker and Simoncelli estimated the prior from one set of conditions and tested it on others; Jazayeri and Shadlen measured the Weber fraction separately. Models are also tested against alternatives with the same number of parameters, as the causal inference model was against fusion and segregation models.

Second, behavior is not always optimal. Reviews of the literature find systematic deviations: suboptimal weights in cue combination, nonoptimal use of priors and heuristics that approximate the Bayesian solution (Rahnev and Denison, 2018). These deviations are expected if the brain uses approximate inference with limited resources, and they are informative: which approximation is made tells which computation is feasible. The ideal Bayesian observer is then the benchmark against which the deviations are measured, not a claim that the brain is optimal.

Third, the framework describes *what* is computed, in the sense of the levels of analysis in [7.16.1](/7.16.1), and says little about *how*. The neural implementations of Section 27.5 make different predictions and remain unresolved; the framework is also silent about how priors are learned, how the generative models are acquired in development, and how complex generative models, such as natural scenes, are inverted in the milliseconds available. Models that learn generative models from data, such as variational autoencoders, and the predictive coding theory, in which feedback connections carry the prior and feedforward connections the prediction error, are attempts to address them ([7.16.48](/7.16.48)).

## Summary

- Perception is modeled as inference (Definition 27.1.1). For Gaussian prior and likelihood the posterior mean is the precision-weighted average of measurement and prior mean and precisions add (Theorem 27.1.1); the estimate is biased towards the prior, with smaller variance.
- Two independent Gaussian cues are combined with weights proportional to their reliabilities and the combined variance is below either (Theorem 27.2.1). Human visual–haptic judgments follow it quantitatively; weights shift with reliability, as in the ventriloquist effect and in the responses of MSTd neurons.
- Causal inference (Definition 27.2.1, Eq. (27.4)) infers whether cues share a source: the influence of one cue on the other first rises and then falls with the disparity.
- Priors cause biases: the slow-speed prior makes low-contrast stimuli appear slower, and the central tendency of judgments follows from a prior learned from the experiment's stimuli and scalar measurement noise. Serial dependence follows from a continuity prior (Eq. (27.5)) and has the derivative-of-Gaussian shape.
- In a probabilistic population code with Poisson-like variability the gain encodes reliability (Theorem 27.5.1) and summing responses multiplies likelihoods (Theorem 27.5.2). In the sampling hypothesis variability itself represents uncertainty.
- The framework needs independently measured components and is a benchmark for deviations; how the brain implements and learns the computations is open.

## Questions

1. A Bayesian observer has a prior $s \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 10^2)$ and a measurement $x = 8$ with noise $\sigma = 5$. Compute the posterior mean and standard deviation, and the weight on the measurement.

   **Answer** By Theorem 27.1.1, $k = \sigma_0^2/(\sigma_0^2 + \sigma^2) = 100/125 = 0.8$, which is the weight on the measurement; $\mu_{\mathrm{post}} = 0.8\times8 = 6.4$ (the prior mean is $0$) and $\sigma_{\mathrm{post}}^2 = (1/25 + 1/100)^{-1} = 20$, so $\sigma_{\mathrm{post}} = 4.47$, narrower than the measurement noise of $5$.
2. Vision and touch have standard deviations of $1$ and $2$ (in units of the size judged). Compute the weights and the combined standard deviation. If the visual noise doubles to $2$, what are they? In which case is the improvement over the best single cue largest?

   **Answer** $w_{\mathrm{v}} = (1/1)/(1/1 + 1/4) = 0.8$, $w_{\mathrm{h}} = 0.2$, and $\sigma_{12}^2 = 1\cdot4/(1 + 4) = 0.8$, $\sigma_{12} = 0.89$, a $11\,\%$ improvement over vision alone. With $\sigma_{\mathrm{v}} = 2$: the weights are $0.5$ and $0.5$ and $\sigma_{12}^2 = 4\cdot4/8 = 2$, $\sigma_{12} = 1.41$, a factor $\sqrt2$ better than either cue. The improvement is largest when the cues are equally reliable (Theorem 27.2.1); when one cue is much more reliable, the other adds almost nothing.
3. A Gaussian prior has standard deviation $\sigma_0 = 0.1\,\mathrm{s}$ around the mean interval, and the measurement of an interval has standard deviation $0.1\,\mathrm{s}$. What is the slope of the reproduced against the presented interval? How would the slope change if the observers were first trained on a narrower range?

   **Answer** The slope is the shrinkage factor $k = \sigma_0^2/(\sigma_0^2 + \sigma^2) = 0.01/0.02 = 0.5$: an interval $0.1\,\mathrm{s}$ longer than the mean is reproduced as only $0.05\,\mathrm{s}$ longer. With a narrower range the prior is sharper (smaller $\sigma_0$), $k$ decreases and the regression to the mean is stronger; with a wider range it weakens, which is the dependence on the range seen by Jazayeri and Shadlen.
4. Two Poisson populations with the same **tuning curves** and gains $1.5$ and $5$ receive two cues. What is the expected weight of population b in the summed population? Why does the weight of one trial deviate from this value?

   **Answer** By Theorem 27.5.1 the precision of the likelihood from a population is $\sum_ir_i/\sigma_f^2$, and the expected total count is proportional to the gain, so the expected weights are $1.5/6.5 = 0.23$ for a and $5/6.5 = 0.77$ for b (Theorem 27.5.2: the summed response weights the two likelihoods by their spike counts). In a single trial the total counts are Poisson, with expected values of about $15$ and $50$ in the example of the figure (41 neurons spaced by $2$, width $8$: $\sum_i f_i \approx g\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma_f/2$) and standard deviations of about $4$ and $7$; in the trial shown they were $20$ and $48$, so the actual weights ($20/68 = 0.29$ and $0.71$) fluctuate from trial to trial: the brain's weights track the reliability of the evidence in each trial, not the average reliability.
5. (Open question) Design an experiment to distinguish a probabilistic population code from a sampling code in visual cortex.

   **Answer** The two proposals assign opposite roles to variability and predict different effects of manipulating the uncertainty of the stimulus. Present stimuli whose uncertainty is controlled independently of the mean drive, for example a grating of fixed orientation but variable noise or blur, and record a population in V1 or V2. A probabilistic population code predicts that uncertainty changes the amplitude (total count) of the response, with Poisson-like variability whose Fano factor stays near $1$; the likelihood decoded from a single trial should predict the width of the animal's report distribution. A sampling code predicts that higher uncertainty gives more variable activity within a trial and a wider distribution of states, such as a larger variance of the population activity at fixed mean and slow fluctuations over time ([7.16.16](/7.16.16#sec-16.4.3)) that persist for the duration of the trial, and that the distribution of spontaneous activity matches the average of the evoked activity. Differences in the time course of the variability within a trial and in its dependence on uncertainty are the discriminating measurements; the choice of stimulus must avoid the confound that contrast changes both uncertainty and gain.
