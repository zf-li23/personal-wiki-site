Motor cortex is where the computations of [7.16.29](/7.16.29), estimating the state of the body, choosing a goal and computing a feedback law, meet the spinal circuits of [7.16.30](/7.16.30) that turn commands into force. The anatomy of the motor areas, the somatotopic map of primary motor cortex, the **corticospinal tract** and the classical description of direction tuning and the **population vector** are given in [7.14.45](/7.14.45#sec-45.1). This chapter follows the change of perspective that the study of motor cortex has undergone over five decades. The first question was representational: which parameter of movement (force, direction, velocity or muscle activity) does a neuron encode? The answers were all partly right, and the question gave way to a dynamical one: how does a population of neurons generate the time-varying commands of a movement, and how does it prepare them without moving? The dynamical view uses the tools of [7.16.28](/7.16.28) (**population state spaces**, **jPCA**) and the trained recurrent networks of [7.16.31](/7.16.31). The same population signals, read out by a computer, drive brain–machine interfaces that restore movement and speech to paralyzed people, the subject of the second half of the chapter.

## 32.1. The Motor Cortical Areas

The frontal motor areas comprise primary motor cortex (M1, area 4), the dorsal and ventral premotor cortex (PMd, PMv) on the lateral surface of area 6, the supplementary and presupplementary motor areas on the medial surface, and the cingulate motor areas below them ([7.14.45](/7.14.45#sec-45.1.2)). All of them send axons into the corticospinal tract, together with primary somatosensory and posterior parietal cortex; M1 contributes about a third of the fibers. In primates the tract contains corticomotoneuronal cells, whose axons contact spinal motor neurons monosynaptically. They lie in the caudal part of M1 in the bank of the central sulcus ("new M1"; Rathelot and Strick, 2009), are most numerous for the muscles of the hand, and their number across species correlates with manual dexterity: they are absent in cats and rodents, in which corticospinal axons end on interneurons. In mice, transient corticomotoneuronal synapses form during development and are eliminated by semaphorin–plexin signaling; preventing their elimination improves skilled grasping (Gu and colleagues, 2017). Most corticospinal output, even in primates, acts through spinal interneurons, the same premotor networks that generate locomotion ([7.16.30](/7.16.30#sec-30.2)).

The map of M1 is coarse. Neurons active during movements of a given finger are intermingled with those for other fingers over the whole hand area, and each neuron is active for several fingers (Schieber and Hibbard, 1993). Brief microstimulation evokes twitches of a few muscles, but trains of $500\,\mathrm{ms}$ evoke coordinated, goal-like movements, bringing the hand to the mouth with a grip and an open mouth, defensive postures or reaches into a region of space, organized in a map of ethological action categories across motor and premotor cortex (Graziano, Taylor and Moore, 2002). Motor cortex is not needed for every movement. Rats with motor cortex lesions walk, climb and eat; Kawai and colleagues (2015) found that motor cortex was needed for learning a precisely timed lever-press sequence but not for executing it once learned, which was then carried out by subcortical circuits. In primates, by contrast, lesions of M1 or of the corticospinal tract cause lasting loss of independent finger movements.

## 32.2. What Do Motor Cortex Neurons Encode?

### 32.2.1. Force, Direction and Muscles

Evarts (1968) recorded pyramidal tract neurons in monkeys flexing and extending the wrist against loads that either opposed or assisted the movement. Many neurons changed their firing with the load, so that they reflected the force to be produced rather than the displacement. Georgopoulos and colleagues (1982) then found that, for reaches in different directions from a central position, the rate of most arm-area neurons varies as the cosine of the angle between the movement direction and a preferred direction ([Eq. (45.1)](/7.14.45#eq-45.1)). The two results are not contradictory, because cosine tuning is a property of any linear encoding of a vector.

**Proposition 32.2.1 (Cosine Tuning from Linear Encoding)** If the rate of a neuron is an affine function $r = b + \boldsymbol{c}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}$ of a vector $\boldsymbol{v}$ (hand velocity, force, or the activations of muscles acting in different directions), then for $\boldsymbol{v}$ of fixed magnitude $v$ in direction $\theta$ it is cosine-tuned, $r(\theta) = b + \lVert\boldsymbol{c}\rVert v\cos(\theta - \theta_{\mathrm{pd}})$, with the preferred direction $\theta_{\mathrm{pd}}$ of $\boldsymbol{c}$ and a depth of modulation proportional to $v$.

**Proof** $\boldsymbol{c}\cdot\boldsymbol{v} = \lVert\boldsymbol{c}\rVert\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\rVert\cos\angle(\boldsymbol{c}, \boldsymbol{v})$. $\square$

Cosine tuning therefore cannot tell us which variable the neuron encodes; the decisive experiments vary the relation between the variables. When monkeys made the same wrist movements with the forearm pronated, in mid-position and supinated, the preferred directions of muscles rotated with the forearm; those of some M1 neurons rotated in the same way ("muscle-like"), those of others stayed fixed in external space ("extrinsic-like"), and in ventral premotor cortex almost all were extrinsic-like (Kakei, Hoffman and Strick, 1999). For reaches with the whole arm, the preferred directions of M1 neurons change with posture and with the loads applied to the shoulder and elbow, and they cluster in two opposite directions, the directions in which the joint torques are largest (Scott, Gribble, Graham and Cabel, 2001). Todorov (2000) showed that a model in which M1 directly specifies muscle activations, combined with the length–velocity dependence of muscle force ([7.14.9](/7.14.9)), reproduces most correlations of M1 activity with kinematics. The tuning of real neurons is also not fixed in time: the preferred direction and depth of modulation change during the course of a single reach, in a way that no fixed parametric model of kinematics or dynamics captures (Churchland and Shenoy, 2007).

### 32.2.2. Decoding the Population

A **population code** is useful only if its readout is unbiased. For cosine-tuned neurons, the **population vector** of [Eq. (45.2)](/7.14.45#eq-45.2) is biased unless the preferred directions are distributed suitably.

**Proposition 32.2.2 (Bias of the Population Vector)** Let $N$ neurons with preferred directions $\theta_i$ have mean responses $r_i - b_i = k_i\cos(\theta - \theta_i)$, and let $\boldsymbol{P} = \sum_i\frac{r_i - b_i}{k_i}\boldsymbol{u}_i$ with $\boldsymbol{u}_i = (\cos\theta_i, \sin\theta_i)$. Writing $\boldsymbol{P}$ and the directions as complex numbers,
$$
\mathbb{E}[P] = \frac N2\Bigl(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta} + \rho_2\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(2\psi - \theta)}\Bigr), \qquad \rho_2\,\mathrm{e}^{2\mathrm{i}\psi} = \frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N\mathrm{e}^{2\mathrm{i}\theta_i}. \tag{32.1}
$$
The population vector points in the movement direction for every $\theta$ if and only if the second circular moment $\rho_2$ of the preferred directions vanishes; uniformity is sufficient but not necessary.

**Proof** $\cos(\theta - \theta_i)\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta_i} = \tfrac12\bigl(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta} + \mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\theta}\mathrm{e}^{2\mathrm{i}\theta_i}\bigr)$; sum over $i$. The second term vanishes for all $\theta$ only if $\rho_2 = 0$; if $\rho_2 > 0$ it pulls $\boldsymbol{P}$ toward the axis $\psi$ except when $\theta = \psi$ or $\theta = \psi + \pi/2$ (modulo $\pi$). $\square$

The bias does not decrease with the number of neurons. It is removed by the *optimal linear estimator* (Salinas and Abbott, 1994), which chooses decoding vectors $\boldsymbol{D}_i$ in $\hat{\boldsymbol{v}} = \sum_i(r_i - b_i)\boldsymbol{D}_i$ to minimize the mean squared error over the distribution of movements. The solution is $D = LQ^{-1}$, where $Q_{ij} = \mathbb{E}[(r_i - b_i)(r_j - b_j)]$ includes the noise covariance and $L_{\cdot j} = \mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{v}(r_j - b_j)]$; in practice it is the least-squares regression of the movement on the rates, the **ridge regression** of brain–machine interfaces (32.5). With noise that is independent across neurons, its error decreases as $N^{-1/2}$ (figure below). Neither decoder implies that the brain reads out motor cortex in this way: the population vector is a measure of the information that the population carries, and the downstream readout is the spinal cord, whose "decoder" is the set of corticospinal weights onto interneurons and motor neurons.

## 32.3. Preparatory Activity

### 32.3.1. Preparation Without Movement

In an instructed-delay task the target of a reach is shown, and the movement is allowed only after a go cue that follows a variable delay. Many neurons in PMd and M1 change their firing during the delay, in a way specific to the upcoming movement, although nothing moves (Tanji and Evarts, 1976; Weinrich and Wise, 1982). Preparation is useful: reaction times after a delay are about $100\,\mathrm{ms}$ shorter than without one, and on trials in which the delay activity is far from its typical value for the target, the reaction time is longer (Churchland, Yu, Ryu, Santhanam and Shenoy, 2006). Subthreshold microstimulation of PMd during the delay, which disrupts this activity, delays the movement by tens of milliseconds, whereas the same stimulation at the go cue has less effect (Churchland and Shenoy, 2007). The onset of the target also reduces the trial-to-trial variability of the firing of neurons in many cortical areas (the **Fano factor** falls), as if the population state converged on a preparatory region of the state space (Churchland and colleagues, 2010). Even when there is no delay, a brief preparatory phase of about $50\,\mathrm{ms}$ precedes the movement (Lara, Elsayed, Zimnik, Cunningham and Churchland, 2018).

**Definition 32.3.1 (Preparatory Activity)** **Preparatory activity** is the movement-specific activity of neurons in premotor and motor cortex (and in connected areas) between the specification of a movement and its initiation; it is not accompanied by movement or muscle activity and sets the state from which the movement is generated.

### 32.3.2. The Output-Null Hypothesis

Why does preparatory activity in neurons that project to the spinal cord not cause movement? An early proposal was a gate, inhibition that blocks the output until the go cue, but putative inhibitory interneurons are not more active during the delay, and many neurons are more active during the delay than during the movement. Kaufman, Churchland, Ryu and Shenoy (2014) proposed a geometric answer.

**Definition 32.3.2 (Output-Null Space)** Let the muscle activity $\boldsymbol{m}$ depend on the population rates $\boldsymbol{r} \in \mathbb{R}^N$ through a linear readout, $\boldsymbol{m} = C(\boldsymbol{r} - \bar{\boldsymbol{r}})$ with $C \in \mathbb{R}^{M\times N}$, $M < N$. The **output-null space** is the null space $\mathcal{N}(C)$, the directions of population activity that do not change the output; its orthogonal complement, the row space of $C$, is the *output-potent space*. Changes of activity confined to the output-null space produce no movement.

With two neurons that drive a muscle through the sum of their rates, any change that raises one rate and lowers the other by the same amount is output-null. With $N$ neurons and $M$ muscles the null space has $N - M$ dimensions, most of the state space. Kaufman and colleagues estimated $C$ by regressing the muscle activity on the population activity during movement and found that during the delay the activity changed mainly in the output-null space and moved into the output-potent space only around movement onset. Preparatory and movement activity occupy nearly orthogonal subspaces, and the transition between them is rapid (Elsayed, Lara, Kaufman, Churchland and Cunningham, 2016). Preparation is thus not suppressed but invisible to the spinal cord, and the go cue changes the dynamics, not the output weights. ### 32.3.3. Causal Tests in Rodents

Optogenetic methods in rodents have tested whether preparatory activity is necessary. Rats trained to orient left or right according to the rate of a train of clicks, after a delay, have neurons in the frontal orienting field whose delay activity predicts the direction, and silencing this area during the delay impairs orienting to the contralateral side (Erlich, Bialek and Brody, 2011). Mice trained to lick left or right according to the position of a pole felt with the whiskers, after a delay, depend on the anterior lateral motor cortex (ALM): a screen in which each cortical area was silenced in turn by activating its inhibitory interneurons found that only ALM silencing during the delay biased the later choice (Guo and colleagues, 2014). Preparatory activity in ALM is carried by pyramidal tract neurons and by intratelencephalic neurons that project to the other hemisphere, and it is maintained by a loop with the motor thalamus, whose silencing abolishes it (Li, Chen, Guo, Gerfen and Svoboda, 2015; Guo and colleagues, 2017). After brief unilateral silencing the delay activity recovers its pre-perturbation pattern within a few hundred milliseconds, through input from the other hemisphere: the preparatory state behaves as a point attractor that is robust to perturbations along some directions but not others (Li, Daie, Svoboda and Druckmann, 2016; Inagaki, Fontolan, Romani and Svoboda, 2019), the kind of discrete **attractor** analyzed in [7.16.43](/7.16.43) and [7.16.45](/7.16.45).

## 32.4. Motor Cortex as a Dynamical System

### 32.4.1. Initial Conditions and Rotations

The dynamical systems view (Shenoy, Sahani and Churchland, 2013) treats the population state $\boldsymbol{x}$ of motor cortex as obeying $\dot{\boldsymbol{x}} = \boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x}) + \boldsymbol{u}(t)$, with inputs $\boldsymbol{u}$ from thalamus and other cortical areas, and asks which dynamics generate the commands. Preparation sets an initial condition, and the movement unfolds from it. For linear dynamics $\dot{\boldsymbol{x}} = A\boldsymbol{x}$ after the go cue, the output $\boldsymbol{m}(t) = C\mathrm{e}^{At}\boldsymbol{x}_0$ is a linear function of the preparatory state $\boldsymbol{x}_0$: different initial conditions select different combinations of the same temporal basis functions, the modes of $A$, and the requirement that $C\boldsymbol{x}_0 = \boldsymbol{0}$ is the output-null condition. Since muscle commands for a reach are multiphasic, a natural choice of basis is a set of damped oscillations.

**Definition 32.4.1 (Rotational Dynamics)** **Rotational dynamics** of a neural population is a pattern in which, after a preparatory initial state, the population state rotates in one or more planes of the state space with a common direction across conditions, so that its time course is well fitted by a linear dynamical system $\dot{\boldsymbol{x}} = M\boldsymbol{x}$ with skew-symmetric $M$ (Theorem 28.5.1).

Churchland and colleagues (2012) found such rotations by **jPCA** (Definition 28.5.2) in the motor and premotor cortex of monkeys making non-rhythmic reaches: the state rotates for one to two cycles at $1$–$3\,\mathrm{Hz}$ during the reach, with phase and amplitude set by the preparatory state. Rotations are also prominent in motor cortex during rhythmic cycling movements and speech in humans, whereas the population structure of visual cortex, which is driven by its input, is of a different kind (Seely and colleagues, 2016). Rotations do not by themselves prove a dynamical mechanism: sequences of activity, in which different neurons peak at different times, also produce them, and controls against surrogate data that preserve the smoothness and correlations of the responses are needed ([7.16.28](/7.16.28#sec-28.5.1); Lebedev and colleagues, 2019, and the reply of Churchland and colleagues).

### 32.4.2. Trained Networks and Tangling

Sussillo, Churchland, Kaufman and Shenoy (2015) trained recurrent networks to produce the recorded muscle activity of monkeys for many reaches, starting from a preparatory input that differed between reaches ([7.16.31](/7.16.31#sec-31.5)). When the training was regularized to favor simple, smooth dynamics, the networks developed rotational population dynamics and single-unit responses that resembled M1 neurons more than the muscles did, although they were trained only on the muscles. Stability-optimized networks with strong, balanced excitation and inhibition generate similar transients through **non-normal amplification** ([7.16.15](/7.16.15#sec-15.2.3); Hennequin, Vogels and Gerstner, 2014). Why should a generator have dynamics different from its outputs? Russo and colleagues (2018) gave a quantitative answer.

**Definition 32.4.2 (Trajectory Tangling)** For a population trajectory $\boldsymbol{x}(t)$, the **trajectory tangling** at time $t$ is
$$
Q(t) = \max_{t'}\frac{\lVert\dot{\boldsymbol{x}}(t) - \dot{\boldsymbol{x}}(t')\rVert^2}{\lVert\boldsymbol{x}(t) - \boldsymbol{x}(t')\rVert^2 + \varepsilon}, \tag{32.2}
$$
with a small constant $\varepsilon$; $Q$ is large when two nearby states have very different derivatives.

A trajectory generated by a smooth, autonomous flow field $\dot{\boldsymbol{x}} = \boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x})$ cannot cross itself with different directions, so it has low tangling, and noise added to its state is corrected by the flow rather than amplified. The muscle activity during cycling, by contrast, is highly tangled (the same muscle state recurs with opposite derivatives in forward and backward cycling), and so is the activity of somatosensory cortex, which is driven by the limb. Motor cortex activity has low tangling, achieved by adding dimensions in which forward and backward cycling separate. Low tangling is thus a signature of a population that generates its output by internal dynamics. Those dynamics are not fully autonomous: silencing the motor thalamus in mice interrupts a reach immediately, and cortical activity requires continuous thalamic input during the movement (Sauerbrei and colleagues, 2020), so the "dynamical system" is the thalamocortical loop, with contributions from the cerebellum and basal ganglia. Motor cortex also generates rapid, task-dependent feedback corrections (Pruszynski and colleagues, 2011), the feedback controller of **optimal feedback control** ([7.16.29](/7.16.29#sec-29.5.2)).

## 32.5. Brain–Machine Interfaces

### 32.5.1. Principle

**Definition 32.5.1 (Brain–Machine Interface)** A **brain–machine interface** (BMI, also brain–computer interface) is a system that records neural activity, decodes from it in real time a control signal (the velocity of a cursor, the movements of a robotic arm or of a paralyzed limb stimulated electrically, characters or speech), and acts on the world in a closed loop in which the user sees, and learns from, the result. Stimulating interfaces, which write information into the nervous system, are discussed in 32.6.

That single neurons can be brought under voluntary control was shown by Fetz (1969), who rewarded monkeys for increasing the firing of an M1 neuron displayed as a meter: the rate rose within minutes, a form of **operant conditioning** of a neuron. Real-time control of a robot arm from populations of neurons was achieved in rats (Chapin, Moxon, Markowitz and Nicolelis, 1999) and monkeys (Wessberg and colleagues, 2000; Taylor, Tillery and Schwartz, 2002; Serruya and colleagues, 2002). Monkeys used a robotic arm with a gripper to feed themselves from a population vector decoder (Velliste, Perel, Spalding, Whitford and Schwartz, 2008). In humans with tetraplegia, a $96$-channel silicon array (the Utah array) implanted in the hand area of M1 allowed cursor control (Hochberg and colleagues, 2006) and, later, reaching and grasping with a robotic arm (Hochberg and colleagues, 2012; Collinger and colleagues, 2013) and with the person's own arm, moved by functional electrical stimulation of the muscles (Ajiboye and colleagues, 2017).

### 32.5.2. Decoders

A decoder maps the binned spike counts $\boldsymbol{y}_t$ of the recorded units to the kinematic state $\boldsymbol{x}_t$, usually the velocity of a cursor. Three families are standard.

- **Population vector**: the directional readout of [Eq. (45.2)](/7.14.45#eq-45.2), with the bias of Proposition 32.2.2.
- Linear filter: $\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_t = \sum_{l=0}^{L-1}D_l\boldsymbol{y}_{t-l} + \boldsymbol{d}$, fitted by least squares (or **ridge regression**) to training data in which the user watches or attempts movements; with several lags this is the discrete **Wiener filter**.
- **Kalman filter**: the kinematics are modeled as a linear dynamical system $\boldsymbol{x}_{t+1} = A\boldsymbol{x}_t + \boldsymbol{w}_t$, and the spike counts as noisy linear functions of the kinematics, $\boldsymbol{y}_t = H\boldsymbol{x}_t + \boldsymbol{d} + \boldsymbol{q}_t$ (an **encoding model** with cosine tuning to velocity), and the kinematics are estimated recursively by Algorithm 29.4.1 (Wu and colleagues, 2006).

The linear filter maps neurons to movement directly; the Kalman filter inverts a model of how neurons depend on movement and combines it with a prior on how movements evolve, which smooths the estimate and weighs each neuron by its reliability. The same principle by which the brain estimates the state of the limb ([7.16.29](/7.16.29#sec-29.4)) makes it the better decoder, especially with few neurons (figure below). Gilja and colleagues (2012) improved the Kalman decoder further by refitting it to the user's *intention*: during closed-loop use, the decoded velocities are rotated to point at the target, and the decoder is retrained on these intended velocities (ReFIT); cursor control then approached the speed and accuracy of the native arm. Current decoders include recurrent networks and latent dynamical models of the population (LFADS; Pandarinath and colleagues, 2018), which use the low dimensionality and the dynamics of 32.4.

### 32.5.3. Learning in the Loop

A BMI is a new effector, and the brain learns to use it. When Ganguly and Carmena (2009) kept a decoder fixed over days, the performance of monkeys improved and a stable pattern of tuning of the recorded neurons emerged, recalled immediately each day, like a motor memory; when the decoder was changed, a new pattern formed without erasing the first. When the decoder assigned a rotated preferred direction to a subset of neurons, those neurons changed their tuning more than the others (Jarosiewicz and colleagues, 2008). What can be learned quickly is constrained by the existing structure of population activity. Sadtler and colleagues (2014) recorded about $90$ units in M1 and identified the low-dimensional **neural manifold** (Definition 28.5.1) in which their activity normally lay. They then changed the decoder so that cursor control required either a new combination of activity patterns within the manifold, or patterns outside it. Monkeys learned within-manifold perturbations in one session but not outside-manifold ones, which were learned only over many days of training (Oby and colleagues, 2019). Short-term learning therefore reuses the existing repertoire of population activity, in agreement with the view that the repertoire is set by the network's dynamics.

### 32.5.4. Communication Prostheses

For people who cannot move or speak, communication is the most important function of a BMI. Typing with a point-and-click cursor reached about $40$ correct characters per minute (Pandarinath and colleagues, 2017). Decoding attempted handwriting instead, with a recurrent network that classified the neural activity of each attempted character, raised this to $90$ characters per minute with $94\,\%$ accuracy before language-model correction (Willett, Avansino, Hochberg, Henderson and Shenoy, 2021), because handwriting produces more distinct, temporally structured patterns than straight cursor movements. Speech neuroprostheses decode attempted speech from arrays in ventral premotor cortex (Willett and colleagues, 2023: $62$ words per minute, $24\,\%$ word errors with a $125\,000$-word vocabulary) or from electrocorticography over speech cortex (Metzger and colleagues, 2023: $78$ words per minute, with a synthesized voice and an animated face), and the accuracy of such systems continues to improve with longer use and larger language models. These systems show that the representations of articulation in motor cortex remain intact years after paralysis.

Sensory feedback closes the loop. Without it, BMI users must watch the device; with intracortical microstimulation of somatosensory cortex, which evokes localized sensations of touch felt in the paralyzed hand, a person controlling a robotic arm completed grasping tasks in about half the time (Flesher and colleagues, 2016, 2021).

## 32.6. Stimulation and Neuromodulation Interfaces

Interfaces that stimulate are older and more widespread than those that record. The cochlear implant, an array of electrodes in the cochlea that stimulates the auditory nerve according to the energy in frequency bands of the sound (the **place principle**, [7.16.24](/7.16.24)), has restored speech perception to more than a million people. Deep brain stimulation (DBS) delivers continuous high-frequency pulses (about $130\,\mathrm{Hz}$) through electrodes in the subthalamic nucleus or the internal globus pallidus to treat the motor symptoms of **Parkinson's disease**, and in the ventral intermediate nucleus of the thalamus for essential tremor; it is also used for dystonia and obsessive–compulsive disorder. Its mechanism is not a simple lesion: high-frequency stimulation disrupts the pathological synchronized beta oscillations ($13$–$30\,\mathrm{Hz}$) of the **basal ganglia** loop and replaces them with regular, information-poor activity ([7.16.40](/7.16.40)). Because beta power tracks the severity of symptoms, adaptive DBS turns stimulation on when beta power rises, with equal or better benefit at lower energy (Little and colleagues, 2013), and such closed-loop systems have entered clinical use. Responsive neurostimulation for **epilepsy** detects the onset of **seizures** in recordings from implanted electrodes and stimulates to abort them ([7.16.52](/7.16.52)). Epidural stimulation of the lumbar spinal cord with spatiotemporal patterns that target the motor pools of individual muscles, timed to the intended movement, restored walking with crutches or walkers to people with chronic spinal cord injury (Wagner and colleagues, 2018), and coupled to a cortical decoder, it forms a brain–spine interface that restores voluntary control of the timing of steps (Lorach and colleagues, 2023), engaging the spinal locomotor networks of [7.16.30](/7.16.30).

Every interface raises questions beyond engineering: the durability and safety of implants over decades, the privacy of decoded neural data, the agency and identity of users whose movements or words are partly produced by an algorithm, and equitable access. These are not reasons to stop but conditions for responsible development.

## Summary

| Question | Answer | Where |
|---|---|---|
| What does a neuron encode? | Cosine tuning follows from any linear encoding; muscle-like and extrinsic-like cells coexist; tuning changes during a reach | Proposition 32.2.1 |
| Is the population vector accurate? | Only if the second circular moment of preferred directions vanishes; the optimal linear estimator removes the bias | Proposition 32.2.2, Eq. (32.1) |
| How can activity prepare without moving? | Preparatory activity lies in the output-null space of the readout | Definitions 32.3.1, 32.3.2 |
| How is a movement generated? | From a preparatory initial condition by dynamics with rotational structure and low tangling | Definitions 32.4.1, 32.4.2 |
| How is activity turned into control? | Linear or Kalman decoders, refit to intention; the brain learns within its neural manifold | Definition 32.5.1 |

- The motor areas project in parallel into the corticospinal tract; corticomotoneuronal cells in primate M1 support dexterity, but most output acts through spinal interneurons. Long microstimulation evokes ethological actions.
- Cosine tuning (Proposition 32.2.1) does not identify the encoded variable; experiments that dissociate muscles and kinematics find both kinds of neurons. The population vector is biased by nonuniform preferred directions (Proposition 32.2.2); the optimal linear estimator is not.
- Preparatory activity (Definition 32.3.1) shortens reaction times and is causally needed (ALM, frontal orienting field); it lies in the output-null space (Definition 32.3.2) and is maintained by a thalamocortical loop with attractor-like robustness.
- In the dynamical view, preparation sets an initial condition and the dynamics produce the movement; motor cortex shows rotational dynamics (Definition 32.4.1) and low tangling (Definition 32.4.2), as do networks trained to produce muscle activity.
- Brain–machine interfaces (Definition 32.5.1) decode movement with linear or Kalman filters; users learn new decoders quickly within the neural manifold and slowly outside it; handwriting and speech decoders restore communication; stimulating interfaces (cochlear implants, deep brain and spinal cord stimulation) write information into the nervous system.

## Questions

1. Three neurons have preferred directions $0^\circ$, $90^\circ$ and $180^\circ$. Compute the second circular moment $\rho_2$ and the expected population vector for a movement at $45^\circ$. In which direction does it point?

   **Answer** $\frac13(\mathrm{e}^{0} + \mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\pi} + \mathrm{e}^{2\mathrm{i}\pi}) = \frac13$, so $\rho_2 = 1/3$ and $\psi = 0$. By Eq. (32.1), $\mathbb{E}[P] = \frac32\bigl(\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\pi/4} + \tfrac13\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\pi/4}\bigr) = \frac32\cdot\frac{\sqrt2}{2}\bigl(\tfrac43 + \tfrac23\mathrm{i}\bigr)$, whose angle is $\arctan(1/2) = 26.6^\circ$: the vector is pulled toward the $0^\circ$–$180^\circ$ axis, along which two of the three preferred directions lie. Directly: the responses are $\cos45^\circ$, $\cos45^\circ$ and $-\cos45^\circ$, and $\boldsymbol{P} = \frac{\sqrt2}{2}\bigl[(1, 0) + (0, 1) - (-1, 0)\bigr] = \frac{\sqrt2}{2}(2, 1)$, the same direction.

2. A population of $100$ neurons drives two muscles through a linear readout $C$ of rank 2. How many dimensions does the output-null space have? If preparatory activity were distributed isotropically across all directions, what fraction of its variance would reach the muscles, and what did Kaufman and colleagues find instead?

   **Answer** $\dim\mathcal{N}(C) = 100 - 2 = 98$. Isotropic activity would put $2/100 = 2\,\%$ of its variance in the potent space, so even a random preparatory pattern would move the muscles only slightly; what makes the hypothesis informative is the comparison with movement activity. Kaufman and colleagues found that the ratio of potent to null variance was much lower during preparation than during movement, and lower than expected for random subspaces, so preparatory activity avoids the potent directions specifically, while movement activity concentrates in them.

3. Why is a Kalman filter decoder smoother and more accurate than a linear filter with the same neurons, and when might the linear filter be preferable?

   **Answer** The Kalman filter combines two sources of information: a prior that movements evolve smoothly ($\boldsymbol{x}_{t+1} = A\boldsymbol{x}_t + \boldsymbol{w}_t$) and the likelihood of the spike counts given the movement; each update weighs new spikes by their reliability relative to the prediction (the Kalman gain), so isolated noisy counts change the estimate little. A linear filter with a few lags averages the counts over a short window with fixed weights and has no model of movement; with few neurons its output is noisy. The linear filter can be preferable when the assumed dynamics are wrong (sudden stops and starts, which the smoothness prior delays), when the tuning model is strongly nonlinear, or when its simplicity and lack of lag matter; refitting either decoder to the user's intentions in closed loop (ReFIT) often matters more than the choice between them.

4. Sadtler and colleagues found that within-manifold perturbations of a BMI decoder were learned in hours and outside-manifold perturbations were not. What does this imply about the mechanisms of short-term motor learning, and how could the network dynamics of 32.4 explain it?

   **Answer** Short-term learning seems to reassign existing activity patterns to new outputs (changing which patterns are produced, or the inputs that select them), rather than creating new patterns, which would require changing the recurrent connectivity that confines activity to the manifold. If the manifold is the set of states that the recurrent dynamics can reach from the available inputs, then within-manifold learning can be achieved by changing inputs to motor cortex (from premotor areas, thalamus or cerebellum), a fast process, whereas outside-manifold learning requires synaptic changes within motor cortex that alter the dynamics, a slow process. The finding that outside-manifold perturbations can be learned over days of training, with the appearance of new activity patterns, fits this two-time-scale view.

5. (Open question) Motor cortex activity has low tangling while muscle activity is highly tangled. Explain why a network that generates its output from its own dynamics needs low tangling, and suggest how one could test whether a given brain area is a generator or a relay.

   **Answer** In an autonomous dynamical system the derivative is a function of the state, $\dot{\boldsymbol{x}} = \boldsymbol{f}(\boldsymbol{x})$; if two nearby states required very different derivatives, $\boldsymbol{f}$ would have to vary steeply, and small noise in the state would send the trajectory onto the wrong branch. A generator therefore needs extra dimensions in which the states that look alike in the output are separated, which lowers tangling. An area driven by external input (a relay of sensory feedback, or one following a downstream generator) has no such constraint, because its derivatives are set from outside. Tests: compute tangling for the area and for its candidate inputs and outputs across behaviors; perturb the area briefly and see whether the trajectory returns to the same path (a generator) or follows the input; silence its inputs (as for the motor thalamus) to measure how autonomous the dynamics are; and compare with networks trained to produce the same outputs, which must have low tangling to be robust to noise.
