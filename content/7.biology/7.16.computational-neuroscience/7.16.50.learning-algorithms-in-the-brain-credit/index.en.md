Learning in a network of many neurons requires deciding which synapses are responsible for an outcome, a problem that becomes acute when the synapse is several layers or several hundred milliseconds away from the error. Artificial neural networks solve it with backpropagation ([4.7.2](/4.7.2#sec-2.4)), which computes the exact gradient of a loss with respect to every weight. This chapter asks whether, and how, the brain could do something comparable with the local plasticity rules of [7.16.14](/7.16.14) and [7.16.22](/7.16.22). It reviews the arguments against literal backpropagation, the biologically plausible alternatives that have been proposed and tested in simulation, the special problem of spiking networks, and the engineering counterpart: neuromorphic hardware that computes with spikes and analog synapses for a fraction of the energy of digital computers. It ends with the division of labor among the three classical learning paradigms in the brain. The generative and self-supervised objectives that such rules might optimize are in [7.16.48](/7.16.48); reinforcement learning, the dopamine prediction error and policy gradients in [7.16.39](/7.16.39) and [7.16.41](/7.16.41); the cerebellar learning machine in [7.16.33](/7.16.33).

## 50.1. The Credit Assignment Problem

**Definition 50.1.1 (Credit Assignment Problem)** The **credit assignment problem** is the problem of determining, for each adjustable parameter of a learning system (in the brain, each synapse), its contribution to an outcome, so that it can be changed in the direction that improves future outcomes. It has a *spatial* part (which synapses, possibly far from the output, caused the error) and a *temporal* part (which past activity caused a delayed outcome).

For a layered network $\boldsymbol{h}_\ell = f(W_\ell\boldsymbol{h}_{\ell - 1})$ with loss $L$, backpropagation solves the spatial part exactly. With $\boldsymbol{a}_\ell = W_\ell\boldsymbol{h}_{\ell - 1}$ and errors $\boldsymbol{\delta}_\ell = \partial L/\partial\boldsymbol{a}_\ell$,
$$
\boldsymbol{\delta}_\ell = f'(\boldsymbol{a}_\ell) \odot W_{\ell + 1}^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol{\delta}_{\ell + 1}, \qquad \Delta W_\ell = -\eta\,\boldsymbol{\delta}_\ell\boldsymbol{h}_{\ell - 1}^{\mathsf T}. \tag{50.1}
$$
The weight update itself is local, a product of a presynaptic activity and a postsynaptic error, like a **three-factor learning rule** ([Definition 14.9.1](/7.16.14#def-14.9.1)). The difficulty is the computation of $\boldsymbol{\delta}_\ell$. Four features of Eq. (50.1) have no obvious counterpart in cortex (Lillicrap, Santoro, Marris, Akerman and Hinton, 2020).

1. *Weight transport.* The error is sent backward through $W_{\ell + 1}^{\mathsf T}$, the transpose of the forward weights; a biological feedback pathway would need synapses whose strengths mirror those of the forward synapses, which are made by other neurons onto other cells (Grossberg, 1987).
2. *Separate signals.* The same neurons would have to carry activities forward and errors backward without the two interfering, or the network would need separate error neurons in exact correspondence with the forward ones.
3. *Derivatives and signs.* The error must be multiplied by the derivative $f'(\boldsymbol{a}_\ell)$ at the current operating point and can be positive or negative, while firing rates are nonnegative; for spiking neurons the derivative of the spike threshold does not exist (50.4).
4. *Time.* For recurrent networks, the temporal part requires backpropagation through time: storing the full history of activity and running it backward, which a brain cannot do.

These objections do not show that the brain does not approximate gradients; they show that it cannot use Eq. (50.1) literally. The empirical question is whether learning in cortex improves performance faster than undirected methods would allow, and whether the changes of neural representations during learning resemble those predicted by gradient-based training. Both are hard to test, because the full gradient is unknown for a real circuit; tests compare the time course of representational change during learning with that predicted by different rules, or ask which learning problems are solved quickly (in monkeys using a **brain–computer interface**, decoders that require activity patterns within the existing low-dimensional manifold of motor cortex are learned within a session, those outside it are not, [7.16.32](/7.16.32)).

## 50.2. Biologically Plausible Alternatives

### 50.2.1. Feedback Alignment

**Definition 50.2.1 (Feedback Alignment)** **Feedback alignment** (Lillicrap, Cownden, Tweed and Akerman, 2016) replaces the transposed forward weights in Eq. (50.1) by a fixed random feedback matrix $B_\ell$: $\boldsymbol{\delta}_\ell = f'(\boldsymbol{a}_\ell) \odot B_\ell\boldsymbol{\delta}_{\ell + 1}$. In *direct* feedback alignment (Nøkland, 2016) the output error is sent through a random matrix directly to every layer.

That random feedback works is surprising, and the reason is that the forward weights learn to fit the feedback. A random $B$ gives the hidden layer an error signal that is consistent from trial to trial; the hidden units change in the direction $B\boldsymbol{e}$, and the output weights then learn to use these changes, which brings $W_2^{\mathsf T}$ into alignment with $B$ until $\boldsymbol{e}^{\mathsf T}W_2B\boldsymbol{e} > 0$ for typical errors, that is, until the feedback signal lies within $90^\circ$ of the true gradient. Once aligned, the hidden updates descend the loss (figure below). Feedback alignment learns small networks about as well as backpropagation; it lags behind for deep convolutional networks on hard tasks such as ImageNet (Bartunov and colleagues, 2018), where the alignment within narrow bottleneck layers is poor. Variants that adjust the feedback weights with their own local rule, for example the "Kolen–Pollack" rule that applies the same update and weight decay to forward and feedback weights so that they converge, close most of the gap.

### 50.2.2. Target Propagation and Errors in Dendrites

*Target propagation* (Bengio, 2014; Lee, Zhang, Fischer and Bengio, 2015) sends targets instead of errors: each layer computes, through learned approximate inverses of the layers above, the activity it should have had, and learns locally to approach it. *Equilibrium propagation* (Scellier and Bengio, 2017) runs a recurrent network twice, once freely and once with the output weakly nudged toward the target, and changes each weight by the difference of the Hebbian products in the two phases, which approximates the gradient of the loss; it is a modern form of the contrastive Hebbian learning of Boltzmann machines and of the two phases of wake–sleep ([Algorithm 48.1.1](/7.16.48#alg-48.1.1)).

The cortex may solve the problem of separate signals with the anatomy of its pyramidal cells. Layer 5 pyramidal neurons receive feedforward input on basal dendrites near the soma and feedback from higher areas on the apical tuft in layer 1, electrically separated by the long apical trunk ([7.16.13](/7.16.13#sec-13.6)). Coincidence of the two inputs converts single spikes into bursts (Larkum, 2013). In models of *dendritic error* learning, the apical compartment compares the top-down signal with a prediction supplied by local interneurons, and the difference, an error, drives plasticity of the basal synapses (Guerguiev, Lillicrap and Richards, 2017; Sacramento, Ponte Costa, Bengio and Senn, 2018). Payeur, Guerguiev, Zenke, Richards and Naud (2021) proposed that the same axon multiplexes both signals: the event rate carries the feedforward activity and the fraction of events that are bursts carries the feedback, and that a burst-dependent plasticity rule (potentiation after bursts, depression after single spikes) approximates gradient descent in hierarchies. Consistent with the idea of compartment-specific rules, two-photon imaging in mouse motor cortex during learning found that synapses on the apical and basal dendrites of the same layer 2/3 neurons follow different plasticity rules (Wright, Hedrick and Komiyama, 2025). The plateau-driven plasticity of hippocampal CA1 ([Definition 14.8.1](/7.16.14#def-14.8.1)), where an instructive signal from entorhinal cortex arriving at the apical tuft determines which synapses are potentiated, is the clearest example of an instructive dendritic signal.

### 50.2.3. Three-Factor Rules and Eligibility Traces

For the temporal part of credit assignment, the brain has a candidate mechanism: the **eligibility trace** of a **three-factor learning rule** (Definition 14.9.1). A synapse marks its recent pre–post coincidence with a decaying trace, and a later signal, broadcast by neuromodulators or delivered by specific feedback, converts the trace into a weight change. The algorithm *e-prop* (Bellec and colleagues, 2020) shows that this structure can approximate backpropagation through time in recurrent spiking networks: the exact gradient factorizes into a sum over time of products of local eligibility traces, which depend only on the history of the synapse and its postsynaptic neuron, and learning signals, which are the errors at the output sent back to each neuron through (random or learned) feedback weights; e-prop drops the nonlocal terms that propagate through the recurrent connections. With this approximation, recurrent spiking networks learn tasks that require **working memory** over seconds, almost as well as with backpropagation through time.

### 50.2.4. Perturbation Learning and Evolution Strategies

The most radical alternative needs no feedback pathway at all. If the network tries small random variations of its activity or weights and keeps those that improve a global scalar reward, it estimates the gradient by correlating perturbations with outcomes. In *node perturbation* the noise is added to the activities, in *weight perturbation* to the weights; the **REINFORCE** rule of [7.16.41](/7.16.41) is the same principle for stochastic policies (Williams, 1992).

**Proposition 50.2.1 (Gradient Estimation by Perturbation)** Let $F(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ be a fitness (reward) of parameters $\boldsymbol{\theta} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ and $F_\sigma(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon} \sim \mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0}, I)}F(\boldsymbol{\theta} + \sigma\boldsymbol{\varepsilon})$ its Gaussian smoothing. Then
$$
\nabla F_\sigma(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \frac{1}{\sigma}\,\mathbb{E}\bigl[F(\boldsymbol{\theta} + \sigma\boldsymbol{\varepsilon})\,\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\bigr] = \frac{1}{2\sigma}\,\mathbb{E}\bigl[\bigl(F(\boldsymbol{\theta} + \sigma\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}) - F(\boldsymbol{\theta} - \sigma\boldsymbol{\varepsilon})\bigr)\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\bigr], \tag{50.2}
$$
so the average of $N$ sampled terms is an unbiased estimate of the gradient of $F_\sigma$. For smooth $F$ and small $\sigma$, the variance of the antithetic estimate is about $\lVert\nabla F\rVert^2(n + 1)/N$, which grows linearly with the number of parameters.

**Proof** $F_\sigma(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \int F(\boldsymbol{y})\,\varphi_\sigma(\boldsymbol{y} - \boldsymbol{\theta})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{y}$ with $\varphi_\sigma$ the density of $\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0}, \sigma^2I)$, and $\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\varphi_\sigma(\boldsymbol{y} - \boldsymbol{\theta}) = \varphi_\sigma(\boldsymbol{y} - \boldsymbol{\theta})(\boldsymbol{y} - \boldsymbol{\theta})/\sigma^2$; substituting $\boldsymbol{y} = \boldsymbol{\theta} + \sigma\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}$ gives the first form, and averaging it with the same expression for $-\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}$ (which has the same distribution) gives the second. For small $\sigma$, the antithetic term is $(\nabla F \cdot \boldsymbol{\varepsilon})\boldsymbol{\varepsilon} + O(\sigma^2)$, whose covariance for Gaussian $\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}$ is $\lVert\nabla F\rVert^2I + \nabla F\,\nabla F^{\mathsf T}$, so its total variance is $(n + 1)\lVert\nabla F\rVert^2$. $\square$

*Evolution strategies* (Salimans, Ho, Chen, Sidor and Sutskever, 2017) use Eq. (50.2) with large populations of perturbed networks and often replace $F$ by its rank within the population (fitness shaping), which makes the update invariant to monotonic transformations of the reward and robust to outliers. Their variance scaling makes them slow for large networks with dense rewards, but they need only forward passes, can be parallelized with almost no communication (only random seeds and scalar rewards are exchanged), and work with nondifferentiable networks, including spiking ones with binary connections. Wang, Sun, Cheng and Song (2023) trained recurrent spiking networks of excitatory and inhibitory neurons with 1-bit connections by sampling connectivity from learned connection probabilities and updating the probabilities with rank-coded returns; on simulated robotic locomotion they matched networks trained with backpropagation-based reinforcement learning.

The brain has natural sources of perturbation. Synaptic release is stochastic, with release probabilities often far below 1 ([7.16.9](/7.16.9)), and Seung (2003) showed that synapses whose release probability changes with the product of the reward and their recent release history (a "hedonistic synapse") perform gradient ascent on the reward. In songbirds, the nucleus LMAN, the output of the basal ganglia loop of the anterior forebrain pathway, injects variability into the motor pathway during song learning; the variability is required for learning, and a model in which RA synapses change by the correlation of LMAN-induced perturbations with a reinforcement signal accounts for song learning (Fiete, Fee and Seung, 2007). Perturbation learning is likely to be useful where the reward is a single scalar and the number of relevant parameters is modest, as in motor skill learning.

## 50.3. Shallow and Recurrent Circuits

The view of the cortex as a deep feedforward hierarchy that must be trained end to end is itself an approximation. Rapid object categorization, with differential EEG responses about $150\,\mathrm{ms}$ after image onset (Thorpe, Fize and Marlot, 1996), leaves time for only about ten synaptic steps, but anatomically every cortical area, not just the last, receives thalamic input and projects to subcortical structures, and pathways skip levels of the hierarchy. The *shallow brain hypothesis* (Suzuki, Pennartz and Aru, 2023) proposes that cortical computation is dominated by many parallel loops between each area and the thalamus and basal ganglia, rather than by long chains of cortical areas. Shallow, recurrent architectures reduce the spatial credit assignment problem: a network of two or three stages in which most synapses are a single step from a teaching signal can learn with local rules. They turn the problem into a temporal one, which **eligibility traces** (50.2.3) and the transient dynamics of recurrent networks, exploited by **reservoir computing** with a trained readout ([7.16.31](/7.16.31)), are suited to. A reservoir combined with perturbation learning of a few parameters may be a good model for motor learning, where the dimension of the representation is expanded, while Hebbian and self-supervised rules may dominate in sensory systems, where it is reduced.

## 50.4. Training Spiking Neural Networks

**Definition 50.4.1 (Spiking Neural Network)** A **spiking neural network** is a network of model neurons, typically **leaky integrate-and-fire** neurons ([7.16.8](/7.16.8)), that communicate by discrete spikes. In discrete time with step $\Delta t$ and decay $\beta = \mathrm{e}^{-\Delta t/\tau_{\mathrm{m}}}$,
$$
V_i[t + 1] = \beta V_i[t] + (1 - \beta)\sum_j w_{ij}I_j[t] - \theta\,s_i[t], \qquad s_i[t] = H\bigl(V_i[t] - \theta\bigr), \tag{50.3}
$$
where $I_j$ are the synaptic currents generated by presynaptic spikes, $H$ is the Heaviside step function and the last term resets by subtraction after a spike.

Because $H$ has zero derivative everywhere except at the threshold, where it is undefined, the gradient of any loss with respect to the weights through Eq. (50.3) is zero almost everywhere: a small change of a weight changes spike times by a small amount only if spikes are treated as continuous, and changes the number of spikes discontinuously.

**Definition 50.4.2 (Surrogate Gradient)** The **surrogate gradient** method trains spiking networks by backpropagation through time in which the derivative $\mathrm{d}s/\mathrm{d}V = \delta(V - \theta)$ is replaced in the backward pass by a smooth pseudo-derivative, such as the fast-sigmoid surrogate $\sigma'(V) = 1/(1 + k\lvert V - \theta\rvert)^2$, while the forward pass keeps the true spikes (Neftci, Mostafa and Zenke, 2019).

The surrogate is a design choice, not the derivative of anything, and the resulting gradient is biased, but training is robust to the shape of the surrogate and its slope $k$ over a wide range (Zenke and Vogels, 2021). With surrogate gradients spiking networks reach accuracies close to those of conventional networks on vision and speech benchmarks, with sparse activity (figure below). In terms of the problems of 50.1, the surrogate solves the problem of derivatives but not the others; combined with e-prop or feedback alignment it gives learning rules that are local in space and time.

## 50.5. Neuromorphic Hardware and Energy

A human brain computes with about $20\,\mathrm{W}$ ([7.14.46](/7.14.46#sec-46.7)). The energy budget of Attwell and Laughlin (2001) attributes most of it to synaptic transmission and action potentials; one vesicle release costs about $1.6 \times 10^5$ ATP molecules, about $10^{-14}\,\mathrm{J}$ (with $\approx 5 \times 10^{-20}\,\mathrm{J}$ per ATP), and the brain performs on the order of $10^{14}$–$10^{15}$ synaptic events per second. A digital computer spends about $4 \times 10^{-12}\,\mathrm{J}$ for a 32-bit floating-point multiply–add and about $6 \times 10^{-10}\,\mathrm{J}$ to fetch a 32-bit word from off-chip memory (45-nm technology; Horowitz, 2014): moving data costs far more than computing on it, and the brain avoids the cost by storing weights in the synapses where they are used. A current GPU draws about $700\,\mathrm{W}$, and training a large language model consumes on the order of $10^3\,\mathrm{MWh}$.

**Definition 50.5.1 (Neuromorphic Computing)** **Neuromorphic computing** is computation in hardware whose architecture follows principles of nervous systems: memory co-located with processing, massive parallelism, event-driven communication by sparse spikes, and often analog or mixed-signal circuits whose physics implements the dynamics of neurons and synapses.

Digital neuromorphic chips include IBM's TrueNorth (2014; one million neurons and 256 million synapses on a chip consuming about $70\,\mathrm{mW}$, about $26\,\mathrm{pJ}$ per synaptic event), Intel's Loihi (2018; 128 cores, about 130 000 neurons, programmable on-chip learning rules including **STDP** and three-factor rules), the SpiNNaker system of ARM cores for simulating large spiking networks, and the Tianjic chip (Pei and colleagues, 2019), which supports both conventional and spiking networks on the same cores and was demonstrated in a self-driving bicycle. Event-driven processing pays off when activity is sparse; estimates for spiking networks trained by evolution strategies on Loihi suggest training energies about two orders of magnitude below those on a GPU ($\approx 28\,\mathrm{kJ}$ against $2\,\mathrm{MJ}$ for one locomotion task, Wang and colleagues, 2023).

**Definition 50.5.2 (Memristor)** A **memristor** is a two-terminal circuit element whose resistance depends on the history of the current that has flowed through it, so that it stores an analog state without power; predicted by Chua (1971) as the fourth basic circuit element relating charge and magnetic flux, it was identified in titanium dioxide thin films whose resistance changes by the drift of oxygen vacancies (Strukov, Snider, Stewart and Williams, 2008).

A crossbar of memristors with conductances $G_{ij}$ computes a matrix–vector product in one step by physics: applying voltages $V_i$ to the rows gives column currents $I_j = \sum_iG_{ij}V_i$ by Ohm's and Kirchhoff's laws, without moving the weights. Memristive synapses can implement STDP when overlapping pre- and postsynaptic voltage pulses produce a voltage across the device that depends on their timing (Jo and colleagues, 2010), and networks with memristive dendrites have been built. Device variability, limited precision (a few bits), drift and nonlinear, asymmetric updates are the main obstacles; they make learning rules that tolerate noise and low precision, such as perturbation and three-factor rules, attractive for such hardware, the same constraints under which biological synapses operate.

## 50.6. Rank-Coded and Distributional Reward Signals

A global reward signal must be scaled to be useful: the same physical reward is large or small depending on what was expected. Rank-based fitness shaping in evolution strategies (50.2.4) uses only the order of outcomes within a population. **Dopamine** neurons show a related adaptation: their responses to rewards scale with the range of rewards available in a given context, so that the best outcome in each context evokes a similar response (Tobler, Fiorillo and Schultz, 2005). They also show diversity. In **distributional reinforcement learning** ([7.16.39](/7.16.39)), each value predictor $v_i$ is updated with different learning rates $\alpha_i^+$ and $\alpha_i^-$ for positive and negative prediction errors, and converges to the $\tau_i$-expectile of the reward distribution with
$$
\tau_i = \frac{\alpha_i^+}{\alpha_i^+ + \alpha_i^-}; \tag{50.4}
$$
a population of predictors with different $\tau_i$ thus encodes the whole distribution of rewards, not just its mean. Dabney and colleagues (2020) found in mouse VTA that dopamine neurons differ in their reversal points (the reward size at which their response changes sign) and in their asymmetry of scaling, and that these two properties are correlated across neurons as the distributional model predicts, so that the reward distribution could be decoded from the population. A rank-coded or distributional teaching signal is a possible solution to the problem of learning from rewards whose scale varies, and links the learning rules of this chapter with the value learning of the basal ganglia.

## 50.7. Learning Algorithms Across Brain Structures

Machine learning distinguishes three paradigms by the information in the teaching signal: unsupervised learning (no teacher; the structure of the input), supervised learning (a target output for each input) and reinforcement learning (a scalar reward for actions). Doya (1999) proposed that three major brain structures specialize in them (figure below).

- *Cerebral cortex: unsupervised learning.* Hebbian plasticity with normalization extracts principal components and sparse features of the input ([7.16.22](/7.16.22), [7.16.20](/7.16.20)), the development of **ocular dominance columns** is the classic example ([7.16.21](/7.16.21)), and self-supervised prediction may be the cortical objective ([7.16.48](/7.16.48)).
- *Cerebellum: supervised learning.* Climbing fibers from the inferior olive deliver an error signal to **Purkinje cells**, and the coincidence of climbing fiber and **parallel fiber** activity depresses the parallel fiber synapses; this is the perceptron-like learning of the **Marr–Albus–Ito theory**, which learns **internal models** and precise timing ([7.16.33](/7.16.33)).
- *Basal ganglia: reinforcement learning.* **Dopamine** neurons report reward prediction errors, and corticostriatal synapses change by three-factor rules with **eligibility traces** ([7.16.39](/7.16.39), [7.16.40](/7.16.40)).

![Three learning paradigms and the brain structures proposed to specialize in them (after Doya, 1999). For each structure: the teaching signal, the form of the plasticity rule and what is learned. The structures are connected through cortico-cerebellar and cortico-basal ganglia–thalamic loops, so that a behavior is usually learned by all three at once.](./plots/learning-paradigms.py)

The division is a useful first approximation, not a strict assignment. The cortex also receives error and reward signals (dopamine and **acetylcholine** projections, mismatch responses in sensory cortex, [7.16.48](/7.16.48#sec-48.4)); the hippocampus learns from single episodes with **BTSP** and replays them to cortex ([7.16.42](/7.16.42)); and the prefrontal cortex appears to implement fast learning in its activity dynamics on top of slow synaptic learning (meta-learning, [7.16.41](/7.16.41)). What the brain lacks, compared with deep learning, is a single algorithm that trains all synapses on one objective; what it has is a combination of local rules, specialized teaching signals and architectures shaped by evolution, which together achieve fast, energy-efficient learning from little data.

## Summary

- The credit assignment problem (Definition 50.1.1) has a spatial and a temporal part; backpropagation (Eq. (50.1)) solves the spatial part exactly but requires weight transport, separate error signals, derivatives and backpropagation through time.

| Approach | Credit signal | Locality | Main limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Backpropagation | $W^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol{\delta}$ | Nonlocal (weight transport) | Biologically implausible |
| Feedback alignment | Random feedback $B\boldsymbol{\delta}$ | Local given feedback | Lags on deep, hard tasks |
| Target / equilibrium propagation | Targets or nudged phase | Local, two phases | Needs learned inverses or phases |
| Dendritic error, bursts | Apical–basal difference | Local in a neuron | Circuit details unconfirmed |
| Three-factor / e-prop | Eligibility × learning signal | Local in space and time | Approximate gradient |
| Perturbation / evolution strategies | Reward × perturbation | Fully local, scalar reward | Variance grows with $n$ (Proposition 50.2.1) |

- Feedback alignment (Definition 50.2.1) works because forward weights align with the fixed feedback; perturbation methods estimate the gradient of a smoothed reward without any backward pass (Eq. (50.2)).
- Cortical circuits may be shallower and more recurrent than deep networks, turning spatial into temporal credit assignment.
- Spiking networks (Definition 50.4.1) are trained with surrogate gradients (Definition 50.4.2) that replace the derivative of the spike threshold in the backward pass.
- Neuromorphic hardware (Definition 50.5.1) and memristive synapses (Definition 50.5.2) co-locate memory and computation; the brain spends about $10^{-14}\,\mathrm{J}$ per synaptic event against picojoules for a digital operation and more for memory access.
- Rank-coded and distributional reward signals (Eq. (50.4)) are observed in dopamine neurons. Cortex, cerebellum and basal ganglia are specialized, to a first approximation, for unsupervised, supervised and reinforcement learning.

## Questions

1. In a linear network $y = W_2W_1x$ trained with feedback alignment ($\Delta W_1 = -\eta B\boldsymbol{e}\boldsymbol{x}^{\mathsf T}$, $\Delta W_2 = -\eta\boldsymbol{e}(W_1\boldsymbol{x})^{\mathsf T}$, $\boldsymbol{e} = y - y^*$), show that $W_2$ accumulates a component proportional to $B^{\mathsf T}$ when both start near zero, and explain why this makes the hidden updates useful.

   **Answer** Starting from $W_1 \approx 0$, the first changes of $W_1$ are $\Delta W_1 = -\eta B\boldsymbol{e}\boldsymbol{x}^{\mathsf T}$, so after some time $W_1 \approx -\eta B\sum\boldsymbol{e}\boldsymbol{x}^{\mathsf T} = -BM$ with $M = \eta\sum\boldsymbol{e}\boldsymbol{x}^{\mathsf T}$. The hidden activity is then $W_1\boldsymbol{x} = -BM\boldsymbol{x}$, and the update of $W_2$ is $-\eta\boldsymbol{e}\boldsymbol{x}^{\mathsf T}W_1^{\mathsf T} = \eta\,\boldsymbol{e}\boldsymbol{x}^{\mathsf T}M^{\mathsf T}B^{\mathsf T}$; summed over samples, $\sum\boldsymbol{e}\boldsymbol{x}^{\mathsf T}M^{\mathsf T} \propto MM^{\mathsf T}$ is positive semidefinite, so $W_2$ grows as $(\text{positive semidefinite matrix})\,B^{\mathsf T}$. Then $W_2B = SB^{\mathsf T}B$ with $S$ positive semidefinite, and for a random $B$ with many more hidden units than outputs $B^{\mathsf T}B$ is close to a multiple of the identity, so $\boldsymbol{e}^{\mathsf T}W_2B\boldsymbol{e} \approx c\,\boldsymbol{e}^{\mathsf T}S\boldsymbol{e} \ge 0$: the true backpropagated signal $W_2^{\mathsf T}\boldsymbol{e}$ and the feedback signal $B\boldsymbol{e}$ make an angle of less than $90^\circ$. The hidden update along $B\boldsymbol{e}$ therefore has a positive projection on the gradient direction and decreases the loss (Lillicrap and colleagues, 2016, prove convergence for the linear case).

2. Using Proposition 50.2.1, estimate how many perturbation trials per update are needed for the antithetic ES estimate of a gradient in $n = 10^4$ dimensions to have a signal-to-noise ratio (squared norm of the mean over total variance) of 1. What does this imply for learning by synaptic noise in a brain?

   **Answer** The mean of the estimate is $\nabla F$ with squared norm $\lVert\nabla F\rVert^2$, and the total variance of the average of $N$ antithetic terms is $(n + 1)\lVert\nabla F\rVert^2/N$. The ratio is $N/(n + 1)$, so $N \approx 10^4$ trials are needed per update for a ratio of 1; with fewer trials the estimate still points uphill on average (its expected projection on the gradient is positive) but learning is slow, with progress per trial decreasing as $1/n$. For a brain this means that undirected perturbation can train only small numbers of effective parameters per reward signal (for example, the few dozen motor parameters of a song syllable), or must be combined with structure that reduces the effective dimension: perturbations restricted to a low-dimensional subspace, eligibility traces that localize credit in time, or a fixed reservoir with only a readout learned.

3. Why does the surrogate gradient method keep the true spikes in the forward pass instead of training a network with smooth sigmoid units and replacing them by spikes afterward? What is lost by treating the reset as a constant?

   **Answer** Keeping the spikes in the forward pass means that the loss is evaluated on the actual spiking network, with its discrete events, refractoriness, reset and the precise timing that the task may require; the gradient is approximate, but it is computed at the operating point of the network that will be used. A network trained with smooth units and converted to spikes afterward suffers from conversion errors (rates must be represented by many spikes, which costs latency and energy, and timing codes are lost). Treating the reset as a constant removes the term by which a spike lowers the subsequent membrane potential; the gradient then ignores the fact that an extra spike now delays the next one. In practice this simplification improves training stability, because the reset term contributes gradients of alternating sign through long chains of spikes, at the cost of a small additional bias.

4. A rank-based fitness transform replaces rewards $(1.1, 1.2, 0.8, 0.9)$ by centered ranks. Compute the transformed values, and state one advantage and one disadvantage compared with subtracting the mean reward.

   **Answer** The ranks from lowest to highest are $0.8 \to 0$, $0.9 \to 1$, $1.1 \to 2$, $1.2 \to 3$; dividing by $3$ and subtracting $0.5$ gives $(0.167, 0.5, -0.5, -0.167)$ for $(1.1, 1.2, 0.8, 0.9)$. Mean subtraction gives $(0.1, 0.2, -0.2, -0.1)$. The rank transform is invariant to any monotonic transformation and to the scale of the rewards, so the step size need not be retuned when rewards change by orders of magnitude, and a single outlier cannot dominate the update. Its disadvantage is that it discards the magnitude of differences: a perturbation that improves the reward enormously counts the same as one that improves it slightly, which reduces efficiency when reward differences are informative and well scaled.
