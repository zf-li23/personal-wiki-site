Molecular biology took living systems apart: it identified genes one at a time, purified their products and traced pathways step by step. **Systems biology** asks the complementary question, how the parts, measured all at once and in their natural context, produce the behavior of a cell, a tissue or a patient. The question became practical only when technologies appeared that measure every transcript, protein, chromatin state or metabolite of a sample in one experiment, and it is these **omics** data, not the classification of organisms, that the field is about. Its central objects are networks and models. The work falls into three tasks: measuring the states of many components, connecting them into networks of interactions, regulation and dependence, and explaining what such networks do in time. Most of it is an **inverse model** problem: from counts, spectra and images we infer abundances, then edges, then rate constants, and each step needs a statistical model of the measurement and a null model against which a pattern counts as real. The **forward model** of a small circuit, which computes behavior from wiring and parameters, closes the loop by telling us what an inferred network could do.

The chapters follow these three tasks. After the questions, the history and the layers of data, the first part treats the measurements one omics layer at a time, always asking what is measured, how reads or spectra become numbers and which statistical model fits them: bulk transcriptomes from library to **differential expression**, the gene sets that turn lists into processes, the layers beyond gene levels (splicing, translation, RNA modification), single cells, their positions in tissue in **spatial omics**, the trajectories and fates read from snapshots, the epigenome and the folded genome, and the proteome and metabolome. The second part treats networks as objects built from these data: the **interactome** and genetic-interaction maps and how far they can be trusted, the **gene regulatory network** inferred from binding, correlation, information and perturbation, the analysis of biological networks and its use in medicine, and cancer as the disease in which all of these methods meet. The third part gives the edges rates: kinetic models and the identifiability of their parameters, the **network motifs** of transcription networks and what each computes, feedback loops that make decisions and clocks, and finally noise, **robustness** and the evolution of networks. The course summary lays the omics methods side by side and shows that a few structures, counts with two sources of variation, mixtures, null models, Markov chains on graphs and the sign rule of feedback loops, recur across all three parts.

Systems biology has no sharp boundary with the two neighboring courses, and the pages divide the ground explicitly. Statistics is the foundation: the count models, normalization and **false discovery rate** of omics analysis are derived in [biostatistics](/7.7), largely on the same airway RNA-seq data that open the first part here. [Bioinformatics](/7.8) treats linear and semi-linear structures, sequences, alignments, genomes, variants and trees, and ends where its methods have produced counts; this course begins there and owns the networks. The general mathematics of networks, random graphs, **degree distributions**, **centrality**, **community structure**, percolation and the statistics of motifs, is developed in [network science](/1.4.7) within graph theory and used here without repetition. The dynamics draw on [ordinary differential equations](/2.4) and on the quantitative-biology chapters of [biophysics](/7.6) ([7.6.32](/7.6.32)–[7.6.41](/7.6.41)), where the master equation, toggle switches, oscillators and robust adaptation are already derived; the methods of dimension reduction and prediction come from [statistics](/3) and [machine learning](/4.6); and the molecular mechanisms behind every layer of data are those of [molecular biology](/7.2), [cell biology](/7.3) and [genetics](/7.4).

The name is easily confused with biological systematics, but the subject is the analysis of omics data, and I see biostatistics, bioinformatics and systems biology as three courses without a clear border between them. I regard statistics as the basis on which bioinformatics is learned, and I prefer to place the newer omics, RNA sequencing and the measurement of proteomes among them, within systems biology, because what they produce is knowledge from which networks can be built. That is why the first part of these pages is written as a sequence of measurements that each end in a kind of edge, and the second part takes the edges as the object of study. The Tsinghua course spent much of its time on sequencing; of the two Peking University courses, one began with network science and turned to systems biology in its second half, and the other organized genomic data analysis around writing one's own code, which is why many chapters here carry their analyses as code. The field still has few systematic textbooks, and Alon's book on the design principles of biological circuits is the only one I have used; its motifs and designs form the third part of the course.

My strongest impression of the field is an asymmetry. Methods for measuring the parts keep appearing in large numbers, new omics layers, finer resolutions, whole tissues and cohorts, while our understanding of networks remains shallow: degree distributions and motifs were found two decades ago, and the questions of causality, context and dynamics are still open ([7.9.18](/7.9.18#sec-18.9)). For this reason I think the connection to graph theory and network science matters more as the data grow, not less, and it is why network science has a chapter of its own in [graph theory](/1.4). **Spatial omics** ([7.9.6](/7.9.6)), one of the newest of these layers, which measures expression together with the arrangement of cells in a tissue, is my main research direction and has its own chapter here.
