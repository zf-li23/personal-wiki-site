The preceding chapters analyzed what network motifs compute when their components behave as averages: a feed-forward loop delays, a feedback loop switches or oscillates ([7.9.15](/7.9.15), [7.9.16](/7.9.16)). Real cells deviate from these averages in three ways, and each raises a question that this chapter addresses. Molecules are few, so every network is noisy, and the noise of one gene becomes an input of the next: how does variability spread through a network, and when is it useful rather than harmful (17.1)? Parameters vary between cells, individuals and environments: which designs make a function insensitive to them, inside a cell and at the level of tissues and organs (17.2, 17.3)? And networks are products of evolution: what determines whether a gene is regulated at all, which motif is chosen, why networks are modular and how they grow (17.4)? The last section turns the design principles into engineering, synthetic biology, whose failures are as instructive as its successes (17.5).

The chapter builds on the general theory in biophysics and does not repeat it. Stochastic kinetics, the chemical master equation, the **Gillespie algorithm** and the **linear noise approximation** are derived in [7.6.32](/7.6.32); the biology of **intrinsic noise** and **extrinsic noise** and of transcriptional bursts is in [7.2.22](/7.2.22#sec-22.5); the definitions of **robustness**, **logarithmic sensitivity**, sloppiness, **modularity** and Pareto optimality are in [7.6.40](/7.6.40), exact adaptation and **integral feedback** in [7.6.35](/7.6.35), the economics of protein expression in [7.6.39](/7.6.39) and the evolution of sequences on **fitness landscapes** in [7.6.41](/7.6.41). ## 17.1. Noise in Networks

### 17.1.1. Sources of Variability

Two genetically identical cells in the same medium contain different numbers of a given protein, typically by tens of percent. The variability has two parts ([Definition 22.5.3](/7.2.22#def-22.5.3) of 7.2.22): **intrinsic noise**, from the random timing of the reactions that make and destroy the molecules of this gene, and **extrinsic noise**, from fluctuations of everything that affects the gene from outside, the numbers of polymerases and ribosomes, cell size and stage, and the levels of upstream regulators. The second part is where networks enter: the extrinsic noise of a gene includes the intrinsic noise of its regulators, transmitted along the edges of the network.

Measurements across the proteome of *E. coli* and of yeast show a common pattern (Bar-Even and colleagues, 2006; Taniguchi and colleagues, 2010). For proteins of low abundance the squared coefficient of variation $\eta^2 = \operatorname{Var}(x)/\langle x\rangle^2$ falls in inverse proportion to the mean, as expected for intrinsic noise; for abundant proteins it levels off at a floor that does not depend on the protein, the extrinsic noise shared by all genes. The linear noise approximation of the two-stage model of expression gives the decomposition (Example 32.6.1 of [7.6.32](/7.6.32#sec-32.6.2), for mRNA much shorter-lived than protein),

$$
\eta_p^2 \approx \frac{1}{\langle p\rangle} + \frac{\gamma_p}{k_m} + \eta_{\mathrm{ext}}^2,
$$
where $k_m$ is the transcription rate and $\gamma_p$ the removal rate of the protein: Poisson noise of the proteins, noise of the few mRNAs from which they are made, and a floor.

### 17.1.2. Propagation through a Cascade

Let a transcription factor $X$ regulate a gene $Y$. The fluctuations of $X$ change the production rate of $Y$, and $Y$ inherits them, filtered by its own dynamics.

**Definition 17.1.1 (Propagated Noise)** In a network in which a species $Y$ is produced at a rate that depends on another species $X$, the **propagated noise** of $Y$ is the part of its variance caused by the fluctuations of $X$, as opposed to the intrinsic noise generated by the reactions of $Y$ itself and the global noise shared by all genes.

**Theorem 17.1.1 (Noise in a Two-Stage Cascade)** Let $X$ be made at rate $k_x$ and removed with rate constant $\gamma_x$, and let $Y$ be made at rate $f(x)$ and removed with rate constant $\gamma_y$. In the linear noise approximation, at the steady state $(\langle x\rangle, \langle y\rangle)$,

$$
\eta_y^2 = \frac{1}{\langle y\rangle} + H^2\,\frac{\gamma_y}{\gamma_x + \gamma_y}\,\eta_x^2, \qquad H = \frac{\mathrm{d}\ln f}{\mathrm{d}\ln x}\Big|_{\langle x\rangle}, \qquad \eta_x^2 = \frac{1}{\langle x\rangle}. \tag{17.1}
$$

**Proof** With $\boldsymbol{x} = (x, y)$, the Jacobian and the diffusion matrix of [Definition 32.6.2](/7.6.32#def-32.6.2) are

$$
J = \begin{bmatrix} -\gamma_x & 0 \\ f' & -\gamma_y \end{bmatrix}, \qquad D = \begin{bmatrix} 2\gamma_x\langle x\rangle & 0 \\ 0 & 2\gamma_y\langle y\rangle \end{bmatrix},
$$
with $f' = f'(\langle x\rangle)$, since at steady state synthesis and removal of each species occur at equal rates. The Lyapunov equation $JC + CJ^{\mathsf T} + D = 0$ gives, entry by entry, $C_{xx} = \langle x\rangle$, then $f'C_{xx} - (\gamma_x + \gamma_y)C_{xy} = 0$, so $C_{xy} = f'\langle x\rangle/(\gamma_x + \gamma_y)$, and $2f'C_{xy} - 2\gamma_yC_{yy} + 2\gamma_y\langle y\rangle = 0$, so

$$
C_{yy} = \langle y\rangle + \frac{f'^2\langle x\rangle}{\gamma_y(\gamma_x + \gamma_y)}.
$$
Since $\gamma_y\langle y\rangle = f(\langle x\rangle)$, the gain is $H = f'\langle x\rangle/(\gamma_y\langle y\rangle)$, so $f'^2 = H^2\gamma_y^2\langle y\rangle^2/\langle x\rangle^2$. Dividing $C_{yy}$ by $\langle y\rangle^2$ gives Eq. (17.1). $\square$

The formula has three factors with separate meanings. The gain $H$, a logarithmic sensitivity, measures how strongly the promoter of $Y$ responds to relative changes of $X$: a Hill function with coefficient $n$ has $H$ up to $n$ below its midpoint, and a saturated promoter has $H \approx 0$ and transmits nothing. The factor $\gamma_y/(\gamma_x + \gamma_y) = \tau_x/(\tau_x + \tau_y)$ is a time average: $Y$ integrates its production over its lifetime $\tau_y = 1/\gamma_y$, and fluctuations of $X$ that are faster than this are averaged out. And $\eta_x^2$ is the noise of the input; the derivation holds whenever the fluctuations of $X$ relax with the single rate $\gamma_x$, so $\eta_x^2$ may also include the propagated noise of the regulators of $X$. Applied stage by stage, Eq. (17.1) describes a cascade: noise is amplified along a chain with gains $H > 1$ and filtered with gains $H < 1$. Adding global extrinsic noise gives the three-part decomposition, intrinsic, propagated and global, that Pedraza and van Oudenaarden (2005) measured in a synthetic cascade in *E. coli* by fluorescent reporters of each stage; Hooshangi, Thiberge and Weiss (2005) found that longer synthetic repressor cascades were more ultrasensitive and more variable from cell to cell.

**Example 17.1.1** Let $\langle x\rangle = 50$, $Y$ be activated with $f(x) = 200\gamma_y\,x^2/(50^2 + x^2)$, so that $\langle y\rangle \approx 100$ and $H = 1$ at $x = 50$. If the two proteins have equal lifetimes, Eq. (17.1) gives $\eta_y^2 = 0.010 + 0.5 \times 0.020 = 0.020$, half intrinsic and half propagated; an exact Gillespie simulation gives $0.0196$. If $X$ turns over ten times faster than $Y$, the propagated part falls to $0.020/11 = 0.0018$ and $\eta_y^2 = 0.0118$ (simulation $0.0119$).

### 17.1.3. Motifs, the Noisiest Step and the Limits of Feedback

The analysis of 7.6.32 and 7.2.22 gives the effect of the simplest motifs on noise. Negative autoregulation reduces the Fano factor to $1/(1 + H)$ (Eq. (32.17) of [7.6.32](/7.6.32#sec-32.6.3)) and, at equal mean, narrows the distribution of a protein, as Becskei and Serrano (2000) observed; positive autoregulation broadens it, and near a bifurcation the distribution becomes bimodal, two subpopulations that the deterministic model would describe as two stable states (**bistability**). Because noise passes through every link with a gain and a time average, the architecture determines where it is amplified. A feed-forward loop whose output requires both inputs (the AND-gated coherent loop, [Definition 15.4.1](/7.9.15#def-15.4.1)) ignores brief fluctuations of its input in the same way as brief signals, a filter that the delay of the loop sets.

Within a single gene, the position of the noisiest step matters (McAdams and Arkin, 1999; Ozbudak and colleagues, 2002). Each step multiplies the fluctuations of the steps before it, so a step with few molecules early in the chain dominates the noise of the output.

**Example 17.1.2** Two genes make on average $100$ proteins per hour. Gene A makes one mRNA per hour (Poisson) that is translated into a geometrically distributed number of proteins with mean $100$; gene B makes $100$ mRNAs per hour, each translated into a mean of $1$ protein. The number of proteins made in an hour is a compound Poisson variable with variance $\lambda\,\mathbb{E}[B^2] = \lambda(b + 2b^2)$ for $\lambda$ mRNAs of burst size $b$: $20\,100$ for A and $300$ for B, coefficients of variation $1.42$ and $0.17$. The two genes have the same mean and the same cost in proteins, but A pays a factor of eight in noise for saving $99$ transcripts.

:::

Feedback cannot remove noise without limit. Lestas, Vinnicombe and Paulsson (2010) treated a molecule whose level is controlled by any feedback that acts through a second signaling molecule made in discrete events, and derived from information theory a lower bound on the achievable variance: the standard deviation can decrease at most with the fourth root of the number of signaling events, so that a tenfold reduction requires about ten thousand times more of them. Noise suppression by feedback is therefore expensive, and the observed noise of a gene reflects a trade-off between precision and cost, not only the limits of chemistry; essential proteins and subunits of complexes are among the least noisy in yeast, and stress-response proteins among the noisiest (Bar-Even and colleagues, 2006).

### 17.1.4. Using Noise: Bet Hedging and Fate Decisions

When the future is unpredictable, variability among the members of a clone can be an advantage. A population whose cells all make the same choice is wiped out when the choice is wrong; a population that spreads its cells over several phenotypes loses some in every environment but never all.

**Definition 17.1.2 (Bet Hedging)** **Bet hedging** is a strategy in which a genotype produces, without sensing the environment, a distribution of phenotypes that lowers the mean fitness in each typical environment but raises the long-term growth rate in a fluctuating environment.

The long-term growth rate is the right measure because populations multiply: after $T$ periods with growth factors $w_1, \dots, w_T$ the size is $\prod_tw_t$, and its growth rate per period is the mean of $\ln w_t$, not of $w_t$. Consider two environments, $\mathrm{E}_1$ with probability $p$ and $\mathrm{E}_2$ with probability $1 - p$ independently in each period, and two phenotypes, and let every cell give a fraction $q$ of its offspring phenotype A. If phenotype A multiplies by $a_1$ in $\mathrm{E}_1$ and $a_2$ in $\mathrm{E}_2$, and B by $b_1$ and $b_2$, the long-term growth rate is

$$
\Lambda(q) = p\ln\left[qa_1 + (1 - q)b_1\right] + (1 - p)\ln\left[qa_2 + (1 - q)b_2\right]. \tag{17.2}
$$

**Theorem 17.1.2 (Proportional Bet Hedging)** If each phenotype survives only in its own environment ($a_2 = b_1 = 0$), $\Lambda(q)$ is maximal at $q^* = p$, whatever $a_1$ and $b_2$, and

$$
\Lambda(q^*) = p\ln a_1 + (1 - p)\ln b_2 - H(p), \qquad H(p) = -p\ln p - (1 - p)\ln(1 - p).
$$

**Proof** Here $\Lambda(q) = p\ln q + (1 - p)\ln(1 - q) + p\ln a_1 + (1 - p)\ln b_2$, a concave function of $q$ with derivative $p/q - (1 - p)/(1 - q)$, which vanishes at $q = p$. Substituting gives the value. $\square$

The result is the Kelly criterion of gambling (Kelly, 1956), here applied to offspring: bet on each outcome in proportion to its probability. The term $p\ln a_1 + (1 - p)\ln b_2$ is the growth rate of a population that senses the environment and always expresses the right phenotype, so the loss of the bet-hedger is exactly the entropy $H(p)$ of the environment: the value of perfect information, in nats per period, equals the **mutual information** between environment and phenotype that sensing would provide ([7.6.35](/7.6.35#sec-35.3)). Sensing pays when its cost is below this value; with sensing that is costly, slow or unreliable, random switching can be the better design, and the optimal switching rates match the statistics of the environment (Kussell and Leibler, 2005).

**Example 17.1.3** With $a_1 = 3$, $b_2 = 2$ and $p = 0.8$, proportional hedging gives $\Lambda = 0.517$ per period, sensing $1.018$; the difference is $H(0.8) = 0.500$ nats ($0.72$ bits). In the more realistic model of the figure, a growing phenotype that multiplies by $4$ in good periods but survives with $2\,\%$ under stress, and a dormant phenotype that keeps $100\,\%$ and $80\,\%$, the optimum at $p = 0.8$ is $q^* = 0.754$, with $\Lambda = 0.636$, against $0.327$ for a population that always grows and $-0.045$ for one that always rests; hedging is optimal for $0.245 < p < 0.981$, and outside this window a pure strategy is best.

Bacterial persistence is the best-studied case. A small fraction of an *E. coli* population, the persisters, grows slowly or not at all and survives antibiotics that kill growing cells; Balaban, Merrin, Chait, Kowalik and Leibler (2004) followed single cells in microfluidic channels and showed that the persisters exist before the drug is added, as a phenotypic state that cells enter and leave stochastically, not as a response to it. The ecology and the clinical consequences of persistence belong to microbiology ([7.11](/7.11)).

Noise also drives transient differentiation. In *Bacillus subtilis*, a small and variable fraction of cells becomes competent for DNA uptake: the master regulator ComK activates its own gene, and fluctuations occasionally push a cell over the threshold of this positive loop, after which a slower negative loop returns it, an excitable circuit that turns noise into rare, timed excursions (Süel, Garcia-Ojalvo, Liberman and Elowitz, 2006). The lysis–**lysogeny** decision of phage λ is a third example. Arkin, Ross and McAdams (1998) simulated the early network of λ, with the activator CII, the repressor CI and Cro, by the Gillespie algorithm and showed that the fraction of infected cells choosing lysogeny follows from the random timing of the production of these few molecules; the model reproduced the rise of lysogeny with the number of phages infecting a cell, because more genomes make CII faster than the host protease can degrade it ([7.2.11](/7.2.11)). Later single-cell experiments showed that part of what had been attributed to noise is deterministic variation in cell volume and in the number of infecting phages (St-Pierre and Endy, 2008; Zeng and colleagues, 2010): the separation of a decision into chance and hidden variables is itself an empirical question.

## 17.2. Robust Circuits

### 17.2.1. A Map of Robust Designs

[Definition 40.1.1](/7.6.40#def-40.1.1) of 7.6.40 makes robustness precise: a property is robust to a class of perturbations if it is maintained over a stated range of them. The circuits studied in this course and in biophysics show a small set of designs that make particular properties robust; the table collects them, with the chapter where each is derived.

| Design | Robust property | To what | Principle | Derived in |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **kinetic proofreading** | error rate of recognition (translation, T-cell receptor) | small differences in binding energy | each irreversible, energy-consuming step multiplies the discrimination | [7.6.38](/7.6.38#sec-38.5.2), [7.2.19](/7.2.19) |
| negative autoregulation | steady-state level | production rate | sensitivity $1/(1 + H)$ | [7.6.40](/7.6.40#sec-40.1.2) |
| bifunctional sensor | input–output curve | levels of all circuit proteins | output set by a ratio of rate constants | 17.2.2 |
| **integral feedback** | precision of adaptation | protein levels, step size | the controller integrates the error | [7.6.35](/7.6.35#sec-35.6.2) |
| **fold-change detection** | whole response to relative changes | absolute input level, background | scale invariance | [7.6.35](/7.6.35#sec-35.6.3), [7.9.15](/7.9.15#sec-15.4.4) |
| self-enhanced morphogen degradation | position of pattern boundaries | morphogen production rate | power-law gradients | [7.5.11](/7.5.11#sec-11.6), [7.6.40](/7.6.40#sec-40.2.3) |
| dynamical compensation | dynamics of a physiological output | sensitivity of target tissues | tissue size as a slow integrator | 17.3 |

Three common features stand out. A robust quantity is a ratio or a set point fixed by structure (a conservation law, a balance of two fluxes catalyzed by the same molecule, the zero of an integrator), not the value of any concentration. The robustness is usually bought, with energy (proofreading, adaptation), with extra components or with slower dynamics. And robustness to one class of perturbations comes with fragility to another (the "robust yet fragile" principle, [7.6.40](/7.6.40#sec-40.4.3)), a theme to which the tissue circuits of 17.3 add a striking example.

### 17.2.2. Robust Signaling by Bifunctional Components

Most bacterial **two-component systems** ([Definition 15.5.1](/7.1.15#def-15.5.1) of 7.1.15) transmit a signal from a membrane histidine kinase to a response regulator. Their input–output curve, the level of phosphorylated regulator as a function of the stimulus, must be reliable, although the levels of both proteins vary between cells by tens of percent. A design with a kinase $X$ and a separate phosphatase $Z$ cannot achieve this: at steady state $vX(Y_{\mathrm{T}} - Y_{\mathrm{p}}) = v_ZZY_{\mathrm{p}}$, so $Y_{\mathrm{p}} = vXY_{\mathrm{T}}/(vX + v_ZZ)$ depends on all three protein levels. In the osmosensor EnvZ–OmpR of *E. coli*, the sensor is bifunctional: EnvZ autophosphorylates with ATP, transfers the phosphoryl group to OmpR, and in its unphosphorylated form also dephosphorylates OmpR-P (Russo and Silhavy, 1993).

**Formula 17.2.1 (Input–Output Curve of a Bifunctional Sensor)** Let the sensor $X$ autophosphorylate with rate constant $k_{\mathrm{a}}(S)$, which depends on the signal $S$, let $X_{\mathrm{p}}$ transfer its phosphate to $Y$, and let unphosphorylated $X$ dephosphorylate $Y_{\mathrm{p}}$ through a complex $XY_{\mathrm{p}}$ with Michaelis constant $K_2$ and catalytic constant $k_{\mathrm{p}}$. Then at any steady state with free $X > 0$,

$$
Y_{\mathrm{p}} = \frac{k_{\mathrm{a}}(S)\,K_2}{k_{\mathrm{p}}},
$$
independent of the total amounts of $X$ and $Y$.

**Proof** Phosphoryl groups enter the circuit only by autophosphorylation, at the rate $k_{\mathrm{a}}X$, and leave only by dephosphorylation of $Y_{\mathrm{p}}$, at the rate $k_{\mathrm{p}}[XY_{\mathrm{p}}]$; at steady state the two are equal. The complex is at steady state too, $[XY_{\mathrm{p}}] = XY_{\mathrm{p}}/K_2$. Hence $k_{\mathrm{a}}X = k_{\mathrm{p}}XY_{\mathrm{p}}/K_2$, and the free sensor $X$ cancels. $\square$

The cancellation requires every detail of the mechanism (Shinar, Milo, Martínez and Alon, 2007): if the phosphatase were a separate protein, or if the kinase transferred phosphate from ATP directly without phosphorylating itself, a protein level would remain in the balance. The units of concentration of the output come from the ratio of a first-order to a second-order rate constant, properties of the protein structures that are the same in every cell. Batchelor and Goulian (2003) varied the level of OmpR over a wide range in *E. coli* and found that the output at a given osmolarity changed by less than $20\,\%$ over a tenfold change. The robustness holds only while the predicted $Y_{\mathrm{p}}$ is below the available regulator; a cell with too little OmpR saturates.

## 17.3. Dynamical Compensation in Physiological Circuits

Circuits made of cells face problems that circuits made of molecules do not. Cells arise from cells, so their numbers grow or decline exponentially unless their proliferation and death are balanced by feedback; a hormone acts on distant tissues whose responsiveness varies between individuals; and among the billions of cell divisions of a tissue, mutants arise that misread the feedback signal. The insulin–glucose system shows how one design addresses all three (Karin, Swisa, Glaser, Dor and Alon, 2016; Karin and Alon, 2017). The physiology of the endocrine pancreas belongs to [7.14](/7.14); here the circuit is analyzed as a network.

### 17.3.1. The Minimal Model Is Not Robust

Blood glucose $G$ is raised by meals and by production in the liver and lowered by uptake into muscle, liver and fat, which **insulin** promotes. Insulin $I$ is secreted by the β cells of the pancreatic islets, of total functional mass $B$, at a rate that increases with glucose. The minimal model, in the form used by Bergman and colleagues (1979) for clinical tests, is

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}G}{\mathrm{d}t} = m - sIG, \qquad \frac{\mathrm{d}I}{\mathrm{d}t} = qBf(G) - \gamma I, \tag{17.3}
$$
with meal and production input $m$, insulin sensitivity $s$ (the effect of a unit of insulin on the rate of glucose removal), secretion per unit β-cell mass $qf(G)$ and insulin removal rate $\gamma$. Insulin sensitivity varies several-fold between healthy people with exercise, pregnancy, infection, obesity and genetic background; low sensitivity is **insulin resistance**. Yet fasting glucose is held near $5\,\mathrm{mM}$ in most of them, and the glucose curve after a standard oral load is nearly the same.

**Formula 17.3.1 (Steady State of the Minimal Model)** For a constant input $m_0$ and $f(G) = G^2$, the steady state of Eq. (17.3) is

$$
G_{\mathrm{st}} = \left(\frac{\gamma m_0}{sqB}\right)^{1/3},
$$
since $sI_{\mathrm{st}}G_{\mathrm{st}} = m_0$ and $qBG_{\mathrm{st}}^2 = \gamma I_{\mathrm{st}}$.

A tenfold fall of $s$ therefore raises fasting glucose by $10^{1/3} = 2.15$, from $5$ to $10.8\,\mathrm{mM}$, a diabetic level, and the excursion after a meal becomes larger and longer (figure, A). With fixed β-cell mass, the circuit is not robust to the very parameter that varies most.

### 17.3.2. A Slow Feedback on Cell Number

The β-cell mass is not fixed. Glucose controls the proliferation and death of β cells: at low glucose they die faster than they divide, and the two rates cross near $5\,\mathrm{mM}$ (Topp, Promislow, de Vries, Miura and Finegood, 2000). Writing the net growth rate as $\mu(G)$, with $\mu(G_0) = 0$ and $\mu'(G_0) > 0$, extends the minimal model to the β-cell–insulin–glucose (BIG) model,

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}G}{\mathrm{d}t} = m - sIG, \qquad \frac{\mathrm{d}I}{\mathrm{d}t} = qBf(G) - \gamma I, \qquad \frac{\mathrm{d}B}{\mathrm{d}t} = B\,\mu(G). \tag{17.4}
$$
The third equation can be at steady state only at $G = G_0$, whatever the other parameters: it is an integral controller ([Definition 35.6.2](/7.6.35#def-35.6.2) of 7.6.35), with the cell mass as the integrating variable and weeks as its time scale. It explains more than the steady state.

**Definition 17.3.1 (Dynamical Compensation)** A system has **dynamical compensation** with respect to a parameter if, starting from its steady state, the entire time course of its output in response to any input is independent of the value of that parameter, although the parameter affects the output when the system starts away from steady state.

**Theorem 17.3.1 (Dynamical Compensation of the BIG Model)** The glucose response $G(t)$ of the BIG model (17.4) to any input $m(t)$, starting from its steady state, does not depend on the insulin sensitivity $s$ or on the secretion parameter $q$.

**Proof** Let $\tilde I = sI$ and $\tilde B = sqB$. Multiplying the insulin equation by $s$ and the β-cell equation by $sq$ gives

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}G}{\mathrm{d}t} = m - \tilde IG, \qquad \frac{\mathrm{d}\tilde I}{\mathrm{d}t} = \tilde Bf(G) - \gamma\tilde I, \qquad \frac{\mathrm{d}\tilde B}{\mathrm{d}t} = \tilde B\,\mu(G),
$$
in which $s$ and $q$ do not appear. The initial steady state is also independent of them: $G_{\mathrm{st}} = G_0$ from the third equation, $\tilde I_{\mathrm{st}} = m_0/G_0$ from the first and $\tilde B_{\mathrm{st}} = \gamma m_0/(G_0f(G_0))$ from the second. Equations and initial conditions being free of $s$ and $q$, so is the solution $G(t)$. $\square$

The proof shows what the compensation requires: the β-cell equation is linear in $B$ (cells come from cells) and secretion is proportional to $B$ (each cell secretes), so a change of $s$ can be absorbed into the cell mass. Insulin itself does depend on $s$: $sI_{\mathrm{st}} = m_0/G_0$ is constant, so less sensitive people have proportionally more insulin and more β cells. This is the hyperbolic relation between insulin sensitivity and insulin secretion observed among healthy people (Kahn and colleagues, 1993), whose deviation below the hyperbola marks failing compensation. The same argument covers changes of blood volume, which act like changes of $q$; there is no compensation for the insulin removal rate $\gamma$, which sets the time scale of the insulin equation. Karin and colleagues showed that the conditions are general: a hormone secreted in proportion to the mass of its gland, whose growth is controlled by the regulated variable, gives dynamical compensation, and the parathyroid control of blood calcium has this structure.

**Example 17.3.1** In the illustrative model of the figure ($G_0 = 5\,\mathrm{mM}$, $\gamma = 2\,\mathrm{h^{-1}}$, $\mu$ with slope $\mu'(G_0)G_0 = 0.1\,\mathrm{d^{-1}}$), halving $s$ raises fasting glucose at once from $5.0$ to $6.3\,\mathrm{mM}$. Over the following weeks the β-cell mass doubles, half of the way in $27$ days (the linearization, with glucose at its quasi-steady state $G \propto (sB)^{-1/3}$, predicts a rate $\mu'(G_0)G_0/3$ and a half time of $21$ days), and glucose returns to $5\,\mathrm{mM}$. After compensation, the glucose response to a meal, peaking at $8.52\,\mathrm{mM}$, coincides with that before the change, and the insulin peak is twice as high ($4.21$ against $2.11$ in model units). During the transient the response is abnormal.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.integrate import solve_ivp

G0, G1, m0, gam, mu0 = 5.0, 12.0, 5.0, 2.0, 0.1   # mM, mM, mM/h, 1/h, 1/day
qB0 = gam * (m0 / G0) / G0**2                     # steady state with s = 1, B = 1

def mu(G):                                        # net beta-cell growth rate, 1/day
    return mu0 * (G / G0 - 1) * (1 - G / G1) * G1 / (G1 - G0)

def slow(t, z, s):                                # glucose and insulin at quasi-steady state
    G = (m0 * gam / (s * qB0 * np.exp(z[0]))) ** (1 / 3)
    return [mu(G)]

sol = solve_ivp(slow, (0, 150), [0.0], args=(0.5,), dense_output=True, rtol=1e-9)
t = np.linspace(0, 150, 15001)
B = np.exp(sol.sol(t)[0])
print("half-compensation time (days):", t[np.argmax(B >= 1.5)])
```

### 17.3.3. Glucotoxicity, Instability and Type 2 Diabetes

At very high glucose the death rate of β cells rises again, a phenomenon called glucotoxicity, which includes loss of function by exhaustion, dedifferentiation and death and is mediated in part by reactive oxygen species from accelerated glycolysis. The net growth rate $\mu(G)$ then has a second zero $G_1 > G_0$. Linearizing the slow dynamics at a zero shows that $G_0$, where $\mu' > 0$, is stable, and $G_1$, where $\mu' < 0$, is unstable: above $G_1$ a rise of glucose kills β cells, which lowers insulin and raises glucose further. This vicious circle is a natural model of the progression of **type 2 diabetes**. It explains the main risk factors qualitatively: diets that keep glucose high for long periods push the system towards $G_1$; with age, β-cell proliferation slows, which lowers $G_1$ and raises $G_0$ slightly; and genetic variation of the glucotoxicity curve moves $G_1$ closer to $G_0$. In the model of the figure, with $G_1 = 12\,\mathrm{mM}$ and glucose at its quasi-steady state $G \propto (sB)^{-1/3}$, a sudden fall of insulin sensitivity by a factor larger than $(G_1/G_0)^3 = 13.8$ throws the system beyond the unstable point, whereas a slow fall of any size is compensated.

### 17.3.4. Mutant Resistance

Why should evolution have built in an instability? Karin and Alon (2017) pointed to the third problem of tissues. A gram of tissue contains of the order of $10^9$ cells; with turnover and a point **mutation rate** of the order of $10^{-9}$ per base pair and division, every possible point mutation arises in some cell. Among them are mutations that make a β cell misread glucose, for instance by lowering the half-saturation constant of **glucokinase**, the glucose sensor of β cells, which is half-saturated near $8\,\mathrm{mM}$ (hexokinases of other cells are saturated below $0.1\,\mathrm{mM}$, [7.1.18](/7.1.18)). A cell that perceives $\lambda G$ instead of $G$ with $\lambda > 1$ proliferates as if glucose were high, has a growth advantage at normal glucose, and its clone takes over the tissue; it then regulates glucose to the level it perceives as $G_0$, the real level $G_0/\lambda$, a dangerous hypoglycemia.

**Definition 17.3.2 (Mutant Resistance)** A tissue feedback circuit has **mutant resistance** if cells that misread the feedback signal have a growth disadvantage at the normal set point, so that their clones are eliminated rather than taking over the tissue.

A monotonic $\mu(G)$ gives every mutant with $\lambda > 1$ an advantage. A biphasic, U-shaped dependence of death on the signal gives mutants that strongly misread it a disadvantage: a cell that sees $\lambda G_0 > G_1$ is in the region of net death and removes itself. The price is the unstable point $G_1$ and with it the vulnerability to disease: resistance to mutants and resistance to the vicious circle are traded against each other. Mutants of intermediate effect, $1 < \lambda < G_1/G_0$, still spread, but slowly, and the division of the β-cell mass into islets of about a thousand cells confines each clone to one islet. Biphasic responses are common in physiology, in the toxicity of overexcitation and underexcitation for neurons and of very low and very high antigen levels for lymphocytes, and the argument suggests one reason.

**Example 17.3.2** In the model of the figure, a clone with $\lambda = 5$ that arises as a fraction $10^{-6}$ of the β-cell mass grows at $0.4\,\mathrm{d^{-1}}$ in a tissue with monotonic $\mu$, reaches half of the mass in $36$ days and drives glucose to $1\,\mathrm{mM}$. With glucotoxicity the same clone has a net growth rate of $-0.74\,\mathrm{d^{-1}}$ and vanishes. A clone with $\lambda = 1.5$ grows at $0.032\,\mathrm{d^{-1}}$, reaches half of the mass after $424$ days and lowers fasting glucose to $3.3\,\mathrm{mM}$.

## 17.4. The Evolution of Network Structure

### 17.4.1. To Regulate or Not

Robustness and noise concern networks as they are; evolution determines what they are. The simplest question is why some genes are regulated and others expressed constitutively. Optimal expression levels under constant conditions were treated with the cost–benefit model of the lac operon in [7.6.39](/7.6.39#sec-39.3.3); here the environment changes.

**Formula 17.4.1 (When Regulation Pays)** Let a gene product $Z$ give a benefit $b$ only in a condition that occurs a fraction $p$ of the time (the demand), let its production cost $c$ while it is expressed, and let a regulatory system that expresses $Z$ only when needed cost $r$ at all times. The fitness of constitutive expression, of regulation and of the absence of the gene are

$$
F_1 = pb - c, \qquad F_2 = p(b - c) - r, \qquad F_3 = 0,
$$
so regulation is selected when $r/(b - c) < p < 1 - r/c$, constitutive expression when $p > \max(c/b, 1 - r/c)$, and loss of the gene otherwise.

Regulation is favored at intermediate demand, and in constant environments, where $p$ is $0$ or $1$ for every gene, regulation should disappear. The endosymbiont *Buchnera aphidicola*, a relative of *E. coli* living inside aphid cells, has kept about $600$ of the roughly $4500$ genes of its free-living relatives and almost no transcription factors, whereas soil bacteria with variable environments have large genomes rich in regulators; across bacteria, the number of transcription factors grows faster than linearly with the number of genes, about as its square (van Nimwegen, 2003). When the environment changes faster than regulation can follow, the bet hedging of 17.1.4 becomes the fourth strategy.

### 17.4.2. Environmental Selection of the Feed-Forward Loop

Many of the *E. coli* genes regulated by two transcription factors are regulated by a coherent feed-forward loop, the others by two independent inputs. Dekel, Mangan and Alon (2005) asked under which environments the delay of the loop (Proposition 15.4.1 of [7.9.15](/7.9.15#prop-15.4.1)) pays for itself. Let the input arrive in pulses of duration $D$, during which simple regulation makes $Z$ at rate $\beta$, with $Z(t) = (\beta/\alpha)(1 - \mathrm{e}^{-\alpha t})$, at a cost $c\beta$ per unit time and a benefit $b(Z)$. The fitness gained during a pulse is

$$
\varphi(D) = -c\beta D + \int_0^D b(Z(t))\,\mathrm{d}t \approx -c\beta D + \frac{b'\beta D^2}{2} \qquad (\alpha D \ll 1), \tag{17.5}
$$
negative for pulses shorter than $D_{\mathrm{c}} \approx 2c/b'$: brief pulses pay the cost of making $Z$ before it accumulates enough to be useful. A feed-forward loop with delay $T_{\mathrm{ON}}$ ignores pulses shorter than $T_{\mathrm{ON}}$ and uses the others for $D - T_{\mathrm{ON}}$ only, so with a distribution $P(D)$ of pulses, far apart,

$$
\Phi_{\mathrm{simple}} = \int_0^\infty P(D)\varphi(D)\,\mathrm{d}D, \qquad \Phi_{\mathrm{FFL}} = \int_{T_{\mathrm{ON}}}^\infty P(D)\varphi(D - T_{\mathrm{ON}})\,\mathrm{d}D.
$$

**Theorem 17.4.1 (Memoryless Pulses Do Not Select the FFL)** If the pulse durations are exponentially distributed, $P(D) = D_0^{-1}\mathrm{e}^{-D/D_0}$, then $\Phi_{\mathrm{FFL}} = \mathrm{e}^{-T_{\mathrm{ON}}/D_0}\Phi_{\mathrm{simple}}$ for every delay, so the FFL is never fitter than simple regulation when either is worth having.

**Proof** Substituting $D = T_{\mathrm{ON}} + u$ gives $\Phi_{\mathrm{FFL}} = \int_0^\infty D_0^{-1}\mathrm{e}^{-(T_{\mathrm{ON}} + u)/D_0}\varphi(u)\,\mathrm{d}u = \mathrm{e}^{-T_{\mathrm{ON}}/D_0}\Phi_{\mathrm{simple}}$. If $\Phi_{\mathrm{simple}} > 0$ this is smaller; if $\Phi_{\mathrm{simple}} \le 0$, neither circuit improves on not expressing $Z$. $\square$

The exponential distribution is memoryless: a pulse that has lasted for $T_{\mathrm{ON}}$ is no more likely to last longer than a new one, so waiting gains no information. A delay is useful only when the duration already elapsed predicts the remaining duration.

**Formula 17.4.2 (Bimodal Pulses)** Let pulses be brief, of duration $D_1 \ll D_{\mathrm{c}}$, with probability $p$, and long, $D_2 \gg 1/\alpha$, with probability $1 - p$. With the optimal delay $T_{\mathrm{ON}} = D_1$,

$$
\Phi_{\mathrm{simple}} = (1 - p)\left[b(Z_{\mathrm{st}}) - c\beta\right]D_2 - pc\beta D_1, \qquad \Phi_{\mathrm{FFL}} = (1 - p)\left[b(Z_{\mathrm{st}}) - c\beta\right](D_2 - D_1),
$$
and the FFL is selected when $b(Z_{\mathrm{st}}) > c\beta$ and $p > 1 - c\beta/b(Z_{\mathrm{st}})$.

The condition follows from $\Phi_{\mathrm{FFL}} - \Phi_{\mathrm{simple}} = D_1\left[pc\beta - (1 - p)(b(Z_{\mathrm{st}}) - c\beta)\right] > 0$. The FFL is favored when brief pulses are frequent and when the benefit-to-cost ratio is modest, so that wasted production matters. The arabinose and lactose systems of *E. coli* share the input cAMP–CRP and so experience the same pulses of glucose starvation. The arabinose system, with an FFL, expresses seven abundant proteins (AraA, AraB, AraD, AraE and the three subunits of AraFGH) and gains less energy per molecule of sugar than the lactose system, which expresses three (LacZ, LacY, LacA) and is not regulated by an FFL: the comparison agrees with the theory, although it is a consistency check rather than a test.

The argument can be inverted. If circuits are near optimal for their environment, their parameters contain information about it: the delay of the arabinose FFL estimates the duration of the brief, useless pulses of its natural input. This program of reading the environment from the circuit is called inverse ecology; it is limited by how well the cost and benefit functions are known.

### 17.4.3. The Evolution of Modularity

Biological networks are modular at every level: domains in proteins, binding-site modules in enhancers (deleting the stripe-2 enhancer of *even-skipped* removes one of the seven stripes of the *Drosophila* embryo and leaves the others), pathways and complexes in interaction networks, organs in bodies ([7.6.40](/7.6.40#sec-40.3.3) defines **modularity** and its measurement; the transcription network of *E. coli* has a normalized modularity of about $0.54$, [7.9.15](/7.9.15#sec-15.2.3)). Simulated evolution, by contrast, usually produces non-modular solutions, because for a fixed task there are many more wirings that break modularity than wirings that keep it, and the non-modular ones are often more efficient. Something in biological evolution must favor modules.

**Definition 17.4.1 (Modularly Varying Goals)** An evolutionary regime of **modularly varying goals** is one in which the goal (the fitness function) switches over time between several goals that share the same subproblems but combine them differently.

Kashtan and Alon (2005) evolved circuits of NAND gates by mutation and selection towards the four-input goal $G_1 = (X \operatorname{XOR} Y) \operatorname{AND} (Z \operatorname{XOR} W)$. Under the constant goal, perfect circuits appeared only after many thousands of generations and were non-modular, with the minimal ten gates. When the goal switched every $20$ generations between $G_1$ and $G_2 = (X \operatorname{XOR} Y) \operatorname{OR} (Z \operatorname{XOR} W)$, perfect solutions for both goals appeared several times faster although the task is harder, and they were modular: two XOR modules feeding a module that switches between AND and OR by two mutations, at the price of an eleventh gate. When the switching stopped, modularity decayed within tens of generations. For more complex goals the speedup grew with the difficulty of the goal, roughly as a power $0.7$–$0.9$ of the number of generations needed under a constant goal (Kashtan, Noor and Alon, 2007). Varying goals change the fitness landscape before the population is trapped on a local peak, and when the goals share subproblems, the modules that solve them are useful under both and accumulate.

Modularly varying goals are one mechanism among several. A cost of connections, as in nervous and vascular systems, also produces modules in simulations (Clune, Mouret and Lipson, 2013), and **duplication** of modules spreads modularity once it exists, but cannot create it. A qualitative prediction is that systems whose goals have varied should be more modular than systems with an unchanging task: the ribosome, which has translated the genetic code for billions of years, is a tightly integrated machine, whereas chemotaxis systems, which have been adapted to many ligands and motors, are built of exchangeable receptors, a signaling core and motors. The link between modularity and evolvability, and the hidden variation of robust modules, are discussed in [7.6.40](/7.6.40#sec-40.5).

### 17.4.4. Growth by Duplication and Divergence

Networks grow when genes are duplicated. A duplicated gene initially has the same interactions, the same regulators and the same targets as its parent; mutations then remove some of them from one copy or the other, and occasionally add new ones. The process is a copying mechanism of the kind that produces heavy-tailed **degree distributions** without explicit preferential attachment ([1.4.7](/1.4.7#sec-7.5.6)).

**Definition 17.4.2 (Duplication–Divergence Model)** In the **duplication–divergence model** of an interaction network, at each step a uniformly chosen vertex $u$ is duplicated: the new vertex is joined to each neighbor of $u$ independently with probability $q$ (retention) and to $u$ itself with probability $p$.

**Theorem 17.4.2 (Mean Degree under Duplication and Divergence)** Let $E_N$ be the number of edges when the network has $N$ vertices. Then $\mathbb{E}[E_{N+1}] = (1 + 2q/N)\,\mathbb{E}[E_N] + p$, and for large $N$ the mean degree $\bar k = 2E/N$ satisfies

$$
\bar k \to \frac{2p}{1 - 2q} \quad (q < \tfrac12), \qquad \bar k \sim 2p\ln N \quad (q = \tfrac12), \qquad \bar k \propto N^{2q - 1} \quad (q > \tfrac12).
$$

**Proof** The duplicated vertex is uniform, so its expected degree is $2E_N/N$; the new vertex receives on average $q\cdot2E_N/N$ edges from the copied neighborhood and $p$ from the link to its parent, which gives the recursion. In the continuum limit, $\mathrm{d}E/\mathrm{d}N = 2qE/N + p$, a linear equation with solution $E = CN^{2q} + pN/(1 - 2q)$ for $q \neq \frac12$ and $E = N(p\ln N + C)$ for $q = \frac12$. For $q < \frac12$ the second term dominates and $\bar k \to 2p/(1 - 2q)$; for $q > \frac12$ the first, and $\bar k \propto N^{2q - 1}$. $\square$

Unless most interactions are lost at each duplication, the network becomes denser as it grows (Ispolatov, Krapivsky and Yuryev, 2005). The evidence from real genomes is consistent with rapid divergence. Pairs of duplicated yeast genes share fewer and fewer interaction partners as their sequences diverge, and the protein interaction network loses and gains interactions quickly on an evolutionary time scale (Wagner, 2001); in transcription networks, duplicated factors and duplicated targets have retained part of their regulatory interactions, which has contributed substantially to network growth (Teichmann and Babu, 2004). Duplication also supplies **robustness**: deletions of yeast genes that have a close **paralog** are less often lethal than deletions of singletons (Gu and colleagues, 2003), and the partition of ancestral functions between the copies (**subfunctionalization**) or the acquisition of a new function by one of them (**neofunctionalization**) determines whether both copies are kept. Motifs, on the other hand, are not inherited as wholes: homologous genes regulated by feed-forward loops in different species usually have regulators that are not homologous ([7.9.15](/7.9.15#sec-15.4.5)), so the motif has evolved convergently. Duplication explains the statistics of networks; selection for function explains their motifs. The simulation of 7.9.12 uses this model to compare the robustness of a network and of its incomplete map ([7.9.12](/7.9.12)).

## 17.5. Synthetic Biology: Design Principles as Engineering

### 17.5.1. Parts, Standards and Orthogonality

**Definition 17.5.1 (Synthetic Biology)** **Synthetic biology** is the design and construction of new biological functions, genetic circuits, pathways and cells, from characterized parts according to explicit models, and the use of such constructions to test the principles that the analysis of natural systems proposes.

The second half of the definition is the reason for treating synthetic biology in a course on systems biology. The two founding circuits, the **genetic toggle switch** (Gardner, Cantor and Collins, 2000) and the **repressilator** (Elowitz and Leibler, 2000), were built to test whether mutual repression gives bistability and whether a three-repressor ring oscillates, predictions of the dynamical models of [7.6.33](/7.6.33). Both worked, and both revealed what the models had left out: the repressilator oscillated in only part of the cells and with irregular periods. Potvin-Trottier, Lord, Vinnicombe and Paulsson (2016) traced the irregularity to specific sources of noise, a leaky reporter and the titration of repressor by a plasmid, and their revised repressilator kept phase for hundreds of generations: a design principle (remove the noisiest steps, 17.1.3) applied to a circuit.

Engineering requires parts with known behavior. Genetic circuits are built from **promoters**, ribosome binding sites, coding sequences and terminators, described graphically by the SBOL Visual standard and assembled by standardized methods. Parts must also be orthogonal: a repressor should act on its own operator and no other. Libraries of homologous repressors that do not cross-react (sixteen orthogonal TetR-family repressors in Stanton and colleagues, 2014), orthogonal RNA polymerases and **CRISPR interference** with guide RNAs designed for different promoters (Qi and colleagues, 2013; Nielsen and Voigt, 2014) provide such sets. With characterized NOT and NOR gates, circuit design can be automated: from a logic specification the program Cello chose and connected gates for sixty circuits in *E. coli*, of which forty-five behaved correctly in every output state (Nielsen and colleagues, 2016).

### 17.5.2. Context: Retroactivity and Shared Resources

Electronic engineering relies on modules that behave the same whatever they are connected to. Genetic modules do not, for two reasons.

**Definition 17.5.2 (Retroactivity)** **Retroactivity** is the effect of a downstream system on the dynamics of the upstream system that drives it, caused by the interaction through which the signal is transmitted, such as the binding of a transcription factor to downstream promoters.

**Formula 17.5.1 (Slowdown by a Load)** Let a transcription factor $X$ be made at rate $k(t)$ and removed with rate constant $\gamma$, and bind $p_{\mathrm{T}}$ downstream sites with dissociation constant $K_{\mathrm{D}}$, fast compared with $\gamma$, the bound form being protected from removal. With the bound amount at quasi-equilibrium, $C = p_{\mathrm{T}}X/(K_{\mathrm{D}} + X)$, the free factor obeys

$$
\left[1 + \frac{p_{\mathrm{T}}K_{\mathrm{D}}}{(K_{\mathrm{D}} + X)^2}\right]\frac{\mathrm{d}X}{\mathrm{d}t} = k(t) - \gamma X. \tag{17.6}
$$

The equation follows by differentiating the total $X + C$, whose rate of change is $k - \gamma X$. The load does not change the steady state but slows all changes by the factor in brackets, largest when $X$ is small (Del Vecchio, Ninfa and Sontag, 2008). A module that works as an oscillator or a pulse generator in isolation may fail when it drives many targets. Insulation devices with a large flux and fast dynamics of their own, such as phosphorylation cycles, reduce retroactivity, at a cost in energy and protein.

**Example 17.5.1** With $\gamma = 1$, steady state $X = 10$, $K_{\mathrm{D}} = 10$ and $p_{\mathrm{T}} = 100$ sites, the factor is $1 + 100 \times 10/400 = 3.5$ at the steady state and $1 + 100/10 = 11$ at $X = 0$. Switching on production from $X = 0$, the free factor reaches half of its final level after $0.69$ time units without load, $1.58$ with $20$ sites and $5.11$ with $100$; an oscillating input $k(t) = 10(1 + 0.8\sin t)$ produces oscillations of free $X$ with amplitudes $5.66$, $4.40$ and $2.19$.

The second source of context dependence is that all genes draw on the same RNA polymerases, ribosomes, amino acids and energy. Expressing a synthetic circuit reduces the growth rate (the proteome cost of [7.6.39](/7.6.39#fml-39.3.2)) and couples genes that have no regulatory link: inducing one gene lowers the expression of another because they compete for ribosomes. The outputs of genes that share a resource lie under a line of constant total resource use, an isocost line (Gyorgy and colleagues, 2015), and circuits that ignore it misbehave when the host's physiology changes. Feedback controllers that adjust expression to the free resource, and orthogonal ribosomes and polymerases that do not compete with the host, are current remedies.

### 17.5.3. Circuits and the Design–Build–Test–Learn Cycle

Beyond switches and oscillators, synthetic circuits have implemented most of the functions of natural motifs and some new ones: a fast, robust and tunable oscillator combining positive and negative feedback (Stricker and colleagues, 2008; the design principle is discussed in [7.9.16](/7.9.16)); cells that detect a band of signal concentration and form bull's-eye patterns around a source, through a combination of high- and low-threshold responses like an incoherent feed-forward loop (Basu and colleagues, 2005); a bacterial lawn that detects the edges of a projected light image by combining light sensing with cell–cell communication (Tabor and colleagues, 2009); and in mammalian cells, synthetic receptors that activate chosen genes when they contact a target cell (synNotch; Morsut and colleagues, 2016), which have been used to program multicellular patterns and to make engineered T cells more selective. Metabolic engineering, the reconstruction of pathways in microbes, is the most successful application in industry: yeast engineered with a plant pathway produces artemisinic acid, a precursor of the antimalarial artemisinin (Paddon and colleagues, 2013).

The working method of the field is a cycle: design a circuit from models and characterized parts, build it, test it with quantitative single-cell measurements, and learn from the discrepancies, which become the data for better models and better parts. Each turn of this cycle is also an experiment on the principles of this chapter. A circuit that fails because of noise, retroactivity or resource competition reveals a constraint that natural networks must also satisfy, and a design that works only in a narrow range of parameters is, by the standard of [7.6.40](/7.6.40#def-40.1.1), not robust; natural networks are the designs that have passed this test for a long time.

## Summary

- Noise in networks: proteome-wide, $\eta^2 \approx 1/\langle p\rangle + \gamma_p/k_m + \eta_{\mathrm{ext}}^2$. In a cascade (Theorem 17.1.1) the target inherits propagated noise $H^2\,\frac{\gamma_y}{\gamma_x + \gamma_y}\,\eta_x^2$ (Definition 17.1.1): gain, time averaging and input noise; chains amplify for $H > 1$ (Example 17.1.1). The noisiest step dominates (Example 17.1.2); negative feedback narrows and positive feedback broadens distributions, and information theory bounds what feedback can achieve (standard deviation at best $\propto N^{-1/4}$ in signaling events).
- Using noise: the long-term growth rate is the mean of $\ln w$ (Eq. (17.2)); with exclusive phenotypes the optimal bet hedging (Definition 17.1.2) is proportional, $q^* = p$, and costs the entropy $H(p)$ relative to sensing (Theorem 17.1.2, Example 17.1.3). Persisters, competence and the λ decision use or are shaped by noise.

- Robustness and fragility: each robust design has a cost and a weak point: energy for proofreading and adaptation, saturation for bifunctional sensors, the unstable point of glucotoxicity, the load sensitivity of genetic modules.
- Evolution: environments select regulation, motifs and modules; duplication grows networks and supplies redundancy. Synthetic biology tests these principles by construction (Definition 17.5.1).

## Questions

1. Why does a long-lived protein $Y$ transmit less of the noise of a short-lived regulator $X$ than of a long-lived one, at the same gain and the same noise of $X$?

   **Answer** $Y$ integrates its production rate over its lifetime $\tau_y$. Fluctuations of $X$ with correlation time $\tau_x \ll \tau_y$ are averaged over many independent values, and their contribution is reduced by the factor $\gamma_y/(\gamma_x + \gamma_y) = \tau_x/(\tau_x + \tau_y)$ of Theorem 17.1.1. A slowly fluctuating $X$ stays high or low for a whole lifetime of $Y$ and is passed on almost entirely.

2. In a cascade $X \to Y$ with $\langle x\rangle = 20$, $\langle y\rangle = 200$, equal removal rates and gain $H = 2$, compute $\eta_y^2$ and the fraction that is propagated.

   **Answer** $\eta_y^2 = 1/200 + 2^2 \times \tfrac12 \times \tfrac1{20} = 0.005 + 0.100 = 0.105$. The propagated part is $0.100/0.105 = 95\,\%$: the noise of a regulator present in few copies, amplified by a cooperative promoter, dominates that of an abundant target.

3. Explain why, in Example 17.1.2, the coefficient of variation of gene A is about eight times that of gene B, and how a cell could make a protein both cheaply and precisely.

   **Answer** For compound Poisson production the variance is $\lambda(b + 2b^2)$: with $\lambda = 1$, $b = 100$ it is $20\,100$; with $\lambda = 100$, $b = 1$ it is $300$; the ratio of standard deviations is $\sqrt{20\,100/300} = 8.2$. Noise is set by the smallest number in the chain, the mRNAs. Precision at low transcriptional cost needs other mechanisms: negative autoregulation, stable proteins that average many bursts (long $\tau_p$ makes $\gamma_p/k_m$ small), or more gene copies with lower burst size.

4. A population faces good periods with probability $p = 0.6$; the phenotype that grows in good periods dies in bad ones and vice versa. What fraction should switch to each phenotype, and how much is perfect sensing worth?

   **Answer** By Theorem 17.1.2, $q^* = p = 0.6$, independent of the growth factors. Sensing increases the long-term growth rate by $H(0.6) = -0.6\ln0.6 - 0.4\ln0.4 = 0.673$ nats ($0.971$ bits) per period; a sensor that costs less than this in growth rate per period is selected.

5. In the minimal model with $f(G) = G^2$, insulin sensitivity falls threefold. What is the new fasting glucose if it was $5\,\mathrm{mM}$? What does the BIG model predict after compensation for glucose, insulin and β-cell mass?

   **Answer** By Formula 17.3.1, $G_{\mathrm{st}}$ scales as $s^{-1/3}$: $5 \times 3^{1/3} = 7.21\,\mathrm{mM}$. In the BIG model glucose returns to $G_0 = 5\,\mathrm{mM}$; since $sI_{\mathrm{st}} = m_0/G_0$ and $sB_{\mathrm{st}}$ are constant, fasting insulin and β-cell mass both triple, and the glucose response to a meal is unchanged (Theorem 17.3.1).

6. Show that the BIG model has no dynamical compensation for the insulin removal rate $\gamma$. Which feature of the equations is responsible?

   **Answer** The steady state is unaffected for glucose ($G_0$) and insulin ($I_{\mathrm{st}} = m_0/(sG_0)$), and the β-cell mass adjusts, $B_{\mathrm{st}} = \gamma I_{\mathrm{st}}/(qf(G_0))$. But $\gamma$ also appears as a rate in $\mathrm{d}I/\mathrm{d}t = qBf(G) - \gamma I$; rescaling $B$ cannot remove it, because a factor multiplying $I$ cannot be absorbed into a factor multiplying $B$. Faster removal makes the insulin response to a meal faster and changes the shape of $G(t)$. Compensation requires that the parameter enter only as a factor that a change of cell mass can absorb (the homogeneity condition of Karin and colleagues).

7. Why is the high fixed point $G_1$ of the BIG model with glucotoxicity unstable? Use the slow dynamics with glucose at its quasi-steady state.

   **Answer** On the slow time scale $G \propto (sB)^{-1/3}$, so $\mathrm{d}\ln B/\mathrm{d}t = \mu(G)$ becomes, near a zero $G^*$, $\mathrm{d}\,\delta\ln B/\mathrm{d}t = \mu'(G^*)G^*\,\delta\ln G = -\tfrac13\mu'(G^*)G^*\,\delta\ln B$. The perturbation decays if $\mu'(G^*) > 0$ (at $G_0$, where the growth rate rises through zero) and grows if $\mu'(G^*) < 0$ (at $G_1$, where glucotoxicity makes it fall through zero). Above $G_1$, more glucose means net loss of β cells, less insulin and still more glucose.

8. A gene with benefit $b = 3c$ and a regulatory system of cost $r = 0.2c$. Which design is selected at demand $p = 0.5$ and at $p = 0.9$, and where is the boundary?

   **Answer** At $p = 0.5$: $F_1 = 1.5c - c = 0.5c$, $F_2 = 0.5 \times 2c - 0.2c = 0.8c$; regulation wins. At $p = 0.9$: $F_1 = 2.7c - c = 1.7c$, $F_2 = 0.9 \times 2c - 0.2c = 1.6c$; constitutive expression wins. The boundary is $p = 1 - r/c = 0.8$; the lower limit of regulation is $p = r/(b - c) = 0.1$ (Formula 17.4.1).

9. In a bimodal pulse environment, the benefit-to-cost ratio of a gene is $b(Z_{\mathrm{st}})/(c\beta) = 2$. How frequent must brief pulses be for the coherent FFL to be selected? What happens at a ratio of $0.8$?

   **Answer** By Formula 17.4.2, $p > 1 - 1/2 = 0.5$: more than half of the pulses must be brief. At a ratio of $0.8$ even long pulses do not pay ($b < c\beta$), so neither design is selected and the gene should not be induced by this signal.

10. In the duplication–divergence model with retention $q = 0.3$ and parent link probability $p = 0.2$, what is the limiting mean degree? What retention would be needed for a sparse network to keep a mean degree of $6$ with $p = 0.3$?

    **Answer** $\bar k \to 2p/(1 - 2q) = 0.4/0.4 = 1.0$. For $\bar k = 6$ with $p = 0.3$: $0.6/(1 - 2q) = 6$, so $1 - 2q = 0.1$ and $q = 0.45$. Any $q \geq 1/2$ makes the mean degree grow without bound, so realistic sparse networks require that more than half of the inherited interactions be lost, consistent with the rapid divergence of duplicated yeast genes.

11. A transcription factor with steady-state free level $X = 5$ (in units of $K_{\mathrm{D}} = 5$) drives a reporter plasmid carrying $50$ binding sites. By what factor is its response slowed near steady state, and how could the design be changed?

    **Answer** By Formula 17.5.1, $1 + p_{\mathrm{T}}K_{\mathrm{D}}/(K_{\mathrm{D}} + X)^2 = 1 + 50 \times 5/100 = 3.5$. Remedies: fewer sites (a low-copy plasmid or a single genomic copy), weaker sites (larger $K_{\mathrm{D}}$, at the cost of lower occupancy), a faster upstream module (larger production and removal, so that the load is small compared with the flux), or an insulating stage such as a phosphorylation cycle between the factor and the load.

12. (Design) How would you measure the intrinsic, propagated and global components of the noise of a target gene $Y$ regulated by $X$ in bacteria?

    **Answer** Build a strain with three reporters: a fluorescent fusion or transcriptional reporter of $X$, and two copies of the $Y$ promoter driving reporters of two other colors (the dual-reporter method of Elowitz and colleagues). The difference between the two $Y$ reporters in each cell gives the intrinsic noise of $Y$; their correlation gives the extrinsic noise; regressing the common part on the level of $X$ (and on its recent history, using time-lapse microscopy, since $Y$ integrates $X$ over its lifetime) separates the part transmitted from $X$ from the global part shared with unrelated genes, which a fourth, constitutive reporter can measure directly. Varying the level or the gain of $X$, for instance with an inducer, tests the $H^2$ dependence of Theorem 17.1.1, as in the experiments of Pedraza and van Oudenaarden.

13. Two-component systems are robust because of a bifunctional sensor, yet the chemotaxis system, also a two-component system, uses a separate phosphatase CheZ. Why does this not make chemotaxis fragile?

    **Answer** Chemotaxis needs robustness of a different property. Its output, CheY-P, must adapt exactly to the background concentration of attractant, not report the absolute concentration; exact adaptation is provided by integral feedback through receptor methylation ([7.6.35](/7.6.35#sec-35.6)), which is robust to protein levels by its own mechanism. The adapted level of CheY-P itself is not robust, and the motor compensates by adapting its own sensitivity. Different functions require robustness of different quantities, and the designs differ accordingly.
