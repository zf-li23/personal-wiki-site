More microbial cells live on and in a human being than the body has cells of its own, and nearly all of them are harmless or useful. They ferment the dietary fiber we cannot digest, make vitamins, occupy the surfaces that pathogens would otherwise colonize, and are needed for the normal development of the gut and of immunity. Since cultivation-independent sequencing made it possible to survey them ([7.11.16](/7.11.16), [7.8.14](/7.8.14)), the human microbiome has become one of the most active and one of the most over-interpreted fields of biology: thousands of associations have been reported between microbial composition and disease, and only a few of them have been shown to be causal. This chapter describes what lives where, how the communities assemble and change, what they do, and what is known, and not known, about their role in disease. Because the literature is so uneven, every causal claim here is labeled with the kind of evidence behind it, in the scale of Section 32.1.3. The microbial ecology of other habitats is in [7.11.29](/7.11.29) and symbioses with other animals in [7.11.31](/7.11.31); the sequencing methods are in [7.8.14](/7.8.14) and the immune system in [7.15](/7.15).

## 32.1. Concepts and Methods

### 32.1.1. Microbiota and Microbiome

**Definition 32.1.1 (Microbiota and Microbiome)** The **microbiota** of a habitat is the community of microorganisms living in it — bacteria, archaea, fungi, protists and viruses — described by which taxa are present and in what abundance; the **microbiome** is that community together with its genes and gene products ([Definition 14.1.1](/7.8.14#def-14.1.1)), and the term is often extended to the community and its whole habitat. The human microbiome is the sum of the microbiomes of the body's habitats: skin, mouth, airways, gut, urogenital tract.

A distinction of long standing in medical microbiology is between resident organisms, which maintain populations at a site, and transient ones, which are deposited there and die out or are removed; both belong to what older texts call the normal microbiota. The host together with its persistent microbiota is a **holobiont** ([7.5.38](/7.5.38#sec-38.9)).

How many microbial cells are there? The "ten bacteria for every human cell" repeated for decades came from a back-of-the-envelope estimate made in 1972. Recomputing both numbers from anatomical data, Sender, Fuchs and Milo (2016) obtained about $3.8\times10^{13}$ bacteria, almost all of them in the colon, and about $3.0\times10^{13}$ human cells, of which about 90 % are red blood cells and platelets: a ratio of roughly 1.3 : 1, which a single defecation changes appreciably ([7.11.1](/7.11.1#sec-1.4)). By mass the microbiota amounts to only about 0.2 kg. The number of microbial genes, however, exceeds the number of human genes by two orders of magnitude, and it is this gene content, not the cell count, that matters biologically.

### 32.1.2. How the Microbiome Is Studied

Four approaches are combined.

- *Sequencing surveys*: 16S rRNA gene amplicons for bacteria and archaea and ITS amplicons for fungi give composition cheaply; shotgun metagenomics gives species- and strain-level resolution and gene content; metatranscriptomics, metaproteomics and metabolomics show what is being expressed and produced. Their analysis, including the compositional nature of the data, is treated in [7.8.14](/7.8.14) and not repeated here. Large reference projects (the NIH Human Microbiome Project from 2007, MetaHIT, and later population cohorts) established what a "healthy" community looks like and how variable it is.
- *Cultivation*: "unculturable" has turned out to be mostly "not yet cultured". Culturomics — many parallel culture conditions with identification by mass spectrometry — and media designed from metagenomic predictions have brought most abundant gut taxa into culture, which is what makes mechanistic experiments possible.
- *Gnotobiotic animals*: the decisive tool for causality.

**Definition 32.1.2 (Germ-Free and Gnotobiotic Animals)** A **germ-free animal** (axenic) carries no detectable microorganisms; it is obtained by aseptic cesarean delivery or sterilization of eggs and reared in an isolator on sterilized food. A *gnotobiotic* animal is one whose entire microbiota is known: germ-free, or colonized with a defined strain or defined consortium. A germ-free animal colonized with a human-derived community is called *humanized*.

Germ-free rodents differ from conventional ones in many ways: an enlarged cecum, thin intestinal villi with slow epithelial turnover, poorly developed gut-associated lymphoid tissue, fewer and altered T-cell subsets, an immature microglial phenotype, a requirement for dietary vitamin K and B vitamins, and altered behavior. They are the experimental system in which a community or a single strain can be shown to *cause* a phenotype.

- *Human studies*: cross-sectional association studies, longitudinal cohorts, mendelian randomization, and interventions — the strongest being randomized controlled trials of a defined intervention such as a probiotic strain, a fiber, an antibiotic or a fecal transplant.

### 32.1.3. A Scale of Evidence

Microbiome associations are peculiarly liable to confounding and to reverse causation. Diet, drugs (metformin, proton-pump inhibitors, laxatives and many non-antibiotic drugs all shift the gut community), transit time, age, geography and the disease itself all change the microbiota, so a difference between patients and controls is weak evidence that microbes caused the disease. Stool consistency alone explains a large part of the variation between people. Two further problems are specific to this field: compositional data make any increase look like a decrease in everything else ([7.8.14](/7.8.14#sec-14.5)), and small studies with many taxa tested produce many false positives.

Throughout this chapter, claims are marked as follows.

| Label | What it means |
|---|---|
| *(association)* | a correlation in human observational data; direction of causation unknown |
| *(animal experiment)* | a causal demonstration in gnotobiotic or antibiotic-treated animals, usually mice; transfer to humans uncertain |
| *(randomized trial)* | a controlled intervention in humans with clinical outcomes |

The strongest evidence combines all three: an association in people, a mechanism demonstrated in gnotobiotic animals with defined strains, and a trial in which changing the microbes changes the outcome.

## 32.2. The Communities of the Body

### 32.2.1. Skin

The skin is a cool, dry, salty, acidic and nutrient-poor surface, continually shed, defended by antimicrobial peptides, lysozyme and the free fatty acids released from sebum, and yet carries roughly $10^{6}$ bacteria per square centimeter. Its communities are determined less by the person than by the kind of site:

| Site type | Examples | Dominant genera |
|---|---|---|
| sebaceous (oily) | forehead, back, behind the ear | *Cutibacterium* (formerly *Propionibacterium*) *acnes*, which hydrolyzes sebum triglycerides to free fatty acids; *Malassezia* yeasts |
| moist | armpit, groin, navel, between the toes | *Staphylococcus*, *Corynebacterium* |
| dry | forearm, palm | the most diverse: Actinomycetota, Bacillota, Pseudomonadota, Bacteroidota |

Fungi (*Malassezia*, lipid-dependent yeasts), bacteriophages and a few ammonia-oxidizing archaea complete the community, which is stable over months in an adult and differs between individuals; age, hygiene, climate, cosmetics and contact with other people and with pets change it.

Members of the skin community interact chemically with *Staphylococcus aureus*, which colonizes the nose of about a third of people and is a major pathogen when it enters the skin or blood. *Staphylococcus epidermidis* produces a serine protease that disperses *S. aureus* biofilms and induces antimicrobial peptides in keratinocytes; *S. lugdunensis* makes the antibiotic lugdunin; *S. hominis* makes lantibiotics that act together with the human peptide LL-37 (all animal experiment and in vitro). *Cutibacterium acnes* is a normal resident of sebaceous follicles and is present in people with and without acne; whether a given strain is associated with acne appears to depend on the strain and on the host's inflammatory response rather than on the mere presence of the species *(association)*.

### 32.2.2. Mouth

Saliva is nutrient-poor and contains lysozyme, lactoperoxidase, lactoferrin and secretory antibodies, but it washes over surfaces that are continuously supplied with food. More than 700 bacterial species have been identified in the mouth; each surface — tooth enamel above and below the gum line, tongue, cheek, palate — carries a different community, and the mouth is the one site whose composition is relatively similar between people.

Dental plaque is the best-studied **biofilm** of the human body ([7.11.12](/7.11.12)). Early colonizers, mostly streptococci (*Streptococcus mitis*, *S. oralis*, *S. sanguinis*), attach to the acquired pellicle of salivary proteins on enamel; *Fusobacterium nucleatum* then coaggregates with both early and late colonizers and bridges them; obligate anaerobes follow as oxygen is consumed, and the mature plaque is spatially organized (imaging of plaque shows "hedgehog" structures with *Corynebacterium* filaments radiating from the center and other taxa arranged in defined zones around them).

Two diseases follow from shifts in this community. In dental caries, frequent sugar supports acid-producing and acid-tolerant bacteria, chiefly *Streptococcus mutans* and lactobacilli, which ferment sucrose to lactate; the pH at the enamel surface falls below about 5.5 and the mineral dissolves. *S. mutans* also makes extracellular glucans from sucrose, which strengthen the biofilm. Caries is therefore not an infection by a single species but an ecological shift driven by diet *(association, with a well-understood chemical mechanism)*. In periodontitis, the subgingival community shifts toward anaerobes (*Porphyromonas gingivalis*, *Treponema denticola*, *Tannerella forsythia*), and the host's inflammatory response destroys the tissue and bone that hold the tooth. *P. gingivalis* is a *keystone pathogen*: in mice it causes bone loss only when a normal community is present, and at very low abundance, by altering the whole community and the host response *(animal experiment)*. Periodontitis is associated in people with cardiovascular disease, diabetes and adverse pregnancy outcomes, but whether treating it changes those outcomes is not settled *(association)*.

### 32.2.3. Airways

Air carries microorganisms into the respiratory tract constantly; most are trapped in mucus, swept up by the ciliated epithelium and swallowed, and only particles below about 10 µm reach the alveoli. The upper tract is colonized: the anterior nares by *Staphylococcus*, *Corynebacterium* and *Cutibacterium*, the nasopharynx additionally by *Dolosigranulum*, *Moraxella*, *Haemophilus* and *Streptococcus pneumoniae*, and the oropharynx by a diverse community close to that of saliva. Carriage of potential pathogens is normal: *S. aureus* in the nose of about a third of adults, *S. pneumoniae* in the nasopharynx of about a tenth of adults and a third or more of young children.

The lungs were long considered sterile. Sensitive sequencing shows that they are not, but that they have no resident community of their own: they hold a low-biomass, transient population continually seeded by microaspiration from the oropharynx and removed by mucociliary clearance and immunity — a community defined by immigration and elimination rather than by growth. When clearance fails, the picture changes completely. In cystic fibrosis, thick mucus allows chronic biofilm infection, typically by *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*, which cannot be eradicated by antibiotics; quorum-sensing signals detected in sputum were among the first direct evidence of biofilm growth in a human infection (Singh et al. 2000; [7.11.11](/7.11.11#sec-11.7), [7.11.12](/7.11.12)). Work on low-biomass sites must control for contamination of reagents, which can dominate the sequences recovered.

### 32.2.4. Stomach and Small Intestine

The stomach at pH 1–2 kills most swallowed organisms, and holds only about $10^{3}$–$10^{4}$ cells per gram, largely transients plus organisms in the mucus layer, where the pH is near neutral. Its characteristic inhabitant is *Helicobacter pylori*, which neutralizes its surroundings with urease and lives in the gastric mucus; it colonizes a large fraction of people worldwide, usually acquired in childhood within the family, persists for decades, and is a cause of peptic ulcer and of gastric cancer — the one member of the human microbiota that is classified as a group 1 carcinogen. Its prevalence has fallen in high-income countries, and so has gastric cancer. Its strains track human migrations closely enough to be used to trace them ([7.11.16](/7.11.16)).

Along the small intestine the pH rises, bile and pancreatic secretions are diluted and numbers increase from about $10^{3}$–$10^{4}$ per gram in the duodenum to $10^{7}$–$10^{8}$ in the ileum. The small intestine is a fast-flowing habitat: contents pass in a few hours, so only organisms that grow quickly on simple sugars or adhere to the mucosa persist (streptococci, lactobacilli, *Veillonella*, enterobacteria). Because host and microbes compete here for the same easily absorbed nutrients, bacterial overgrowth of the small intestine impairs absorption.

### 32.2.5. The Large Intestine

The colon is the densest microbial habitat known: about $10^{11}$ cells per gram of contents, $3\times10^{13}$ in all, in a slow-flowing (12–48 h), anoxic, near-neutral fermenter continually fed with what the small intestine could not absorb. Facultative anaerobes consume the little oxygen that diffuses in, and obligate anaerobes dominate at a ratio of about 100 : 1 or more. Two phyla, Bacillota (Firmicutes; above all the families Lachnospiraceae and Ruminococcaceae) and Bacteroidota (*Bacteroides*, *Prevotella*), make up most of the community, with Actinomycetota (*Bifidobacterium*), Pseudomonadota and Verrucomicrobiota (*Akkermansia muciniphila*, which grows on mucin) as smaller components. Methanogenic archaea, chiefly *Methanobrevibacter smithii*, are present in a majority of people and detectable as breath methane in about a third; fungi (*Candida*, *Saccharomyces*) are a small fraction, and the gut virome, mostly bacteriophages, is numerically comparable to the bacteria and highly individual ([7.11.26](/7.11.26#sec-26.7), [7.11.23](/7.11.23)).

Composition varies enormously between people — the ratio of Bacillota to Bacteroidota ranges over orders of magnitude — while an individual's community is remarkably stable, most strains persisting for years. The proposal that people fall into three discrete "enterotypes" dominated by *Bacteroides*, *Prevotella* or *Ruminococcus* has not held up as a set of discrete types; the variation is better described as continuous, with the *Prevotella*–*Bacteroides* axis tracking long-term diet (fiber-rich and plant-based versus high in animal protein and fat) *(association)*.

The community is also spatially organized. Goblet cells secrete mucin, which forms two layers in the colon: a dense inner layer, essentially free of bacteria, which keeps the community away from the epithelium, and a looser outer layer that bacteria colonize and partly use as food. Antimicrobial peptides from Paneth cells and epithelial cells and secretory IgA reinforce the separation. Butyrate consumed by the colonocytes keeps them oxidizing fatty acids, which keeps the lumen anoxic and so favors obligate anaerobes over facultative ones such as *Escherichia* and *Salmonella* — a feedback that links host metabolism to community composition *(animal experiment)*.

### 32.2.6. Urogenital Tract

The upper urinary tract and bladder were long held to be sterile; sensitive culture and sequencing show a low-biomass bladder community, whose significance is still being worked out. The distal urethra carries skin and gut organisms, and ascending *Escherichia coli* is the usual cause of urinary tract infection.

The vagina of an adult is unusual among human habitats in being dominated by a single genus. Estrogen causes the epithelium to accumulate glycogen; lactobacilli (*Lactobacillus crispatus*, *L. gasseri*, *L. jensenii*, *L. iners*) ferment its breakdown products to lactic acid and hold the pH near 4, which excludes most other organisms. Five community types are commonly distinguished, four dominated by one of these species and a fifth, more diverse, with anaerobes such as *Gardnerella*, *Atopobium* and *Prevotella* and a higher pH; this last state corresponds to bacterial vaginosis, which is associated with increased risk of preterm birth and of sexually transmitted infection *(association)*. Communities differ in stability: *L. crispatus*-dominated communities are the most resilient, *L. iners*-dominated ones shift more readily. Before puberty and after menopause, when glycogen is absent, the pH is near neutral and the community resembles that of the skin and gut.

## 32.3. Assembly and Change Over a Lifetime

### 32.3.1. Birth and the First Years

Whether the fetus is colonized before birth was debated for a decade; careful studies with proper contamination controls found no reproducible microbiota in placenta or meconium, and the current consensus is that the healthy fetus is essentially sterile and that reports to the contrary reflect reagent and sampling contamination (de Goffau et al. 2019; Kennedy et al. 2021).

Colonization begins at birth and is strongly affected by its mode: vaginally born infants acquire a community resembling the mother's vaginal and fecal communities, infants born by cesarean section one resembling skin and the hospital environment, with *Bacteroides* often delayed or missing. The difference narrows over the first year but is still detectable at 12 months *(association)*; whether it has lasting health consequences, and whether "vaginal seeding" is beneficial, is unresolved.

Feeding then dominates. Human milk contains, after lactose and lipids, a third major component: human milk oligosaccharides, more than a hundred structures that the infant cannot digest at all. They are food for particular bacteria, above all *Bifidobacterium longum* subsp. *infantis*, which has a gene cluster encoding transporters that import the intact oligosaccharides and intracellular glycosidases that degrade them, so that it monopolizes the resource. Its fermentation produces acetate and lactate, which lower the pH of the infant colon and suppress enterobacteria. Some milk oligosaccharides also resemble the glycans of the gut surface and act as decoys for pathogen adhesins. Formula-fed infants develop a more diverse, more adult-like and less bifidobacteria-dominated community. The community becomes adult-like at about two to three years of age, and the decisive step is the cessation of breastfeeding rather than the introduction of solid food, after which fiber degraders (*Bacteroides*, *Roseburia*, *Faecalibacterium*) expand.

### 32.3.2. Adulthood and Old Age

In an adult the community is stable over years: most strains persist, and relatives, especially those living together, share strains. Perturbations — a course of antibiotics, travel, an intestinal infection, a change of diet — displace it temporarily, and it usually, but not always, returns (Section 32.5.1). Long-term diet, not single meals, sets the broad composition, although controlled feeding experiments show measurable shifts within a day or two.

Across human populations the largest differences are geographic and dietary: communities of people eating traditional, high-fiber diets (rural African and South American populations, hunter-gatherers such as the Hadza) are richer in fiber-degrading taxa, often *Prevotella*-dominated, and more diverse than those of industrialized populations, which have lost taxa over generations *(association)*. In old age the community becomes more variable between individuals; reduced diversity is associated with frailty, residence in long-term care and a less varied diet, and it is hard to separate the effects of diet, medication and health *(association)*.

## 32.4. What the Gut Microbiota Does

### 32.4.1. Fermentation and Short-Chain Fatty Acids

Humans encode about 17 enzymes for digesting dietary carbohydrates and cannot break down most plant polysaccharides. The gut community encodes thousands of carbohydrate-active enzymes; *Bacteroides* species carry dozens of polysaccharide utilization loci, each a cluster for binding, importing and degrading one class of glycan ([7.11.18](/7.11.18#sec-18.7)). The products are fermented to the **short-chain fatty acids** acetate, propionate and butyrate ([Definition 31.6.2](/7.11.31#def-31.6.2)) with $\ce{CO2}$ and $\ce{H2}$; the hydrogen is consumed by methanogens, acetogens and sulfate reducers, and the sulfide produced by the last contributes to the odor of feces. Fermentation of 20–60 g of fiber a day yields a few hundred millimoles of SCFAs, which supply on the order of 5–10 % of human energy intake — far less than in a ruminant ([7.11.31](/7.11.31#sec-31.6)), because the human fermenter is small and comes last.

The SCFAs are more than calories.

- Butyrate is the main fuel of the colonocytes, which oxidize it and thereby keep the mucosa anoxic.
- Propionate is absorbed and used for gluconeogenesis in the liver; acetate reaches the peripheral circulation and is a substrate for lipid synthesis.
- SCFAs act as signals through G-protein-coupled receptors (FFAR2/GPR43, FFAR3/GPR41) on gut endocrine and immune cells, stimulating the release of the hormones GLP-1 and PYY, and butyrate inhibits histone deacetylases, which is how it induces regulatory T cells in the colon *(animal experiment)*.

When fiber is scarce, the community turns to the host's own mucus glycans; in mice fed a fiber-free diet the mucus layer thins and a pathogen reaches the epithelium more easily *(animal experiment)*.

### 32.4.2. Vitamins, Bile Acids and Drugs

Gut bacteria synthesize vitamin K (menaquinones) and B vitamins (B$_{12}$, folate, riboflavin, thiamine, biotin); how much of this is absorbed in the colon rather than consumed by the bacteria themselves is uncertain, but germ-free animals require dietary supplements of vitamin K and some B vitamins *(animal experiment)*. They also deconjugate and 7α-dehydroxylate the primary **bile acids** secreted by the liver, producing secondary bile acids such as deoxycholate and lithocholate, which are absorbed and act as ligands of the host receptors FXR and TGR5, influencing lipid and glucose metabolism, and which inhibit the germination and growth of *Clostridioides difficile* (Section 32.5.2). The community also metabolizes drugs and other xenobiotics: it inactivates the cardiac glycoside digoxin (*Eggerthella lenta*), converts the prodrug sulfasalazine to its active form, and removes the protecting glucuronide from the metabolite of the anticancer drug irinotecan, which causes the drug's intestinal toxicity. Dietary choline and carnitine are converted by gut bacteria to trimethylamine, which the liver oxidizes to trimethylamine *N*-oxide (TMAO); TMAO is associated in human cohorts with cardiovascular disease and accelerates atherosclerosis in mice *(association; animal experiment)*.

### 32.4.3. Colonization Resistance

**Definition 32.4.1 (Colonization Resistance)** **Colonization resistance** is the capacity of a resident microbiota to prevent incoming pathogens, or overgrowth by resident opportunists, from establishing themselves. It is the clearest function of the human microbiota, demonstrated by the ease with which germ-free or antibiotic-treated animals are colonized by doses of a pathogen that a conventional animal resists *(animal experiment)*.

Its mechanisms are direct and host-mediated:

- *Nutrient competition*: residents consume the sugars and amino acids a pathogen needs. Commensal *E. coli* strains that use the same sugars as an incoming pathogenic strain exclude it most effectively.
- Inhibitory substances: bacteriocins, microcins and colicins kill close relatives of the producer; type VI secretion systems inject toxins into neighboring Gram-negative cells ([7.11.34](/7.11.34)).
- *Chemical environment*: fermentation acids lower the pH, undissociated SCFAs are toxic to enterobacteria, and secondary bile acids inhibit *C. difficile*.
- *Host-mediated*: microbial products induce antimicrobial peptides (C-type lectins such as RegIIIγ) and secretory IgA, and butyrate keeps the lumen anoxic, denying facultative anaerobes such as *Salmonella* the respiratory electron acceptors that give them an advantage in inflammation.

### 32.4.4. Development of the Immune System and Tolerance

The immune system must tolerate a vast load of foreign antigen at the mucosa while responding to pathogens. Germ-free mice show how much of this depends on microbes: their gut-associated lymphoid tissue is underdeveloped, Peyer's patches and isolated lymphoid follicles are small, IgA production is low, T-helper subsets are skewed, and the number and function of colonic regulatory T cells are reduced *(animal experiment)*. Defined microbes restore defined parts of this: the capsular polysaccharide A of *Bacteroides fragilis* corrects T-cell development and protects mice from experimental colitis (Mazmanian et al. 2005); a consortium of clostridia, and butyrate itself, induce colonic regulatory T cells; segmented filamentous bacteria, which attach to the ileal epithelium, induce Th17 cells in mice *(all animal experiment)*. The immune system in turn shapes the community, through IgA, antimicrobial peptides and the mucus barrier. The cellular immunology is treated in [7.15](/7.15).

### 32.4.5. The Gut–Brain Axis

**Definition 32.4.2 (Gut–Brain Axis)** The **gut–brain axis** is the set of bidirectional routes connecting the gut and its microbiota with the central nervous system: the vagus nerve and the enteric nervous system, the immune system and its cytokines, the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis, and microbial metabolites — SCFAs, bile acids, tryptophan metabolites and neuroactive compounds — carried in the blood.

The strongest results are from rodents. Germ-free mice differ from conventional mice in anxiety-like behavior, in the stress response of the HPA axis, in microglial maturation and in blood–brain barrier permeability; some behavioral differences are reversed by colonization early in life, and some are transferred with the microbiota *(animal experiment)*. In a mouse model of maternal immune activation, offspring show autism-like behaviors together with an altered gut community, increased intestinal permeability and a large increase in the circulating bacterial metabolite 4-ethylphenyl sulfate; feeding *Bacteroides fragilis* corrected several of these, and the metabolite alone induced anxiety-like behavior in untreated mice (Hsiao et al. 2013) *(animal experiment)*. What this means for people is much less clear: human studies are mostly cross-sectional associations between composition and depression, anxiety or autism, in which diet, medication and the behavioral consequences of the condition are obvious confounders, and the few randomized trials of probiotics for mood have given small and inconsistent effects *(association; randomized trials inconclusive)*. The gut–brain axis is real as a set of routes; specific claims that particular bacteria cause particular human behaviors are not established.

## 32.5. Dysbiosis and Disease

**Definition 32.5.1 (Dysbiosis)** **Dysbiosis** is a persistent alteration of the composition or function of a microbiota, relative to that of healthy individuals of the same population, that is associated with disease. The word describes a state, not a mechanism, and it does not by itself say whether the alteration is a cause or a consequence.

### 32.5.1. Antibiotics

A course of an oral antibiotic can halve the diversity of the gut community within days. In the classic study, three adults given ciprofloxacin for five days lost about a third of their taxa; the community largely resembled its initial state four weeks later, but some taxa had not returned after six months, and a second course led to a less complete recovery (Dethlefsen and Relman). Repeated courses, especially in the first years of life when the community is assembling, are associated in cohorts with later obesity, asthma and inflammatory bowel disease *(association)*, and antibiotic treatment of young mice causes lasting metabolic changes *(animal experiment)*. This is an argument for limiting unnecessary antibiotic use, alongside the resistance argument of [7.11.15](/7.11.15).

### 32.5.2. *Clostridioides difficile* Infection

The clearest case of microbiota-dependent disease. *Clostridioides difficile* (formerly *Clostridium difficile*) is a spore-forming anaerobe carried asymptomatically by a minority of healthy adults and by many infants. Antibiotic treatment removes the bacteria whose secondary bile acids and nutrient competition keep it in check; its spores germinate, it grows and produces the toxins TcdA and TcdB, which inactivate Rho GTPases of the colonic epithelium, break the cytoskeleton and the tight junctions and provoke inflammation, causing diarrhea and, in severe cases, pseudomembranous colitis. Infection follows antibiotic use in hospital most often, but also occurs in the community; the spores resist alcohol disinfection and persist in the environment, and recurrence after antibiotic treatment is common because the treatment prevents the community from recovering. This is also the one indication for which restoring the community is established therapy (Section 32.6.3) — so the causal chain runs: antibiotics → loss of colonization resistance → infection → restoration of the community cures it *(randomized trials)*.

### 32.5.3. Inflammatory Bowel Disease

Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis arise from an inappropriate immune response to the gut microbiota in genetically susceptible people: susceptibility genes (NOD2, autophagy genes, IL23R) concern microbial sensing and handling, lesions occur where bacterial numbers are highest, diverting the fecal stream can improve Crohn's disease, and germ-free animals with the same genetic susceptibility do not develop colitis *(animal experiment)*. Patients' communities have lower diversity, fewer butyrate producers such as *Faecalibacterium prausnitzii*, more Enterobacteriaceae, and reduced functional gene content *(association)*. No single causal organism has been found, and much of the compositional change is a consequence of inflammation itself, which makes the gut oxidizing and favors facultative anaerobes. The accepted view is of a disordered interaction among host genetics, immunity, the community and the environment rather than an infection.

### 32.5.4. Obesity and Metabolic Disease

The claim that gut microbes contribute to obesity rests on a strong animal result and a weaker human one. Germ-free mice have less body fat than conventional mice on the same intake and gain fat when colonized; transferring the community from an obese mouse transfers greater adiposity; and in the most-cited experiment, germ-free mice that received the community of the obese member of a human twin pair discordant for obesity gained more fat than mice receiving the lean twin's community, on identical diets — and co-housing the two groups, which lets mice acquire each other's bacteria, prevented the gain, but only on a high-fiber diet (Ridaura et al. 2013) *(animal experiment)*. In people, the early report that obesity is accompanied by a higher ratio of Bacillota to Bacteroidota has not replicated consistently; what is reproducible is an association of obesity and metabolic syndrome with lower microbial gene richness *(association)*. Proposed mechanisms include a modest increase in energy harvest from the diet, SCFA signaling to appetite hormones, bile acid modification, and low-grade inflammation driven by bacterial products such as lipopolysaccharide crossing a more permeable mucosa ("metabolic endotoxemia"), which is supported in mice and remains hypothetical in people. Fecal transplants from lean donors to people with metabolic syndrome transiently improved insulin sensitivity in small trials, with effects that waned and varied between recipients *(small randomized trials)*.

### 32.5.5. Allergy, Cancer and Drug Response

- *Allergy and the hygiene hypothesis*: allergic disease is more common in urban, industrialized settings, and less common in children who grow up on traditional farms, with older siblings or with pets *(association)*. The original idea that infections prevent allergy has been reformulated: what matters appears to be early and diverse exposure to microorganisms, including the microbiota itself, during the period when immune regulation is being established. Specific mechanisms (induction of regulatory T cells by commensal bacteria and their metabolites) are supported in mice *(animal experiment)*, and trials of early-life probiotics for preventing allergy have given mixed results.
- Tumors: besides *H. pylori* and gastric cancer, colibactin-producing *E. coli* of the B2 phylogroup damage DNA and leave a distinctive mutational signature in human colorectal tumors; enterotoxigenic *Bacteroides fragilis* and *Fusobacterium nucleatum* are enriched in colorectal tumors and promote tumors in mouse models *(association plus animal experiment)*. The microbiota also affects treatment: the response of melanoma patients to immune checkpoint inhibitors is associated with community composition, germ-free or antibiotic-treated mice respond poorly, and transferring responders' communities into mice improves the response *(association plus animal experiment)*; early trials of fecal transplantation to convert non-responders have shown responses in a minority of patients *(small uncontrolled trials)*.

## 32.6. Modifying the Microbiome

### 32.6.1. Diet

Diet is the largest modifiable influence on the gut community. Fiber — the fermentable carbohydrates of whole grains, legumes, vegetables and fruit — determines how much SCFA is produced and supports the taxa that produce it; controlled feeding studies show shifts within days, and high-fiber intake is associated with lower rates of colorectal cancer and cardiovascular disease *(association, with a plausible SCFA mechanism)*. Trials of fiber supplements show that responses differ greatly between individuals, which is often attributable to whether their community contains the organisms able to degrade the particular substrate.

### 32.6.2. Probiotics, Prebiotics and Beyond

**Definition 32.6.1 (Probiotic and Prebiotic)** A **probiotic** is a live microorganism which, administered in adequate amounts, confers a health benefit on the host; a **prebiotic** is a substrate selectively used by host microorganisms that confers a health benefit. (Definitions of the FAO/WHO and of the International Scientific Association for Probiotics and Prebiotics.) A *synbiotic* combines the two; *postbiotic* denotes a preparation of inanimate microorganisms or their components with a health benefit.

Both definitions require a demonstrated benefit, so a product containing live bacteria is a probiotic only if that strain, at that dose, has been shown to do something. Most marketed products are species of *Lactobacillus* (now split into many genera) and *Bifidobacterium* in fermented milks; most do not establish themselves in the colon, and their effects, where present, are transient and strain-specific. The evidence is best for a few indications: reduction of the duration of acute infectious diarrhea in children, prevention of antibiotic-associated diarrhea, and prevention of necrotizing enterocolitis in preterm infants, each supported by meta-analyses of randomized trials, with effects that depend on the strain *(randomized trials)*. For most other claims the evidence is weak or absent, and in severely ill or immunocompromised patients live organisms can themselves cause infection. Prebiotics (inulin, fructo- and galacto-oligosaccharides) reliably increase bifidobacteria; clinical benefits are less clear. The field is moving toward defined consortia of gut commensals chosen for a mechanism, and toward engineered strains.

### 32.6.3. Fecal Microbiota Transplantation

**Definition 32.6.2 (Fecal Microbiota Transplantation)** **Fecal microbiota transplantation** (FMT) is the transfer of a processed fecal preparation from a screened healthy donor into the gut of a patient, by colonoscopy, enema, nasoenteric tube or encapsulated freeze-dried material, in order to restore a functional microbial community.

For recurrent *C. difficile* infection it works: in randomized trials, resolution rates of about 80–90 % compared with roughly a quarter to a half for repeated antibiotic therapy, and the transferred strains persist for months to years. Defined preparations derived from donor stool, and a defined consortium of spore-forming bacteria, have since been licensed in some countries, which replaces an undefined material with a controlled one. For every other indication tried — inflammatory bowel disease, metabolic syndrome, irritable bowel syndrome, autism, improving the response to immunotherapy — results are inconsistent or preliminary, with evident donor-by-recipient variability.

FMT is a transfer of living organisms, and the donor material can carry pathogens that screening did not cover; the transmission of a multidrug-resistant organism from a screened donor to two immunocompromised recipients, one of whom died, led regulators in 2019 to require screening for resistant organisms (US FDA safety alert, June 2019). Donor screening is correspondingly extensive, and long-term consequences of transferring a whole community are unknown.

## Summary

- **Microbiota** and **microbiome** (Definition 32.1.1): the organisms and their genes. Humans carry about $3.8\times10^{13}$ bacteria against about $3.0\times10^{13}$ human cells, a ratio near 1.3 : 1 (Sender et al. 2016), almost all in the colon.
- Methods: amplicon and shotgun sequencing for composition and gene content ([7.8.14](/7.8.14)), culturomics for mechanisms, **germ-free** and gnotobiotic animals (Definition 32.1.2) for causality, and human trials. Claims in this chapter are labeled *association*, *animal experiment* or *randomized trial* (see the evidence figure in Section 32.1.3), because confounding by diet, drugs and transit time and reverse causation are pervasive (Section 32.1.3).
- Sites (see the body-site figure in Section 32.2.5): skin by sebaceous, moist and dry microenvironments; the mouth as the best-described biofilm, with caries and periodontitis as ecological shifts; airways with no resident lung community but transient immigration; stomach with *Helicobacter pylori*; a sparse, fast-flowing small intestine; the colon with $10^{11}$ cells per gram, dominated by Bacillota and Bacteroidota and spatially organized by the two mucus layers; the vagina dominated by lactobacilli at pH 4.
- Assembly (see the community-assembly figure in Section 32.3.1): the fetus is essentially sterile; colonization at birth depends on mode of delivery; milk oligosaccharides select *Bifidobacterium longum* subsp. *infantis*; weaning drives the shift to an adult, fiber-degrading community by two to three years; adult communities are individual and stable, and change again in old age.
- Functions: fermentation of fiber to **short-chain fatty acids** supplying 5–10 % of energy and acting as signals (see the gut-function figure in Section 32.4.3); synthesis of vitamins; conversion of **bile acids** and of drugs; **colonization resistance** (Definition 32.4.1); maturation of the immune system and mucosal tolerance; the **gut–brain axis** (Definition 32.4.2), strong in rodents and largely correlational in people.
- **Dysbiosis** (Definition 32.5.1) is a state, not a mechanism. Antibiotics reduce diversity with incomplete recovery; *C. difficile* infection is the clearest causal case and is cured by restoring the community; IBD, obesity, allergy and cancer involve the microbiota but with evidence of mixed strength (see the evidence figure in Section 32.1.3).
- Interventions: diet above all; **probiotics** and **prebiotics** (Definition 32.6.1) with strain-specific and limited evidence; **fecal microbiota transplantation** (Definition 32.6.2), established for recurrent *C. difficile* infection and experimental elsewhere, with real safety risks.

## Questions

1. A study reports that the feces of patients with a chronic disease contain fewer butyrate producers than those of healthy controls, and concludes that loss of butyrate producers causes the disease. List four alternative explanations, and describe the experiments that would support a causal claim.

   **Answer** (a) Reverse causation: the disease, its inflammation or its altered transit changes the community. (b) Confounding by diet: patients may eat less fiber. (c) Confounding by medication: antibiotics, proton-pump inhibitors, metformin and laxatives all shift the community. (d) Compositional artifact: an increase in another taxon lowers all other relative abundances; and small samples with many taxa tested give false positives. Support for causality would need: a prospective cohort in which the change precedes disease onset; colonization of gnotobiotic animals with patient and control communities, or with the candidate strains, reproducing the phenotype; a mechanism (here, butyrate acting on regulatory T cells or on colonocyte oxygen consumption); and finally a randomized trial in which supplying the organisms or their product improves outcomes.

2. Why does a course of a broad-spectrum antibiotic predispose to *C. difficile* infection, and why does fecal microbiota transplantation cure recurrent infection when further antibiotics often do not?

   **Answer** The antibiotic removes much of the resident community and with it colonization resistance: the bacteria that compete for nutrients, produce inhibitory substances and, in particular, 7α-dehydroxylate primary bile acids to secondary bile acids that inhibit the germination and growth of *C. difficile*. Its spores survive the antibiotic, germinate and the vegetative cells produce toxins. Treating with another antibiotic kills vegetative *C. difficile* but also prevents the community from recovering, so spores that survive germinate again after treatment ends — hence recurrence. FMT re-establishes a complete community, restoring bile acid metabolism and nutrient competition, so the niche is no longer available.

3. Human milk oligosaccharides are indigestible by the infant. Why does the mother make them, and what does the strategy of *Bifidobacterium longum* subsp. *infantis* illustrate about the selection of a microbiota?

   **Answer** They are a nutrient directed not at the infant but at particular bacteria: the host feeds a selected member of its own microbiota, a private resource that most other organisms cannot use, and the fermentation products (acetate, lactate) acidify the colon and suppress enterobacteria. Some oligosaccharides also act as decoys for pathogen adhesins. *B. infantis* imports the intact oligosaccharides and degrades them intracellularly, so it does not share the products with competitors — a monopolizing strategy. The example shows that a host can shape its microbiota by supplying substrates, as plants do with root exudates ([7.11.31](/7.11.31#sec-31.3.3)).

4. The rumen supplies a cow with most of its energy, while colonic fermentation supplies a human with 5–10 %. Give three reasons from the anatomy and position of the fermentation chamber.

   **Answer** (a) Size and retention: the rumen holds 100–150 L and retains feed for 20–50 h, long enough to degrade cellulose, whereas the human colon is small and retains contents for about a day with most fiber incompletely fermented. (b) Position: the rumen precedes the acid stomach and small intestine, so both the fermentation acids and the microbial cells — their protein and vitamins — are digested and absorbed, whereas in a hindgut fermenter the microbes are lost in the feces. (c) Diet and digestion: a human diet contains much less fiber and much more starch, protein and fat that are digested and absorbed in the small intestine before the colon is reached ([7.11.31](/7.11.31#sec-31.6)).

5. A probiotic yogurt is advertised as "restoring the gut flora". What would have to be shown for the claim to meet the definition of a probiotic, and why do most such products not change the composition of an adult's gut community?

   **Answer** The definition requires a specified strain, at a specified dose and viability at the end of shelf life, with a demonstrated health benefit in controlled human trials for a defined use — benefits are strain-specific and cannot be extrapolated from the species. Most products do not change composition because the adult community is stable and saturated: the resident organisms occupy the available niches (colonization resistance), the ingested strains are mostly food-fermentation organisms poorly adapted to the colon, and they are washed out within days. Where effects exist they are usually transient and due to the metabolites or immune signals produced during passage rather than to establishment.
