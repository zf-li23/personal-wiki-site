Before the 1940s a bacterial pneumonia, an infected wound or puerperal fever was treated with rest and hope; the great majority of patients with pneumococcal pneumonia and bacteremia died. Antimicrobial drugs changed that within a decade, and they made possible much of modern medicine: surgery, cancer chemotherapy, organ transplantation and intensive care all depend on our ability to prevent and treat bacterial infections. This chapter describes how these drugs were found, how their activity is measured and what they do to the bacterial cell. The drugs are organized by target, and each target is a part of the cell described in earlier chapters: the peptidoglycan wall and its synthesis ([7.11.2](/7.11.2), [7.11.8](/7.11.8)), the membranes, the ribosome ([7.2.19](/7.2.19)), DNA gyrase ([7.2.3](/7.2.3)), RNA polymerase ([7.2.10](/7.2.10)) and the folate pathway. The kinetics of inhibition ([7.1.9](/7.1.9)) and the Hill equation ([7.1.7](/7.1.7)) supply the quantitative language; agents used outside the body, such as disinfectants, are in [7.11.10](/7.11.10). How bacteria escape these drugs is the subject of [7.11.15](/7.11.15), and antiviral drugs are treated with vaccines in [7.11.28](/7.11.28).

## 14.1. From Antisepsis to Antibiotics

### 14.1.1. Magic Bullets and Sulfa Drugs

Once the germ theory had been established, the first response was to keep microorganisms out of wounds. In 1867 Joseph Lister published his system of antiseptic surgery, in which carbolic acid (phenol) was applied to wounds, dressings and instruments; it greatly reduced postoperative sepsis. But phenol and the other **antiseptics** ([7.11.10](/7.11.10)) damage human tissue as much as bacteria and cannot be given to cure an infection that is already inside the body.

The idea of a drug that would harm the microbe and spare the host came from Paul Ehrlich. He had noticed that some dyes stain bacteria but not animal cells, which suggested that the two kinds of cell differ chemically and that a chemical might be found that binds only to the microbe, a "magic bullet". Ehrlich and his colleagues, among them Sahachiro Hata, tested hundreds of organic arsenic compounds against the syphilis spirochete *Treponema pallidum*; in 1909 compound number 606, arsphenamine, cured infected animals, and as Salvarsan it became the first synthetic antimicrobial drug. It was toxic and hard to administer, but it showed that selective chemotherapy was possible.

The next class came from the German dye industry. In 1932 Gerhard Domagk found that a red sulfonamide dye, Prontosil, protected mice against lethal streptococcal infections, although it had no effect on streptococci in a test tube. In 1935 a group at the Pasteur Institute showed why: in the body Prontosil is split to sulfanilamide, which is the active compound. Sulfanilamide was cheap and unpatentable, and hundreds of derivatives, the sulfa drugs, followed; they were the first drugs to cure streptococcal and pneumococcal infections widely. The episode carried a lesson that is still relevant: a compound screened only against bacteria in culture would have been missed. Domagk was awarded the Nobel Prize in 1939 (the National Socialist government forced him to decline it; he received the medal in 1947).

### 14.1.2. Penicillin and Streptomycin

In 1928 Alexander Fleming noticed that colonies of *Staphylococcus aureus* near a contaminating mold on one of his plates were lysing. He identified the mold as a *Penicillium*, called the active substance penicillin and showed that culture filtrates killed many Gram-positive bacteria without harming rabbits or mice, but he could not purify the unstable compound and did not pursue its therapeutic use. Ten years later Howard Florey and Ernst Chain at Oxford took it up; in 1940 they showed that penicillin protects mice against streptococcal infection, and in 1941 they treated their first patients. Industrial production by deep-tank fermentation, developed in the United States during the Second World War, made penicillin G widely available by 1944. Fleming, Florey and Chain shared the Nobel Prize in 1945.

The search then turned to soil microorganisms. In 1943 Albert Schatz, a graduate student in Selman Waksman's laboratory at Rutgers University, isolated streptomycin from *Streptomyces griseus*; it was the first drug effective against tuberculosis, and Waksman received the Nobel Prize in 1952. Waksman's group found many more such compounds, and he gave the word antibiotic its modern meaning.

**Definition 14.1.1 (Antimicrobial Drug and Antibiotic)** An **antimicrobial drug** is a chemical agent used to treat or prevent infection by killing microorganisms or inhibiting their growth inside the body. An **antibiotic**, in the strict sense, is an antimicrobial substance produced by a microorganism that kills or inhibits other microorganisms at low concentration; in medical usage the word also covers *semisynthetic* derivatives of such natural products (ampicillin, methicillin, amikacin) and, loosely, entirely *synthetic* antibacterial drugs (sulfonamides, trimethoprim, quinolones, isoniazid).

Most natural antibiotics come from a few groups of soil organisms: the actinomycetes (streptomycin, tetracyclines, erythromycin, chloramphenicol, vancomycin, daptomycin), above all *Streptomyces*, which produce about two thirds of the natural antibiotics in clinical use; *Bacillus* and *Paenibacillus* (bacitracin, polymyxins); and fungi (penicillins from *Penicillium*, cephalosporins from *Acremonium*, formerly *Cephalosporium*). They are **secondary metabolites**, made in stationary phase by large biosynthetic gene clusters; in the soil they probably serve both as weapons and, at lower concentrations, as signals. The names reflect the source: drugs from *Streptomyces* end in *-mycin*, those from *Micromonospora* in *-micin* (gentamicin).

### 14.1.3. The Golden Age and the Discovery Void

Between about 1940 and 1962 nearly all the antibiotic classes in use today were discovered, most by screening soil isolates for zones of inhibition against test bacteria (figure below). The method then ran dry: the same compounds were found again and again, and among the classes found later, few reached wide clinical use. Most new drugs since the 1960s are derivatives of old scaffolds, designed to evade resistance or to widen the spectrum; the oxazolidinones (linezolid, approved in 2000), the lipopeptides (daptomycin, 2003) and the diarylquinolines (bedaquiline, 2012, for tuberculosis only) are the main exceptions.

The gap is not only scientific. Antibiotics are taken for days, not for life; a new drug that works against resistant bacteria is held in reserve, so that resistance to it develops slowly, and sells little; and resistance will eventually erode its value anyway. Several companies that brought new antibiotics to market in the 2010s went bankrupt soon after, and most large pharmaceutical companies have left the field. Proposals to separate the reward for a new antibiotic from the volume sold, such as the subscription contracts introduced in the United Kingdom, address this problem ([7.11.15](/7.11.15)).

## 14.2. Properties of Antimicrobial Drugs

### 14.2.1. Selective Toxicity

**Definition 14.2.1 (Selective Toxicity and Therapeutic Index)** **Selective toxicity** is the ability of a drug to harm the pathogen at concentrations that do little harm to the host. It is measured by the **therapeutic index**,

$$
\mathrm{TI} = \frac{\mathrm{TD}_{50}}{\mathrm{ED}_{50}},
$$
the ratio of the dose that is toxic in half of the subjects to the dose that is effective in half of them.

Selective toxicity comes from differences between the targets of the pathogen and the host. It is greatest when the target has no counterpart in the host: penicillin inhibits peptidoglycan synthesis, which animals do not have, and its therapeutic index is very high (its main adverse effect, allergy, is not a toxic effect). Drugs that act on bacterial ribosomes discriminate between the bacterial 70S and the eukaryotic 80S ribosome, but they are less selective because mitochondria have bacterial-type ribosomes; drugs against nucleic acid synthesis rely on smaller differences still. Agents too toxic for systemic use, such as bacitracin, neomycin and polymyxin B, are used topically, on skin and mucous membranes. The five main targets, and the order of selectivity, are shown in the figure below; Sections 14.4–14.8 take them in turn.

### 14.2.2. Spectrum of Activity

**Definition 14.2.2 (Spectrum of Activity)** The **spectrum of activity** of an antimicrobial drug is the range of microorganisms it kills or inhibits. *Narrow-spectrum* drugs act on a limited group (vancomycin on Gram-positive bacteria, isoniazid on mycobacteria); *broad-spectrum* drugs act on many Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria (tetracyclines, carbapenems, fluoroquinolones).

The spectrum follows from where the target is found and whether the drug can reach it. The **outer membrane** of Gram-negative bacteria excludes large or hydrophobic molecules such as vancomycin and most macrolides ([7.11.2](/7.11.2)); mycoplasmas, which lack a cell wall, are unaffected by every drug that acts on peptidoglycan; and the waxy, **mycolic acid**-rich envelope of mycobacteria excludes many drugs. Broad-spectrum drugs are valuable when a life-threatening infection must be treated before the pathogen is identified, but they also suppress the normal microbiota: a typical consequence is diarrhea or colitis caused by *Clostridioides difficile* (formerly *Clostridium difficile*), which is normally kept in check by competing gut bacteria ([7.11.32](/7.11.32)). Good practice is therefore to start broadly if necessary and narrow the treatment once the pathogen and its susceptibility are known.

### 14.2.3. Static and Cidal Drugs

Drugs are **bacteriostatic** or **bactericidal** in the sense of [7.11.10](/7.11.10) (Definition 10.1.2). With a bacteriostatic drug, such as a tetracycline, a sulfonamide or a macrolide, the viable count stops increasing but does not fall, and clearing the infection depends on the host's defenses; such drugs may fail in patients whose immune system is suppressed or at sites with few phagocytes, such as heart valves (endocarditis) and the cerebrospinal fluid (meningitis), where bactericidal drugs are preferred. The distinction is not absolute: it depends on the concentration, the organism and its growth state (most cidal drugs kill only growing cells), and in clinical trials of common infections bacteriostatic drugs have generally done as well as bactericidal ones.

Why some drugs kill is itself an interesting question. Each cidal drug causes a characteristic primary lesion (holes in the wall, double-strand breaks, mistranslated membrane proteins), which in turn triggers secondary damage to membranes and DNA. Whether reactive oxygen species formed through respiration contribute to killing by all bactericidal drugs, as proposed in 2007, remains disputed.

### 14.2.4. Getting the Drug to the Pathogen

A drug is useful only if it reaches the site of infection at a concentration above that needed to inhibit the pathogen, for long enough, without accumulating to toxic levels. This is the subject of *pharmacokinetics*, the movement of a drug through the body: absorption (oral drugs must survive stomach acid; penicillin G does not, and aminoglycosides are not absorbed from the gut, so both are given by injection), distribution (few drugs enter the cerebrospinal fluid unless the meninges are inflamed; aminoglycosides do not enter host cells and so cannot reach intracellular bacteria), metabolism and excretion (most β-lactams are excreted rapidly by the kidneys, and their dose must be reduced in renal failure). For a dose given intravenously the plasma concentration in the simplest, one-compartment model falls exponentially, $C(t) = C_{\max}\,\mathrm{e}^{-k_{\mathrm{el}}t}$, with half-life $t_{1/2} = \ln 2/k_{\mathrm{el}}$; the peak concentration $C_{\max}$ and the area under the concentration–time curve (AUC) summarize the exposure. Clinical microbiology relates these quantities to what the drug does to the pathogen (Section 14.3.4).

## 14.3. Measuring Antimicrobial Activity

### 14.3.1. Dilution Tests: the MIC and MBC

**Definition 14.3.1 (Minimum Inhibitory and Bactericidal Concentrations)** The **minimum inhibitory concentration** (MIC) of a drug for a bacterial strain is the lowest concentration that prevents visible growth of a standard inoculum in a standard medium after a standard incubation. The **minimum bactericidal concentration** (MBC) is the lowest concentration that kills at least $99.9\,\%$ of the inoculum in the same time.

In the reference method, broth microdilution, a series of twofold dilutions of the drug in Mueller–Hinton broth (for example $0.06$ to $128\,\mu\mathrm{g\,mL^{-1}}$) in the wells of a microtiter plate is inoculated with $5\times10^5$ colony-forming units per milliliter and incubated at $35\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ for $16$–$20$ hours ([7.11.10](/7.11.10) shows the tube version). Each condition matters. The MIC rises with the size of the inoculum (the *inoculum effect*, strongest for drugs destroyed by bacterial enzymes, such as β-lactams attacked by β-lactamases); Mueller–Hinton medium is used because it is reproducible from batch to batch, supports most nonfastidious pathogens and contains little thymidine, which would let bacteria bypass sulfonamides and trimethoprim. Because the dilutions are twofold, the MIC is known only to within a factor of two, and a difference of one dilution between two measurements is within the error of the method. Automated instruments, descended from a system developed for NASA in the 1970s, now read miniaturized broth dilutions photometrically and report MICs within hours.

To find the MBC, samples from the clear wells are plated on drug-free agar; the lowest concentration from which fewer than $0.1\,\%$ of the inoculum grows is the MBC. A drug is usually called bactericidal for an organism if its MBC is at most four times the MIC.

**Example 14.3.1** A strain of *Escherichia coli* grows in wells containing $0.25$ to $2\,\mu\mathrm{g\,mL^{-1}}$ of ciprofloxacin and not at $4\,\mu\mathrm{g\,mL^{-1}}$ or above, so its MIC is $4\,\mu\mathrm{g\,mL^{-1}}$ (it lies between $2$ and $4$). The susceptible *E. coli* wild type has an MIC of about $0.015\,\mu\mathrm{g\,mL^{-1}}$: the strain is eight dilution steps, about $250$-fold, less susceptible, as expected of a strain with mutations in two target genes ([7.11.15](/7.11.15)).

### 14.3.2. Disk Diffusion

**Definition 14.3.2 (Disk Diffusion Test)** The **disk diffusion test** (Kirby–Bauer test) measures susceptibility from the diameter of the zone of inhibition around a paper disk containing a defined amount of drug, placed on an agar plate whose surface has been inoculated with a standardized suspension of the organism.

In the standardized method, published by Bauer, Kirby and colleagues in 1966, Mueller–Hinton agar $4\,\mathrm{mm}$ deep is swabbed with a suspension of $1$–$2\times10^8$ cells per milliliter, up to a dozen disks are applied, and zone diameters are measured after $16$–$20$ hours at $35\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$. The method is cheap, flexible and still widely used. Why the zone diameter tells us the MIC is a problem in diffusion. The drug diffuses out of the disk into the agar while the bacteria begin to grow. The edge of the zone is fixed at a *critical time* $T_0$ of a few hours, when the lawn has become dense enough not to be stopped by the drug that arrives later; at the edge, the concentration at $T_0$ just equals the MIC.

**Formula 14.3.1 (Zone of Inhibition)** Treat the disk as an instantaneous point source releasing an amount $M$ of drug into an agar layer of depth $h$, in which the drug has diffusion coefficient $D$. The concentration at distance $r$ after time $t$ is the two-dimensional diffusion kernel ([2.5](/2.5)),

$$
C(r, t) = \frac{M}{4\pi Dht}\exp\left(-\frac{r^2}{4Dt}\right).
$$
Setting $C(r_{\mathrm{z}}, T_0) = \mathrm{MIC}$ gives the radius of the zone,

$$
r_{\mathrm{z}}^2 = 4DT_0\left[\ln\frac{M}{4\pi DhT_0} - \ln\mathrm{MIC}\right]. \tag{14.1}
$$

The squared zone diameter is a linear function of $\ln\mathrm{MIC}$ with slope $-16DT_0$; equivalently, each doubling of the MIC reduces $d^2 = 4r_{\mathrm{z}}^2$ by the same amount, $16DT_0\ln 2$. The model also explains the rules of the method: the zone depends on the agar depth $h$, the amount in the disk $M$, the diffusion coefficient of the drug and the growth rate of the organism (through $T_0$), so zone sizes of different drugs cannot be compared with one another, and every variable must be standardized. Large molecules that diffuse slowly, such as vancomycin and colistin, give small zones even against susceptible strains, and disk diffusion is unreliable for them.

**Example 14.3.2** With $M = 30\,\mu\mathrm{g}$, $D = 5\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{cm^2\,s^{-1}} = 0.018\,\mathrm{cm^2\,h^{-1}}$, $h = 0.4\,\mathrm{cm}$ and $T_0 = 4\,\mathrm{h}$, the first term in Eq. (14.1) is $\ln(30/0.362) = 4.42$ (concentrations in $\mu\mathrm{g\,mL^{-1}}$). A strain with $\mathrm{MIC} = 1\,\mu\mathrm{g\,mL^{-1}}$ gives $r_{\mathrm{z}}^2 = 0.288 \times 4.42 = 1.27\,\mathrm{cm^2}$, a zone of $22.6\,\mathrm{mm}$; a strain with $\mathrm{MIC} = 8\,\mu\mathrm{g\,mL^{-1}}$ gives $16.4\,\mathrm{mm}$. Each doubling of the MIC removes $16DT_0\ln 2 = 0.80\,\mathrm{cm^2}$ from $d^2$; when the bracket becomes negative, around $\mathrm{MIC} = 80\,\mu\mathrm{g\,mL^{-1}}$, no zone forms beyond the $6\,\mathrm{mm}$ disk.

### 14.3.3. Breakpoints, Standards and Gradient Tests

An MIC or a zone diameter becomes a clinical result only by comparison with breakpoints, the values that divide strains into categories: S, susceptible at the standard dose; R, resistant, with a high probability of treatment failure; and an intermediate category, which the European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST) has defined since 2019 as "susceptible, increased exposure" (the infection is likely to respond to a higher dose or at a site where the drug is concentrated). Breakpoints are set drug by drug and organism by organism, by the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) in the United States and by EUCAST in Europe, from three kinds of evidence: the distribution of MICs in the wild-type population of the species, the concentrations the drug reaches in patients, and the outcome of treatment in relation to the MIC. Zone-diameter breakpoints are then derived by measuring the MIC and the zone diameter for many isolates (figure above, panel C). The upper end of the wild-type MIC distribution is the *epidemiological cutoff* (ECOFF), which separates strains without acquired resistance mechanisms from those with them, whatever their clinical significance.

The *gradient strip test* (Etest is the trade name) combines the two methods: a plastic strip carrying a continuous exponential gradient of the drug is placed on an inoculated plate, and the MIC is read where the elliptical zone of inhibition meets the scale printed on the strip. It is convenient for single drugs and for organisms, such as many anaerobes, that grow poorly in broth. Molecular tests that detect resistance genes directly (for example *mecA* in *S. aureus*, or *rpoB* mutations in *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* by the Xpert MTB/RIF assay) give results in hours instead of days, but only for the mechanisms they look for.

### 14.3.4. Time–Kill Curves and Pharmacodynamics

The MIC is a single number read at one time; the *time–kill curve* follows the viable count of a culture exposed to a constant concentration of drug over several hours. It shows how fast a drug kills and how this depends on concentration, which leads to the most useful quantitative description of drug action, a dose–response curve for the net growth rate of the population.

**Formula 14.3.2 (Pharmacodynamic Function)** At a constant drug concentration $c$ the population changes exponentially, $N(t) = N_0\mathrm{e}^{\psi(c)t}$, with net growth rate

$$
\psi(c) = \psi_{\max} - \frac{(\psi_{\max} - \psi_{\min})(c/\mathrm{MIC})^\kappa}{(c/\mathrm{MIC})^\kappa - \psi_{\min}/\psi_{\max}}, \tag{14.2}
$$
where $\psi_{\max} > 0$ is the growth rate without drug, $\psi_{\min} < 0$ the net rate at saturating concentration (its magnitude is the maximal killing rate) and $\kappa$ a Hill coefficient that sets the steepness (Regoes and colleagues, 2004). The function decreases from $\psi_{\max}$ at $c = 0$ through $0$ at $c = \mathrm{MIC}$ to $\psi_{\min}$ as $c \to \infty$.

The form is that of the **Hill equation** ([7.1.7](/7.1.7)) for the killing effect, shifted so that the net rate is zero at the MIC; setting $c = \mathrm{MIC}$ in Eq. (14.2) gives $\psi = \psi_{\max} - \psi_{\max} = 0$. Its parameters sort drugs into two types (figure below). For **β-lactams** and glycopeptides the curve is steep (large $\kappa$) and the killing rate saturates at a few times the MIC: raising the concentration further adds little, and what matters is the *time* during which the free concentration stays above the MIC, as a fraction of the dosing interval ($fT_{>\mathrm{MIC}}$). These *time-dependent* drugs are given often or by continuous infusion. For aminoglycosides and fluoroquinolones the curve is shallow and killing keeps increasing over a wide range: these *concentration-dependent* drugs are best given as large, infrequent doses, and their efficacy correlates with $C_{\max}/\mathrm{MIC}$ or $\mathrm{AUC}/\mathrm{MIC}$. Many drugs also suppress regrowth for some hours after the concentration has fallen below the MIC (the *postantibiotic effect*), which further favours once-daily aminoglycosides. A bacteriostatic drug has $\psi_{\min}$ close to zero.

## 14.4. Inhibitors of Cell Wall Synthesis

Every step of peptidoglycan synthesis, in the cytoplasm, the membrane and outside the membrane, is the target of at least one drug; the pathway and the sites of action are described in [7.11.8](/7.11.8#sec-8.2). Because animals have no peptidoglycan, these are the most selective of all antibacterial drugs, and they account for more than half of all antibiotics used.

### 14.4.1. β-Lactam Antibiotics

**Definition 14.4.1 (β-Lactam Antibiotic)** A **β-lactam antibiotic** is an antibacterial drug containing a β-lactam ring (a four-membered cyclic amide) that acylates the active-site serine of the penicillin-binding proteins, the transpeptidases that cross-link peptidoglycan. The main classes are the *penicillins*, *cephalosporins*, *carbapenems* and *monobactams*.

The **penicillin-binding proteins** (PBPs) owe their name to this covalent reaction. The four-membered ring is strained, and its amide is much more reactive than an ordinary peptide bond. The ring and its substituents mimic the D-Ala–D-Ala end of the peptidoglycan precursor ([7.1.8](/7.1.8#sec-8.4.5) compares the two structures); the active-site serine of the transpeptidase attacks the β-lactam carbonyl as it would attack the D-Ala–D-Ala peptide bond, but the resulting acyl-enzyme hydrolyzes extremely slowly, so the enzyme is inactivated ([7.11.8](/7.11.8#sec-8.2.2)). The classes differ in the ring fused to the β-lactam (a thiazolidine in penicillins, a dihydrothiazine in cephalosporins, a carbon-containing five-membered ring in carbapenems, none in monobactams), and members of each class differ in their side chains, which determine the spectrum, the stability to stomach acid and β-lactamases, and the pharmacokinetics. Penicillin G ([7.1.8](/7.1.8#sec-8.4.5)) is compared here with cefalexin (1, a cephalosporin), meropenem (2, a carbapenem) and aztreonam (3, a monobactam):

```smiles

C[C@@H]1[C@@H]2[C@H](C(=O)N2C(=C1S[C@H]3C[C@H](NC3)C(=O)N(C)C)C(=O)O)[C@@H](C)O 2
C[C@H]1[C@@H](C(=O)N1S(=O)(=O)O)NC(=O)/C(=N\OC(C)(C)C(=O)O)/c2csc(n2)N 3
```

- *Penicillins* are derivatives of 6-aminopenicillanic acid with different acyl side chains. The natural penicillin G (benzylpenicillin) is active mainly against Gram-positive bacteria and must be injected; penicillin V is acid-stable and can be taken orally. Semisynthetic penicillins, made from 6-aminopenicillanic acid obtained by fermentation, include ampicillin and amoxicillin (broader spectrum: their polar side chains pass through the porins of some Gram-negative bacteria), methicillin, oxacillin and flucloxacillin (resistant to the penicillinase of staphylococci, [7.11.15](/7.11.15)) and piperacillin (active against *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*).
- *Cephalosporins*, derived from cephalosporin C of the fungus *Acremonium* (isolated from a sewage outfall in Sardinia in 1945), are grouped into generations: later generations are generally more active against Gram-negative bacteria and more stable to β-lactamases (ceftriaxone, ceftazidime), and the "fifth-generation" ceftaroline also binds the PBP2a of methicillin-resistant *S. aureus*. Cross-allergy with penicillins is uncommon, about $1$–$2\,\%$ of penicillin-allergic patients, and occurs mostly when the side chains are similar.
- *Carbapenems* (imipenem, meropenem, ertapenem), derived from thienamycin of *Streptomyces cattleya*, have the broadest spectrum of the β-lactams and resist most β-lactamases; they are drugs of last resort for severe infections by multidrug-resistant Gram-negative bacteria, which makes the spread of carbapenemases so alarming ([7.11.15](/7.11.15)).
- *Monobactams* (aztreonam) have the β-lactam ring alone. They act only against aerobic Gram-negative bacteria, rarely cross-react in allergy and are not hydrolyzed by the metallo-β-lactamases.

The effect on a growing cell is dramatic: rods treated with a β-lactam bulge at the site of division and burst within an hour or two. Lysis is not a direct consequence of blocking cross-linking. It requires the autolysins, which continue to cut the wall that can no longer be repaired, and inhibited transpeptidases leave the glycosyltransferases running, so that uncross-linked strands are made and immediately degraded, a futile cycle that exhausts the cell's precursors (Cho, Uehara and Bernhardt, 2014). β-Lactams kill only growing cells; nongrowing cells, including persisters, survive ([7.11.15](/7.11.15)). Different PBPs have different affinities for each drug, which also shapes the spectrum; an organism's PBPs may be intrinsically insensitive (the PBP5 of *Enterococcus faecium*, which makes enterococci resistant to cephalosporins).

### 14.4.2. Glycopeptides and Other Wall Inhibitors

**Definition 14.4.2 (Glycopeptide Antibiotic)** A **glycopeptide antibiotic** is a glycosylated cyclic or polycyclic peptide, such as vancomycin or teicoplanin, that binds the D-Ala–D-Ala end of the pentapeptide of peptidoglycan precursors and thereby blocks both transglycosylation and transpeptidation.

Vancomycin was isolated in 1953 from a soil actinomycete from Borneo, now called *Amycolatopsis orientalis*. Its rigid, cup-shaped heptapeptide forms five hydrogen bonds with the D-Ala–D-Ala terminus of lipid II and of nascent peptidoglycan; it acts by binding the substrate, not the enzyme. The molecule (about $1450\,\mathrm{Da}$) is too large to cross the outer membrane, so it acts only on Gram-positive bacteria. It became the main treatment for infections by methicillin-resistant *S. aureus* and other resistant Gram-positive bacteria, and its overuse led to vancomycin-resistant enterococci, which replace the terminal D-Ala by D-lactate ([7.11.15](/7.11.15)). Lipoglycopeptides (telavancin, dalbavancin, oritavancin) carry a lipid tail that anchors them in the membrane next to their target and, for dalbavancin, gives a half-life of more than a week.

Three other drugs act earlier in the pathway ([7.11.8](/7.11.8#sec-8.2.3)). *Bacitracin*, a cyclic peptide from *Bacillus licheniformis* (the producing strain was originally identified as *B. subtilis*), binds undecaprenyl pyrophosphate and prevents the recycling of the lipid carrier; it is too nephrotoxic for systemic use and is applied to skin. *Fosfomycin*, a phosphoenolpyruvate analog, covalently inactivates MurA, the first enzyme of the pathway; it is used mainly as a single oral dose for urinary tract infections. *D-Cycloserine*, an analog of D-alanine, inhibits alanine racemase and D-Ala–D-Ala ligase and is a second-line drug against tuberculosis.

The mycobacterial envelope offers its own targets (Section 14.8.1): isoniazid blocks the synthesis of mycolic acids, and ethambutol that of arabinogalactan, the polysaccharide linking mycolic acids to peptidoglycan.

## 14.5. Inhibitors of Protein Synthesis

The bacterial ribosome is the target of more classes of antibiotic than any other structure. Most of these drugs bind ribosomal RNA at the functional centers of the 30S or 50S subunit, and their sites and effects on each step of translation are described in [7.2.19](/7.2.19#sec-19.7). Here we consider the classes as drugs.

**Definition 14.5.1 (Ribosome-Targeting Antibiotic Classes)** **Aminoglycosides** are polycationic compounds of amino sugars linked by glycosidic bonds to an aminocyclitol (streptomycin, kanamycin, gentamicin, tobramycin, amikacin); they bind the decoding center of 16S rRNA in the 30S subunit and cause misreading of mRNA. **Tetracyclines** have a linear four-ring (naphthacene) system (tetracycline, doxycycline, minocycline, tigecycline); they bind the 30S subunit and block binding of aminoacyl-tRNA to the A site. **Macrolides** have a 14- to 16-membered lactone ring carrying amino and neutral sugars (erythromycin, clarithromycin, azithromycin); they bind 23S rRNA in the nascent peptide exit tunnel of the 50S subunit.

| Class | Subunit and site | Effect | Action | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Aminoglycosides | 30S, decoding center (helix 44 of 16S rRNA) | misreading; inhibition of translocation | cidal | uptake needs the proton motive force, so anaerobes and streptococci are intrinsically resistant; nephrotoxic and ototoxic |
| Tetracyclines | 30S, near the A site | block aminoacyl-tRNA binding | static | broad spectrum, incl. chlamydias and rickettsias; chelate $\ce{Ca^2+}$, discolor developing teeth |
| Macrolides | 50S, exit tunnel | context-specific stalling of the nascent chain | static | Gram-positive bacteria, *Legionella*, mycoplasmas; few side effects |
| Lincosamides (clindamycin) | 50S, peptidyl transferase center | block peptide bond formation | static | anaerobes, staphylococci; high risk of *C. difficile* colitis |
| Chloramphenicol | 50S, peptidyl transferase center | blocks accommodation of aminoacyl-tRNA | static | very broad; rare fatal aplastic anemia, reserved for life-threatening infections |
| Oxazolidinones (linezolid) | 50S, A site of the peptidyl transferase center | context-specific inhibition of peptide bond formation | static | synthetic; MRSA and vancomycin-resistant enterococci; myelosuppression in long courses |
| Others | fusidic acid (EF-G), mupirocin (isoleucyl-tRNA synthetase), pleuromutilins (peptidyl transferase center) | | | mostly topical or for Gram-positive infections |

The *aminoglycosides* deserve a closer look, because they are cidal although they act on the ribosome. Their uptake is driven by the membrane potential: a small amount enters, causes misreading, and the mistranslated membrane proteins disrupt the membrane, which lets more drug in, an accelerating cycle that kills the cell. This explains why aminoglycosides are inactive against anaerobic bacteria, which have a lower membrane potential, and why they act synergistically with cell wall inhibitors, which ease their entry (Section 14.9). Their toxicity to the inner ear and kidney comes partly from binding to mitochondrial ribosomes, and a mutation in mitochondrial 12S rRNA (m.1555A>G) makes its carriers highly susceptible to aminoglycoside-induced deafness. Amikacin and plazomicin are semisynthetic derivatives designed to escape the modifying enzymes that inactivate older aminoglycosides ([7.11.15](/7.11.15)).

The *tetracyclines* are broad-spectrum and active against intracellular bacteria, which they enter; they are a treatment of choice for chlamydias, rickettsias and Lyme disease. Resistance by efflux and ribosome protection led to the glycylcycline tigecycline, which escapes both. The *macrolides* were once thought to plug the exit tunnel completely; ribosome profiling showed that they allow many proteins to be made and stall translation only at specific sequence motifs, so the cell's proteome is distorted rather than shut down.

## 14.6. Inhibitors of Nucleic Acid Synthesis

Replication and transcription use enzymes that are conserved in all organisms, so few drugs against them are selective. Two classes, both cidal, are of great importance.

**Definition 14.6.1 (Quinolone)** **Quinolones** are synthetic antibacterial drugs with a 4-quinolone (4-oxo-1,4-dihydroquinoline-3-carboxylic acid) nucleus that inhibit the bacterial type II topoisomerases, DNA gyrase and topoisomerase IV, by trapping them on DNA as cleavage complexes. *Fluoroquinolones* (ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin, moxifloxacin) carry a fluorine at position 6.

The first quinolone, nalidixic acid (1962), was found as a by-product of the synthesis of the antimalarial chloroquine and was used only for urinary infections; adding the fluorine and a piperazine ring in the 1980s produced the broad-spectrum fluoroquinolones. **DNA gyrase** introduces negative supercoils and relieves the positive supercoils ahead of replication forks, and topoisomerase IV unlinks daughter chromosomes ([7.2.3](/7.2.3#sec-3.3.2)). Quinolones bind at the interface of the enzyme and the cleaved DNA and prevent religation, turning the enzyme into a poison that holds double-strand breaks; collisions of replication forks with these complexes release the breaks and kill the cell ([7.2.3](/7.2.3#sec-3.3.3)). In most Gram-negative bacteria the primary target is gyrase (GyrA), in many Gram-positive bacteria topoisomerase IV (ParC); stepwise mutations in the two genes give high resistance ([7.11.15](/7.11.15)). Fluoroquinolones are orally active and penetrate tissues well, which made them among the most prescribed antibiotics; their adverse effects include tendon rupture, which led regulators to restrict their use for uncomplicated infections.

The *rifamycins* (rifampicin, also called rifampin, and rifabutin, rifapentine), semisynthetic derivatives of a product of *Amycolatopsis rifamycinica*, bind the β subunit of bacterial **RNA polymerase** in the RNA exit channel close to the active site. They do not stop an elongating polymerase; they block the RNA after two or three nucleotides have been joined, so they inhibit initiation ([7.2.10](/7.2.10), Question 5). Rifampicin is the most important drug against tuberculosis and leprosy because it kills nongrowing and intracellular mycobacteria; single point mutations in a short region of *rpoB* make it resistant, at a frequency of about $10^{-8}$, so it is never used alone. It turns urine and tears orange-red and strongly induces liver cytochrome P450 enzymes, which accelerates the breakdown of many other drugs. Fidaxomicin binds a different site of RNA polymerase and is used against *C. difficile*.

## 14.7. Antimetabolites

### 14.7.1. Sulfonamides, Trimethoprim and Sequential Blockade

An *antimetabolite* (growth factor analog) is a compound similar enough to a metabolite to bind the enzyme that uses it, but different enough to block it, an application of **competitive inhibition** ([7.1.9](/7.1.9#sec-9.4)). The folate pathway is the classic target (figure below): tetrahydrofolate carries one-carbon units for the synthesis of purines, thymidylate and methionine and for formylating the initiator tRNA, so a folate-starved cell cannot make DNA. Bacteria must synthesize folate; humans take it up from the diet and lack the enzyme that the sulfonamides inhibit.

**Definition 14.7.1 (Sulfonamide)** **Sulfonamides** (sulfa drugs) are synthetic analogs of $p$-aminobenzoic acid (PABA) with a sulfonamide group in place of the carboxyl group (sulfanilamide, sulfamethoxazole, sulfadiazine). They competitively inhibit dihydropteroate synthase (DHPS), which condenses PABA with a pterin pyrophosphate, and some are themselves incorporated into dead-end pterin–sulfonamide adducts.

```smiles
Nc1ccc(cc1)C(=O)O PABA
Nc1ccc(cc1)S(N)(=O)=O 4
COc1cc(Cc2cnc(N)nc2N)cc(OC)c1OC 5
```

(PABA, sulfanilamide 4 and trimethoprim 5.) Trimethoprim, a diaminopyrimidine, inhibits dihydrofolate reductase (DHFR), the enzyme that reduces dihydrofolate to tetrahydrofolate. Humans also have DHFR, but trimethoprim binds the bacterial enzyme several thousand times more tightly; methotrexate, by contrast, binds the human enzyme and is an anticancer drug ([7.1.9](/7.1.9#sec-9.4.3)). Alone each drug is bacteriostatic. The combination of trimethoprim and sulfamethoxazole (co-trimoxazole) is synergistic and often bactericidal: blocking two successive steps of one pathway lowers the flux more than either block alone, and in addition each drug potentiates the other through a feedback in the folate pathway, trimethoprim reducing the supply of the pterin substrate of DHPS (Minato and colleagues, 2018). Co-trimoxazole is used for urinary tract infections and, at high doses, for pneumonia caused by the fungus *Pneumocystis jirovecii*, whose folate enzymes resemble those of bacteria. Sulfonamides were displaced by better drugs for most other purposes; dapsone, a related sulfone, is used against leprosy.

### 14.7.2. Isoniazid and Other Prodrugs

Isoniazid, a hydrazide of isonicotinic acid, is active only against mycobacteria. It is a structural analog of nicotinamide, but its mechanism is more specific than that of a simple antimetabolite: it is a *prodrug*, oxidized inside the bacterium by the mycobacterial catalase–peroxidase KatG to a radical that combines with NAD to form an isonicotinoyl–NAD adduct, which binds tightly to InhA, the enoyl–acyl carrier protein reductase of the type II fatty acid synthase that elongates mycolic acids. Most resistant clinical isolates have mutations in *katG* (the drug is no longer activated) or in the promoter of *inhA* (the target is overproduced). Several other antimycobacterial drugs are also prodrugs activated by bacterial enzymes (pyrazinamide, ethionamide, pretomanid), and so is the antiprotozoal metronidazole (Section 14.10.2).

## 14.8. Drugs That Act on Membranes and Combined Therapy of Tuberculosis

### 14.8.1. Membrane-Active Drugs

Bacterial and animal membranes are made of similar lipids, so membrane-active drugs tend to be toxic, but two are important. The *polymyxins* (polymyxin B and polymyxin E, colistin) from *Paenibacillus polymyxa* are cationic cyclic lipopeptides that bind the negatively charged phosphates of lipid A in the **lipopolysaccharide** of the outer membrane, displace the $\ce{Mg^2+}$ and $\ce{Ca^2+}$ that bridge neighboring LPS molecules, and insert their fatty acyl tail; they then disrupt the outer membrane and, by binding LPS in transit, the cytoplasmic membrane. They act only on Gram-negative bacteria. Their kidney and nerve toxicity confined them to topical use for decades, but colistin has returned as a last-resort drug against carbapenem-resistant Gram-negative bacteria, which made the discovery of transferable colistin resistance in 2015 a serious blow ([7.11.15](/7.11.15)). *Daptomycin*, a cyclic lipopeptide from *Streptomyces roseosporus*, inserts into the cytoplasmic membrane of Gram-positive bacteria in a calcium-dependent manner, binding phosphatidylglycerol, depolarizes it and disrupts membrane-associated wall synthesis; it is bactericidal against MRSA and vancomycin-resistant enterococci, but it is inactivated by pulmonary surfactant and cannot be used for pneumonia.

### 14.8.2. Why Tuberculosis Needs Four Drugs

Tuberculosis shows most of the principles of this chapter at once. *M. tuberculosis* divides only about once a day, much of the population in a lesion is nongrowing or lives inside macrophages and in the caseous centers of granulomas, where drugs penetrate poorly, and the mycolic acid envelope excludes many drugs. A patient with cavitary disease can carry $10^8$ to $10^9$ bacilli, more than the reciprocal of the frequency of resistance mutations to any single drug, so treatment with one drug selects resistant mutants that already exist (the arithmetic is in [7.10.34](/7.10.34#sec-34.6.5) and [7.6.41](/7.6.41#sec-41.5)). The standard regimen for drug-susceptible tuberculosis therefore uses four drugs with different targets for two months (isoniazid, rifampicin, pyrazinamide and ethambutol), then isoniazid and rifampicin for four more months:

- isoniazid kills the rapidly growing bacilli and lowers the bacterial load in the first weeks;
- rifampicin and pyrazinamide sterilize the lesions by killing slowly metabolizing and nongrowing bacilli; pyrazinamide, a prodrug converted by the bacterial amidase PncA to pyrazinoic acid, acts in the acidic environment of inflamed tissue, and its target is still debated (the coenzyme A pathway enzyme PanD is the best-supported candidate);
- ethambutol inhibits the arabinosyltransferases that make arabinogalactan and protects the other drugs from resistance while susceptibility is unknown.

The six-month course is long because of the slowly killed subpopulations, and patients who stop early relapse, often with resistant strains; supervised treatment ("directly observed therapy") was introduced to ensure adherence. Since 2022 the World Health Organization also recommends a four-month regimen with rifapentine and moxifloxacin for drug-susceptible tuberculosis and a six-month regimen of bedaquiline, pretomanid, linezolid and moxifloxacin for multidrug-resistant tuberculosis ([7.11.15](/7.11.15)).

## 14.9. Drug Combinations

Combinations are used for four reasons: to obtain synergy, to prevent the emergence of resistance (tuberculosis, HIV), to cover all likely pathogens before identification in a severely ill patient, and to treat mixed infections. Combinations also have costs: more adverse effects, more disruption of the microbiota, a false sense of security, and sometimes antagonism. Whether a combination is synergistic depends on what one expects if the two drugs do not interact, and there are two standard answers.

**Definition 14.9.1 (Synergy, Additivity and Antagonism)** Two drugs are *additive* if their combined effect equals that expected from a null model of noninteraction, *synergistic* if it is greater and *antagonistic* if it is smaller. In the *Loewe additivity* model, a drug is treated as a dilution of itself: a combination of concentrations $a$ and $b$ is additive if $a/A + b/B = 1$, where $A$ and $B$ are the concentrations of each drug alone that give the same effect. In the *Bliss independence* model, the drugs act independently, so that the fractions of cells they fail to inhibit multiply: $1 - E_{AB} = (1 - E_A)(1 - E_B)$, where $E$ is the fractional inhibition.

**Formula 14.9.1 (Fractional Inhibitory Concentration Index)** In a *checkerboard* assay, twofold dilutions of two drugs are combined in all pairs on a microtiter plate. If the combination of concentrations $a$ and $b$ inhibits growth, while the MICs of the drugs alone are $\mathrm{MIC}_A$ and $\mathrm{MIC}_B$,

$$
\mathrm{FICI} = \frac{a}{\mathrm{MIC}_A} + \frac{b}{\mathrm{MIC}_B},
$$
minimized over all inhibitory combinations. By convention, $\mathrm{FICI} \le 0.5$ is called synergy, $\mathrm{FICI} > 4$ antagonism, and values in between indifference.

The FIC index is the Loewe criterion at the MIC: on the straight additive isobole it equals 1. Because the dilutions are twofold, a single test cannot distinguish values between $0.5$ and $2$ reliably, which is why the conventional thresholds are so wide. The two null models make different predictions when dose–response curves are steep: in panel C of the figure above, Bliss independence for two drugs with Hill coefficient 2 lies close to, but not on, the Loewe line, and for other parameters the two can differ widely, so that a combination may be called synergistic in one model and additive in the other. Systematic screens of drug pairs in *E. coli* (Yeh, Kishony and colleagues, 2006) found that interactions are largely predictable from the drugs' mechanisms: drugs that act on the same cellular function tend to interact in the same way.

Classic examples of each kind:

- *Synergy*: trimethoprim with sulfamethoxazole (sequential blockade); a β-lactam or vancomycin with an aminoglycoside, used for enterococcal endocarditis, because damage to the wall increases aminoglycoside uptake; a β-lactam with a β-lactamase inhibitor ([7.11.15](/7.11.15)).
- *Antagonism*: a bacteriostatic drug with a drug that kills only growing cells, such as a tetracycline with penicillin; in a study of pneumococcal meningitis published in 1951, patients given penicillin with chlortetracycline died more often than those given penicillin alone.
- *Suppression of resistance*: in tuberculosis, the combination works because resistance to each drug arises independently (Section 14.8.2), even though the drugs do not interact in the sense of Definition 14.9.1.

## 14.10. Antifungal and Antiprotozoal Drugs

### 14.10.1. Antifungal Drugs

Fungi are eukaryotes, so their ribosomes, polymerases and most enzymes resemble ours, and there are only a few classes of systemic antifungal drugs; the biology of fungi is in [7.11.21](/7.11.21). The main targets are those that differ from human cells:

| Class | Example | Target | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Polyenes | amphotericin B, nystatin | bind ergosterol, the sterol of fungal membranes; amphotericin B forms aggregates that extract ergosterol from the membrane and ion channels | fungicidal, broad; amphotericin B is nephrotoxic (lipid formulations less so); nystatin topical |
| Azoles | fluconazole, voriconazole, clotrimazole | lanosterol 14α-demethylase (CYP51, Erg11) of ergosterol synthesis | mostly fungistatic; oral; widely used, resistance increasing |
| Allylamines | terbinafine | squalene epoxidase of ergosterol synthesis | dermatophyte infections of skin and nails |
| Echinocandins | caspofungin, micafungin | β-1,3-glucan synthase of the cell wall | intravenous; first-line for invasive candidiasis |
| Pyrimidine analog | flucytosine | converted by fungal cytosine deaminase (absent from human cells) to 5-fluorouracil, which disrupts RNA and DNA synthesis | resistance arises quickly, used in combination with amphotericin B |
| Others | griseofulvin | microtubules (mitosis) | dermatophytes |

The emergence of *Candida auris*, first described in 2009 and now spreading in hospitals worldwide, often resistant to azoles and sometimes to all three main classes, and of azole-resistant *Aspergillus fumigatus*, selected by azole fungicides in agriculture, has made antifungal resistance a growing problem.

### 14.10.2. Antiprotozoal Drugs

Antiprotozoal drugs are similarly few, often toxic, and in several cases their mechanism was understood only after they were in use. Against malaria, chloroquine and related quinolines accumulate in the acidic food vacuole of *Plasmodium* and prevent the crystallization of the toxic heme released from digested hemoglobin into hemozoin; widespread chloroquine resistance led to *artemisinin*, isolated from sweet wormwood (*Artemisia annua*) by Tu Youyou's group in 1972 (Nobel Prize, 2015), whose endoperoxide bridge is activated by heme iron to radicals that damage parasite proteins. The WHO recommends artemisinin-based combination therapies, an artemisinin derivative with a longer-acting partner drug, to protect both from resistance. Pyrimethamine inhibits parasite DHFR like trimethoprim, and several antibacterial drugs (doxycycline, clindamycin) are active against apicomplexans because they inhibit the bacterial-type ribosomes of the apicoplast, a relict plastid ([7.11.20](/7.11.20)). *Metronidazole* is a prodrug that is reduced to toxic radicals only in organisms with low-redox-potential electron carriers such as ferredoxin; it is selective for anaerobes and microaerophiles, both protists (*Giardia*, *Trichomonas*, *Entamoeba*) and anaerobic bacteria (*Bacteroides*, *C. difficile*). Antiviral drugs, which must target virus-specific steps, are treated in [7.11.28](/7.11.28).

- **Antibiotics** and other **antimicrobial drugs** (Definition 14.1.1) were introduced from 1909 (Salvarsan), 1932–1935 (sulfonamides), 1941 (penicillin) and 1943 (streptomycin); most classes were found from soil microorganisms by 1962, and few since.
- **Selective toxicity** is measured by the therapeutic index $\mathrm{TD}_{50}/\mathrm{ED}_{50}$ (Definition 14.2.1); the **spectrum of activity** (Definition 14.2.2) depends on the target and on access through the envelope.
- The **MIC** and **MBC** (Definition 14.3.1) are measured by standardized dilution; in the **disk diffusion test** (Definition 14.3.2) the squared zone diameter falls linearly with $\ln\mathrm{MIC}$ (Formula 14.3.1). Breakpoints (CLSI, EUCAST) classify strains as S, I or R; the ECOFF separates wild-type from non-wild-type strains.
- Killing follows the pharmacodynamic function, a Hill-type curve of net growth rate against concentration (Formula 14.3.2): steep and saturating for time-dependent drugs (β-lactams: $fT_{>\mathrm{MIC}}$), shallow for concentration-dependent drugs (aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones: $C_{\max}/\mathrm{MIC}$, $\mathrm{AUC}/\mathrm{MIC}$).
- Combinations are synergistic, additive or antagonistic relative to Loewe additivity or Bliss independence (Definition 14.9.1); the FIC index (Formula 14.9.1) is the Loewe criterion at the MIC. Tuberculosis needs four drugs because resistance mutations pre-exist in large bacillary populations and some bacilli are killed slowly.
- Antifungal drugs target ergosterol and its synthesis, β-1,3-glucan synthesis and fungus-specific prodrug activation; antiprotozoal drugs include quinolines, artemisinins, antifolates and metronidazole.

## Questions

1. Explain why penicillin has a much higher therapeutic index than chloramphenicol, and why both have a higher one than antifungal drugs.

   **Answer** Penicillin inhibits transpeptidation of peptidoglycan, which human cells lack entirely, so its toxicity is limited to allergy. Chloramphenicol acts on the 50S ribosomal subunit; human cytoplasmic ribosomes differ, but mitochondrial ribosomes are of bacterial type and bind it, which explains its bone marrow toxicity. Fungi are eukaryotes whose ribosomes, polymerases and most enzymes resemble human ones, so only a few targets (ergosterol, β-1,3-glucan synthase, cytosine deaminase) differ enough for selective drugs.

2. In a disk diffusion test the zone diameter for a strain with MIC $1\,\mu\mathrm{g\,mL^{-1}}$ is $24\,\mathrm{mm}$, and for a strain with MIC $4\,\mu\mathrm{g\,mL^{-1}}$ it is $20\,\mathrm{mm}$. Using Eq. (14.1), predict the zone for a strain with MIC $16\,\mu\mathrm{g\,mL^{-1}}$. What happens to all zones if the agar is poured twice as deep?

   **Answer** $d^2$ is linear in $\ln\mathrm{MIC}$: $576$ and $400\,\mathrm{mm^2}$, a loss of $176\,\mathrm{mm^2}$ per fourfold increase. For MIC 16, $d^2 = 400 - 176 = 224\,\mathrm{mm^2}$, $d \approx 15\,\mathrm{mm}$. Doubling $h$ halves the concentration at every point, which is equivalent to doubling the MIC: every $d^2$ decreases by $16DT_0\ln 2$ ($88\,\mathrm{mm^2}$ here, half of the loss per fourfold step), so all zones shrink, and susceptible strains could be misclassified as resistant. This is why the depth is standardized.

3. Distinguish time-dependent from concentration-dependent killing in terms of the pharmacodynamic function (Formula 14.3.2), and explain how the distinction changes the dosing of a β-lactam and of an aminoglycoside.

   **Answer** For a time-dependent drug $\psi(c)$ is steep (large $\kappa$) and reaches its minimum a few times above the MIC, so raising the concentration does not kill faster; the effect depends on how long the free concentration stays above the MIC. β-Lactams, with short half-lives, are therefore given frequently or by extended or continuous infusion. For a concentration-dependent drug $\psi(c)$ is shallow and keeps falling over a wide range, so higher peaks kill faster; aminoglycosides are given as a single large daily dose, which maximizes $C_{\max}/\mathrm{MIC}$, exploits the postantibiotic effect and, by allowing a long drug-free interval, reduces accumulation in the kidney and inner ear.

4. A checkerboard assay gives the MICs of drug A alone $8\,\mu\mathrm{g\,mL^{-1}}$ and of drug B alone $2\,\mu\mathrm{g\,mL^{-1}}$. The inhibitory combinations with the lowest concentrations are ($1$, $0.5$) and ($2$, $0.25$) in $\mu\mathrm{g\,mL^{-1}}$. Calculate the FIC index and classify the interaction. Why would combining a bacteriostatic macrolide with a β-lactam be unwise?

   **Answer** $1/8 + 0.5/2 = 0.375$ and $2/8 + 0.25/2 = 0.375$; FICI $= 0.375 \le 0.5$, synergy. A β-lactam kills only cells that are synthesizing peptidoglycan; a bacteriostatic drug that stops growth removes this activity, so the combination can be less effective than the β-lactam alone (antagonism), as observed for tetracycline and penicillin in pneumococcal meningitis.

5. Explain why isoniazid, rifampicin, pyrazinamide and ethambutol are given together in the first two months of treatment of tuberculosis, and why the treatment then continues for four more months.

   **Answer** A cavity contains about $10^8$–$10^9$ bacilli, and resistance to each drug arises by mutation at frequencies of about $10^{-6}$–$10^{-8}$, so mutants resistant to any one drug are already present; with several drugs of unrelated targets, a cell resistant to all of them is very unlikely (frequencies multiply). The drugs also have complementary roles: isoniazid rapidly kills growing bacilli, rifampicin and pyrazinamide kill slowly metabolizing ones (pyrazinamide in acidic lesions), and ethambutol protects against resistance while susceptibility is unknown. The continuation phase with isoniazid and rifampicin is needed to kill the nongrowing and slowly killed subpopulations that survive the first months; stopping early leads to relapse.
