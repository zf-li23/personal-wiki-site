Microbiology is the study of organisms too small to be seen with the naked eye, together with the viruses that depend on them. Its objects are not one branch of the tree of life but a way of living and a set of methods: bacteria, archaea, microscopic eukaryotes and viruses are studied with the same tools (the microscope, sterile culture, the pure culture, the enrichment) and share the problems of being small, numerous and fast-growing. Microbes were the only life on Earth for most of its history, they still carry out most of its chemistry, and they are the agents of infectious disease, the workhorses of fermentation and biotechnology and the model systems in which molecular biology was discovered.

Microbiology is also a natural starting point for a systematic survey of biological diversity. The number of microbial groups that have been described and cultivated is still manageable, each of them is closely tied to human health, agriculture or the environment, and their biology is built from the molecules and cells treated in the earlier courses: most terms of this course have already appeared in cell biology ([7.3](/7.3)), molecular biology ([7.2](/7.2)) and genetics ([7.4](/7.4)), so little knowledge of organismal biology is needed. The diversity met here, from the three domains of cellular life to the viruses, is the basis on which the later courses on plants, animals and ecosystems build. This chapter defines the field, estimates the size of the microbial world, follows the history of the discipline through the experiments that shaped its methods and describes the range of microbial sizes. The cell as a unit of life, the three domains and the viruses are introduced in [7.3.1](/7.3.1); the origin of life, the last universal common ancestor and the early diversification of prokaryotes in [7.10.27](/7.10.27); and the tree of life in [7.10.2](/7.10.2). The structure of the prokaryotic cell follows in [7.11.2](/7.11.2) and [7.11.3](/7.11.3).

## 1.1. What Microbiology Studies

### 1.1.1. Microorganisms

**Definition 1.1.1 (Microorganism)** A **microorganism** (microbe) is an organism that is microscopic, that is, smaller than about $0.1\,\mathrm{mm}$ in at least its individual cells, and that lives either as a single cell or as a simple aggregate of cells (filaments, colonies, mycelia) without differentiated tissues. By convention, the acellular **viruses** and subviral agents are included in the microbial world, although they are not organisms in the strict sense.

The definition is one of size and organization, not of descent. Microorganisms belong to all three domains of the **three-domain system** and include the viruses, which belong to none:

| Group | Cellular? | Domain | Examples | Chapters |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bacteria | Yes, prokaryotic | Bacteria | *Escherichia coli*, *Bacillus subtilis*, **cyanobacteria** | [7.11.17](/7.11.17), [7.11.18](/7.11.18) |
| Archaea | Yes, prokaryotic | Archaea | methanogens, extreme halophiles, *Sulfolobus*, marine ammonia oxidizers | [7.11.19](/7.11.19) |
| Protists and microalgae | Yes, eukaryotic | Eukarya | *Paramecium*, *Plasmodium*, diatoms, *Chlamydomonas* | [7.11.20](/7.11.20) |
| Fungi (yeasts and molds) | Yes, eukaryotic | Eukarya | *Saccharomyces cerevisiae*, *Aspergillus*, *Penicillium* | [7.11.21](/7.11.21) |
| Viruses and subviral agents | No | none | bacteriophage T4, influenza virus, viroids, prions | [7.11.22](/7.11.22)–[7.11.28](/7.11.28) |

The boundary is blurred at both ends. Some microorganisms are visible to the eye (the sulfur bacterium *Thiomargarita*, giant amoebae, the fruiting bodies of molds), and some viruses are larger than the smallest bacteria (1.9.1). Mushrooms and seaweeds are not microorganisms, but their relatives are studied with microbial methods. "Microorganism" is therefore not a taxon: it groups organisms that share a size range, a style of life and a toolkit, much as "tree" groups plants of many lineages that share a growth form.

**Definition 1.1.2 (Microbiology)** **Microbiology** is the biological science of microorganisms: their structure, physiology, genetics, diversity, evolution and ecology, their interactions with other organisms, including disease, and their use by humans.

### 1.1.2. Microorganisms as Cells

A microbial cell is a complete organism. Like every cell ([7.3.1](/7.3.1#sec-1.2)) it is bounded by a cytoplasmic membrane, contains its **genome** in a **nucleoid** or nucleus and carries out its chemistry in the cytoplasm with **ribosomes** and **enzymes**. Six properties characterize living microbial cells, and each is the subject of later chapters:

| Property | What it means for a microorganism | Chapters |
|---|---|---|
| Metabolism | Uptake of nutrients and their transformation; catabolism releases energy, anabolism builds cell material | [7.11.4](/7.11.4)–[7.11.7](/7.11.7) |
| Reproduction (growth) | Chemicals from the environment become a new cell, mostly by **binary fission** or budding | [7.11.8](/7.11.8) |
| Differentiation | Some cells form new structures, such as endospores, heterocysts or fruiting bodies | [7.11.12](/7.11.12) |
| Communication | Cells signal to one another with chemicals, for example in quorum sensing | [7.11.11](/7.11.11) |
| Movement | Swimming with flagella, gliding and twitching on surfaces | [7.11.3](/7.11.3) |
| Evolution | Mutation, selection and the exchange of genes between cells | [7.11.13](/7.11.13), [7.11.15](/7.11.15) |

Two views of a cell are useful. As a *machine*, it catalyzes chemical transformations with enzymes and converts energy; as a *coding device*, it stores, copies and expresses information in DNA and passes it on. Growth connects the two: a cell must make all its parts before it divides. Microorganisms are especially convenient for studying both, because a culture contains billions of genetically identical cells that double in hours or minutes. This is why comparative biochemists of the Delft school stressed the unity of life ("from elephant to butyric acid bacterium, it is all the same", A. J. Kluyver), and why Jacques Monod could later say that what is true for *E. coli* is true for the elephant. Bacteria and their viruses became the **model organisms** of molecular biology (1.6.2).

## 1.2. Microorganisms in the Biosphere

### 1.2.1. Numbers and Biomass

Microorganisms are present wherever liquid water is available, from saturated brines and acidic mine drainage to hot springs, ice and rock kilometers below the surface. Surface seawater contains about $10^5$–$10^6$ prokaryotic cells per milliliter and fertile soil about $10^9$ per gram. Global estimates of the number of prokaryotic cells on Earth lie between about $1\times10^{30}$ and $5\times10^{30}$; the first estimate (Whitman, Coleman and Wiebe, 1998) was revised downward when direct counts showed fewer cells in marine sediments than assumed. Either number dwarfs the roughly $10^{22}$–$10^{24}$ stars of the observable universe. Most of these cells live not in the oceans or soils but in the subsurface, in sediments and in the pores of rock.

By carbon, plants dominate the biosphere, but microorganisms make up most of the rest (Bar-On, Phillips and Milo, 2018). Bacteria hold about $70$ gigatonnes of carbon, roughly 13 percent of the total of about $550\,\mathrm{Gt\,C}$, against about $2\,\mathrm{Gt\,C}$ for all animals; the estimates for bacteria and archaea are uncertain by about an order of magnitude (a factor of about 10 and 13), mainly because the subsurface is poorly sampled. Microbial cells are also richer in nitrogen and phosphorus than plant tissue (they contain more protein and nucleic acid and less cellulose), so a large share of the biosphere's nitrogen and phosphorus is held in microbes.

![Global biomass of the main groups of organisms in gigatonnes of carbon, on a logarithmic axis, with the share of the total of about 550 Gt C. Plants about 450; bacteria about 70; fungi about 12; archaea about 7; protists about 4; animals about 2; viruses about 0.2. Error bars show the reported fold uncertainties (about 1.2 for plants, 10 for bacteria, 3 for fungi, 13 for archaea, 4 for protists, 5 for animals and 20 for viruses). Data from Bar-On, Phillips and Milo (2018)](./plots/biomass.py)

Within the human body the ratio of bacterial to human cells is about $1.3 : 1$ (about $3.8\times10^{13}$ bacteria, mostly in the colon, against $3.0\times10^{13}$ human cells, of which most are red blood cells; Sender, Fuchs and Milo, 2016), not the "ten to one" quoted for decades, but the bacterial gene content exceeds that of the human genome by about two orders of magnitude. The human **microbiome** is treated in [7.11.32](/7.11.32).

### 1.2.2. Communities and Habitats

In nature, microorganisms live in populations (cells descended from one ancestor) that share a habitat with other populations to form *microbial communities*; a community together with its physical and chemical environment is an ecosystem. Most cells live attached to surfaces in **biofilms** rather than suspended in liquid ([7.11.12](/7.11.12)). Communities change their habitats: aerobic bacteria fed by fertilizer runoff consume the oxygen of coastal waters and create "dead zones" in which fish die; acid-producing bacteria dissolve rock and tooth enamel; photosynthetic **microbial mats** build layered sediments. Organisms that live under conditions that would kill most others, at high temperature, pH, salinity or pressure, are called **extremophiles** ([7.11.9](/7.11.9)); their limits define the physical limits of life. These themes are the subject of **microbial ecology** ([7.11.29](/7.11.29)–[7.11.32](/7.11.32)).

### 1.2.3. Microorganisms in Earth History

For more than half of the history of life, the only organisms were microbial. Layered **stromatolites** built by microbial mats are known from rocks about $3.43\,\mathrm{Ga}$ old, and isotopic and microfossil evidence of life may go further back. Anoxygenic phototrophs and chemolithotrophs preceded the **cyanobacteria**, whose oxygenic photosynthesis led to the **Great Oxidation Event** about $2.4\,\mathrm{Ga}$ ago; eukaryotic cells appear in the fossil record about $1.7\,\mathrm{Ga}$ ago, and animals only in the last $0.6\,\mathrm{Ga}$. The evidence and its controversies are discussed in [7.10.27](/7.10.27), the history of atmospheric oxygen and the geological time scale in [7.10.29](/7.10.29). The microbial inventions of this period, photosynthesis, respiration, **nitrogen fixation** and the main biogeochemical cycles, still run the planet ([7.11.30](/7.11.30)).

### 1.2.4. General Features of Microbial Life

Several general features follow from small size and simple organization. Older textbooks list them as slogans ("small body, large surface; fast uptake, fast conversion; fast growth; easy variation; wide distribution"); each can be given an order of magnitude:

| Feature | Order of magnitude | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Large surface-to-volume ratio | About $5\,\mu\mathrm{m}^{-1}$ for *E. coli*, $0.3\,\mu\mathrm{m}^{-1}$ for a $20\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ animal cell (Formula 1.9.1) | Rapid exchange of nutrients and wastes with the medium; the membrane does the work of organs |
| High specific metabolic rate | A respiring *E. coli* consumes about $20\,\mathrm{mmol}$ of $\ce{O2}$ per gram of dry mass per hour, roughly a thousand times the rate of a resting human per unit mass | A small biomass can transform large amounts of matter |
| Rapid reproduction | Doubling times of about $10$–$20\,\mathrm{min}$ for *Vibrio natriegens* and *E. coli* in rich media, about a day for *Mycobacterium tuberculosis*, days to years in soils and sediments | Populations of $10^9$ cells per milliliter in a day; fast experiments ([7.11.8](/7.11.8)) |
| Large mutation supply | About $10^{-3}$ new mutations per *E. coli* genome per generation; a culture of $10^9$ cells produces about $10^6$ new mutations in each generation | Rapid adaptation, including antibiotic resistance; **horizontal gene transfer** adds whole genes ([7.4.12](/7.4.12), [7.11.13](/7.11.13)) |
| Ubiquity and abundance | About $10^{30}$ cells; growth from below $-15\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ to $122\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ and from pH $0$ to above $11$ | Microbes are present in every habitat and most samples |

### 1.2.5. Microorganisms and Human Society

Infectious diseases were the leading causes of death in the industrialized countries at the beginning of the twentieth century (pneumonia and influenza, tuberculosis, diarrheal diseases); in these countries they have since been displaced by heart disease and cancer, through sanitation, **vaccines** and **antibiotics**, but they remain a major cause of death worldwide ([7.11.33](/7.11.33)–[7.11.35](/7.11.35)). Most microorganisms, however, are harmless or beneficial:

| Area | Role of microorganisms | Chapters |
|---|---|---|
| Agriculture | Root-nodule bacteria (rhizobia) fix $\ce{N2}$ for legumes; rumen microbes digest cellulose for cattle and sheep; microbial insecticides; plant pathogens | [7.11.31](/7.11.31) |
| Food | Fermentation of bread, yoghurt, cheese, sauerkraut, soy sauce, vinegar, wine and beer (in China with *qu*, a mold-grown grain starter, for millennia); spoilage and food-borne disease | [7.11.10](/7.11.10) |
| Energy | Methane from anaerobic digestion, ethanol and other fuels from fermentation | [7.11.5](/7.11.5) |
| Environment | Wastewater treatment, bioremediation of oil spills and pollutants, the global cycles of carbon, nitrogen and sulfur | [7.11.30](/7.11.30) |

## 1.3. Seeing Microbes and the End of Spontaneous Generation

### 1.3.1. Hooke, Leeuwenhoek and Cohn

Microbiology began with the microscope ([7.3.1](/7.3.1#sec-1.1.1)). Robert Hooke described and drew the fruiting structures of molds in *Micrographia* (1665). Antoni van Leeuwenhoek, a Delft draper, ground single lenses that magnified up to about $270$ times and in letters to the Royal Society from 1676 onward described "animalcules" in rainwater, infusions and the plaque between his teeth: the first observations of bacteria and protists. For nearly two centuries the observations remained descriptive, because no one knew how to grow these organisms or what they did.

Ferdinand Cohn (1828–1898), a botanist in Breslau, laid the foundations of bacteriology in the 1870s. He classified bacteria by shape and discovered the heat-resistant endospores of *Bacillus* ([7.11.3](/7.11.3)), which explained why some boiled infusions still became cloudy. Endospores showed that the heat treatments of that time did not always sterilize, a point that weighed in the dispute over spontaneous generation and later shaped sterilization procedures ([7.11.10](/7.11.10)).

### 1.3.2. Pasteur

Louis Pasteur (1822–1895) was a chemist. Studying the crystals of tartaric acid he discovered optical isomers (1848), and he later found that a mold consumed only one of them; this suggested that some reactions considered purely chemical were carried out by living organisms. In the 1850s and 1860s he showed that alcoholic fermentation is the work of yeast and that the souring of beet-juice fermentations was due to rod-shaped bacteria making lactic acid; that each kind of fermentation has its own organism; and that some microorganisms live without air (the butyric fermentation, 1861).

*Spontaneous generation*, the doctrine that living organisms arise from nonliving matter, was then still widely held for microorganisms, which appear in any broth left to stand ([7.10.27](/7.10.27#sec-27.2.1)). Pasteur argued that the organisms came from cells carried in by air or present on the material, and that a broth made sterile and kept sterile would never putrefy. To counter the objection that his sealed flasks excluded the "fresh air" needed for generation, he used flasks with a long, downward-bent neck (early 1860s): the boiled broth stayed in contact with air, but dust and cells settled in the bend of the neck and never reached the liquid.

![Pasteur's swan-necked flask experiment. A: broth is poured into a flask and its neck is drawn out and bent in a flame; B: the broth is boiled, steam drives air out, and on cooling air re-enters slowly, but dust and microorganisms are trapped in the bend of the neck; the broth stays sterile indefinitely. C: if the flask is tipped so that broth touches the contaminated bend, or the neck is broken off, microorganisms reach the broth and it becomes turbid within days](./plots/swan-neck-flask.py)

The experiment ended the debate for microorganisms and established the practice of **sterilization**: killing or removing all living cells from a material. Pasteur went on to apply mild heating to preserve wine and beer (**pasteurization**, now used mainly for milk), and to develop **vaccines** against chicken cholera, anthrax (1881) and rabies (first given to a human, the boy Joseph Meister, in 1885); the Pasteur Institute in Paris was founded in 1888. His work also supported the idea that specific microbes cause specific diseases. The physician Ignaz Semmelweis had already reduced the deaths from childbed fever by making doctors wash their hands in chlorinated lime (1847), without being able to explain why; Joseph Lister, reasoning from Pasteur's results, introduced antiseptic surgery with carbolic acid in 1867.

## 1.4. Koch, Pure Cultures and the Germ Theory of Disease

### 1.4.1. Pure Culture on Solid Media

The proof that particular microorganisms cause particular diseases came from Robert Koch (1843–1910), a German physician. In 1876 he grew the anthrax bacillus, *Bacillus anthracis*, outside the body and showed that after many transfers in culture it still caused anthrax in mice. Such work required cultures containing a single kind of organism, and Koch's laboratory developed the methods still used today.

**Definition 1.4.1 (Pure Culture, Colony and Aseptic Technique)** A **pure culture** is a culture that contains cells of only one kind of microorganism, in practice the descendants of a single cell. A **colony** is a visible mass of cells grown on a solid medium from one cell or a few cells. **Aseptic technique** is the set of practices (sterilized media and instruments, flaming, work in a clean air flow) that prevents contamination of sterile media and cultures by microorganisms from the environment.

Koch first used slices of potato and then nutrient broth solidified with gelatin, which melts at body temperature and is digested by many bacteria. In 1881–1882 Fanny Hesse, the wife of his associate Walther Hesse, suggested agar, a polysaccharide of red algae used to set jellies: it melts at about $85$–$100\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$, sets at about $32$–$40\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ and is not degraded by most bacteria. Richard Petri introduced the shallow covered dish in 1887. On a solid surface each cell grows into a colony with a characteristic shape and color, and colonies picked and restreaked give pure cultures. Koch also noted that colonies breed true, which suggested that bacteria could be classified into species like plants and animals. The media, the streak plate and the counting of colonies are treated in [7.11.4](/7.11.4) and [7.11.8](/7.11.8).

### 1.4.2. Koch's Postulates

With pure cultures, staining and animal experiments, Koch identified *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* as the cause of tuberculosis (announced in 1882), then responsible for about one death in seven in Europe; he received the Nobel Prize in 1905. The criteria he used became the standard of proof for infectious disease.

**Definition 1.4.2 (Koch's Postulates)** **Koch's postulates** are four criteria that establish a microorganism as the cause of an infectious disease: (1) the suspected pathogen is present in all cases of the disease and absent from healthy animals; (2) it can be isolated and grown in pure culture; (3) cells from the pure culture cause the disease when inoculated into a healthy, susceptible animal; (4) the same organism can be reisolated from the experimentally infected animal.

![Koch's postulates as an experiment. 1: blood or tissue of a diseased animal is examined by microscopy and the suspected pathogen is seen (it is absent from healthy animals); 2: the organism is isolated on an agar plate and grown in pure culture; 3: cells of the pure culture are inoculated into a healthy animal, which develops the disease; 4: the same organism is reisolated in pure culture from the experimentally infected animal and shown to be identical with the original isolate](./plots/koch-postulates.py)

**Remark 1.4.1** The postulates are sufficient but not necessary, and many genuine pathogens fail them. Some cannot be grown in culture (*Treponema pallidum*, *Mycobacterium leprae*, many viruses). Healthy carriers, which Koch himself found for cholera, violate the first postulate, and so do diseases with several possible agents or caused by communities rather than single species. Agents restricted to humans have no animal model, so the third postulate can be tested only indirectly. Two extensions are used today. Thomas Rivers (1937) adapted the criteria to viruses. The *molecular Koch's postulates* of Stanley Falkow (1988) apply them to genes: a virulence trait should be associated with the pathogenic strains, inactivating the gene should reduce virulence, and restoring it should restore virulence. Sequence-based criteria (Fredricks and Relman, 1996) identify uncultured pathogens by their DNA in diseased tissue.

**Example 1.4.1** Peptic ulcers were long attributed to stress and acid. Robin Warren and Barry Marshall cultured a curved bacterium, now *Helicobacter pylori*, from the stomachs of patients with gastritis and ulcers (1982–1983). Since no animal model was available, Marshall drank a culture in 1984 and developed gastritis, from which the organism was reisolated; ulcers were later cured with antibiotics. The work fulfilled the postulates in a human volunteer and was recognized by the Nobel Prize in 2005.

Koch's students and those who followed them identified the agents of most major bacterial diseases within two decades (cholera, diphtheria, typhoid fever, plague, tetanus and others). The same period produced the Gram stain (1884, [7.11.2](/7.11.2)) and the acid-fast stain for mycobacteria.

## 1.5. The Discovery of Microbial Diversity

Pasteur and Koch used rich media that grow many organisms. The two founders of general and environmental microbiology, Martinus Beijerinck (1851–1931) in Delft and Sergei Winogradsky (1856–1953), worked with soil and water and designed media that favor organisms with a particular metabolism.

**Definition 1.5.1 (Enrichment Culture)** An **enrichment culture** is a culture in which a medium and incubation conditions are chosen to favor the growth of a particular metabolic or physiological group of microorganisms from a mixed natural sample, so that the members of that group outgrow the others and can then be isolated in pure culture.

Beijerinck isolated root-nodule bacteria (1888), sulfate-reducing bacteria, lactic acid bacteria and the aerobic nitrogen fixer *Azotobacter* (1901). In his studies of tobacco mosaic disease (1898) he confirmed that the agent passes through filters that retain bacteria, as Dmitri Ivanovsky had found in 1892, and showed that it multiplies only in living tissue; he called it a *contagium vivum fluidum*, the first recognition of a virus ([7.11.22](/7.11.22)). Winogradsky studied the sulfur bacterium *Beggiatoa* and showed (1887) that it oxidizes $\ce{H2S}$ to obtain energy: the concept of chemolithotrophy, energy from inorganic compounds, in organisms that he showed also fix $\ce{CO2}$ (**chemolithotrophs**, [7.11.7](/7.11.7)). He isolated the nitrifying bacteria (1890) and the anaerobic nitrogen-fixing *Clostridium pasteurianum*, and the sediment column that bears his name is still the classic demonstration of microbial succession along gradients of light, oxygen and sulfide. Their successors in Delft, A. J. Kluyver and C. B. van Niel, made comparative biochemistry the basis of general microbiology (1.1.2).

## 1.6. Chemotherapy and the Molecular Era

### 1.6.1. Chemotherapy and Antibiotics

Paul Ehrlich, who had developed dyes for staining bacteria, sought chemicals that would bind to microbes but not to human cells, "magic bullets" with **selective toxicity**. After testing hundreds of arsenic compounds his group found arsphenamine (Salvarsan, 1910), the first effective treatment of syphilis. The sulfonamides followed (Gerhard Domagk, 1932–1935), then penicillin, discovered by Alexander Fleming in 1928 and developed as a drug by Howard Florey, Ernst Chain and colleagues in the early 1940s, and streptomycin from a soil actinomycete (Albert Schatz and Selman Waksman, 1943). The targets of these drugs are the subject of [7.11.14](/7.11.14), the resistance that followed them of [7.11.15](/7.11.15).

### 1.6.2. Microbes and Molecular Biology

In the twentieth century bacteria and their viruses became the experimental systems in which the molecular basis of heredity was found. Frederick Griffith (1928) found that harmless rough strains of *Streptococcus pneumoniae* could be converted into virulent smooth, capsulated ones by heat-killed smooth cells (bacterial **transformation**); Oswald Avery, Colin MacLeod and Maclyn McCarty (1944) showed that the transforming principle is DNA. Salvador Luria and Max Delbrück (1943) showed that bacterial mutations arise spontaneously before selection ([7.4.12](/7.4.12)); Joshua Lederberg and Edward Tatum (1946) discovered **conjugation**; the phage experiments of Alfred Hershey and Martha Chase (1952) confirmed DNA as the genetic material of a virus. The genetic code, the operon (François Jacob and Monod, 1961), restriction enzymes and recombinant DNA (1973) all came from work with *E. coli* and its phages ([7.2.1](/7.2.1), [7.4.10](/7.4.10), [7.2.11](/7.2.11)). The first complete genome of a free-living organism, that of *Haemophilus influenzae*, was published in 1995; in 2010 a bacterial cell was booted up with a chemically synthesized genome of *Mycoplasma mycoides*, and its reduced descendant JCVI-syn3.0 is discussed in [7.3.1](/7.3.1#sec-1.3.4).

## 1.7. Woese and the Tree of Life

Bacteria have few morphological characters and leave few fossils, so until the 1970s their evolutionary relationships were unknown; Ernst Haeckel (1866) put them at the base of the tree as "Monera", and Robert Whittaker's five kingdoms (1969) still treated prokaryotes as one kingdom. Following the suggestion of Emile Zuckerkandl and Linus Pauling that molecular sequences record evolutionary history, Carl Woese chose the small-subunit ribosomal RNA (the **16S rRNA gene** of prokaryotes) as a molecular chronometer, because it is (1) present in all cells, (2) functionally constant, (3) changes slowly and (4) is long enough to carry phylogenetic information. In 1977 Woese and George Fox found that methanogens differ from other bacteria as much as from eukaryotes; the Archaea were recognized as a third primary lineage and, in 1990, as one of three domains, Bacteria, Archaea and Eukarya. The history of classification from two kingdoms to three domains is told in [7.10.2](/7.10.2#sec-2.7.3), the case for a two-domain tree with eukaryotes arising within the Archaea in [7.10.27](/7.10.27#sec-27.4).

Sequencing rRNA genes directly from environmental DNA (Norman Pace and others, from the 1980s) showed that cultured microbes are a small minority of those present. The number of known bacterial phyla rose from about $11$ in Woese's 1987 survey of cultured species to more than $80$ by the late 2010s, many of them known only from sequences, including the ultrasmall cells of the **candidate phyla radiation** ([7.11.18](/7.11.18)). That most cells seen under the microscope do not form colonies on agar is the **great plate count anomaly** ([7.11.8](/7.11.8)). Sequence-based classification of microorganisms is the subject of [7.11.16](/7.11.16); the methods of **phylogenetic trees** are in [7.8.6](/7.8.6), those of metagenomics in [7.8.14](/7.8.14).

## 1.9. The Scale of the Microbial World

### 1.9.1. From Viruses to Giant Bacteria

Microorganisms span more than five orders of magnitude in length. The smallest viruses (circoviruses) are about $17$–$20\,\mathrm{nm}$ across, close to the size of a ribosome; the giant viruses (mimiviruses, pandoraviruses) reach about $1\,\mu\mathrm{m}$, larger than the smallest bacteria. Most cultured rod-shaped bacteria are $0.5$–$4\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ wide and less than $15\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ long; *E. coli* is about $1\times2\,\mu\mathrm{m}$. *Pelagibacter ubique* (SAR11), perhaps the most abundant organism in the oceans, measures about $0.2\times0.5\,\mu\mathrm{m}$, and cells about $0.2\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ across have been imaged in groundwater (Luef and colleagues, 2015); the space needed for a genome, ribosomes and the minimal set of proteins puts the lower limit of free-living cells at about $0.15$–$0.2\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ ([7.3.1](/7.3.1#sec-1.3.4)). The smallest free-living eukaryote known, the green alga *Ostreococcus tauri*, is under $1\,\mu\mathrm{m}$.

At the other end, *Epulopiscium fishelsoni* from the gut of surgeonfish reaches about $80\times600\,\mu\mathrm{m}$, and the spherical cells of the sulfur bacterium *Thiomargarita namibiensis* about $750\,\mu\mathrm{m}$. In 2022 Volland and colleagues described *Candidatus* Thiomargarita magnifica, a filamentous cell from mangrove sediments that reaches about $1$–$2\,\mathrm{cm}$ in length, so that a single bacterial cell can be visible to the eye.

### 1.9.2. The Surface-to-Volume Ratio

Small size is an advantage for an organism that takes up all its nutrients through its surface. The rate of uptake scales with membrane area, the demand with volume.

**Formula 1.9.1 (Surface-to-Volume Ratio)** For a sphere of radius $r$, $S/V = 3/r$. For a rod modeled as a cylinder of radius $r$ and length $L$ capped by two hemispheres (total length $L + 2r$),

$$
\frac{S}{V} = \frac{2\pi rL + 4\pi r^2}{\pi r^2 L + \frac{4}{3}\pi r^3} = \frac{2}{r}\cdot\frac{L + 2r}{L + \frac{4}{3}r}, \tag{1.1}
$$
which lies between $3/r$ (a sphere, $L = 0$) and $2/r$ (a long rod).

**Example 1.9.1** For *E. coli* ($r = 0.5\,\mu\mathrm{m}$, total length $2\,\mu\mathrm{m}$, so $L = 1\,\mu\mathrm{m}$), Eq. (1.1) gives $S/V = 4\cdot 2/1.67 \approx 4.8\,\mu\mathrm{m}^{-1}$; for a sphere of *Thiomargarita namibiensis* ($r = 375\,\mu\mathrm{m}$), $S/V = 0.008\,\mu\mathrm{m}^{-1}$, six hundred times less. A rod of a given volume has more surface than a sphere of the same volume, which is one advantage of the elongated shape.

Large surface per volume, together with short diffusion distances, explains why small cells can grow quickly and why smaller cells tend to dominate where nutrients are scarce, as in the open ocean. Very large prokaryotes escape the limit by not filling their volume with cytoplasm. *Thiomargarita* and *Beggiatoa* cells are mostly a central vacuole that stores nitrate, with the cytoplasm a thin layer under the membrane, and *Thiomargarita magnifica* keeps its DNA in many membrane-bound compartments distributed along the cell; *Epulopiscium* contains tens of thousands of copies of its genome, so that every part of the cytoplasm has a genome nearby. The diffusion-limited radius of a respiring cell ([7.3.1](/7.3.1#sec-1.3.2)) and the numbers of molecules in a bacterium ([7.6.20](/7.6.20)) make the argument quantitative.

### 1.9.3. Microscopy in Brief

The resolution of a light microscope is limited by diffraction to about $0.2\,\mu\mathrm{m}$, about the size of the smallest cells; its principles are treated in [7.3.2](/7.3.2) and those of electron microscopy in [7.6.14](/7.6.14). The methods most used in microbiology are:

| Method | Principle | Microbiological use |
|---|---|---|
| **Bright-field microscopy** | Absorption of light; cells are nearly transparent and are usually stained | Stained smears: Gram stain and other stains ([7.11.2](/7.11.2)) |
| **Phase-contrast microscopy** | Converts differences in refractive index into differences in brightness | Living, unstained cells; endospores appear bright |
| **Dark-field microscopy** | Only scattered light reaches the objective; cells bright on a dark background | Thin cells such as spirochetes (*Treponema pallidum*); motility and flagella |
| **Fluorescence microscopy** | Fluorescent dyes or proteins excited by light of a shorter wavelength | Counting cells with DNA stains (DAPI); identifying groups with fluorescent rRNA probes (FISH, [7.11.16](/7.11.16)); GFP fusions |
| Differential interference contrast and confocal microscopy | Contrast from gradients of refractive index; optical sections | Three-dimensional images of cells and biofilms |
| Super-resolution microscopy | Localization of single fluorescent molecules beyond the diffraction limit | Positions of proteins within bacterial cells |
| **Transmission electron microscope** | Electrons transmitted through thin sections or negatively stained specimens; resolution below $1\,\mathrm{nm}$ | Internal structure, viruses, flagella; cryo-electron tomography of intact cells |
| **Scanning electron microscope** | Electrons scattered from a metal-coated surface | Surfaces of cells and communities in three dimensions |

## Summary

- A **microorganism** is a microscopic organism living as a single cell or a simple aggregate without tissues; viruses are included by convention. The category is defined by size and methods, not by descent: it spans Bacteria, Archaea, microbial eukaryotes and the acellular viruses.
- Microbial cells metabolize, reproduce, differentiate, communicate, move and evolve; their speed and number made them the model systems of biochemistry and molecular biology.
- There are about $10^{30}$ prokaryotic cells on Earth, mostly in the subsurface; bacteria alone hold about $70\,\mathrm{Gt\,C}$, second only to plants. Microbes were the only life for most of Earth's history and still drive the biogeochemical cycles.
- General features with their orders of magnitude: high surface-to-volume ratio, high specific metabolic rate, doubling times down to minutes, large mutation supply, ubiquity.
- History and methods: Leeuwenhoek saw bacteria (1676); Pasteur showed that fermentations are made by microbes and disproved spontaneous generation with swan-necked flasks; Koch introduced solid media, pure cultures and the postulates (Definition 1.4.2), whose limits are met by molecular and sequence-based criteria (Remark 1.4.1); Beijerinck and Winogradsky invented enrichment culture (Definition 1.5.1) and discovered chemolithotrophy and viruses; Ehrlich began chemotherapy; bacteria and phages founded molecular biology; Woese's rRNA tree revealed the Archaea and the three domains.
- Sizes range from $20\,\mathrm{nm}$ viruses through $0.2\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ bacteria to centimeter-long *Thiomargarita* cells; $S/V = 3/r$ for spheres and Eq. (1.1) for rods (Formula 1.9.1). Giant cells survive by vacuoles, genome compartments or polyploidy.

## Questions

1. Why is "microorganism" not a taxonomic group? Give one microorganism from each domain and one acellular agent.

   **Answer** A taxon is a group defined by common descent, whereas microorganisms are defined by size and organization. Microbes occur in all three domains and include viruses, which have no place on the tree of cellular life, and the microbial members of each domain are more closely related to macroscopic organisms of the same domain (yeasts to animals) than to microbes of other domains. Examples: *Escherichia coli* (Bacteria), *Halobacterium salinarum* (Archaea), *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* (Eukarya), bacteriophage T4.

2. What objection did the swan-necked flask answer that a sealed flask could not, and why did Cohn's discovery of endospores matter to the debate?

   **Answer** Defenders of spontaneous generation argued that sealing excluded the fresh air required for generation. The open bent neck admitted air but trapped dust and cells, so sterile broth stayed clear indefinitely unless it touched the contaminated bend. Endospores survive boiling, so infusions containing spore-forming bacteria (hay infusions in particular) could become turbid after boiling, which seemed to support spontaneous generation; once spores were known, these results were explained by inadequate sterilization.

3. Which of Koch's postulates fail for (a) *Mycobacterium leprae*, (b) cholera with symptomless carriers, (c) a human-specific virus? How is causation established instead?

   **Answer** (a) Postulate 2: the organism cannot be grown in axenic culture (it is propagated in armadillos and mouse footpads). (b) Postulate 1: the pathogen is present in healthy carriers. (c) Postulate 3: there is no susceptible animal, and human experiments are rarely ethical. Causation is then supported by molecular Koch's postulates (the virulence genes and their inactivation), by sequence-based detection of the agent in lesions, by epidemiological association and dose–response, and by the effect of specific treatment or vaccination.

4. Compute $S/V$ for a spherical coccus of diameter $1\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ and for a rod of the same volume with radius $0.35\,\mu\mathrm{m}$. Which has the larger ratio?

   **Answer** Sphere: $V = \frac{4}{3}\pi(0.5)^3 = 0.524\,\mu\mathrm{m}^3$, $S/V = 3/0.5 = 6.0\,\mu\mathrm{m}^{-1}$. Rod: $\pi r^2 L + \frac{4}{3}\pi r^3 = 0.524$ gives $0.385L + 0.180 = 0.524$, so $L = 0.89\,\mu\mathrm{m}$; by Eq. (1.1), $S/V = (2/0.35)(0.89 + 0.70)/(0.89 + 0.47) = 5.71\cdot1.17 \approx 6.7\,\mu\mathrm{m}^{-1}$. The rod has the larger ratio.

5. A culture of $10^9$ *E. coli* cells grows for one generation. With a point mutation rate of $2\times10^{-10}$ per base pair per generation and a genome of $4.6\times10^6\,\mathrm{bp}$, how many new point mutations arise, and how does this compare with the number of possible single-base substitutions?

   **Answer** $10^9 \times 2\times10^{-10} \times 4.6\times10^6 \approx 9\times10^5$ new mutations. There are $3 \times 4.6\times10^6 \approx 1.4\times10^7$ possible substitutions, so each specific substitution arises with probability about $0.07$ per generation in this culture; a few dozen generations of a dense culture produce nearly every possible single-base change, including those conferring resistance.
