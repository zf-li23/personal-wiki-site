The envelope of [7.11.2](/7.11.2) is the part of the prokaryotic cell that every species has. This chapter turns to structures that only some prokaryotes make, and that determine much of how they live: layers and appendages on the surface that attach cells to each other, to rocks or to host tissue; inclusions in the cytoplasm that store carbon, phosphate or sulfur, float the cell or concentrate an enzyme; the endospore, a dormant cell that survives boiling and can wait for centuries; and the machines of motility with which cells swim, glide and twitch toward better conditions. The chapter ends with a short comparison of prokaryotic cells with those of microbial eukaryotes.

Several of these structures have been treated from other points of view and are only linked here: the physics of swimming at low **Reynolds number** in [7.6.22](/7.6.22), the rotary mechanism and torque–speed curve of the flagellar motor in [7.6.28](/7.6.28#sec-28.4), the physical limits and the adaptation of bacterial **chemotaxis** in [7.6.35](/7.6.35), the physics of magnetosomes in [7.6.19](/7.6.19#sec-19.2), and the gene-regulatory program of sporulation in [7.11.12](/7.11.12) and [7.9.15](/7.9.15). The signal transduction of chemotaxis follows in [7.11.11](/7.11.11).

## 3.1. Cell Surface Structures

### 3.1.1. Capsules and Slime Layers

Many prokaryotes secrete sticky polymers that coat the cell outside the wall, collectively called the glycocalyx.

**Definition 3.1.1 (Capsule)** A **capsule** is a well-organized layer of polymer, usually polysaccharide, firmly attached to the cell wall and with a sharp outer boundary that excludes particles such as India ink. A loosely attached, diffuse layer of the same kind of material that is easily washed off is called a *slime layer*.

Most capsules are polysaccharides, but the capsule of *Bacillus anthracis* is a polypeptide of D-glutamic acid. Capsules are invisible in ordinary stains and are shown by negative staining as clear halos ([7.11.2](/7.11.2#sec-2.5.3)), or in the electron microscope after stabilization with antibodies. Their functions are several:

- *Attachment.* Capsules and slime bind cells to surfaces and to each other and form the matrix of **biofilms** ([7.11.12](/7.11.12)). *Streptococcus mutans* makes glucans from sucrose that cement it to tooth enamel and start dental plaque.
- *Resistance to phagocytosis and to complement.* The slippery, often host-like surface prevents white blood cells from engulfing the cell. In Griffith's experiment, only the capsulated S strain of *Streptococcus pneumoniae* killed mice ([7.11.1](/7.11.1#sec-1.6.2)); the capsule is the major virulence factor of the pneumococcus and of many other pathogens ([7.11.33](/7.11.33)).
- *Protection against drying.* The highly hydrated layer slows water loss.

The chemistry of capsules is highly variable; *S. pneumoniae* alone has about a hundred capsular serotypes, which is why pneumococcal vaccines combine capsular polysaccharides of many serotypes ([7.11.28](/7.11.28)).

### 3.1.2. Fimbriae and Pili

Thin protein filaments, too fine to see with the light microscope, protrude from many prokaryotes. The terms *fimbriae* and *pili* are not used consistently; we follow the common usage in which fimbriae are the many short adhesive filaments and pili the fewer, longer ones with special functions.

**Definition 3.1.2 (Fimbria)** A **fimbria** (plural fimbriae) is a thin ($3$–$10\,\mathrm{nm}$), hair-like protein filament on the surface of a prokaryotic cell, present in numbers up to about a thousand per cell, that attaches the cell to surfaces or host cells by an adhesin, usually at its tip.

The type 1 fimbriae of *E. coli* carry the adhesin FimH, which binds mannose residues on host glycoproteins; P fimbriae of uropathogenic strains bind glycolipids of the kidney, and their absence makes strains far less able to cause pyelonephritis. Both are assembled by the chaperone–usher pathway of the outer membrane. Curli are amyloid fibers of *E. coli* and *Salmonella* that bind cells into biofilms.

**Definition 3.1.3 (Sex Pilus)** A **sex pilus** (conjugative pilus) is a pilus encoded by a conjugative plasmid that connects a donor cell to a recipient cell and retracts to bring them together before DNA is transferred by **conjugation**.

The F pilus of *E. coli*, encoded by the **F plasmid**, is the classic example; it is assembled by a **type IV secretion system** and is also the receptor of some phages. Its role in gene transfer is described in [7.4.10](/7.4.10#sec-10.4) and [7.11.13](/7.11.13).

**Definition 3.1.4 (Type IV Pilus)** A **type IV pilus** (T4P) is a long ($1$–$10\,\mu\mathrm{m}$), thin ($6$–$8\,\mathrm{nm}$) pilus built from pilin subunits by a membrane-embedded machine that can rapidly add subunits at its base to extend the pilus and remove them to retract it, the extension and retraction being powered by the cytoplasmic ATPases PilB and PilT.

Type IV pili are found in many Gram-negative and some Gram-positive bacteria and, in modified form, in archaea. A pilus that extends, attaches at its tip and retracts pulls the cell forward, producing *twitching motility* (3.4.3); a single retraction motor of *Neisseria* exerts forces above $100\,\mathrm{pN}$, among the strongest known molecular motors. Type IV pili also mediate attachment to host cells (*Neisseria gonorrhoeae*, *Vibrio cholerae*, whose toxin-coregulated pilus is required for colonization of the intestine), the uptake of DNA in natural **transformation** ([7.11.12](/7.11.12)), the formation of microcolonies and biofilms, and in *Geobacter* the transfer of electrons to minerals ([7.11.6](/7.11.6)). They are homologous to the pseudopili of type II secretion and to the archaellum (3.4.2).

### 3.1.3. Attachment Structures of Archaea

Archaea have their own appendages. A group of uncultured archaea from cold, sulfidic springs and the deep subsurface (originally called SM1, now *Candidatus* Altiarchaeum) carries about a hundred *hami* per cell: filaments a few micrometers long, studded with prickles and ending in a three-pronged grappling hook about $60\,\mathrm{nm}$ across, which anchor cells to surfaces and to one another in networked biofilms. The hyperthermophile *Pyrodictium* forms *cannulae*, hollow glycoprotein tubes about $25\,\mathrm{nm}$ wide that connect daughter cells into networks after division, apparently joining their periplasm-like spaces. Many archaea also have type IV-like pili for adhesion.

## 3.2. Cell Inclusions

Inclusions are granules or bodies in the cytoplasm, visible in the light or electron microscope. Many are storage materials: when a nutrient is plentiful and another limits growth, cells put the surplus into polymers that do not raise the internal osmotic pressure and draw on them later.

### 3.2.1. Carbon, Phosphate, Sulfur and Mineral Inclusions

**Definition 3.2.1 (Polyhydroxyalkanoate and Polyhydroxybutyrate)** **Polyhydroxyalkanoates** (PHAs) are bacterial polyesters of $(R)$-3-hydroxy fatty acids, synthesized and stored as intracellular granules as a reserve of carbon and energy. The most common is **polyhydroxybutyrate** (PHB), the homopolymer of 3-hydroxybutyrate.

PHA granules are bounded by a layer of proteins (phasins, the synthase and depolymerases) and appear as refractile bodies that stain with lipophilic dyes such as Nile red. Cells accumulate them when carbon is in excess and nitrogen, phosphorus or oxygen limits growth: *Cupriavidus necator* (formerly *Ralstonia eutropha*) can fill most of its dry mass with PHB. More than a hundred different monomers, with side chains from methyl to long alkyl groups, have been found in PHAs, which gives polymers ranging from brittle to rubbery. Because they are thermoplastic and biodegradable, PHAs are produced industrially as bioplastics ([7.11.36](/7.11.36)). Many bacteria and archaea instead store glucose as **glycogen**, as granules of an $\alpha(1\to4)$ glucan branched by $\alpha(1\to6)$ bonds ([7.1.10](/7.1.10#sec-10.3.2)).

**Definition 3.2.2 (Metachromatic Granule)** A **metachromatic granule** (volutin granule) is an inclusion of polyphosphate, a linear polymer of orthophosphate joined by phosphoanhydride bonds, which stains reddish purple with basic blue dyes such as methylene blue or toluidine blue.

Polyphosphate stores phosphate for nucleic acid and phospholipid synthesis and, through its anhydride bonds, energy; it also binds metal ions and helps cells survive stress. The granules of *Corynebacterium diphtheriae* are a classical identifying feature. Bacteria that take up phosphate in excess and store it as polyphosphate are used to remove phosphorus from wastewater ([7.11.30](/7.11.30)).

Sulfur-oxidizing bacteria such as *Beggiatoa* and the purple sulfur bacteria oxidize $\ce{H2S}$ to elemental sulfur and store it as globules, which are formed in invaginations of the membrane and lie in the periplasm; when sulfide runs out, the sulfur is further oxidized to sulfate ([7.11.6](/7.11.6)). Some bacteria precipitate minerals inside the cell: the large sulfur bacterium *Achromatium* is filled with calcite granules, and some cyanobacteria (*Gloeomargarita lithophora*) form intracellular amorphous carbonates.

Magnetotactic bacteria make **magnetosomes**, crystals of magnetite ($\ce{Fe3O4}$) or greigite ($\ce{Fe3S4}$) about $35$–$120\,\mathrm{nm}$ in size, each enclosed in an invagination of the cytoplasmic membrane and aligned in a chain along an actin-like filament (MamK). The chain makes the cell a compass needle that is passively oriented by the Earth's magnetic field (3.5.2). Their definition, physics and size limits are in [7.6.19](/7.6.19#sec-19.2), their diversity in [7.11.17](/7.11.17).

### 3.2.2. Gas Vesicles

**Definition 3.2.3 (Gas Vesicle)** A **gas vesicle** is a hollow, gas-filled, spindle- or cylinder-shaped protein structure in the cytoplasm of some aquatic bacteria and archaea, about $45$–$200\,\mathrm{nm}$ wide and up to about $1\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ long, whose wall is permeable to gases but not to liquid water; clusters of gas vesicles reduce the cell's density and make it buoyant.

The wall, only about $2\,\mathrm{nm}$ thick, consists of ribs of a small, hydrophobic, rigid protein, GvpA, arranged in a low-pitch helix and strengthened on the outside by a second protein, GvpC. The inner surface is hydrophobic, so water does not condense inside, and gases diffuse in and out; the gas inside has the composition and pressure of the gas dissolved in the surrounding water. Gas vesicles are common in cyanobacteria that form blooms in lakes (*Microcystis*, *Aphanizomenon*, *Dolichospermum*), in some purple and green phototrophs and in halophilic archaea (*Halobacterium salinarum*). They let cells move up and down in the water column to the light and nutrient conditions they need, without spending energy on motility.

Gas vesicles collapse irreversibly under pressure, and narrower vesicles withstand higher pressures; species from deep lakes have narrower vesicles than those from shallow ones. Cyanobacteria regulate their buoyancy without making or destroying vesicles quickly: in bright light they accumulate dense photosynthetic carbohydrate (and in some species raise turgor pressure enough to collapse the weakest vesicles) and sink; in dim light they consume the carbohydrate and rise.

**Example 3.2.1** What fraction of a cell's volume must be gas vesicles to make it float? Let $f$ be the volume fraction of gas vesicles, $\rho_{\mathrm{c}}$ the density of the rest of the cell and $\rho_{\mathrm{v}}$ the effective density of the vesicles (protein wall plus gas). The mean density of the cell is $\rho = (1-f)\rho_{\mathrm{c}} + f\rho_{\mathrm{v}}$, and the cell is neutrally buoyant in water of density $\rho_{\mathrm{w}}$ when $\rho = \rho_{\mathrm{w}}$:

$$
f^* = \frac{\rho_{\mathrm{c}} - \rho_{\mathrm{w}}}{\rho_{\mathrm{c}} - \rho_{\mathrm{v}}}. \tag{3.1}
$$
With $\rho_{\mathrm{c}} \approx 1.10\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$ for a cyanobacterial cell rich in protein and carbohydrate, $\rho_{\mathrm{v}} \approx 0.12\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$ and $\rho_{\mathrm{w}} = 1.00\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$, $f^* \approx 0.10$: a tenth of the cell volume. Each extra $0.01\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$ of ballast (for example, from accumulating carbohydrate of density about $1.6\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$) raises $f^*$ by about one percentage point, which is how cells tip from floating to sinking within hours.

### 3.2.3. Carboxysomes and Other Microcompartments

Some prokaryotic enzymes are enclosed in protein shells rather than membranes.

**Definition 3.2.4 (Carboxysome)** A **carboxysome** is a polyhedral bacterial microcompartment, about $100$–$200\,\mathrm{nm}$ across, whose protein shell encloses the enzymes ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate carboxylase/oxygenase (RuBisCO) and carbonic anhydrase; it is found in cyanobacteria and many chemolithoautotrophs.

Bicarbonate, accumulated in the cytoplasm by transporters, passes through pores in the shell and is converted by carbonic anhydrase to $\ce{CO2}$, which reaches a high concentration around RuBisCO because the shell slows its escape. The carboxysome is thus the center of a $\ce{CO2}$-concentrating mechanism that compensates for the low affinity of RuBisCO for $\ce{CO2}$ and suppresses its oxygenase reaction ([7.11.6](/7.11.6)). Other bacterial microcompartments with homologous shells enclose pathways with toxic or volatile intermediates, such as the degradation of 1,2-propanediol (Pdu) and ethanolamine (Eut) in *Salmonella*, where they retain the aldehyde intermediates. Encapsulins, unrelated shells of a protein that resembles the capsid protein of tailed phages, enclose iron-storing and other enzymes.

## 3.3. Endospores

### 3.3.1. Structure and Chemistry

Some Gram-positive bacteria respond to starvation by forming a cell inside a cell.

**Definition 3.3.1 (Endospore)** An **endospore** is a dormant, highly resistant, dehydrated cell formed inside a mother cell by certain Bacillota and released when the mother cell lyses; it has no detectable metabolism and can survive heat, radiation, desiccation and chemicals that kill growing cells, and it can return to vegetative growth by germination.

Endospores are formed by *Bacillus*, *Clostridium*, *Clostridioides*, *Geobacillus*, *Sporosarcina*, *Desulfotomaculum* and other members of the phylum Bacillota (formerly Firmicutes), all descended from a common spore-forming ancestor. In the light microscope they are highly refractile and resist ordinary stains (the endospore stain of [7.11.2](/7.11.2#sec-2.5.3) uses heat). Their position in the mother cell (terminal, subterminal or central) and whether they swell it are species characteristics: *Clostridium tetani* forms terminal spores that give the cell a drumstick shape.

From outside to inside, a mature spore consists of:

| Layer | Composition | Function |
|---|---|---|
| Exosporium | Thin protein–glycoprotein balloon (in *B. anthracis* and *B. cereus*, not *B. subtilis*) | Contact with the environment and host |
| Spore coat | Dozens of layered proteins, cross-linked | Barrier to lytic enzymes and many chemicals |
| Outer membrane | Lipid membrane derived from the mother cell | Formed during engulfment |
| Cortex | Loosely cross-linked peptidoglycan with muramic δ-lactam | Maintains the dehydration of the core |
| Germ cell wall | Ordinary peptidoglycan | Becomes the wall of the new vegetative cell |
| Inner membrane | Lipid membrane of very low permeability | Excludes damaging chemicals |
| Core | DNA, ribosomes, enzymes, calcium dipicolinate, small acid-soluble proteins; low water content | The future vegetative cell |

**Definition 3.3.2 (Dipicolinic Acid)** **Dipicolinic acid** (DPA, pyridine-2,6-dicarboxylic acid) is a compound found in endospores but not in vegetative cells, which accumulates in the core as a chelate with $\ce{Ca^2+}$ and makes up about $5$–$15\,\%$ of the spore's dry mass.

Three features of the core explain the spore's resistance:

- *Dehydration.* The core contains only about $10$–$25\,\%$ water, against $80$–$90\,\%$ in a vegetative cell, so proteins and DNA are immobilized and protected from heat denaturation; the pH of the core is also about one unit lower.
- *Calcium dipicolinate.* Ca–DPA replaces much of the water and stabilizes DNA, partly by intercalating between its bases.
- *Small acid-soluble spore proteins* (SASPs), made only during sporulation, coat the DNA, change it to a more A-like form and protect it against heat, drying, ultraviolet light and chemicals; on germination they are degraded and serve as a source of amino acids.

| Property | Vegetative cell | Endospore |
|---|---|---|
| Structure | Gram-positive wall | Thick cortex, spore coat, sometimes exosporium |
| Water content | $80$–$90\,\%$ | $10$–$25\,\%$ in the core |
| Calcium, DPA, SASPs | Low or absent | High; present |
| Metabolism, macromolecular synthesis | Active | None detectable |
| Enzyme activity | High | Low |
| Heat, radiation, chemicals, lysozyme | Sensitive | Resistant |
| Appearance | Not refractile | Highly refractile |

### 3.3.2. Formation, Dormancy and Germination

Sporulation in *B. subtilis* takes about eight hours. It begins when cells sense starvation and high cell density and is controlled by a cascade of **σ factors** that are activated in turn in the forespore and the mother cell, each compartment checking the progress of the other; the genetics and the molecular program are the subject of [7.11.12](/7.11.12) (and its gene-expression waves of [7.9.15](/7.9.15)). Morphologically, the cell divides near one pole, the mother cell engulfs the forespore so that it is surrounded by two membranes, the cortex is laid down between them and the coat outside, and the forespore core is dehydrated and filled with Ca–DPA before the mother cell lyses (stages I–VII).

A spore can remain dormant for very long periods: viable spores have been recovered from sediments decades to centuries old, and claims of spores revived from amber (25–40 million years) and from salt crystals (250 million years) remain disputed because contamination by modern spores cannot be excluded. The return to growth has three steps. *Activation*, often by sublethal heating, prepares the spore. *Germination* is triggered within minutes when specific nutrients (L-alanine, inosine and others) bind germinant receptors in the inner membrane: Ca–DPA is released, the cortex is hydrolyzed, the core takes up water, and the spore loses its refractility and resistance. *Outgrowth* follows as the cell swells, resumes metabolism and macromolecular synthesis, and emerges from the coat.

Endospores matter in practice. They are the most heat-resistant living structures commonly met, so the standard conditions for **sterilization** by moist heat ($121\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ for at least $15\,\mathrm{min}$) and the biological indicators used to test autoclaves (spores of *Geobacillus stearothermophilus*) are designed to kill them ([7.11.10](/7.11.10)). Spore-forming pathogens cause anthrax (*B. anthracis*), tetanus (*C. tetani*), botulism (*Clostridium botulinum*), gas gangrene (*Clostridium perfringens*) and the antibiotic-associated colitis of *Clostridioides difficile*, whose spores persist in hospitals. Spores of *Bacillus thuringiensis* carry insecticidal crystal proteins used in agriculture ([7.11.36](/7.11.36)). The diversity of spore formers is surveyed in [7.11.18](/7.11.18). Endospores should not be confused with the spores of actinomycetes, fungi or plants, or with the akinetes of cyanobacteria and the myxospores of myxobacteria, which are resting cells formed by other routes and much less resistant.

## 3.4. Motility

Many prokaryotes move, and they do so by several unrelated mechanisms: swimming in liquids with rotating flagella or archaella, and moving over surfaces by gliding, twitching or swarming.

### 3.4.1. Bacterial Flagella

**Definition 3.4.1 (Bacterial Flagellum)** A **bacterial flagellum** is a long, helical filament of the protein flagellin, attached through a flexible hook to a rotary motor (the basal body) embedded in the cell envelope; the motor, powered by the flow of protons or sodium ions across the cytoplasmic membrane, rotates the filament, which acts as a propeller.

The arrangement of flagella is a useful descriptive feature. *Monotrichous* (polar) cells have a single flagellum at one pole (*Vibrio cholerae*); *lophotrichous* cells a tuft at one or both poles (*Spirillum*, some *Pseudomonas*); *amphitrichous* cells one at each pole; and *peritrichous* cells many flagella over the whole surface (*E. coli*, *Salmonella*, *Bacillus*). In spirochetes the flagella (endoflagella or periplasmic flagella) lie in the periplasm, wound around the cell between the cell body and the outer membrane; their rotation makes the whole helical cell corkscrew, which allows spirochetes to move through viscous media such as mucus and tissue.

The flagellum has three parts:

- The *filament*, about $20\,\mathrm{nm}$ wide and $5$–$20\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ long, is a hollow tube of thousands of flagellin subunits (FliC in *E. coli*) in eleven protofilaments, coiled into a rigid helix. The variable surface domain of flagellin is the H antigen used to type strains (*E. coli* O157:H7), and conserved parts are recognized by the innate immune receptor TLR5.
- The *hook*, about $55\,\mathrm{nm}$ long, is a flexible universal joint that transmits torque from the motor to the filament.
- The basal body consists of a rod passing through a series of rings: in Gram-negative bacteria the L ring in the LPS layer of the outer membrane and the P ring in the peptidoglycan, which together act as a bushing; the MS ring in the cytoplasmic membrane; and the C ring on its cytoplasmic face. Gram-positive bacteria, without an outer membrane, have only the MS and C rings.

The motor has a rotor (MS ring, rod and the C ring, whose proteins FliG, FliM and FliN form the switch) and up to about eleven stator units around it, each a complex of MotA and MotB (five MotA around two MotB) anchored to the peptidoglycan. Protons flowing through each stator unit, down the **proton-motive force**, drive conformational changes that push on FliG; each revolution consumes on the order of a thousand protons. *Vibrio* and alkaliphilic *Bacillus* species use $\ce{Na+}$-driven stators (PomA–PomB, MotP–MotS). Rotation speed is proportional to the driving force and reaches about $300\,\mathrm{Hz}$ in *E. coli* and over $1000\,\mathrm{Hz}$ in the $\ce{Na+}$ motors of *Vibrio*; swimming speeds are $20$–$100\,\mu\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, tens of body lengths per second. Because inertia is negligible at this scale, a bacterium stops within a fraction of a nanometer when the motor stops ([7.6.22](/7.6.22)). The motor can reverse. In *E. coli* counterclockwise rotation (viewed from outside the cell) makes the left-handed filaments bundle and push the cell forward in a *run*; clockwise rotation of one or more motors unravels the bundle and the cell *tumbles*. The quantitative model of the motor is in [7.6.28](/7.6.28#sec-28.4).

A flagellum is built from the inside out. Some 25 kinds of protein assemble in order: first the MS ring, C ring and export apparatus, then the rod and rings, the hook and finally the filament. The axial components are exported through the hollow structure by the flagellar export apparatus, a **type III secretion system** (the virulence-associated injectisomes of Gram-negative pathogens are homologous to it, [7.11.34](/7.11.34)), and are added at the distal tip. Flagellin molecules travel unfolded through the $2\,\mathrm{nm}$ central channel and are inserted under a cap protein (FliD); a filament therefore grows more slowly as it lengthens. The genes are expressed in a hierarchy coupled to assembly: the late genes for flagellin are transcribed only after the hook is complete, when an anti-σ factor is exported through the finished hook and the flagellar σ factor ($\sigma^{28}$) is released ([7.11.11](/7.11.11)).

### 3.4.2. Archaella

**Definition 3.4.2 (Archaellum)** An **archaellum** (plural archaella) is the rotating swimming appendage of archaea: a thin filament, about $10$–$14\,\mathrm{nm}$ wide, built of archaellins that are homologous to the pilins of type IV pili, and driven by the hydrolysis of ATP rather than by an ion gradient.

The archaellum shares a name and a function with the bacterial flagellum but nothing else: it is in effect a rotating type IV pilus. Its subunits are made with signal peptides cleaved by a dedicated peptidase, it has no central channel and grows by adding subunits at its base, and its motor contains an ATPase (FlaI) of the same family as the pilus extension ATPase. Archaella rotate in both directions, switching between pushing and pulling the cell. They have been studied in *Halobacterium salinarum*, *Sulfolobus acidocaldarius* and *Pyrococcus furiosus*.

| | Bacterial flagellum | Archaellum | Eukaryotic flagellum |
|---|---|---|---|
| Filament | Flagellin, about $20\,\mathrm{nm}$ | Archaellins, $10$–$14\,\mathrm{nm}$ | Axoneme of microtubules, about $200\,\mathrm{nm}$, inside the membrane |
| Homologous to | Type III secretion system | Type IV pili | — |
| Growth | At the tip, via a central channel | At the base | At the tip, by intraflagellar transport |
| Energy | $\ce{H+}$ or $\ce{Na+}$ gradient | ATP | ATP (dynein) |
| Motion | Rotation | Rotation | Bending waves |

The eukaryotic flagellum and **cilium** are treated in [7.3.14](/7.3.14#sec-14.6.5).

### 3.4.3. Surface Motility

**Definition 3.4.3 (Gliding Motility)** **Gliding motility** is a smooth, continuous movement of cells over a solid surface in the direction of their long axis without flagella or type IV pili, typically at speeds of up to about $10\,\mu\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$.

Gliding is widespread but has evolved several times, and its mechanisms differ:

- In *Flavobacterium johnsoniae* (phylum Bacteroidota), the cell-surface adhesin SprB is moved along a closed helical track over the cell surface by a motor powered by the proton-motive force; where the adhesin binds the substratum, the cell is propelled forward and rotates like a screw. The motor is associated with a type IX secretion system that also exports the adhesins.
- In *Myxococcus xanthus*, "adventurous" (A) gliding of single cells is driven by focal adhesion complexes that attach to the surface and are moved along the cell by motors powered by the proton-motive force; "social" (S) motility of groups is twitching by type IV pili. Gliding underlies the predatory swarms and fruiting bodies of myxobacteria ([7.11.12](/7.11.12), [7.11.17](/7.11.17)).
- Filamentous cyanobacteria (*Oscillatoria*) glide in association with the secretion of polysaccharide slime through pores near the cell junctions, and type IV pili are also involved.
- The wall-less *Mycoplasma mobile* glides on leg-like proteins that cyclically grab and release sialylated glycoproteins of the host, powered by ATP.

**Definition 3.4.4 (Twitching Motility)** **Twitching motility** is an intermittent, jerky movement of cells over a surface produced by repeated extension, attachment and retraction of type IV pili.

Twitching moves *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*, *Neisseria* and *Myxococcus* at about $0.1$–$1\,\mu\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ and helps them spread over host tissues and form biofilms; in flowing fluids, *P. aeruginosa* attached by its pili moves against the flow ([7.11.17](/7.11.17)). *Swarming* is a third kind of surface motility: on moist surfaces, cells of *Proteus*, *Serratia* and many other flagellated bacteria differentiate into elongated, hyperflagellated swarmer cells that move together in rafts, aided by secreted surfactants, and can cover an agar plate in hours.

## 3.5. Taxis

Motile cells respond to gradients in their environment by moving toward favorable and away from unfavourable conditions, a behavior called *taxis*. Prokaryotes are too small to compare concentrations reliably over their own length; they detect gradients in time, by comparing the present concentration with that of a few seconds before as they swim ([7.6.35](/7.6.35#sec-35.2)).

### 3.5.1. Chemotaxis

In uniform conditions an *E. coli* cell swims in runs of about one second separated by tumbles of about $0.1\,\mathrm{s}$, each of which reorients the cell in a nearly random direction (on average by about $68^\circ$); its path is a three-dimensional **random walk** ([7.6.5](/7.6.5#sec-5.1)). When the cell swims up a gradient of an attractant (sugars, amino acids) or down a gradient of a repellent, tumbles are suppressed and runs lengthen; runs in the unfavourable direction are not shortened much. The result is a *biased random walk* with a net drift up the attractant gradient at a few percent to about a tenth of the swimming speed. Polarly flagellated bacteria (*Vibrio*, *Pseudomonas*) reverse instead of tumbling, and *V. alginolyticus* adds a "flick" of the flexible hook that randomizes the new direction.

Chemotaxis was first measured by Wilhelm Pfeffer in the 1880s and quantitatively by Julius Adler in the 1960s with the *capillary assay*: a fine capillary tube containing a test chemical is inserted into a suspension of bacteria; the chemical diffuses out and forms a gradient around the mouth, and after about an hour the cells that have entered the capillary are counted on plates. A capillary containing an attractant accumulates many more cells than one containing buffer, and one with a repellent fewer. Other methods are swarm plates of soft agar, on which a population consuming an attractant spreads in rings as it chases the gradient it creates, microfluidic gradients and tracking microscopes that follow single cells in three dimensions (Howard Berg, 1972). The receptors, the Che signaling pathway and its adaptation by methylation are described in [7.11.11](/7.11.11); the physics of the run-and-tumble strategy and of the drift in [7.6.35](/7.6.35#sec-35.4).

### 3.5.2. Phototaxis, Aerotaxis and Magnetotaxis

Other stimuli are sensed with the same or similar signaling machinery. Phototrophic purple bacteria swim toward light by a *scotophobic* response: they tumble or reverse when they swim from light into darkness, so that they accumulate in illuminated regions. Cyanobacteria such as *Synechocystis* move toward or away from light by twitching motility, the cell acting as a lens that focuses light on its far side. *Aerotaxis*, movement toward an optimal oxygen concentration, was used by Theodor Engelmann in the 1880s: he spread aerotactic bacteria over a filament of the alga *Cladophora* illuminated with a spectrum and saw them collect where red and blue light, the wavelengths absorbed by chlorophyll, released oxygen, the first action spectrum of photosynthesis. Cells also respond to temperature (thermotaxis) and to osmolarity (osmotaxis).

**Magnetotaxis** combines a passive magnetic alignment with active swimming. The magnetosome chain of a magnetotactic bacterium orients the cell along the field lines of the Earth, which in the northern hemisphere point downward, so that cells swimming "north" move down toward the oxic–anoxic boundary in sediments, where they live; aerotaxis then stops them at their preferred oxygen level (magneto-aerotaxis). Cells from the southern hemisphere are predominantly south-seeking. The physics is in [7.6.19](/7.6.19#sec-19.2), the organisms (*Magnetospirillum*, magnetotactic cocci) in [7.11.17](/7.11.17).

## 3.6. The Cells of Microbial Eukaryotes

Microbial eukaryotes (protists, algae and fungi, [7.11.20](/7.11.20), [7.11.21](/7.11.21)) have the eukaryotic cell plan described in [7.3.1](/7.3.1#sec-1.4.1): a nucleus bounded by a double membrane with pores, an endomembrane system (endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi apparatus, lysosomes, vacuoles), a cytoskeleton of microtubules and actin, eukaryotic flagella and cilia, and **mitochondria** and, in algae, **chloroplasts** that descend from bacterial endosymbionts (**endosymbiotic theory**, [7.10.28](/7.10.28)). Their walls, where present, are of cellulose (many algae), chitin (fungi), silica (the frustules of diatoms) or protein pellicles, and freshwater protists expel water with contractile vacuoles. Prokaryotes and microbial eukaryotes thus differ in organization rather than in size: a yeast cell is only a few times larger than a large bacterium, and the smallest eukaryotes are smaller than many bacteria.

Many microbial eukaryotes live in anoxic habitats, and their mitochondria have been reduced to organelles that no longer respire with oxygen.

**Definition 3.6.1 (Hydrogenosome)** A **hydrogenosome** is a double-membrane organelle of some anaerobic eukaryotes, derived from mitochondria, that lacks cristae and the citric acid cycle and makes ATP by substrate-level phosphorylation while oxidizing pyruvate to acetate, $\ce{CO2}$ and $\ce{H2}$.

**Definition 3.6.2 (Mitosome)** A **mitosome** is a highly reduced organelle of mitochondrial origin, bounded by two membranes, that produces no ATP and whose main known function is the assembly of iron–sulfur clusters.

Hydrogenosomes occur in the human parasite *Trichomonas vaginalis*, in ciliates and chytrid fungi of the rumen and in protists of anoxic sediments; in some of them methanogenic archaea live inside the cytoplasm as endosymbionts and consume the $\ce{H2}$ and $\ce{CO2}$ released by the hydrogenosomes ([7.11.31](/7.11.31)). Mitosomes occur in *Giardia*, *Entamoeba histolytica* and the microsporidia. The flagellate *Monocercomonoides* has lost the mitochondrion entirely, having acquired a bacterial pathway for iron–sulfur cluster assembly (Karnkowska and colleagues, 2016). All these organisms descend from ancestors with mitochondria, a conclusion that supports the view that the last common ancestor of eukaryotes already had a mitochondrion ([7.10.28](/7.10.28)).

## Summary

- *Surface structures.* Capsules and slime layers (Definition 3.1.1) mediate attachment, biofilm formation and resistance to phagocytosis and drying. Fimbriae (Definition 3.1.2) attach cells by adhesins; sex pili (Definition 3.1.3) join mating cells; type IV pili (Definition 3.1.4) extend and retract for twitching, adhesion and DNA uptake. Archaea attach by hami and connect by cannulae.
- *Inclusions.* PHB and other PHAs (Definition 3.2.1) and glycogen store carbon; polyphosphate (metachromatic granules, Definition 3.2.2) stores phosphate; sulfur globules, carbonate minerals and magnetosomes are other inclusions. Gas vesicles (Definition 3.2.3) are rigid protein shells permeable to gas; about a tenth of the cell volume of vesicles floats a cell (Eq. (3.1)), and carbohydrate ballast regulates buoyancy. Carboxysomes (Definition 3.2.4) and other protein-shelled microcompartments concentrate substrates or retain toxic intermediates.
- **Endospores** (Definition 3.3.1) of Bacillota are dormant, layered cells (exosporium, coat, cortex, core) whose resistance comes from core dehydration, calcium dipicolinate (Definition 3.3.2) and SASPs. They form in a σ-factor-controlled program, germinate within minutes in response to nutrients, and set the standard for sterilization.
- *Motility.* Bacterial flagella (Definition 3.4.1) are helical propellers driven by ion-powered rotary motors, assembled through a type III export apparatus and growing at the tip; in *E. coli*, counterclockwise rotation gives runs and clockwise rotation tumbles. Archaella (Definition 3.4.2) are ATP-driven rotating relatives of type IV pili. Gliding (Definition 3.4.3), twitching (Definition 3.4.4) and swarming move cells over surfaces.
- *Taxis.* Chemotaxis is a biased random walk produced by lengthening runs up attractant gradients, measured with capillary assays, swarm plates and tracking; phototaxis, aerotaxis and magnetotaxis use related strategies.
- *Microbial eukaryotes* have nuclei, endomembranes, cytoskeletons and organelles of endosymbiotic origin; in anaerobes mitochondria are reduced to hydrogenosomes (Definition 3.6.1) or mitosomes (Definition 3.6.2) or lost.

## Questions

1. How would you show that an encapsulated strain owes its resistance to phagocytosis to its capsule, and what result would you expect?

   **Answer** Compare the wild type with an isogenic mutant that cannot synthesize the capsule (for example, a deletion of a capsule biosynthesis gene) and with the mutant complemented by the gene on a plasmid. In an assay with phagocytes (or by survival in an animal), the mutant should be engulfed and killed much more readily than the wild type, and complementation should restore resistance. This is an application of the molecular Koch's postulates of [7.11.1](/7.11.1).

2. Use Eq. (3.1) to estimate the volume fraction of gas vesicles needed by a cyanobacterium whose cytoplasm has a density of $1.05\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$ in the morning and $1.12\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$ after a day of photosynthesis. What happens to a cell with $7\,\%$ gas vesicles?

   **Answer** Morning: $f^* = 0.05/0.93 \approx 0.054$; evening: $f^* = 0.12/1.00 = 0.12$. With $f = 0.07$, the cell floats in the morning (its mean density $0.93\cdot1.05 + 0.07\cdot0.12 \approx 0.985\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$) and sinks in the evening ($0.93\cdot1.12 + 0.0084 \approx 1.05\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$), rising again at night as it respires the carbohydrate. Such daily vertical migration is observed in lake cyanobacteria.

3. A culture of *Bacillus subtilis* is heated to $80\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ for $10\,\mathrm{min}$ and plated. Colonies appear from a starved culture but not from an exponentially growing one. Explain, and name the features of the spore responsible.

   **Answer** Exponentially growing cells do not sporulate, and vegetative cells are killed at $80\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$. A starved culture contains endospores, which survive because their core is dehydrated (about $10$–$25\,\%$ water), stabilized by calcium dipicolinate and protected by SASPs bound to DNA; heating also activates them, and on the plate they germinate and grow into colonies. The method is used to count or select spores.

4. Compare how a bacterial flagellum and an archaellum grow and what powers them, and explain what this suggests about their evolutionary origins.

   **Answer** The flagellar filament grows at its tip: flagellin is exported unfolded through the hollow structure by a type III secretion apparatus and inserted under the cap; the motor is powered by $\ce{H+}$ or $\ce{Na+}$ flow. The archaellum grows from its base, like a type IV pilus, from archaellins processed by a signal peptidase; its motor is an ATPase homologous to pilus ATPases. Similar function with unrelated parts indicates convergent evolution: the flagellum is related to type III secretion systems, the archaellum to type IV pili.

5. In a capillary assay, a capillary with $1\,\mathrm{mM}$ L-serine collects $5\times10^5$ cells, one with buffer $2\times10^4$ cells, and one with serine applied to a non-motile mutant $10^3$ cells. What does each control show? What would you expect for a mutant that swims but cannot tumble?

   **Answer** The buffer capillary measures entry by random swimming; the attractant raises entry 25-fold, so the response is chemotactic, not chance. The non-motile mutant measures entry by passive processes (flow, diffusion of cells), which is negligible. A smooth-swimming mutant that cannot tumble swims in long straight runs; it enters the capillary only by random swimming, at about the buffer level, because it cannot bias its walk.
