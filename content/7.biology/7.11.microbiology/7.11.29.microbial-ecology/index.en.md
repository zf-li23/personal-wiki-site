The previous chapters described what microorganisms can do in pure culture. In nature almost none of them lives alone: every gram of soil and every milliliter of seawater holds a community of hundreds to thousands of species, most of them uncultured, interacting with one another and with a chemical environment that they themselves change. Microbial ecology asks three questions about such communities: who is there, what are they doing, and how fast. This chapter treats the methods that answer them and the main habitats of the Earth's surface and subsurface. It builds on the metabolic diversity of [7.11.5](/7.11.5)–[7.11.7](/7.11.7), on the cultivation methods of [7.11.4](/7.11.4) and on the sequence analysis of microbial communities in [7.8.14](/7.8.14), whose algorithms are not repeated here. The element cycles that the communities drive, and the environmental applications, follow in [7.11.30](/7.11.30); symbioses with plants and animals in [7.11.31](/7.11.31); the human microbiome in [7.11.32](/7.11.32).

## 29.1. The Questions of Microbial Ecology

**Definition 29.1.1 (Microbial Ecology)** **Microbial ecology** is the study of the interactions of microorganisms with one another and with their physical and chemical environment: their diversity and distribution, their activities in nature and the effects of these activities on the environment.

The units are those of general ecology. A population is the cells of one species in a habitat; populations in a habitat form a community; a community together with its physical and chemical surroundings and the flows of energy and matter through them is an ecosystem. Microbiologists add one unit of their own.

**Definition 29.1.2 (Guild)** A **guild** is a set of microbial populations that use the same resource in the same way, for example the sulfate reducers, the nitrifiers or the methanogens of a sediment, regardless of their phylogenetic relationships.

Guilds, not species, are the natural units of biogeochemistry: the rate of sulfate reduction in a sediment depends on how active the sulfate-reducing guild is, not on which of its members happen to be present. The three questions then become: which taxa and guilds are present and in what numbers (community composition), which metabolic processes occur and which organisms carry them out (function), and at what rates the processes proceed in situ (activity).

The scale of the subject is large. Roughly $10^{30}$ bacterial and archaeal cells exist on Earth, with estimates between $1\times10^{30}$ and $5\times10^{30}$ ([7.11.1](/7.11.1#sec-1.2.1)); almost all of them are in five habitats, the ocean, the upper ocean sediments, the deep ocean subsurface, the soil to a depth of a few meters and the deep continental subsurface, whereas every other habitat, including all animals and plants together, holds $10^{27}$ cells or fewer (Flemming and Wuertz, 2019). Most of these cells live attached to surfaces, as **biofilms** or as cells in the pores of soil and sediment.

## 29.2. Microenvironments, Surfaces and Mats

### 29.2.1. Gradients at the Scale of a Cell

The environment that matters to a bacterium is the one within a few cell lengths of it. Because diffusion is slow and consumption fast, the concentrations of oxygen, nutrients and products change steeply over micrometers to millimeters.

**Definition 29.2.1 (Microenvironment)** A **microenvironment** is the immediate physical and chemical surroundings of a microbial cell, on the scale of micrometers to millimeters, which can differ greatly from the average conditions of the bulk habitat.

A soil aggregate a few millimeters across is the standard example. Its surface is in contact with air and fully oxic, but respiration in its interior consumes oxygen faster than it diffuses in, so the center of a moist aggregate can be anoxic. Strict aerobes, facultative anaerobes and strict anaerobes such as denitrifiers and even methanogens therefore live in one crumb of soil, a few millimeters apart. Each species occupies the part of this mosaic in which it can grow and compete, its realized **ecological niche** as opposed to the broader range of conditions it could tolerate alone (its fundamental niche).

Nutrient supply in most natural habitats is low and irregular: microorganisms live a feast-or-famine existence in which short periods of growth after an input of nutrients alternate with long periods of starvation. Growth rates in nature are far below those in the laboratory, generation times of days to months rather than minutes to hours, and much of the population at any time is dormant. Starvation survival, the ability to persist with very low maintenance energy, is as important for fitness as fast growth ([7.11.8](/7.11.8)); some bacteria form endospores or cysts, others simply reduce their size and metabolism.

### 29.2.2. Surfaces and Biofilms

Surfaces concentrate nutrients by adsorption, provide attachment and, in flowing water, keep cells in place while new nutrients pass by. Most cells in nature are therefore attached, and many live in **biofilms**, the matrix-enclosed communities described in [7.11.12](/7.11.12#sec-12.9). Inside a biofilm the gradients of oxygen, pH and nutrients are steeper still, and cooperation among species becomes possible: the products of one population feed another, and anaerobes survive below an aerobic surface layer.

### 29.2.3. Microbial Mats

**Definition 29.2.2 (Microbial Mat)** A **microbial mat** is a laminated, multilayered biofilm, millimeters to centimeters thick, in which steep vertical gradients of light, oxygen and sulfide separate functionally different microbial populations into distinct layers.

Phototrophic mats are built by cyanobacteria (filamentous forms such as *Microcoleus* and *Oscillatoria*) at the surface, with anoxygenic purple and green bacteria beneath them and sulfate-reducing bacteria and other anaerobes below; a mat can be pictured as a Winogradsky column ([7.11.7](/7.11.7#sec-7.6)) compressed into a few millimeters. Such mats are found today mainly in hypersaline lagoons and in the outflows of hot springs, habitats in which salt or temperature excludes the grazing animals that would otherwise eat them. In shallow seas before the evolution of animals they were widespread, and those that trapped and precipitated sediment built **stromatolites**, laminated rocks known from more than 3.4 billion years ago ([7.10.27](/7.10.27#sec-27.1.2)). Chemolithotrophic mats of large sulfur oxidizers such as *Beggiatoa* and *Thioploca* cover marine sediments below the photic zone where oxygen from the water above meets sulfide from below.

The chemistry of a mat can be measured with microsensors (29.6.2) and understood with a reaction–diffusion model. In one dimension, the steady-state concentration $C(z)$ of a solute at depth $z$ obeys

$$
D\,\frac{\mathrm{d}^2 C}{\mathrm{d}z^2} = R(z), \qquad J = -D\,\frac{\mathrm{d}C}{\mathrm{d}z},
$$
where $D$ is its diffusion coefficient in the mat and $R(z)$ its net volumetric rate of consumption (negative where it is produced); the second equation, Fick's first law, gives the flux $J$ across any depth. The curvature of a measured profile thus gives the local rate, and its slope at the surface gives the exchange with the water.

![Chemical microprofiles in a phototrophic microbial mat, from a one-dimensional reaction–diffusion model (O₂ and H₂S with diffusion coefficient 1.5 × 10⁻⁹ m² s⁻¹; z < 0 is the diffusive boundary layer of the overlying water, which is air-saturated with about 250 µM O₂). A: by day, oxygenic photosynthesis in the top millimeter raises O₂ to several times air saturation; O₂ penetrates about 2 mm, and sulfide produced by sulfate reduction in the deeper layers is consumed at the oxic–anoxic interface. B: at night, without photosynthesis, respiration consumes the O₂ within about 0.1 mm of the surface and sulfide rises almost to it. C: process rates by day: oxygenic photosynthesis and respiration in the top layer, a narrow peak of sulfide oxidation by O₂ at the interface, anoxygenic photosynthesis just below it where light remains, and sulfate reduction (inhibited by O₂) below. Real mats show the same day–night switch, which forces their organisms to cope with daily alternation between oxic and sulfidic conditions.](./plots/mat-microprofile.py)

The model reproduces the main observations on real mats: oxygen supersaturation in the light, an oxic layer a few millimeters thick that collapses to a fraction of a millimeter at night, and a sulfide front that moves up and down with the daily cycle. The populations of a mat must cope with these swings: many cyanobacteria of mats tolerate sulfide and switch to anoxygenic photosynthesis or fermentation at night, and motile filaments glide up and down with the gradients.

## 29.3. Culture-Dependent Methods

### 29.3.1. Enrichment and Isolation

Cultivation remains indispensable: only a pure culture allows the physiology, biochemistry and genetics of an organism to be studied directly and its predicted properties to be tested. The classical approach is the **enrichment culture** ([7.11.4](/7.11.4#sec-4.5.3)), a medium and incubation conditions designed to select organisms of a chosen metabolic type, followed by isolation in pure culture by streaking, agar shake dilution or dilution in liquid. A successful enrichment proves that an organism of that type was present, but not that it is abundant or important; the organism that wins an enrichment is the one best adapted to the laboratory conditions, usually a fast-growing "weed" that may be rare in nature (enrichment bias). Diluting the sample before enrichment, so that only the most numerous organisms are still present, and using substrate concentrations close to those in nature, partly correct the bias.

### 29.3.2. The Uncultured Majority

Viable counts of natural samples are typically one to several orders of magnitude lower than microscopic counts, the **great plate count anomaly** ([7.11.8](/7.11.8#sec-8.6.2)). Comparisons of rRNA gene sequences from nature with those of cultures showed that the anomaly is not mainly a matter of dead cells: whole phyla of bacteria and archaea have no cultured representative, and most species in a soil or seawater sample have never been grown. The figure below shows why a medium that supports even several percent of the species can recover far fewer of the cells.

Many uncultured organisms grow slowly, at low substrate concentrations, depend on compounds made by other organisms, or are inhibited by the high nutrient concentrations and oxidative stress of standard media. Methods designed around these properties have brought many of them into culture:

- High-throughput dilution culture (dilution to extinction): a sample is diluted in sterile natural water to about one cell per well of a microtiter plate and incubated for weeks at natural nutrient levels; growth is detected by flow cytometry. This brought into culture *Pelagibacter ubique*, of the SAR11 clade, one of the most abundant organisms in the ocean (Rappé et al. 2002; [7.11.17](/7.11.17#sec-17.3.5)).
- Diffusion chambers and the iChip: cells are diluted into agar between semipermeable membranes, and the device is returned to the natural habitat (sediment, soil), so that nutrients and growth factors from the environment diffuse in while the cells are contained. Colonies form that would not form on Petri dishes, and many of them grow afterwards in the laboratory. The iChip is a multichannel version in which each of hundreds of small wells receives about one cell; among the soil bacteria first cultured this way was *Eleftheria terrae*, the producer of the antibiotic teixobactin ([7.11.14](/7.11.14)).
- Co-culture and helper strains: some isolates grow only next to another organism that supplies a growth factor, for example siderophores made by a neighbor, or that consumes a product, as in **syntrophy** ([7.11.7](/7.11.7#sec-7.5)). Adding the factor, or growing the two organisms together, makes cultivation possible.
- Single-cell isolation by **optical tweezers**, micromanipulation or **flow cytometry** with cell sorting, combined with microfluidic devices in which thousands of cultivations run in parallel ([7.11.4](/7.11.4#sec-4.5.4)).
- Patience and imitation of the habitat: the first Asgard archaeon in culture, a slow-growing organism from deep-sea sediment, was obtained by more than a decade of continuous incubation in a bioreactor that mimicked methane seep conditions, as a syntrophic consortium with a bacterial partner (Imachi et al. 2020; [7.11.19](/7.11.19#sec-19.8)).

## 29.4. Microscopy and Staining

The total number of cells in a natural sample is counted under the epifluorescence microscope after the cells are collected on a black filter and stained with a DNA-binding fluorescent dye: DAPI (4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole, blue fluorescence), acridine orange or SYBR Green (green). These general stains are fast and sensitive, but they do not distinguish live from dead cells or one species from another, and in soils, where particles also stain, background fluorescence makes counting difficult. Viability stains distinguish cells with intact membranes, which exclude a red dye such as propidium iodide, from cells with damaged membranes, which take it up. Cells engineered to make the **green fluorescent protein** or another fluorescent protein can be followed after release into a habitat, and a fluorescent reporter fused to a promoter shows where and when a gene is expressed.

Morphology says little about identity: cells that look alike may belong to different phyla. Identity is revealed by nucleic acid probes. In **fluorescence in situ hybridization** ([7.2.6](/7.2.6)) applied to microbial ecology, fluorescently labeled oligonucleotide probes, usually 15–25 nucleotides long, hybridize to the rRNA inside fixed and permeabilized cells. Because a growing cell contains thousands of ribosomes, a probe against a 16S or 23S rRNA sequence makes the whole cell fluoresce. Probes can be designed at any phylogenetic level, from all bacteria (the probe EUB338) to a single species, and several probes with different dyes identify several groups in one sample; such "phylogenetic stains" give both the identity and the spatial arrangement of the cells, for example the consortia of archaea and sulfate reducers that oxidize methane anaerobically.

**Definition 29.4.1 (CARD-FISH)** **Catalyzed reporter deposition FISH** (CARD-FISH) is a variant of FISH in which the probe carries the enzyme horseradish peroxidase instead of a dye; after hybridization, the peroxidase converts many molecules of a fluorescently labeled tyramide substrate into reactive radicals that bind to proteins near the probe, amplifying the signal ten- to several hundredfold.

CARD-FISH makes visible the slow-growing or starving cells of oligotrophic habitats, whose few ribosomes give too weak a signal with ordinary FISH, and cells with high background autofluorescence. Its drawback is that the large enzyme must enter the cell, which requires careful permeabilization of the cell wall.

**Definition 29.4.2 (Microautoradiography)** **Microautoradiography** (MAR) is the detection of the uptake of a radioactively labeled substrate by individual cells: a sample incubated with the labeled substrate is fixed, covered with a photographic emulsion and exposed in the dark, and silver grains form above the cells that incorporated the label. Combined with FISH (MAR-FISH), it shows both the identity and the activity of each cell.

MAR-FISH answers a question that neither method answers alone: which of the organisms present actually take up a given substrate under in situ conditions. It is the microscopic counterpart of **autoradiography** of gels and tissue sections ([7.3.3](/7.3.3)).

## 29.5. Nucleic Acid Methods

Most of what is known about the composition of natural communities comes from DNA and RNA extracted directly from the environment. The analyses themselves, operational taxonomic units and **amplicon sequence variants**, classification, shotgun **metagenomics**, assembly and binning, diversity statistics and compositional data, are treated in [7.8.14](/7.8.14); this section summarizes what the methods measure and what they have revealed.

- Marker genes. PCR amplification of the **16S rRNA gene** (bacteria and archaea), the 18S rRNA gene or the internal transcribed spacer (ITS) of eukaryotes and fungi, followed by **amplicon sequencing**, gives a census of the taxa in a sample. Older fingerprinting methods (denaturing gradient gel electrophoresis, DGGE; terminal restriction fragment length polymorphism, T-RFLP; automated ribosomal intergenic spacer analysis, ARISA), which separate the amplified variants by electrophoresis, have been almost entirely replaced by sequencing. PCR also targets functional genes that mark a guild: *amoA* (ammonia monooxygenase) for ammonia oxidizers, *nifH* for nitrogen fixers, *dsrAB* for sulfate reducers, *mcrA* for methanogens and anaerobic methane oxidizers, *pmoA* for aerobic methanotrophs. Quantitative PCR gives the number of copies of each gene per gram or milliliter. Amplicons do not measure the number of cells directly: primers have biases, and the copy number of rRNA operons varies from 1 to about 15 per genome.
- Metagenomics. Sequencing all the DNA of a community gives its gene content and, after assembly and binning, **metagenome-assembled genomes** of uncultured organisms. Metagenomes have revealed known metabolisms in unexpected organisms, such as a rhodopsin light-driven proton pump in marine bacteria (proteorhodopsin, discovered in an environmental genome fragment in 2000; [7.11.6](/7.11.6#sec-6.4)) and ammonia monooxygenase genes in marine and soil archaea, later confirmed by the cultivation of *Nitrosopumilus maritimus* ([7.11.19](/7.11.19#sec-19.6.2)); they have also revealed whole new lineages, such as the **candidate phyla radiation** of bacteria ([7.11.18](/7.11.18#sec-18.1.2)), the **DPANN** archaea and the **Asgard archaea**. Metabolic functions inferred from genomes are hypotheses until they are tested in cultures or by activity measurements.
- Metatranscriptomics and metaproteomics measure the RNA and the proteins of a community (methods in [7.9.2](/7.9.2) and [7.9.9](/7.9.9)), and so show which genes are expressed, not only which are present; metabolomics adds the small molecules. Microarrays carrying thousands of probes for rRNA genes or functional genes (PhyloChip, GeoChip) were used for the same purposes before sequencing became cheap.

**Definition 29.5.1 (Single-Cell Genomics)** **Single-cell genomics** is the sequencing of the genome of one cell isolated from a natural sample, usually by fluorescence-activated cell sorting; the femtogram of DNA in the cell is first amplified, typically by multiple displacement amplification with the highly processive DNA polymerase of phage φ29, which copies the template many thousandfold from random primers.

Single-cell genomes are usually incomplete, because amplification is uneven, but they link genes unambiguously to one cell and to its rRNA gene, which binning of metagenomes cannot always do, and they reveal the variation among cells of one population. Single-cell genomes of *Prochlorococcus* from one milliliter of seawater, for example, showed hundreds of coexisting subpopulations that share a core genome but differ in genomic islands for cell-surface and nutrient-uptake genes (Kashtan et al. 2014), a microdiversity hidden in the identical 16S rRNA genes.

## 29.6. Measuring Activity

Composition and gene content say what could happen; rates say what does happen. Activity measurements are made on samples incubated as close to in situ conditions as possible, or with sensors placed in the habitat itself.

### 29.6.1. Radioisotopes and Chemical Assays

The most sensitive measure of a process is the conversion of a radioactively labeled substrate: $\ce{^{14}CO2}$ incorporated into cell material measures autotrophic $\ce{CO2}$ fixation by phototrophs or chemolithotrophs, $\ce{^{14}CO2}$ released from a $^{14}\mathrm{C}$-labeled organic compound measures its mineralization, and $\ce{^{35}SO4^2-}$ converted to $\ce{H2^{35}S}$ measures sulfate reduction even when the sulfate pool is so large that its decrease could not be detected chemically. A biological origin must always be demonstrated with a killed control, for example a sample fixed with formalin or heat-sterilized; photosynthesis is distinguished from dark $\ce{CO2}$ fixation with dark controls or inhibitors. Simple chemical measurements (the disappearance of a substrate, the appearance of a product, the consumption of oxygen in a sealed bottle) suffice where the rates are high. All such measurements give the net activity of the community, not that of individual organisms.

### 29.6.2. Microsensors and Fluxes

**Definition 29.6.1 (Microsensor)** A **microsensor** (microelectrode) is a glass needle with a tip a few to tens of micrometers wide that measures the concentration of a chemical species, such as $\ce{O2}$, $\ce{H2S}$, $\mathrm{pH}$, $\ce{N2O}$ or $\ce{NO3-}$, at one point; driven by a micromanipulator in steps of tens of micrometers, it records concentration profiles through sediments, mats, biofilms and aggregates.

The oxygen microsensor is an electrochemical cell in which $\ce{O2}$ diffusing through a silicone membrane at the tip is reduced at a gold cathode, giving a current proportional to its concentration. Sensors for nitrate and other compounds without a convenient electrochemistry use bacteria immobilized at the tip that convert the compound into one that can be measured, for example nitrate reducers producing $\ce{N2O}$. Banks of microsensors on landers have been used on the deep-sea floor. With Fick's law (29.2.3), a profile gives fluxes and rates without disturbing the sample.

**Example 29.6.1** In a coastal sediment, $\ce{O2}$ falls from $250\,\mu\mathrm{M}$ at the surface to zero at $2\,\mathrm{mm}$ depth, nearly linearly in the top millimeter with a slope of $150\,\mu\mathrm{M\,mm^{-1}}$. With an effective diffusion coefficient in the porous sediment of $D = 1.0\times10^{-9}\,\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$ and a porosity of $0.8$, the oxygen uptake is
$$
J = 0.8 \times 1.0\times10^{-9}\,\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}} \times 1.5\times10^{2}\,\mathrm{mol\,m^{-4}} = 1.2\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm{mol\,m^{-2}\,s^{-1}},
$$
about $10\,\mathrm{mmol\,O_2\,m^{-2}\,d^{-1}}$ (a gradient of $150\,\mu\mathrm{M\,mm^{-1}}$ is $1.5\times10^{2}\,\mathrm{mol\,m^{-4}}$), a typical value for coastal sediments. Since about one $\ce{O2}$ is used per $\ce{CO2}$ respired, this corresponds to the mineralization of roughly $10\,\mathrm{mmol}$ of organic carbon per square meter per day, including the reoxidation of reduced products such as sulfide that diffuse up from below.

### 29.6.3. Stable Isotopes

Many elements have two or more stable isotopes, which differ in mass but not in chemistry: carbon is about $98.9\,\%$ $^{12}\mathrm{C}$ and $1.1\,\%$ $^{13}\mathrm{C}$; sulfur about $95\,\%$ $^{32}\mathrm{S}$ and $4.2\,\%$ $^{34}\mathrm{S}$; nitrogen $99.6\,\%$ $^{14}\mathrm{N}$ and $0.4\,\%$ $^{15}\mathrm{N}$.

**Definition 29.6.2 (Isotopic Fractionation)** **Isotopic fractionation** is the change in the ratio of the isotopes of an element between the substrate and the product of a reaction. Enzymes react slightly faster with molecules containing the lighter isotope, so the products of biological reactions are enriched in the lighter isotope ($^{12}\mathrm{C}$, $^{32}\mathrm{S}$) and depleted in the heavier one relative to the substrate.

**Formula 29.6.1 (Delta Notation)** Isotopic composition is expressed as the deviation of the isotope ratio of a sample from that of a standard, in parts per thousand (per mil, ‰):
$$
\delta^{13}\mathrm{C} = \left(\frac{(^{13}\mathrm{C}/^{12}\mathrm{C})_{\text{sample}}}{(^{13}\mathrm{C}/^{12}\mathrm{C})_{\text{standard}}} - 1\right) \times 1000,
$$
with the carbonate of the Pee Dee belemnite, a Cretaceous fossil (today its replacement Vienna PDB), as the carbon standard; for sulfur, $\delta^{34}\mathrm{S}$ is defined in the same way relative to the troilite (FeS) of the Canyon Diablo meteorite.

Fractionation is a fingerprint of biological activity. Organic carbon made by the Calvin cycle has $\delta^{13}\mathrm{C}$ of about $-20$ to $-30$ ‰, because RubisCO discriminates against $\ce{^{13}CO2}$; biogenic methane is much lighter, $-50$ to $-110$ ‰, so that the methane of a seep or a gas field can be classified as microbial or thermogenic; marine carbonates are near $0$ ‰. Sulfide made by sulfate reducers is depleted in $^{34}\mathrm{S}$ by up to about $70$ ‰ relative to the sulfate it came from. Such signatures preserved in ancient rocks are among the oldest evidence of life and of particular metabolisms ([7.10.27](/7.10.27#sec-27.1.2)).

### 29.6.4. Stable Isotope Probing

**Definition 29.6.3 (Stable Isotope Probing)** **Stable isotope probing** (SIP) identifies the organisms in a community that use a particular substrate: the sample is incubated with the substrate highly enriched in a heavy stable isotope ($^{13}\mathrm{C}$, $^{15}\mathrm{N}$ or $^{18}\mathrm{O}$ in water), organisms that assimilate it incorporate the isotope into their DNA, RNA or other biomarkers, and the heavier nucleic acids are separated by density-gradient ultracentrifugation and analyzed by sequencing.

![Stable isotope probing (DNA-SIP). B: buoyant density of DNA increases with its G + C content (about 1.660 + 0.098 × GC g mL⁻¹) and full ¹³C labeling adds about 0.036 g mL⁻¹ (illustrative distributions, SD 0.004 g mL⁻¹). SIP requires enough incorporation of the label to shift the density, usually days of incubation at substrate concentrations higher than in nature, during which labeled products of the primary users can be passed on to other organisms (cross-feeding). Its quantitative versions compare the density distribution of every taxon between labeled and unlabeled incubations and estimate how much isotope each taxon incorporated. RNA-SIP is faster, because RNA is made without cell division. SIP with $\ce{^{13}CH4}$, $^{13}\mathrm{C}$-labeled pollutants or $\ce{^{15}N2}$ has identified methanotrophs, degraders of aromatic hydrocarbons and nitrogen fixers in soils and sediments that had never been cultured.

### 29.6.5. Activity of Single Cells

**Definition 29.6.4 (NanoSIMS)** **NanoSIMS** (nanometer-scale secondary ion mass spectrometry) images the elemental and isotopic composition of a sample with a spatial resolution of about 50 nm: a focused beam of primary ions ($\ce{Cs+}$ or $\ce{O-}$) sputters the surface, and the secondary ions released ($^{12}\mathrm{C}^-$, $^{13}\mathrm{C}^-$, $^{12}\mathrm{C}^{14}\mathrm{N}^-$, $^{12}\mathrm{C}^{15}\mathrm{N}^-$ and others) are counted in a mass spectrometer as the beam scans the sample.

Combined with incubations with stable isotopes and with FISH on the same cells, NanoSIMS measures how much of a labeled substrate each identified cell has taken up. It showed, for example, that heterotrophic bacteria attached to the heterocysts of filamentous cyanobacteria receive nitrogen fixed by their host, and that individual archaea in methane-oxidizing consortia assimilate carbon from methane. Its limits are that it destroys the sample and that the instruments are few and expensive.

Raman microspectroscopy measures the inelastic scattering of laser light by the molecules of a single cell (**Raman spectroscopy**); the spectrum is a fingerprint of the cell's composition (proteins, nucleic acids, lipids, pigments, storage compounds), and the bands of compounds containing $^{13}\mathrm{C}$ or deuterium shift to lower wavenumbers. Incubation with heavy water ($\ce{D2O}$) labels all active cells with carbon–deuterium bonds whatever substrate they use, which makes Raman a general activity stain; because it is nondestructive, active cells can afterwards be sorted and grown or sequenced. Other single-cell activity methods label newly made proteins with synthetic amino acid analogs that are then tagged with a fluorescent dye by click chemistry (bioorthogonal noncanonical amino acid tagging, BONCAT).

## 29.7. Terrestrial Habitats

### 29.7.1. Soil

Soil is made of mineral particles (about $45\,\%$ of the volume of a typical surface soil), organic matter (about $5\,\%$) and pore space filled with air and water (about $50\,\%$); living organisms are a small part of the organic fraction. Particles are classified by size into clay ($< 2\,\mu\mathrm{m}$), silt and sand, and are bound by organic matter, fungal hyphae and microbial polysaccharides into aggregates. The pores between and within aggregates span micrometers to millimeters, and most bacteria live attached to particle surfaces in water films and in small pores, where they are protected from protozoan grazers. Soils develop horizons: an organic litter layer (O), a dark topsoil rich in humus (A), a subsoil into which clay and minerals are leached (B) and weathered parent material (C). Microbial numbers and activity are highest in the topsoil and fall with depth.

Water is the main factor controlling soil microbial activity. When the soil dries, the water films become thin and discontinuous, diffusion of nutrients stops and motile cells are stranded; when it is rewetted, a burst of respiration follows as the organic matter released from dead cells is consumed. The availability of nutrients, above all of organic carbon from plants, is the second. A gram of fertile topsoil contains about $10^9$ prokaryotic cells, several thousand to tens of thousands of species, meters of fungal hyphae, and protists and nematodes that graze on them. Acidobacteriota, Actinomycetota, Pseudomonadota (formerly Proteobacteria), Verrucomicrobiota, Bacteroidota, Chloroflexota and Planctomycetota are the most abundant bacterial phyla, ammonia-oxidizing archaea of the **Nitrososphaerota** are common, and fungi dominate the decomposition of lignin and cellulose in litter (**mycorrhizas** and decomposers: [7.11.21](/7.11.21#sec-21.6)). Soil pH is the best single predictor of the composition of soil bacterial communities across continents.

In deserts and other dry soils without plant cover, biological soil crusts of cyanobacteria (filamentous *Microcoleus* species), algae, **lichens** and mosses bind the surface particles with their sheaths and polysaccharides, fix carbon and nitrogen and protect the soil against erosion. Cyanobacteria are pioneers also on bare rock and volcanic ash.

### 29.7.2. The Rhizosphere

**Definition 29.7.1 (Rhizosphere)** The **rhizosphere** is the zone of soil, a few millimeters thick, that surrounds and is influenced by plant roots; the root surface itself is called the rhizoplane.

Roots release a large share of the carbon fixed by the plant, estimates range up to about a fifth, as exudates (sugars, organic and amino acids), mucilage and sloughed cells. Microbial numbers and activity in the rhizosphere are therefore much higher than in the bulk soil (the rhizosphere effect), and the community is selected by the plant: the rhizosphere microbiota is a subset of the soil community, enriched in fast-growing Pseudomonadota, Actinomycetota and Bacteroidota. Rhizosphere bacteria mineralize nutrients for the plant, produce plant hormones, solubilize phosphate, compete with pathogens and induce systemic resistance in the plant; some enter the root as endophytes. The most intimate associations, **mycorrhizas** and the **root nodules** of legumes, are symbioses treated in [7.11.31](/7.11.31); the plant side of root–microbe interactions belongs to plant biology.

### 29.7.3. The Deep Biosphere

**Definition 29.7.2 (Deep Biosphere)** The **deep biosphere** is the microbial life in subsurface sediments and rocks, from a few meters to several kilometers below the land surface and the seafloor, cut off from photosynthetic production at the surface.

The subsurface holds a large fraction of all microbial cells on Earth: about $3\times10^{29}$ cells in subseafloor sediments (Kallmeyer et al. 2012) and a similar or larger number in the continental subsurface (Magnabosco et al. 2018), mostly at very low densities. Cell numbers in uncontaminated groundwater range from $10^2$ to $10^8$ per milliliter; in marine sediments they fall from about $10^9$ per cubic centimeter at the seafloor to $10^6$ at hundreds of meters, and cells have been found more than $2\,\mathrm{km}$ below the seafloor and at temperatures up to about $120\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$. The upper temperature limit of life ([7.11.9](/7.11.9#sec-9.1.3)), not depth, ultimately sets the lower boundary of the biosphere.

Energy is the central problem. In sediments the cells live on organic matter buried from above, which becomes ever older and less reactive with depth; electron acceptors are used in the order of their energy yield ([7.11.7](/7.11.7#sec-7.3.1)), and below the zone of sulfate reduction, methanogenesis dominates. Deeper, and in igneous rock, chemolithotrophs can live on hydrogen produced geologically, by the reaction of water with iron(II) minerals (serpentinization) and by the radiolysis of water by natural radioactivity, with $\ce{CO2}$, sulfate or iron(III) as acceptors. A striking example is *Candidatus* Desulforudis audaxviator, a sulfate-reducing bacterium of the Bacillota that forms an almost single-species community in fracture water 2.8 km deep in a South African gold mine, sustained by radiolytic hydrogen and sulfate (Chivian et al. 2008). Estimated generation times in the deep biosphere are years to thousands of years; many cells may be maintaining themselves rather than growing. The subsurface has also been a rich source of new lineages known first from genomes: the DPANN archaea with their tiny cells and reduced genomes ([7.11.19](/7.11.19#sec-19.7)), the Bathyarchaeia with genes for methanogenesis and acetogenesis, and the Asgard archaea, the closest known prokaryotic relatives of eukaryotes, first described from sediments near a hydrothermal field on the Arctic Mid-Ocean Ridge ([7.11.19](/7.11.19#sec-19.8)).

## 29.8. Freshwater Habitats

Lakes, rivers and wetlands are fed by oxygenic phototrophs, algae and cyanobacteria in the plankton (floating) or the benthos (attached to the bottom), and by organic matter washed in from the land. Most of the organic matter produced is respired by bacteria, and whether a body of water stays oxic depends on the balance between the input of organic matter, which consumes oxygen, and mixing, which supplies it; oxygen is only sparingly soluble in water (about $0.3\,\mathrm{mM}$ at $15\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$) and enters from the air slowly.

**Definition 29.8.1 (Thermal Stratification)** In **thermal stratification**, a lake in summer separates into a warm, less dense surface layer, the epilimnion, and a cold, dense bottom layer, the hypolimnion, divided by the **thermocline**, a zone of rapid temperature change across which mixing is very slow. When the surface cools in autumn to the temperature of the bottom water, wind mixes the whole column (turnover).

During stratification the hypolimnion is cut off from the atmosphere, and organic matter sinking from the productive surface is respired there, so the bottom water of a productive lake can become anoxic and sulfidic by late summer; turnover then mixes oxygen down and nutrients up. In lakes with abundant algal and cyanobacterial growth, purple and green sulfur bacteria form dense layers where the light reaching the anoxic hypolimnion meets the sulfide rising from it. Meromictic lakes, with a permanent salt gradient that prevents turnover, and the Black Sea, the largest anoxic water body on Earth below about $100$–$150\,\mathrm{m}$, have such layers permanently.

Rivers receiving sewage show the same balance in space: bacteria consuming the organic load draw the dissolved oxygen down below the outlet, fish die in the "oxygen sag", and the river recovers downstream as the organic matter is used up, protists graze the bacteria and oxygen re-enters from the air, a process called self-purification. The oxygen demand of a water is measured as its **biochemical oxygen demand** (BOD, [7.11.30](/7.11.30#sec-30.9.1)). Excessive input of nitrogen and phosphorus from fields and sewage stimulates algal and cyanobacterial blooms, whose decomposition produces the same depletion: **eutrophication**, whose dynamics are discussed in [7.11.30](/7.11.30#sec-30.3.3).

## 29.9. Marine Habitats

### 29.9.1. The Open Ocean

The open ocean is a vast, dilute and well-oxygenated habitat. Its surface waters are oligotrophic: nitrate, phosphate and iron are scarce away from coasts and upwelling regions, and dissolved organic carbon is mostly refractory. Seawater nonetheless contains about $10^5$–$10^6$ prokaryotic cells per milliliter in the surface layer, and, because of its volume, the ocean holds about $10^{29}$ cells, the largest microbial population on the Earth's surface (estimates vary by a factor of a few).

![The vertical zones of the open ocean and the abundance of Bacteria and Archaea with depth. A: schematic zones (equal heights, not to scale): epipelagic (sunlit, 0–200 m), mesopelagic (twilight, 200–1000 m), bathypelagic (1000–4000 m), abyssopelagic (4000–6000 m) and hadal (trenches, to about 11 000 m), with typical conditions (hydrostatic pressure rises by about 0.1 MPa per 10 m) and characteristic microbial groups. Data from Madigan et al. (2019), after Karner et al. (2001).](./plots/ocean-zones.py)

The primary producers of the open ocean are mostly microbial and small. *Prochlorococcus*, with cells of about $0.6\,\mu\mathrm{m}$, the smallest and most abundant oxygenic phototroph on Earth, dominates the warm oligotrophic gyres, with ecotypes adapted to high and to low light at different depths ([7.11.18](/7.11.18#sec-18.4); **ecotypes**: [7.11.16](/7.11.16)); *Synechococcus* is more abundant in cooler and coastal waters, and the green alga *Ostreococcus*, about $0.8\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ across, is the smallest known free-living eukaryote. Small cells have a high surface-to-volume ratio, which helps in taking up scarce nutrients ([7.11.1](/7.11.1#sec-1.9.2)). Larger phytoplankton, **diatoms** above all, dominate where nutrients are abundant, as in upwelling regions and in spring blooms.

The most abundant heterotrophs are the alphaproteobacteria of the SAR11 clade (*Pelagibacter*), with cells of about $0.2$–$0.5\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ and a streamlined genome of $1.3\,\mathrm{Mb}$ rich in high-affinity ABC transporters, adapted to grow only at the low nutrient concentrations of the open ocean ([7.11.17](/7.11.17#sec-17.3.5)). Members of the Roseobacter clade, Flavobacteriia that degrade polysaccharides released by algal blooms, and the uncultured gammaproteobacterial SAR86 clade are also abundant. Many of these heterotrophs supplement their energy with light: **proteorhodopsin** is found in a large proportion of the bacteria in the surface ocean, and aerobic anoxygenic phototrophs use bacteriochlorophyll *a* ([7.11.6](/7.11.6#sec-6.2.5)). Below the photic zone, ammonia-oxidizing archaea related to *Nitrosopumilus* make up a large share of the cells and are major nitrifiers and chemolithoautotrophs of the ocean.

### 29.9.2. The Microbial Loop and the Viral Shunt

**Definition 29.9.1 (Microbial Loop)** The **microbial loop** is the pathway by which dissolved organic carbon released by phytoplankton (by exudation, cell lysis and the sloppy feeding of grazers) is taken up by heterotrophic bacteria and archaea, which are grazed by heterotrophic nanoflagellates and these by ciliates, so that part of the carbon returns to the classical food chain of zooplankton and fish while nutrients and $\ce{CO2}$ are regenerated at every step.

The concept (Azam et al. 1983) changed the picture of the marine food web: roughly half of marine primary production passes through heterotrophic bacteria as dissolved organic matter, a flux that larger organisms cannot use directly because the molecules are too dilute. Because each step respires part of the carbon, the loop is more a sink of organic carbon (a link that returns carbon to $\ce{CO2}$) than a link to fish, but its regeneration of nitrogen and phosphorus sustains much of the production in the oligotrophic ocean.

![The microbial loop and the viral shunt in the surface ocean (schematic). Phytoplankton (diatoms, cyanobacteria) fix CO₂; part of their production is eaten by copepods (the classic food chain to fish), and a large part is released as dissolved organic carbon (DOC), which heterotrophic bacteria and archaea take up. They are grazed by nanoflagellates, which are eaten by ciliates and these by copepods; respiration at each step regenerates CO₂ and nutrients. Viral lysis of bacteria and phytoplankton returns cell material to DOC (dashed, the viral shunt). Sinking particles export carbon to the deep ocean (the biological pump).](./plots/microbial-loop.py)

Viruses are the most abundant biological entities in the sea, about $10^7$ per milliliter in surface water, roughly ten times the number of prokaryotic cells. Most are bacteriophages. By lysing their hosts they kill a large fraction of the bacteria every day and turn cell material back into dissolved organic matter, the **viral shunt** ([7.11.26](/7.11.26)), which keeps carbon and nutrients in the microbial part of the food web. Because viruses are specific for their hosts and infect abundant hosts more often (they "kill the winner"), they also help maintain the diversity of the community. Particles that sink from the surface, aggregates of dead cells and mucus (marine snow) and the fecal pellets of zooplankton, carry organic carbon to the deep sea, the biological carbon pump ([7.11.30](/7.11.30#sec-30.2.1)).

**Definition 29.9.2 (Oxygen Minimum Zone)** An **oxygen minimum zone** (OMZ) is a layer of the ocean, typically between about $100$ and $1000\,\mathrm{m}$ deep below highly productive surface waters, in which respiration of sinking organic matter and weak ventilation lower the dissolved oxygen to very low or undetectable levels.

The largest OMZs lie off Peru and Chile, off Namibia and in the Arabian Sea. In them, **denitrification** and **anammox** ([7.11.7](/7.11.7#sec-7.2.5)) remove fixed nitrogen from the ocean, and when productivity is exceptionally high, sulfide can even accumulate. OMZs have expanded in recent decades as the warming ocean holds less oxygen and mixes less.

### 29.9.3. The Deep Sea and Its Sediments

More than three quarters of the ocean's water lies below $1000\,\mathrm{m}$: cold ($2$–$4\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$), dark, nutrient-poor and under high pressure, which rises by about $0.1\,\mathrm{MPa}$ ($1\,\mathrm{atm}$) every $10\,\mathrm{m}$ and reaches about $110\,\mathrm{MPa}$ at the bottom of the Mariana Trench. Its organisms are psychrophiles or psychrotolerant, and many are piezophiles, which grow best at high pressure; obligate piezophiles from trenches (species of *Colwellia*, *Moritella*, *Shewanella*, *Psychromonas*) do not grow at atmospheric pressure at all ([7.11.9](/7.11.9#sec-9.5)). Archaea make up a larger share of the cells than at the surface. The sediments below receive only the small fraction of surface production that escapes respiration on the way down; their cell numbers decrease from about $10^9$ per gram at the surface to $10^6$ at depth, and their communities live on ever older organic matter (29.7.3).

### 29.9.4. Hydrothermal Vents and Cold Seeps

**Definition 29.9.3 (Hydrothermal Vent)** A **hydrothermal vent** is a site on the seafloor, usually at a spreading ridge, where seawater that has percolated into the hot crust returns as hot fluid enriched in reduced compounds ($\ce{H2S}$, $\ce{H2}$, $\ce{CH4}$, $\ce{Fe^2+}$, $\ce{Mn^2+}$) and depleted in oxygen; in black smokers the fluid emerges at about $350\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ and precipitates metal sulfides into chimneys, whereas warm diffuse fluids seep from cracks nearby.

Vents are ecosystems based not on light but on chemolithoautotrophy. Sulfur-oxidizing and hydrogen-oxidizing bacteria (many of the Campylobacterota and Gammaproteobacteria) and methanogens fix $\ce{CO2}$ with the reduced compounds of the vent fluid and the oxygen or nitrate of seawater; hyperthermophilic archaea such as *Methanopyrus* and *Pyrolobus* live in the chimney walls (**hyperthermophiles**: [7.11.9](/7.11.9#sec-9.1.3), [7.11.19](/7.11.19#sec-19.9)). Animals thrive around vents at biomass densities far above those of the surrounding deep sea, many of them in symbiosis with chemolithotrophic bacteria: the giant tube worm *Riftia pachyptila*, which has no mouth or gut, harbors sulfur-oxidizing bacteria in an internal organ (the trophosome) and supplies them with sulfide, oxygen and $\ce{CO2}$ through its blood ([7.11.31](/7.11.31)). The oxygen these communities use is ultimately a product of photosynthesis at the surface. At alkaline vents such as the Lost City field on the Mid-Atlantic Ridge, the reaction of seawater with mantle rock (serpentinization) produces warm, $\mathrm{pH}$ 9–11 fluids rich in $\ce{H2}$ and $\ce{CH4}$ that build carbonate chimneys; such systems are discussed as possible sites of the origin of life ([7.10.27](/7.10.27#sec-27.2.3)).

At cold seeps, methane and other hydrocarbons escape from the seafloor along continental margins. Consortia of anaerobic methane-oxidizing archaea (ANME) and sulfate-reducing bacteria consume most of the methane in the sediment ([7.11.7](/7.11.7#sec-7.4.4)), producing sulfide that feeds mats of *Beggiatoa* and symbiotic clams and mussels; methane hydrates, ice-like solids of methane and water, are stable in the cold, high-pressure sediments.

## 29.10. Extreme Habitats and the Built Environment

Extreme habitats are defined by a physical or chemical factor near the limits of life ([7.11.9](/7.11.9)); their communities are often simple, dominated by a few specialized groups, which makes them useful models of ecosystems.

| Habitat | Conditions | Characteristic microorganisms |
|---|---|---|
| Salt lakes and solar salterns (Great Salt Lake, Dead Sea) | saturated NaCl, often alkaline | **extreme halophiles** (haloarchaea, pink with bacteriorhodopsin and carotenoids), *Salinibacter*, the alga *Dunaliella* |
| Soda lakes (East African Rift) | $\mathrm{pH}$ 10–11, high salt, very productive | alkaliphilic cyanobacteria (*Arthrospira*), haloalkaliphilic archaea and sulfur bacteria |
| Hot springs (Yellowstone) | up to boiling, neutral to acidic | **thermophiles** and hyperthermophiles: *Thermus aquaticus*, *Aquifex*, *Sulfolobus*, mats of cyanobacteria (*Synechococcus*) and *Chloroflexus* below about 70 °C |
| Acidic waters (Río Tinto, acid mine drainage) | $\mathrm{pH}$ 0–3, high metals | **acidophiles**: *Acidithiobacillus*, *Leptospirillum*, *Ferroplasma*, eukaryotic algae and fungi ([7.11.30](/7.11.30#sec-30.4)) |
| Permafrost, sea ice, glaciers | below 0 °C, low water activity | **psychrophiles**: *Psychrobacter*, *Colwellia*, snow algae; cells survive for thousands of years in permafrost |
| Deserts and rock surfaces | dryness, radiation | endolithic cyanobacteria and lichens, *Deinococcus*, crust communities |

Permafrost underlies about a quarter of the exposed land of the Northern Hemisphere and stores a large pool of organic carbon; its thaw and microbial decomposition are a feedback on climate warming discussed in [7.11.30](/7.11.30#sec-30.2.1). The atmosphere carries bacteria, fungal spores and viruses, as bioaerosols attached to dust and droplets, over thousands of kilometers; bioaerosols are also a route of transmission of airborne pathogens ([7.11.35](/7.11.35)); some, such as *Pseudomonas syringae*, make ice-nucleating proteins that freeze water at relatively warm temperatures and may affect the formation of rain and snow. Buildings, finally, are habitats too: their air and surfaces carry mostly harmless bacteria and fungi derived from the occupants, pets, plants and outdoor air, and wet places such as shower heads, cooling towers and water pipes host biofilms in which *Legionella* and nontuberculous mycobacteria can grow ([7.11.30](/7.11.30#sec-30.10)).

## 29.11. Microbial Biogeography and Diversity

Microbial cells are small, numerous and easily dispersed by water, wind and animals. This led the Dutch microbiologist Lourens Baas Becking in 1934, building on Beijerinck, to the principle "everything is everywhere, but the environment selects": any microorganism can reach any habitat, and only the local conditions decide whether it grows there.

**Definition 29.11.1 (Baas Becking Hypothesis)** The **Baas Becking hypothesis** states that microorganisms are dispersed so widely that their distribution is determined by environmental selection alone, not by geographic barriers or history ("everything is everywhere, but the environment selects").

The hypothesis is a null model of microbial **biogeography** and has been tested with molecular methods. Much of it holds: the same taxa are found in similar habitats on different continents, and environmental factors (temperature, salinity, $\mathrm{pH}$, oxygen) explain a large part of the variation in community composition. A "seed bank" of dormant rare cells allows a community to respond when conditions change; deep sequencing has shown that most of the taxa in a sample are rare, the so-called rare biosphere. But the hypothesis does not hold strictly. Isolates of the thermoacidophilic archaeon *Sulfolobus islandicus* from hot springs in North America, Kamchatka and Iceland form genetically distinct populations that reflect geographic isolation rather than differences in their springs (Whitaker et al. 2003), and endemic, isolated populations have been found in other island-like habitats.

**Definition 29.11.2 (Distance–Decay Relationship)** The **distance–decay relationship** is the decrease of the similarity in composition between two communities with the geographic distance between them.

A distance–decay relationship arises from two causes: environments that are close together tend to be similar (environmental selection with spatially autocorrelated conditions), and organisms disperse more easily over short distances (dispersal limitation and history). Statistical analysis that controls for the measured environmental variables can separate the two; for many microorganisms both contribute, with dispersal limitation strongest for organisms of isolated habitats. The diversity within and between samples (**alpha diversity** and **beta diversity**), and the effect of sequencing depth on the observed richness, are defined in [7.8.14](/7.8.14#sec-14.4); their place in general community ecology belongs to ecology.

## Summary

- Microbial ecology (Definition 29.1.1) asks who is present, what they do and how fast; guilds (Definition 29.1.2) are its functional units. About $10^{30}$ prokaryotic cells live mainly in the ocean, sediments, soils and the subsurface.
- Microenvironments (Definition 29.2.1) separate aerobes and anaerobes over millimeters; microbial mats (Definition 29.2.2) are laminated by gradients of light, oxygen and sulfide, which a reaction–diffusion model and Fick's law describe quantitatively; their day–night switch is shown by microsensors.
- Cultivation: enrichment proves presence but not importance; dilution culture, diffusion chambers and the iChip, co-culture and long incubations have brought many members of the uncultured majority into culture.

| Question | Methods | What they give |
|---|---|---|
| Who and how many? | DAPI and SYBR counts, FISH and CARD-FISH (Definition 29.4.1), amplicons, qPCR | total and group-specific numbers, spatial arrangement |
| What can they do? | metagenomes, MAGs, single-cell genomes (Definition 29.5.1) | gene content of uncultured organisms |
| What are they doing? | metatranscriptomes, metaproteomes, MAR-FISH (Definition 29.4.2) | expressed genes, substrate uptake by identified cells |
| How fast? | radioisotopes, microsensors (Definition 29.6.1, Example 29.6.1) | process rates and fluxes |
| Who uses which substrate? | SIP (Definition 29.6.3), NanoSIMS (Definition 29.6.4), Raman with $\ce{D2O}$ | substrate users, single-cell activity |
| Biological origin? | isotopic fractionation (Definition 29.6.2, Formula 29.6.1) | biosignatures, process identification |

- Soils: aggregates and water films, $10^9$ cells per gram, pH as the main driver of bacterial composition; the rhizosphere (Definition 29.7.1) is fed by root exudates; the deep biosphere (Definition 29.7.2) lives on buried organic matter and geological hydrogen at extremely slow rates.
- Lakes stratify and turn over (Definition 29.8.1); organic and nutrient loading depletes oxygen. The open ocean is oligotrophic and dominated by small cells (*Prochlorococcus*, *Pelagibacter*, ammonia-oxidizing archaea); the microbial loop (Definition 29.9.1) and the viral shunt recycle dissolved organic matter; OMZs (Definition 29.9.2) are sites of nitrogen loss; vents (Definition 29.9.3) and seeps support chemolithoautotrophic ecosystems.
- Biogeography: environmental selection explains much of microbial distribution, but distance–decay (Definition 29.11.2) and endemism show that the Baas Becking hypothesis (Definition 29.11.1) does not hold strictly.

## Questions

1. Why does a successful enrichment culture not show that the organism obtained is important in the habitat, and how can dilution of the inoculum change the result?

   **Answer** The winner of an enrichment is the organism that grows fastest under the laboratory conditions, which are usually richer than nature, so a rare fast grower can outcompete an abundant slow one (enrichment bias, compare the $R^*$ argument of [7.11.4](/7.11.4#sec-4.5.3)). Diluting the sample until only the most numerous organisms remain in the inoculum removes the rare fast growers, so the enrichments that grow from high dilutions are more likely to yield the abundant organisms.

2. Why is CARD-FISH preferred over FISH for counting cells in the open ocean, and what can MAR-FISH tell you that FISH cannot?

   **Answer** Slowly growing oligotrophic cells contain few ribosomes, so a dye-labeled probe gives too weak a signal; in CARD-FISH a peroxidase on the probe deposits many fluorescent tyramide molecules and amplifies the signal. FISH identifies cells; MAR-FISH also shows which identified cells took up a radioactively labeled substrate, linking identity to activity.

3. In a DNA-SIP experiment with $^{13}\mathrm{C}$-benzoate, a heavy fraction is dominated by sequences of a high-GC actinomycete. Is it a benzoate degrader? What control is needed?

   **Answer** Not necessarily: the buoyant density of DNA rises by about $0.0098\,\mathrm{g\,mL^{-1}}$ per $10\,\%$ G + C, so unlabeled DNA with $70\,\%$ GC is about as dense as fully $^{13}\mathrm{C}$-labeled DNA with $35\,\%$ GC. A parallel incubation with $^{12}\mathrm{C}$-benzoate must be fractionated in the same way; only taxa whose DNA shifts to higher density in the $^{13}\mathrm{C}$ treatment relative to the control incorporated the label. Short incubations also limit cross-feeding.

4. An oxygen microprofile in a sediment drops linearly from $250\,\mu\mathrm{M}$ at the surface to zero at $0.5\,\mathrm{mm}$. With $D = 1.0\times10^{-9}\,\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$ and porosity $0.8$, estimate the oxygen flux, and explain why the profile is curved rather than linear in reality.

   **Answer** The gradient is $250\,\mathrm{mmol\,m^{-3}}/(5\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m}) = 500\,\mathrm{mol\,m^{-4}}$, so $J = 0.8 \times 10^{-9} \times 500 = 4\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm{mol\,m^{-2}\,s^{-1}}$, about $35\,\mathrm{mmol\,m^{-2}\,d^{-1}}$. A linear profile would mean no consumption within the oxic layer; since oxygen is respired throughout that layer, $D\,C'' = R > 0$ and the profile is concave upward.

5. Explain why *Prochlorococcus* and *Pelagibacter* are so small and what the microbial loop does with the organic matter that phytoplankton release.

   **Answer** In oligotrophic water uptake is limited by diffusion to the cell surface; a small cell has a large surface-to-volume ratio and needs fewer nutrients to divide, and both organisms have streamlined genomes and high-affinity transporters. Dissolved organic matter released by phytoplankton is taken up by heterotrophic bacteria, which are grazed by flagellates and ciliates; this returns part of the carbon to the food web and regenerates nutrients, while most of the carbon is respired to $\ce{CO2}$ along the way, and viral lysis recycles cells back to dissolved matter.
