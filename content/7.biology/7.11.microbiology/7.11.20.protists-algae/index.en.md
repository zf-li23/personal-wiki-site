Most of the diversity of eukaryotes is microbial. The amoebae, flagellates, ciliates, diatoms, foraminifera and seaweeds of this chapter belong to every major branch of the eukaryotic tree, and between them they include the main grazers of bacteria, much of the primary production of the planet, the agents of malaria, sleeping sickness and amoebic dysentery, and several of the model organisms on which molecular and cell biology were built. This chapter goes through the supergroups of eukaryotes and gives, for representative groups of each, the features of the cell, the life cycle, the ecology and the relation to humans. The phylogeny itself — the evidence for the supergroups, the origin of the eukaryotic cell and of its organelles by endosymbiosis (**endosymbiotic theory**), and the origins of multicellularity — is in [7.10.28](/7.10.28) and is only used here; the general structure of the eukaryotic cell is in 7.3, and the reduced mitochondria of anaerobic protists were introduced in [7.11.3](/7.11.3#sec-3.6). The fungi, which belong to the same supergroup as animals, have a chapter of their own ([7.11.21](/7.11.21)); the mechanisms by which protozoan parasites evade immunity and cause disease are taken up in [7.11.34](/7.11.34) and 7.15, and their epidemiology in [7.11.35](/7.11.35).

## 20.1. Microbial Eukaryotes and Their Organelles

### 20.1.1. Protists, Protozoa and Algae

The traditional names for microbial eukaryotes describe ways of life, not lineages. "Protozoa" were the motile, phagotrophic, animal-like forms; "algae" the photosynthetic ones, whether unicellular or as large as a kelp; "slime molds" and "water molds" were placed with the fungi because they make spores or hyphae. None of these groups is a **clade**: photosynthetic and phagotrophic species occur in the same lineage, sometimes in the same cell, and the amoeboid habit has evolved many times.

**Definition 20.1.1 (Protist)** A **protist** is any eukaryote that is not an animal, a land plant or a fungus. Protists are mostly unicellular or colonial, but the group also contains multicellular algae such as kelps and red seaweeds; it is a **paraphyletic group** defined by exclusion, and the words *protozoa* and *algae* are informal names for its heterotrophic and photosynthetic members.

The names remain useful as descriptions, and are used in that sense below; the classification follows the supergroups of [7.10.28](/7.10.28#sec-28.4). Figure 1 places the genera discussed in this chapter on that tree and shows how the traits that the old classifications relied on — plastids, reduced mitochondria, mineral skeletons, parasitism — are scattered across it.

### 20.1.2. Organelles and Cell Coverings

Microbial eukaryotes have the cell plan of [7.3.1](/7.3.1): nucleus, endomembrane system, cytoskeleton, **cilia** or flagella with the 9 + 2 axoneme, and **mitochondria**. Three groups of organelles vary in ways that matter for the rest of the chapter.

- Mitochondria: all known eukaryotes descend from an ancestor with a mitochondrion. In anaerobic lineages the organelle has been reduced, independently many times, to a **hydrogenosome**, which makes ATP and $\ce{H2}$ (parabasalids, some ciliates and chytrids), or to a **mitosome**, which makes no ATP (*Giardia*, *Entamoeba*, microsporidia); the oxymonad *Monocercomonoides* has lost it entirely ([7.11.3](/7.11.3#sec-3.6)). Mitochondrial genomes range from the minimal genome of apicomplexans, with three protein genes and fragmented rRNAs, to the bacterium-like genome of the jakobid *Andalucia godoyi*, with about a hundred genes.
- Plastids: the **plastids** of the Archaeplastida descend from a **primary endosymbiosis** with a cyanobacterium and have two envelope membranes. Euglenids and chlorarachniophytes acquired plastids from a green alga, and cryptophytes, haptophytes, stramenopiles and alveolates from a red alga, by **secondary endosymbiosis**; such plastids have three or four membranes, and in two groups a remnant of the alga's nucleus, the **nucleomorph**, survives between them ([7.3.12](/7.3.12#sec-12.8.4), [7.10.28](/7.10.28#sec-28.2.3)). Plastids are lost, reduced to nonphotosynthetic relicts or replaced in many lineages.
- Cell coverings: many protists have no wall and are shaped by the cytoskeleton under the membrane: the protein strips of the euglenid pellicle, the flattened membrane sacs (alveoli) of ciliates and dinoflagellates. Others build walls of cellulose (most green algae, oomycetes), plates of cellulose in the alveoli (armored dinoflagellates), silica (the frustules of diatoms, the skeletons of most radiolarians) or calcium carbonate (the tests of foraminifera, the coccoliths of haptophytes), and the mineral skeletons give these groups a long fossil record. Freshwater protists without walls expel the water that enters by osmosis with contractile vacuoles.

Protists obtain food in three ways — photosynthesis, phagocytosis and the uptake of dissolved organic molecules (osmotrophy) — and many combine them (**mixotrophy**, [7.11.4](/7.11.4)). Mixotrophs photosynthesize and also eat bacteria or other protists; they are common among dinoflagellates, haptophytes, chrysophytes and euglenids, and some ciliates and foraminifera keep the plastids or whole cells of the algae they eat. The distinction between "protozoa" and "algae" is therefore not only phylogenetically meaningless but often physiologically blurred.

### 20.1.3. Life Cycles

Protists show all three types of life cycle in [7.4.1](/7.4.1#sec-1.4) (Definition 1.4.2), often several within one supergroup: **zygotic meiosis** in *Chlamydomonas*, *Plasmodium* and many other haploid protists; **gametic meiosis** in diatoms, ciliates and the brown alga *Fucus*; and **sporic meiosis** with **alternation of generations** in kelps, *Ulva* and red algae. Many protists reproduce asexually for long periods, by binary or multiple fission, budding or fragmentation, and are sexual only under stress or not at all; but genome surveys find the genes of the meiotic machinery in nearly every lineage, including *Giardia* and *Trichomonas*, which were long thought to be asexual, and the last common ancestor of eukaryotes was almost certainly sexual. Resistant resting stages — cysts in many protozoa, zygospores in algae — carry protists through drying and starvation and, in parasites, from one host to the next.

## 20.2. Discoba

The Discoba are flagellates, many of them with a ventral feeding groove and mitochondria with disc-shaped cristae. They comprise the euglenozoans (euglenids, kinetoplastids and diplonemids), the heterolobosean amoeboflagellates such as *Naegleria*, and the jakobids, whose mitochondrial genome is the most bacterium-like known.

### 20.2.1. Euglenids

*Euglena* and its relatives are flagellates of fresh water, rich in organic matter, with a flexible pellicle of interlocking protein strips beneath the membrane that allows the cell to change shape (euglenoid movement). One emergent flagellum pulls the cell; a red eyespot shades a photoreceptor at the base of the flagellum, so that the cell, as it rotates, detects the direction of light and swims towards it. The photosynthetic euglenids have a green-algal plastid of secondary origin, bounded by three membranes, and store a β-1,3-glucan, paramylon, in the cytoplasm. Many are mixotrophic, and *Euglena gracilis* kept in the dark, or treated with streptomycin, which inhibits the plastid's bacterial-type ribosomes, loses its plastids and lives as an osmotroph; most euglenids, however, are colorless phagotrophs, and the photosynthetic ones arose within them.

### 20.2.2. Kinetoplastids

The kinetoplastids are named for the *kinetoplast*, a mass of DNA in their single, large mitochondrion, located next to the basal body of the flagellum and attached to it through the membranes. Free-living bodonids graze on bacteria; the trypanosomatids are parasites of insects, vertebrates and plants, and include the agents of three major human diseases (Figure 2).

**Definition 20.2.1 (Kinetoplast)** The **kinetoplast** is the mitochondrial DNA of kinetoplastids, a network of a few dozen *maxicircles*, which carry the mitochondrial rRNA and protein-coding genes, and thousands of interlocked *minicircles* of about 1 kb, which encode the guide RNAs for the editing of maxicircle transcripts.

The kinetoplastids are the organisms in which several mechanisms of eukaryotic gene expression were discovered. Many maxicircle transcripts are cryptic: they become translatable only after **RNA editing**, in which hundreds of uridines are inserted, and some deleted, at sites specified by base pairing with minicircle-encoded guide RNAs (**guide-directed RNA editing**, [7.2.17](/7.2.17#sec-17.6)). Nuclear genes are transcribed as long polycistronic units without individual promoters; each mRNA receives the same 39-nucleotide capped leader by **trans-splicing**, which also separates the messages, so that gene expression is regulated almost entirely after transcription. Most of glycolysis takes place in *glycosomes*, organelles related to peroxisomes, in which the enzymes are protected from the regulation that operates in the cytosol.

The kinetoplast lies in the single mitochondrion, linked to the basal body; glycosomes carry out glycolysis. *Trypanosoma brucei* causes African trypanosomiasis (sleeping sickness) in humans and nagana in cattle. It is transmitted by tsetse flies (*Glossina*) and lives free in the blood and lymph, and later in the cerebrospinal fluid, of its mammalian host. The bloodstream form is covered by about $10^7$ molecules of one *variant surface glycoprotein* (VSG), expressed from one telomeric expression site at a time out of a repertoire of more than a thousand VSG genes and pseudogenes. Antibodies against the current coat clear most of the parasites, but cells that have switched to another VSG — mostly by **gene conversion**, which copies a silent gene into the active site — escape and give rise to a new wave of parasitemia. This *antigenic variation*, and the comparable systems of bacteria, are discussed in [7.11.34](/7.11.34). In the fly the parasite changes its coat to a procyclic protein, its metabolism from glycolysis to mitochondrial respiration, and migrates to the salivary glands, where it becomes the metacyclic form that infects the next host. *T. cruzi*, the agent of Chagas disease in the Americas, is transmitted by triatomine bugs, not by their bite but through their feces, which are rubbed into the wound or the conjunctiva; in the host it multiplies inside cells, including heart muscle. *Leishmania* species, transmitted by sand flies, multiply inside the phagolysosomes of macrophages as small amastigotes with a reduced flagellum, and cause cutaneous, mucocutaneous or visceral leishmaniasis according to the species.

### 20.2.3. *Naegleria*

*Naegleria* is an amoeboflagellate: it lives in soil and warm fresh water as an amoeba but, when the ionic conditions change, can transform within an hour into a flagellate with newly assembled basal bodies and flagella, and back. Morphology placed it among the amoebae; molecular phylogeny places it in the Discoba (class Heterolobosea), far from the Amoebozoa. *N. fowleri*, a thermophile of warm lakes and poorly chlorinated pools, enters the nose of swimmers, follows the olfactory nerve into the brain and causes primary amoebic meningoencephalitis, which is rare but nearly always fatal.

## 20.3. Metamonada

The Metamonada are anaerobic or microaerophilic flagellates, most of them in the guts of animals, whose mitochondria are reduced to hydrogenosomes or mitosomes.

*Giardia intestinalis* (also called *G. lamblia* or *G. duodenalis*) is a diplomonad: the trophozoite has two nuclei of equal size, four pairs of flagella and a ventral adhesive disc built of microtubules, with which it attaches to the epithelium of the small intestine. It ferments glucose to ethanol, acetate and $\ce{CO2}$, has mitosomes in place of mitochondria and a compact genome of about 12 Mbp with very few introns. It spreads as a cyst with four nuclei and a thick wall that resists chlorination, from the feces of humans and of beavers and other animals into surface water.

*Trichomonas vaginalis* is a parabasalid, named for the parabasal bodies, large Golgi stacks supported by fibers. It has four anterior flagella, a fifth that forms an undulating membrane along the cell, and an axostyle, a rod of microtubules running through the cell. Its hydrogenosomes oxidize pyruvate to acetate, $\ce{CO2}$ and $\ce{H2}$ with substrate-level phosphorylation. *T. vaginalis* forms no cysts and is transmitted sexually; with an estimated incidence of over a hundred million cases a year it is the commonest nonviral sexually transmitted infection. Its genome, of about 160 Mbp with some 60,000 predicted genes, is unexpectedly large for a parasite and full of repeats and transposons. Other parabasalids, such as *Trichonympha*, live in the hindgut of termites, where together with their own bacterial symbionts they digest the cellulose of the wood that the termite eats ([7.11.31](/7.11.31)).

## 20.4. Alveolata

The alveolates — ciliates, dinoflagellates and apicomplexans — are united by the *alveoli*, flattened sacs of membrane beneath the plasma membrane, which support the cell surface, store calcium and, in armored dinoflagellates, contain cellulose plates. With the stramenopiles and rhizarians they form the SAR clade.

### 20.4.1. Ciliates

**Definition 20.4.1 (Ciliate)** A **ciliate** is a member of the alveolate phylum Ciliophora, protists with many cilia arranged in rows (kineties) or in compound structures, and with two kinds of nucleus in each cell: one or more small diploid *micronuclei*, the germline, which are transcriptionally inactive during growth and undergo meiosis in sexual reproduction, and a large polyploid *macronucleus*, the somatic nucleus, which is transcribed and determines the phenotype.

Ciliates are the largest and most complex single cells. *Paramecium* is covered by several thousand cilia, which beat in coordinated metachronal waves; an oral groove lined with compound cilia sweeps bacteria into a cytostome, where they are enclosed in food vacuoles that circulate through the cell and fuse with lysosomes; contractile vacuoles expel water, and trichocysts below the surface discharge protein threads when the cell is attacked. The surface pattern is inherited partly independently of the genes: when a patch of kineties is surgically inverted, the inversion is propagated through many divisions, an example of *cortical inheritance* in which existing structures template new ones. Ciliates also changed the genetic code: in *Tetrahymena* and *Paramecium* the stop codons UAA and UAG encode glutamine, and only UGA terminates translation.

Ciliates divide by transverse binary fission, the macronucleus dividing without mitosis. Sexual reproduction, conjugation, involves no increase in cell number (Figure 3). Two cells of complementary mating type pair; in each the micronucleus undergoes meiosis, three of the four haploid products degenerate, and the fourth divides by mitosis into a stationary and a migratory pronucleus. The cells exchange their migratory pronuclei, which fuse with the stationary ones, so that the two partners end with genetically identical diploid zygotic nuclei. The old macronucleus is destroyed, and new micronuclei and macronuclei develop from the products of the zygotic nucleus.

![Conjugation in a ciliate with one micronucleus per cell (*Tetrahymena*-like, schematic). Blue and orange mark the nuclear lineages of the two partners. After pairing, each micronucleus undergoes meiosis and three of its four haploid products degenerate; the survivor divides by mitosis, and migratory pronuclei are exchanged and fuse with the stationary ones; the old macronuclei break down; the zygotic nucleus divides, and its products give the new micronucleus and the new macronuclei of the two exconjugants, which now carry the same recombined genotype.](./plots/ciliate-conjugation.py)

The development of a new macronucleus is a dramatic rearrangement of the genome. Thousands of internal eliminated sequences, many of them derived from transposons, are cut out by a domesticated transposase, and the chromosomes are broken at specific sites into small pieces that receive new **telomeres** and are amplified — to about 45 copies in *Tetrahymena*, to thousands of tiny "nanochromosomes" carrying one gene each in *Oxytricha*, which discards more than 90% of its germline DNA. Which sequences to eliminate is specified by small RNAs that compare the germline with the old macronucleus, so that changes in the old macronucleus can be inherited epigenetically (**programmed DNA elimination**, [7.4.17](/7.4.17#sec-17.8)). The ciliate *Tetrahymena thermophila* became a **model organism** because of this biology: its many macronuclear minichromosomes and amplified rDNA made it the material in which telomere repeats were first sequenced, **telomerase** was discovered ([7.2.7](/7.2.7)), the first self-splicing RNA was found (the group I intron of its rRNA, the first **ribozyme**, [7.2.16](/7.2.16)), the first motor protein of cilia, dynein, was identified, and histone acetylation was linked to transcription.

Ciliates are major grazers of bacteria and of small algae in water and soil. Some harbor endosymbiotic green algae or bacteria; anaerobic ciliates of sediments and of the **rumen** have hydrogenosomes and endosymbiotic **methanogens** that consume their hydrogen, and the rumen ciliates contribute to the digestion of plant material by their host. *Balantidium coli*, from pigs, is the only ciliate that causes disease in humans, a dysentery resembling amoebiasis.

### 20.4.2. Dinoflagellates

**Definition 20.4.2 (Dinoflagellate)** A **dinoflagellate** is a member of the alveolate group Dinoflagellata, flagellates with two flagella of different form — a transverse, ribbon-like flagellum that beats in an equatorial groove (the cingulum) and makes the cell spin, and a longitudinal flagellum in a ventral groove (the sulcus) that propels it — and with a distinctive nucleus, the dinokaryon, whose chromosomes stay condensed throughout the cell cycle.

Many dinoflagellates are armored with plates of cellulose in their alveoli; others are naked. The dinokaryon is unique among eukaryotes: its chromosomes are packed not mainly by nucleosomes but by proteins of viral origin, and the genomes are among the largest known, often tens of gigabases. About half of the species are photosynthetic, most with a plastid of red-algal origin, surrounded by three membranes and containing the carotenoid peridinin; in several lineages this plastid has been replaced by one taken from a green alga (serial secondary endosymbiosis) or from a diatom or a haptophyte (tertiary endosymbiosis). The rest are heterotrophs or mixotrophs, some of which feed with a peduncle that sucks out the contents of prey, and several are parasites of fish and crustaceans.

Dinoflagellates are a large part of the marine phytoplankton and cause most harmful algal blooms. Dense blooms ("red tides") of toxic species concentrate their toxins in shellfish that filter them: saxitoxin, a blocker of the voltage-gated sodium channel, from *Alexandrium* (formerly classified in *Gonyaulax*) and some other genera causes paralytic shellfish poisoning, and brevetoxin from *Karenia brevis* kills fish and causes respiratory irritation along the coast of Florida; ciguatoxins, from *Gambierdiscus* living on reef algae, accumulate up the food chain in reef fish. Many species are bioluminescent: a luciferin bound to a luciferase in membrane-bound particles, the scintillons, flashes when the cell is deformed, so that disturbed water sparkles at night. Dinoflagellates of the family Symbiodiniaceae (formerly the single genus *Symbiodinium*) live as **zooxanthellae** inside the cells of reef-building corals, giant clams and other animals, supplying most of the carbon the coral needs; their loss under heat stress is **coral bleaching** ([7.11.31](/7.11.31)).

### 20.4.3. Apicomplexa

**Definition 20.4.3 (Apicomplexa)** **Apicomplexa** is the alveolate phylum of obligate parasites of animals whose invasive stages (sporozoites and merozoites) carry at their anterior end an *apical complex* of secretory organelles (rhoptries and micronemes) and, in most, a ring of cytoskeletal fibers (the conoid), with which they attach to host cells and enter them. Most apicomplexans also contain an **apicoplast**, a nonphotosynthetic plastid with four membranes derived from a red alga by secondary endosymbiosis, which synthesizes isoprenoids, fatty acids and part of the heme.

The apicomplexans glide by an actin–myosin motor anchored in the alveoli, which pulls adhesins secreted from the micronemes backwards along the cell surface, and they enter host cells actively, forming a parasitophorous vacuole from the host membrane. The apicoplast shows that the ancestors of these parasites were photosynthetic algae; the coral-associated *Chromera velia*, a photosynthetic relative discovered in 2008, confirmed the relationship. Because the apicoplast is of bacterial origin, it is a target of antibiotics such as doxycycline and clindamycin and of the inhibitor of its isoprenoid pathway fosmidomycin. *Cryptosporidium* has lost it.

The life cycle of *Plasmodium*, the agent of malaria, takes place in two hosts and illustrates the general pattern of the phylum: an asexual multiplication in the vertebrate (schizogony, a multiple fission in which many nuclei form before the cell divides), a sexual phase that begins when gametocytes are taken up by the vector, and a further multiplication (sporogony) after meiosis (Figure 4). The parasite is haploid throughout except for the zygote.

![The life cycle of *Plasmodium falciparum*. A female *Anopheles* mosquito injects sporozoites with its saliva; they reach the liver, where each multiplies in a hepatocyte into tens of thousands of merozoites. Merozoites invade erythrocytes, grow through the ring and trophozoite stages, digest hemoglobin and divide into a schizont, which releases new merozoites after 48 h; the synchronized rupture of infected cells causes the cycles of fever. A few parasites develop into male and female gametocytes, which a mosquito takes up in a blood meal. In the mosquito midgut the gametes fuse; the zygote, the only diploid stage, becomes a motile ookinete that crosses the gut wall and forms an oocyst, in which meiosis and repeated nuclear divisions produce thousands of sporozoites, which migrate to the salivary glands. In *P. vivax* and *P. ovale* some liver stages remain dormant as hypnozoites.](./plots/plasmodium-cycle.py)

Five species of *Plasmodium* cause most human malaria. *P. falciparum*, dominant in Africa, is the most dangerous, because erythrocytes infected with it express parasite adhesins at their surface and stick to the walls of capillaries in the brain and placenta; *P. vivax*, the most widespread outside Africa, and *P. ovale* form dormant liver stages, hypnozoites, which cause relapses months or years later and must be cleared with primaquine or tafenoquine; *P. malariae* has a 72-hour cycle; and *P. knowlesi* is a parasite of macaques that infects humans in Southeast Asia. Malaria remains among the leading infectious causes of death: the World Health Organization estimates about a quarter of a billion cases and about 600,000 deaths a year in the early 2020s, most of them of young children in Africa. Its long history has left marks on the human genome, among them the sickle-cell and thalassemia alleles and the Duffy-negative blood group, which protect against severe malaria or against *P. vivax* ([7.10.17](/7.10.17)). Treatment relies on combinations based on artemisinin, an endoperoxide from the sweet wormwood *Artemisia annua* isolated in the 1970s ([7.11.14](/7.11.14#sec-14.10.2)), now threatened by partial resistance; the first malaria vaccines, RTS,S and R21, both based on a sporozoite surface protein, were recommended by the WHO in 2021 and 2023.

*Toxoplasma gondii* has a sexual cycle only in the intestine of cats, which shed oocysts in their feces; almost any warm-blooded animal can be an intermediate host, in which fast-dividing tachyzoites spread through the tissues and then form tissue cysts of slow bradyzoites, mostly in brain and muscle. Humans are infected by oocysts or by eating undercooked meat with cysts; a large fraction of the world's population carries cysts without symptoms, but a first infection in pregnancy can damage the fetus, and the infection reactivates in people with weakened immunity. *Cryptosporidium parvum* and *C. hominis* multiply in the intestinal epithelium, in a vacuole at the surface of the cell, and shed oocysts that are infective at once and resist chlorine; in 1993 a contaminated municipal water supply in Milwaukee caused diarrhea in about 400,000 people. Other apicomplexans include the coccidia of poultry (*Eimeria*), and *Babesia* and *Theileria*, tick-borne parasites of cattle.

## 20.5. Stramenopiles

The stramenopiles (also called heterokonts) are named for the hairs on their flagella: the anterior flagellum carries two rows of stiff, tripartite tubular hairs, which reverse the direction of thrust, so that a wave travelling from base to tip pulls the cell forward. The group ranges from flagellates a few micrometers long to kelps of tens of meters, and includes the diatoms, the brown and golden algae and, among the heterotrophs, the oomycetes.

### 20.5.1. Diatoms

**Definition 20.5.1 (Diatom)** A **diatom** is a member of the stramenopile class Bacillariophyceae, unicellular or chain-forming photosynthetic algae enclosed in a *frustule*, a wall of amorphous hydrated silica in two overlapping halves: the larger *epitheca* fits over the smaller *hypotheca* like the lid of a box.

Diatoms are centric (radially symmetric, mostly planktonic) or pennate (bilaterally symmetric); many pennate diatoms glide on surfaces by secreting mucilage through a slit in the frustule, the raphe. The plastid, of red-algal origin, contains chlorophylls *a* and *c* and the brown carotenoid fucoxanthin. Silica walls are cheap to build in energy, but they make diatoms dependent on dissolved silicic acid, and diatom blooms in spring often end when the silicate of the surface water is exhausted. Diatoms are diploid; meiosis produces gametes (gametic meiosis).

The frustule constrains division. Each daughter cell receives one half of the parental frustule as its epitheca and builds a new hypotheca inside it, so the daughter that inherits the parental hypotheca is smaller, and the mean size of a clone declines (Figure 5).

**Formula 20.5.1 (MacDonald–Pfitzer Rule)** If each division leaves one daughter at the size of the parent and the other smaller by $d$, then after $g$ synchronous divisions of a clone founded by a cell of diameter $s_0$ the number of cells reduced $k$ times is $\binom{g}{k}$, so the fraction of cells of diameter $s_0 - kd$ is

$$
f_g(k) = \binom{g}{k}\, 2^{-g}, \qquad \bar s_g = s_0 - \frac{g d}{2}.
$$
The distribution of sizes widens as $\sqrt{g}\,d/2$ while its mean falls linearly with the number of generations.

The decline cannot continue indefinitely. When cells fall below a critical size, typically 30–40% of the maximum, they become able to reproduce sexually; the zygote swells into an *auxospore*, bounded by an organic or lightly silicified wall, inside which a cell of maximal size builds a new frustule. Size reduction thus acts as a clock that ensures periodic sex.

B: Formula 20.5.1 for a clone founded by one cell 60 µm across, with a reduction of 1 µm per division for the smaller daughter, after 8, 24 and 48 generations. Below a critical size (here 40 µm, shaded) the cells can undergo sexual reproduction, and the auxospore restores the maximal size.](./plots/diatom-size.py)

Diatoms account for roughly a fifth of the photosynthesis on Earth and dominate the phytoplankton of cold, nutrient-rich waters and upwelling zones. Because they are large and heavy, much of their production sinks to the deep sea and contributes to the export of carbon from the surface ([7.11.29](/7.11.29)), and their frustules accumulate as siliceous ooze and diatomaceous earth. Some species of *Pseudo-nitzschia* produce domoic acid, a glutamate-receptor agonist that causes amnesic shellfish poisoning.

### 20.5.2. Brown and Golden Algae

The brown algae (Phaeophyceae) are all multicellular and almost all marine, and they include the largest protists: the giant kelp *Macrocystis* grows to 50 m. They have the same pigments as diatoms, walls of cellulose and alginate (extracted for food and industry), and a tissue organization with conducting cells in the stipe of kelps, which evolved independently of that of plants. Kelps have a life cycle with sporic meiosis and a dramatic alternation of generations: the large plant is the diploid sporophyte; meiosis produces zoospores that grow into microscopic male and female gametophytes, which produce eggs and sperm. *Fucus*, in contrast, has gametic meiosis like an animal. Kelp forests are among the most productive ecosystems of temperate coasts. The golden algae (chrysophytes) are mostly unicellular flagellates of fresh water, many of them mixotrophic, with siliceous scales or resting cysts.

### 20.5.3. Oomycetes

**Definition 20.5.2 (Oomycete)** An **oomycete** (water mold) is a member of the heterotrophic stramenopile group Oomycota, organisms that grow as branched, coenocytic, diploid hyphae with walls of cellulose and other β-glucans, reproduce asexually by biflagellate zoospores with stramenopile flagella and sexually by fusion of an antheridium with an oogonium, producing thick-walled oospores.

Oomycetes resemble fungi in their hyphae and absorptive nutrition, and were classified with them, but differ from fungi in almost every other feature: their walls contain cellulose rather than chitin, their hyphae are diploid, they synthesize lysine by the diaminopimelate pathway rather than the α-aminoadipate pathway of fungi, and their flagella are those of the stramenopiles. The resemblance is convergence, and it has practical consequences: many fungicides do not work against oomycetes.

Many oomycetes are plant pathogens. *Phytophthora infestans* causes late blight of potato and tomato; its arrival in Europe in 1845 destroyed the potato crops on which the Irish poor depended, and the famine of 1845–1849 killed about a million people and drove a similar number to emigrate. *Plasmopara viticola*, the agent of downy mildew of grapevines, prompted the first widely used fungicide, the copper-based Bordeaux mixture, in the 1880s. *Phytophthora* secretes effector proteins into the plant cell, many with a conserved RXLR motif needed for their entry, which suppress plant immunity; the arms race with plant resistance genes is concentrated in fast-evolving, repeat-rich regions of its genome. *Saprolegnia* and its relatives infect fish and their eggs.

## 20.6. Rhizaria

The Rhizaria are amoebae and amoeboflagellates, most with thin, branching or reticulate pseudopodia, which were united only by molecular phylogenies. The two best-known groups build mineral skeletons.

**Definition 20.6.1 (Foraminifera)** The **foraminifera** are rhizarian protists with a network of fine, branching and fusing pseudopodia (granuloreticulopodia) that stream out through openings in a shell, the *test*, of one or many chambers, built of organic material, of agglutinated mineral particles or, most commonly, of calcium carbonate.

Most foraminifera are benthic, a few planktonic, such as *Globigerina*. The pseudopodia catch bacteria, algae and detritus, and many planktonic and reef-dwelling species carry symbiotic algae. Foraminifera have an excellent fossil record since the Cambrian. Tests of planktonic species cover much of the deep-sea floor as globigerina ooze; the large benthic *Nummulites* of the Eocene formed limestones, among them the stone of the pyramids of Giza; and because the ratio of the oxygen isotopes $^{18}\mathrm{O}/^{16}\mathrm{O}$ in a calcite test depends on the temperature of the water in which it grew and on the volume of ice on land, foraminifera in sediment cores are the main record of past ocean temperatures and glaciations. Their rapid succession of species makes them markers for dating strata in oil exploration.

The radiolarians are planktonic marine amoebae with long, stiff pseudopodia (axopodia) supported by microtubules, radiating from a central capsule. Most (the polycystines) build intricate skeletons of silica, which accumulate as radiolarian ooze and, over geological time, as chert; the acantharians make their spines of strontium sulfate. Many carry algal symbionts. The chlorarachniophytes, such as *Bigelowiella natans*, are small amoeboflagellates with a green-algal plastid acquired by secondary endosymbiosis; between the second and third of its four membranes lies the nucleomorph, a relict nucleus of the engulfed alga with a genome of less than half a megabase, the smallest nuclear genome known. The Rhizaria also include *Plasmodiophora brassicae*, which causes clubroot of cabbage and its relatives, and *Paulinella*, which acquired a second, independent primary plastid from a cyanobacterium ([7.3.12](/7.3.12#sec-12.8.4)).

## 20.7. Amorphea: Amoebozoa and Obazoa

The Amorphea contain two major clades: the Amoebozoa and the Obazoa, to which animals, fungi and their unicellular relatives belong. The choanoflagellates, the closest living relatives of animals, and the origin of animal multicellularity are discussed in [7.10.28](/7.10.28#sec-28.5); the fungi follow in [7.11.21](/7.11.21).

The Amoebozoa are amoebae with broad, lobe-shaped pseudopodia. Free-living lobose amoebae such as *Amoeba proteus*, up to about half a millimeter long, move by the protrusion of pseudopodia driven by actin polymerization and by the contraction of the cortex at the rear, which pushes the fluid endoplasm forward, and feed by **phagocytosis** of bacteria, algae and other protists. *Acanthamoeba*, common in soil and tap water, causes keratitis in wearers of contact lenses and, rarely, encephalitis; its trophozoites and cysts harbor bacteria such as *Legionella*, which survive digestion by the amoeba and, by the same means, inside human macrophages ([7.11.34](/7.11.34)).

*Entamoeba histolytica*, an anaerobe with mitosomes, causes amoebic dysentery. It is transmitted by cysts with four nuclei in fecally contaminated food and water; the trophozoites live in the large intestine, where they can kill epithelial cells on contact, ingest them and ulcerate the mucosa, and they can spread through the portal vein to form abscesses in the liver. Most people who carry *Entamoeba* cysts are infected by *E. dispar*, which is microscopically identical and harmless, so that diagnosis requires antigen or DNA tests. Amoebiasis kills tens of thousands of people a year, mostly where sanitation is poor.

**Definition 20.7.1 (Slime Mold)** A **slime mold** is an amoeboid protist that, when food runs out, forms a stalked or sessile fruiting body bearing spores. In the *plasmodial* (acellular) slime molds (Myxogastria) the feeding stage is a plasmodium, a single multinucleate mass of cytoplasm; in the *cellular* slime molds (Dictyostelia) it consists of separate amoebae that aggregate into a multicellular body only to form the **fruiting body**.

*Dictyostelium discoideum*, the best-known cellular slime mold, aggregates in response to waves of **cyclic AMP**; its life cycle, the regulation of the proportions of stalk and spore cells, and the mathematics of chemotactic aggregation are the subject of [7.5.13](/7.5.13). It also has a sexual cycle, in which two amoebae of different mating type fuse in an aggregate into a giant cell, which engulfs its neighbors and forms a thick-walled macrocyst; the diploid nucleus undergoes meiosis, and the macrocyst releases haploid amoebae.

The plasmodium of *Physarum polycephalum* can grow to tens of centimeters, a yellow network of veins in which the cytoplasm streams back and forth every minute or two (shuttle streaming), driven by rhythmic actomyosin contraction. All its diploid nuclei divide synchronously, which once made it a favorite material for the study of the cell cycle. The network optimizes its own geometry: veins that carry much flow thicken and the others shrink, so that a plasmodium given food at two points finds the shortest path between them through a maze, and one given food at points arranged like the cities around Tokyo builds a network comparable in efficiency and robustness to the railway system. When starved or dried, the plasmodium forms a hard, dormant sclerotium, or sporangia in which meiosis produces haploid spores; these germinate as amoebae or flagellated swarm cells that act as gametes and fuse into a new diploid plasmodium.

## 20.8. Archaeplastida

The Archaeplastida — glaucophytes, red algae, green algae and land plants — share the primary plastid. In the small group of freshwater glaucophytes, such as *Cyanophora paradoxa*, the plastid still has a layer of **peptidoglycan** between its membranes, a remnant of the cyanobacterial wall.

### 20.8.1. Red Algae

The red algae (Rhodophyta) are mostly marine and multicellular, with a few unicellular species in hot springs and soils. Their plastids contain chlorophyll *a* only, and harvest light with **phycobilisomes** on the surface of unstacked thylakoids, as in **cyanobacteria**: the red **accessory pigment** phycoerythrin absorbs the green and blue light that penetrates deepest into the sea, and red algae include the deepest-growing photosynthetic eukaryotes. They store floridean starch in the cytoplasm, and they have no flagella, centrioles or basal bodies at any stage of their life cycle, so their gametes are passive.

Many red algae have a *triphasic* life cycle. In *Polysiphonia*, haploid male and female plants (gametophytes) are formed; nonmotile male cells (spermatia) carried by water fertilize the female organ (carpogonium) on the female plant, and the zygote develops, while remaining attached to and nourished by the female, into a small diploid carposporophyte; its carpospores grow into a free-living diploid tetrasporophyte, which resembles the gametophytes, and meiosis in it produces tetraspores that grow into new gametophytes. The cultivation of nori (*Pyropia*, formerly included in *Porphyra*) became possible after the British phycologist Kathleen Drew-Baker showed in 1949 that a small filamentous alga living in shells, the conchocelis, is a phase of its life cycle. Red algae are the sources of agar and carrageenans, polysaccharides of their walls; coralline red algae deposit calcium carbonate and cement coral reefs. The unicellular Cyanidiales, such as *Galdieria sulphuraria* and *Cyanidioschyzon merolae*, live in acidic hot springs at pH 0.5–4 and up to about 56 °C; *Galdieria* can also grow heterotrophically in the dark, and both carry genes acquired by **horizontal gene transfer** from bacteria and archaea that help them tolerate heat, acid and metals.

### 20.8.2. Green Algae

The green algae have chlorophylls *a* and *b*, store starch inside the plastid and form two clades. The Chlorophyta include most green algae of the sea and of fresh water; the Streptophyta include the charophyte algae and the land plants, which evolved from within them. Phylogenomic studies place the conjugating algae (Zygnematophyceae: *Spirogyra*, the desmids) as the closest living relatives of land plants, so that the colonization of land started from freshwater algae of a group whose living members are small and simple ([7.12](/7.12)).

*Chlamydomonas reinhardtii*, a unicellular flagellate with two anterior flagella, a single cup-shaped plastid with a pyrenoid and an eyespot, is a model organism for photosynthesis, for the assembly and function of cilia — intraflagellar transport was discovered in its flagella — and for light sensing: the channelrhodopsins of its eyespot, light-gated ion channels, became the main tool of optogenetics. It is haploid, with two mating types; the zygote forms a resistant wall and undergoes meiosis on germination (zygotic meiosis), giving tetrads that can be analyzed genetically ([7.4.9](/7.4.9)). Its colonial relatives, from *Gonium* to *Volvox*, form a series from unicells to organisms with a division of labor between somatic and reproductive cells, a model for the evolution of **multicellularity** ([7.10.28](/7.10.28#sec-28.5.2)).

Other green algae show the range of the group: *Ostreococcus tauri*, about a micrometer across, with a genome of about 13 Mbp, is the smallest free-living eukaryote known and is common in the marine picoplankton; *Dunaliella* grows in brines and accumulates β-carotene; snow algae color snowfields red with carotenoids; the sea lettuce *Ulva* has an alternation of identical-looking haploid and diploid generations; *Trebouxia* is the commonest photosynthetic partner of lichens ([7.11.21](/7.11.21)); and *Botryococcus braunii* secretes long-chain hydrocarbons that make up much of its dry weight and have contributed to some oil shales.

## 20.9. Haptista and Cryptista

The haptophytes are mostly marine flagellates named for the *haptonema*, a thread-like appendage between the two flagella, used for attachment and the capture of food. Their plastids are of red-algal origin. The coccolithophores, such as *Emiliania huxleyi* (transferred to *Gephyrocapsa* in 2023), cover themselves with *coccoliths*, ornate plates of calcite formed in a Golgi-derived vesicle and secreted to the surface; their blooms turn hundreds of thousands of square kilometers of ocean milky and are visible from space. Coccoliths accumulated through the Mesozoic make up most of the chalk of the Cretaceous, such as the White Cliffs of Dover. Coccolithophores affect climate in two ways: calcification removes carbonate from surface water and the sinking coccoliths carry carbon to the sediments, and many haptophytes make dimethylsulfoniopropionate, whose breakdown product, dimethyl sulfide, escapes to the atmosphere and seeds clouds. *Phaeocystis* forms large colonial blooms, and *Prymnesium parvum* kills fish in brackish waters.

The cryptophytes (Cryptista) are small flagellates with ejectile organelles (ejectisomes) and a red-algal plastid in which the phycobiliproteins lie inside the thylakoid lumen; like the chlorarachniophytes, they keep the nucleomorph of the engulfed alga, so that a cell of *Guillardia theta* contains four genomes: those of the host nucleus, the host mitochondrion, the nucleomorph and the plastid (the mitochondrion of the engulfed alga has been lost). Cryptophytes are prey of the ciliate *Mesodinium rubrum*, which keeps their plastids, and even their nuclei, working for weeks inside its own cell.

## 20.10. Protists in Ecology and Disease

### 20.10.1. Ecology

Protists occupy every trophic position in microbial communities.

- Primary producers: the ocean accounts for about half of the net primary production of the Earth, and most of it is by microscopic phytoplankton: cyanobacteria, diatoms, dinoflagellates, haptophytes and small green algae, whose relative importance depends on nutrients and temperature ([7.11.29](/7.11.29)). Because the phytoplankton is grazed within days, its standing biomass is tiny compared with that of land plants, but it turns over hundreds of times faster.
- Grazers: heterotrophic flagellates and ciliates are the main consumers of bacteria and picophytoplankton in water and soil, removing a large fraction of the bacterial production each day. They return dissolved organic matter and nutrients to the water and pass carbon to larger animals, the core of the **microbial loop** ([7.11.29](/7.11.29)). Grazing also selects bacterial traits: filaments, aggregates, fast swimming and toxins all protect bacteria against protists, and some of the same defenses later serve bacteria against macrophages.
- Symbionts: protists form symbioses with animals (zooxanthellae in corals, parabasalids in termites, ciliates in the rumen), with bacteria and archaea (endosymbionts of ciliates and amoebae), and with fungi (algae in lichens). Endosymbiosis, from transient associations to the permanent organelles of [7.10.28](/7.10.28), is a recurrent theme of protist evolution.
- Blooms: nutrient enrichment of coastal waters (**eutrophication**) increases the frequency of harmful blooms of dinoflagellates, diatoms and haptophytes, which poison shellfish and fish or deplete oxygen when they decay.

### 20.10.2. Protozoan Diseases of Humans

The protozoan parasites of humans belong to four supergroups: the diseases are united by their effects, not by descent. Many are transmitted by insect vectors or by resistant cysts and oocysts in food and water, so their burden falls overwhelmingly on the tropics and on places without safe water. Because the parasites are eukaryotes, drugs that are selective for them are few and often toxic ([7.11.14](/7.11.14#sec-14.10.2)), and no vaccine is available against any protozoan disease except malaria. ## Summary

- "Protist" (Definition 20.1.1), "protozoa" and "algae" are names of grade, not of clades: protists occur in every supergroup, and photosynthesis, phagotrophy, amoeboid form and parasitism are scattered across the tree. Mitochondria have been reduced to hydrogenosomes or mitosomes in several anaerobic lineages; plastids spread by secondary and tertiary endosymbiosis and were lost or reduced repeatedly; cell coverings include pellicles, alveoli, cellulose, silica and calcium carbonate.
- Discoba: euglenids (pellicle, eyespot, green secondary plastid, mixotrophy); kinetoplastids, with the kinetoplast (Definition 20.2.1), guide-RNA editing, polycistronic transcription with trans-splicing and glycosomes — *Trypanosoma brucei* (VSG coat and antigenic variation), *T. cruzi*, *Leishmania*; *Naegleria*, an amoeboflagellate.
- Metamonada: *Giardia* (two nuclei, adhesive disc, mitosomes, cysts) and *Trichomonas* (hydrogenosomes, no cyst, sexual transmission); termite parabasalids digest wood.
- Alveolata: ciliates (Definition 20.4.1) separate germline and soma in two nuclei, exchange pronuclei in conjugation and rebuild the macronucleus by programmed DNA elimination; *Tetrahymena* yielded telomerase and ribozymes. Dinoflagellates (Definition 20.4.2) have a dinokaryon, cause red tides and shellfish poisoning, glow and live as zooxanthellae in corals. Apicomplexa (Definition 20.4.3) invade cells with the apical complex and keep a relict plastid; *Plasmodium* alternates between human liver and blood and the mosquito (Figure 4).
- Stramenopiles: diatoms (Definition 20.5.1) in silica frustules, which shrink by the MacDonald–Pfitzer rule (Formula 20.5.1) until an auxospore restores their size, fix about a fifth of the Earth's carbon; brown algae include kelps; oomycetes (Definition 20.5.2), fungus-like but with cellulose walls and diploid hyphae, include *Phytophthora infestans*.
- Rhizaria: foraminifera (Definition 20.6.1) and radiolarians, whose calcite and silica skeletons record the history of the oceans; chlorarachniophytes with a nucleomorph.
- Amoebozoa: lobose amoebae, *Entamoeba histolytica*, and the slime molds (Definition 20.7.1), cellular (*Dictyostelium*, [7.5.13](/7.5.13)) and plasmodial (*Physarum*).
- Archaeplastida: glaucophytes with peptidoglycan in the plastid; red algae with phycobilisomes, no flagella and triphasic life cycles; green algae, from *Chlamydomonas* and *Volvox* to the charophytes from which land plants arose.
- Haptista and Cryptista: coccolithophores, whose coccoliths form chalk; cryptophytes with a nucleomorph.
- Protists are primary producers, the main grazers of bacteria and partners in many symbioses; the protozoan diseases of humans are caused by members of four supergroups and are concentrated in the tropics.

## Questions

1. Why is "protozoa" not a natural group, and why do *Euglena* and *Trypanosoma*, one photosynthetic and one parasitic, belong to the same clade while *Euglena* and *Chlamydomonas*, both green flagellates, do not?

   **Answer** "Protozoa" groups organisms by a way of life — motile and heterotrophic — that evolved many times and is shared with photosynthetic relatives, so it is a grade spread over several supergroups. *Euglena* and *Trypanosoma* are both euglenozoans (Discoba), as shown by phylogenomics and shared features such as the structure of their flagella and mitochondria and trans-splicing; *Euglena*'s plastid was acquired from a green alga by secondary endosymbiosis (it has three membranes). *Chlamydomonas* is itself a green alga with a primary plastid (two membranes) in the Archaeplastida. The similarity of *Euglena* and *Chlamydomonas* is in the plastid they share by descent from green algae, not in the host cells.

2. A trypanosome expresses one VSG at a time from a repertoire of more than a thousand. Why would expressing several at once, or switching very often, be disadvantageous for the parasite?

   **Answer** Antibodies raised against any VSG on the surface would clear every cell displaying it, so a cell with several VSGs would be vulnerable to the antibodies against each; expressing one at a time means that only the cells with the current variant are removed while rare switched cells survive. If switching were very frequent, many variants would be present at once and the host would raise antibodies against many of them early, using up the repertoire quickly; a low switching rate spreads the variants over time and prolongs the infection, which increases the chance of transmission to a tsetse fly.

3. In a ciliate, the two partners of a conjugation end up with identical genotypes. Explain why, and say what conjugation achieves if it produces no new cells.

   **Answer** Each partner keeps one haploid meiotic product, divides it into two identical pronuclei and gives one away; each then fuses its own stationary pronucleus with the migratory pronucleus received from the other. Both zygotic nuclei therefore contain the same two haploid genomes. Conjugation achieves recombination — meiosis reshuffles each parent's genome and the zygote combines genomes of two cells — and renews the macronucleus, which in many ciliates degenerates after a limited number of asexual divisions because its amitotic division distributes chromosomes unequally.

4. A clone of a diatom starts with cells 80 µm across. The smaller daughter of each division is 0.8 µm narrower, and cells become sexual below 30 µm. Estimate after how many generations a substantial fraction of the clone can reproduce sexually, and why the answer is not simply (80 − 30)/0.8.

   **Answer** By Formula 20.5.1 the mean diameter after $g$ generations is $80 - 0.4g$, which reaches 30 µm at $g = 125$. The smallest cells, those that inherited the hypotheca at every division, reach 30 µm after $(80-30)/0.8 = 62.5$ divisions, but they are a fraction $2^{-62}$ of the clone; the distribution has standard deviation $\sqrt{g}\,d/2 \approx 4.5$ µm at $g = 125$, so about half of the cells are below the threshold after roughly 125 generations and about a tenth after about 110. In practice growth is not synchronous and the reduction per division varies, but the timescale — months to years at one division a day — is right.

5. *Plasmodium falciparum* is haploid for almost all of its life cycle. Where does meiosis occur, and why does this make it possible for resistance mutations to be selected quickly in the human host?

   **Answer** Meiosis occurs in the mosquito, in the zygote–ookinete–oocyst stage, immediately after fertilization (zygotic meiosis); the zygote is the only diploid stage. In the human host all multiplication is mitotic in haploid cells, so a new mutation conferring drug resistance is expressed at once, without being masked by a dominant susceptible allele, and the enormous number of parasites in the blood ($10^{11}$ or more in a severe infection) makes such mutations likely to be present. Sexual recombination in the mosquito can then combine or break apart resistance alleles at different loci.
