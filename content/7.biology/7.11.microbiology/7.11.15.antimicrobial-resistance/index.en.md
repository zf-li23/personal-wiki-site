Every antibiotic introduced so far has been followed, sooner or later, by resistance. Resistance is evolution by natural selection on a time scale of years, and it is now among the leading causes of death worldwide. This chapter describes how bacteria withstand the drugs of [7.11.14](/7.11.14), where the resistance genes come from and how they spread, and what can be done. Its biochemical part follows the targets of the previous chapter; its genetic part uses the mobile elements of [7.11.13](/7.11.13) and the mechanisms of **horizontal gene transfer** ([7.4.10](/7.4.10)); the population dynamics are those of selection ([7.4.23](/7.4.23#sec-23.3), [7.10.5](/7.10.5)), and the social and evolutionary aspects are summarized in [7.10.34](/7.10.34#sec-34.6.5). Biofilms, which shelter bacteria from drugs, are described in [7.11.12](/7.11.12), and phages as an alternative therapy in [7.11.23](/7.11.23).

## 15.1. The Resistance Crisis

### 15.1.1. Burden

**Definition 15.1.1 (Antimicrobial Resistance)** **Antimicrobial resistance** (AMR) is the ability of a microorganism to grow, or to survive, in the presence of an antimicrobial drug at concentrations that inhibit or kill typical members of its species. Clinically, a strain is resistant when its MIC is above the clinical breakpoint, so that treatment at the usual dose is likely to fail ([7.11.14](/7.11.14#sec-14.3.3)); microbiologically, when its MIC is above the epidemiological cutoff of the wild type, whatever its clinical consequence.

The most complete estimate of the burden, from the Global Research on Antimicrobial Resistance (GRAM) project, is that in 2019 bacterial AMR was directly responsible for about 1.27 million deaths and was associated with 4.95 million, more than HIV/AIDS or malaria (figure below, panel A). Six pathogens, *Escherichia coli*, *Staphylococcus aureus*, *Klebsiella pneumoniae*, *Streptococcus pneumoniae*, *Acinetobacter baumannii* and *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*, accounted for most of these deaths; rates were highest in sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia, where access to second-line drugs, diagnostics and sanitation is poorest. A later analysis by the same group projected about 1.9 million attributable deaths per year by 2050 if present trends continue.

The pattern over time is equally clear (panel B). Resistance to penicillin, through the penicillinase of staphylococci, was known before the drug came into general use: in 1940 Abraham and Chain had already described a bacterial enzyme that destroys penicillin. Methicillin, introduced in 1959 against penicillinase-producing *S. aureus*, met methicillin-resistant strains within two years, and for the most recent drugs resistant isolates have appeared within one or two years of introduction.

### 15.1.2. Priority Pathogens

Two lists guide research and surveillance. The acronym *ESKAPE* (Rice, 2008) names the pathogens that most often cause resistant hospital infections and "escape" treatment: *Enterococcus faecium*, *S. aureus*, *K. pneumoniae*, *A. baumannii*, *P. aeruginosa* and *Enterobacter* species. The World Health Organization's bacterial priority pathogens list, first published in 2017 and updated in 2024, ranks pathogen–resistance combinations for the development of new drugs:

| Priority (2024) | Pathogens |
|---|---|
| Critical | carbapenem-resistant *A. baumannii*; Enterobacterales resistant to third-generation cephalosporins or to carbapenems; rifampicin-resistant *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* |
| High | fluoroquinolone-resistant *Salmonella* Typhi, *Shigella* and nontyphoidal *Salmonella*; vancomycin-resistant *E. faecium*; carbapenem-resistant *P. aeruginosa*; *Neisseria gonorrhoeae* resistant to third-generation cephalosporins or fluoroquinolones; methicillin-resistant *S. aureus* |
| Medium | macrolide-resistant group A streptococci and *S. pneumoniae*; ampicillin-resistant *Haemophilus influenzae*; penicillin-resistant group B streptococci |

Gram-negative bacteria dominate the critical group: their outer membrane and efflux pumps make them intrinsically resistant to many drugs (Section 15.2), and their plasmids carry carbapenemases. The treatment of gonorrhea shows the pace of the problem: penicillin, used into the 1980s, was replaced by ciprofloxacin, which lost much of its efficacy within about ten years and was replaced by ceftriaxone, to which strains with reduced susceptibility are now spreading.

### 15.1.3. Multidrug Resistance

The words "multidrug-resistant" and "superbug" were long used loosely. An international expert group (Magiorakos and colleagues, 2012) proposed standard definitions, now widely used, for *S. aureus*, enterococci, Enterobacteriaceae, *P. aeruginosa* and *Acinetobacter*. They count *antimicrobial categories* (groups of drugs with a common mechanism, such as the aminoglycosides, the carbapenems or the fluoroquinolones), listed separately for each organism, and *non-susceptibility* (resistant or intermediate) to at least one agent of a category, leaving out categories to which the species is intrinsically resistant.

**Definition 15.1.2 (MDR, XDR and PDR)** A bacterial isolate shows **multidrug resistance** (MDR) if it is non-susceptible to at least one agent in three or more antimicrobial categories; *extensive drug resistance* (XDR) if it is non-susceptible to at least one agent in all but two or fewer categories, that is, if it remains susceptible to only one or two categories; and *pandrug resistance* (PDR) if it is non-susceptible to all agents of all categories.

XDR is thus a subset of MDR, and PDR of XDR. Tuberculosis has its own definitions (WHO, revised in 2021): MDR-TB is resistant to at least isoniazid and rifampicin; pre-XDR-TB is MDR-TB that is also resistant to a fluoroquinolone; XDR-TB is pre-XDR-TB that is also resistant to bedaquiline or linezolid.

## 15.2. Intrinsic and Acquired Resistance

**Definition 15.2.1 (Intrinsic Resistance)** **Intrinsic resistance** is resistance shared by all, or nearly all, wild-type members of a species as a consequence of its structure or physiology, as opposed to *acquired resistance*, which a previously susceptible strain obtains by mutation or by horizontal gene transfer.

Examples of intrinsic resistance follow from the drug mechanisms of [7.11.14](/7.11.14): Gram-negative bacteria are intrinsically resistant to vancomycin, which cannot cross the **outer membrane**; mycoplasmas, which have no cell wall, to all β-lactams; anaerobes to aminoglycosides, whose uptake needs a large membrane potential; enterococci to cephalosporins, because of their low-affinity PBP5; and *P. aeruginosa*, whose outer membrane is far less permeable than that of *E. coli* and which expresses several multidrug efflux pumps, to many drugs at once.

Intrinsic resistance limits the spectrum of a drug from the start; acquired resistance takes away drugs that once worked. It arises in two ways. Mutation changes a gene the bacterium already has, typically the target or a regulator of permeability or efflux; it occurs at rates of about $10^{-6}$ to $10^{-9}$ per cell per generation, depending on how many different mutations confer resistance ([7.6.41](/7.6.41#sec-41.5.1)), and is the main route for drugs with a single chromosomal target, such as rifampicin and the fluoroquinolones. *Gene acquisition* by conjugation, transformation or transduction brings in a resistance gene from another bacterium, often of another species, and is the main route for β-lactamases, aminoglycoside-modifying enzymes and many others.

## 15.3. Biochemical Mechanisms of Resistance

Resistance mechanisms fall into a few classes (figure below): the drug is destroyed or chemically modified; its target is altered, protected or replaced; or the drug is kept out of the cell or pumped out of it. A single strain often combines several.

### 15.3.1. Inactivating the Drug

**Definition 15.3.1 (β-Lactamase)** A **β-lactamase** is an enzyme that hydrolyzes the amide bond of the β-lactam ring and thereby inactivates β-lactam antibiotics. An **extended-spectrum β-lactamase** (ESBL) hydrolyzes, besides the penicillins, the third-generation cephalosporins and the monobactams (but not the carbapenems or cephamycins) and is inhibited by clavulanic acid; a *carbapenemase* hydrolyzes carbapenems as well.

β-Lactamases are the most important resistance enzymes, with several thousand variants known. They are grouped by sequence into four Ambler classes. Classes A, C and D are serine enzymes related to the PBPs: they form the same acyl-enzyme with the drug, but hydrolyze it within milliseconds instead of hours, so that one enzyme molecule destroys many drug molecules. Class B are *metallo-β-lactamases*, which use one or two $\ce{Zn^2+}$ ions to activate a water molecule and are not inhibited by the classic inhibitors.

| Class | Active site | Examples | Substrates |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | serine | TEM-1, SHV-1 (penicillinases); CTX-M and TEM/SHV variants (ESBLs); KPC (carbapenemase) | penicillins; ESBLs add cephalosporins; KPC nearly all β-lactams |
| B | $\ce{Zn^2+}$ | NDM, VIM, IMP | all β-lactams except the monobactams |
| C | serine | AmpC (chromosomal in *Enterobacter*, *Citrobacter*, *P. aeruginosa*; plasmid-borne CMY) | cephalosporins, cephamycins |
| D | serine | OXA enzymes, among them the carbapenemases OXA-48 and OXA-23 | variable; OXA-23 in *A. baumannii* |

The history of the plasmid-borne enzymes follows the history of drug use. TEM-1, first reported in 1965 from an isolate in Greece (named after the patient, Temoniera), spread worldwide in Gram-negative bacteria on plasmids and transposons. The third-generation cephalosporins introduced in the 1980s were stable to TEM-1, and within a few years point mutants of TEM and SHV with an enlarged active site, the first ESBLs, hydrolyzed them; since about 2000 the CTX-M family, mobilized from the chromosome of environmental *Kluyvera* species, has become the commonest ESBL worldwide. Use of carbapenems against ESBL producers in turn selected carbapenemases: KPC, found in *K. pneumoniae* in the United States in 1996, NDM, recognized in 2008 in a patient who had been treated in India, and OXA-48. A quick test for β-lactamase activity uses nitrocefin, a chromogenic cephalosporin that turns from yellow to red when its β-lactam ring is opened.

Other enzymes modify rather than cleave. The aminoglycosides, whose many exposed amino and hydroxyl groups are easy to derivatize, are attacked by more than a hundred acetyltransferases (AAC), phosphotransferases (APH) and nucleotidyltransferases (ANT), mostly carried on mobile elements and probably derived from the self-protection genes of aminoglycoside producers; the modified drug no longer binds the ribosome. Chloramphenicol is acetylated by chloramphenicol acetyltransferase, and the flavin monooxygenase Tet(X) oxidizes tetracyclines, tigecycline included.

### 15.3.2. Altering the Target

When the target is changed, the drug no longer binds, but the target must keep working. Target alterations arise by mutation of the target gene, by enzymatic modification of the target, or by acquisition of an alternative, insensitive target.

- *PBP2a and MRSA.* *Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus* (MRSA) carries *mecA* (rarely *mecC*), encoding PBP2a, a transpeptidase with very low affinity for nearly all β-lactams, which takes over cross-linking when the normal PBPs are inhibited. The gene lies on a mobile genomic island, the staphylococcal cassette chromosome *mec* (SCC*mec*), inserted at a specific chromosomal site; it is induced by β-lactams through the sensor MecR1, which on binding a β-lactam cleaves the repressor MecI. MRSA arose first in hospitals, then, from the 1990s, as distinct community-associated lineages such as USA300, and in livestock. Ceftaroline and ceftobiprole bind PBP2a and are the only β-lactams active against MRSA.
- *Ribosome methylation.* Erm methyltransferases dimethylate adenine A2058 of 23S rRNA, in the macrolide binding site of the exit tunnel; the modification also prevents binding of lincosamides and streptogramin B, which share the site (the MLS$_{\mathrm{B}}$ phenotype). Other rRNA methyltransferases confer resistance to aminoglycosides (ArmA, RmtB, acting on 16S rRNA) and to linezolid and chloramphenicol (Cfr).
- *vanA and vancomycin resistance.* The *vanA* operon reprograms the end of the peptidoglycan precursor: VanH reduces pyruvate to D-lactate, the ligase VanA joins D-alanine to D-lactate, and the dipeptidase VanX destroys the normal D-Ala–D-Ala made by the host ligase. The D-Ala–D-Lac terminus has an ester oxygen where the amide NH was, which removes one hydrogen bond to vancomycin and adds a repulsion; the affinity falls about a thousandfold. The two-component system VanS–VanR induces the operon only when the drug is present, because the altered precursor is a burden otherwise. Vancomycin-resistant enterococci appeared in 1986–1988 in Europe and the United States; the first vancomycin-resistant *S. aureus*, in 2002, had acquired *vanA* on the transposon Tn*1546* from an enterococcus in the same patient.
- *Mutated targets of synthetic drugs.* Fluoroquinolone resistance arises by stepwise mutations in the quinolone resistance-determining region of *gyrA* (in *E. coli* typically at Ser83 and Asp87 of GyrA) and of *parC*: a first mutation in the primary target raises the MIC severalfold, a second in the other enzyme raises it much further, and highly resistant clinical isolates carry three or four. Rifampicin resistance arises by single mutations in a short region of *rpoB*.
- *mcr-1 and colistin.* Colistin binds the negatively charged phosphates of lipid A ([7.11.14](/7.11.14#sec-14.8.1)). Chromosomal mutations in the two-component systems PmrAB and PhoPQ, or in their negative regulator MgrB in *K. pneumoniae*, lead to the addition of positively charged 4-amino-4-deoxy-L-arabinose and phosphoethanolamine to lipid A. In 2015 a plasmid-borne phosphoethanolamine transferase gene, *mcr-1* ("mobilized colistin resistance"), was found in *E. coli* from pigs and patients in China, where colistin had been used as a growth promoter in livestock; it was soon reported worldwide.

### 15.3.3. Protecting the Target

Some resistance proteins neither change nor destroy the drug but release it from its target. The ribosome protection proteins Tet(M) and Tet(O), relatives of the elongation factor EF-G, bind the tetracycline-stalled ribosome and, using GTP, dislodge the drug; *tet(M)*, carried by the broad-host-range conjugative transposon Tn*916*, is among the most widespread resistance genes. The plasmid-borne Qnr proteins, built of pentapeptide repeats that mimic the shape and charge of DNA, bind gyrase and topoisomerase IV and reduce quinolone binding; they give low-level resistance, which facilitates the selection of target mutations.

### 15.3.4. Keeping the Drug Out

**Definition 15.3.2 (Efflux Pump)** An **efflux pump** is a membrane transport protein, or protein complex, that exports antibiotics and other toxic compounds from the cell at the expense of ATP or of an ion gradient. *Multidrug efflux pumps* recognize many structurally unrelated substrates.

Efflux pumps belong to six families: the ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporters and five families of secondary transporters driven by the proton or sodium gradient, namely the major facilitator superfamily (MFS), the resistance–nodulation–division (RND) family, the multidrug and toxic compound extrusion (MATE) family, the small multidrug resistance (SMR) family and the proteobacterial antimicrobial compound efflux (PACE) family, described in 2015. Some are specific, such as the MFS tetracycline pump Tet(A) of the transposon Tn*10*; others, above all the RND pumps of Gram-negative bacteria, export bile salts, detergents, dyes and antibiotics of many classes. The best-studied is AcrAB–TolC of *E. coli*: the RND transporter AcrB in the inner membrane, a trimer that captures drugs from the periplasm and from the outer leaflet of the inner membrane and cycles through three conformations powered by proton influx; the periplasmic adaptor AcrA; and the outer membrane channel TolC, the three together spanning the whole envelope. Such pumps evolved to protect enteric bacteria against bile and host defense molecules, and they are a main cause of intrinsic resistance; mutations in their regulators (MarR, AcrR) raise their expression and with it the MIC of many drugs at once.

The outer membrane and efflux work together: drugs that cross the membrane slowly through porins are pumped out faster than they leak in, so the two barriers in series multiply their effects. Loss or mutation of porins adds to resistance. *P. aeruginosa* that loses the porin OprD, through which carbapenems enter, becomes resistant to imipenem, and *K. pneumoniae* lacking OmpK35 and OmpK36 combines this with an ESBL or AmpC to resist carbapenems without any carbapenemase.

### 15.3.5. Bypass and Overproduction

A cell can also escape a drug by acquiring an insensitive version of the target enzyme, so that the pathway runs through it. Resistance to sulfonamides and trimethoprim is mostly of this kind: the plasmid genes *sul1*, *sul2* and *sul3* encode dihydropteroate synthases, and many *dfr* genes encode dihydrofolate reductases, that bind the drugs poorly. PBP2a of MRSA can be read the same way. Finally, *overproduction* of the target titrates the drug: promoter mutations that raise the amount of dihydrofolate reductase in *E. coli*, or of InhA in *M. tuberculosis* (isoniazid, [7.11.14](/7.11.14#sec-14.7.2)), raise the MIC.

## 15.4. Designing Around Resistance

The oldest response to resistance is to modify the drug. Methicillin (1959), with bulky methoxy groups beside the amide, was designed to fit the PBPs but not the staphylococcal penicillinase; amikacin lacks the sites attacked by several aminoglycoside-modifying enzymes; tigecycline binds the ribosome more tightly than older tetracyclines and is neither exported by Tet(A) nor released by Tet(M). The second response is to inhibit the resistance enzyme. *Clavulanic acid*, a β-lactam from *Streptomyces clavuligerus* with little antibacterial activity of its own, is a mechanism-based inactivator of class A β-lactamases: after acylating the active-site serine, its ring system fragments and the enzyme is trapped in a stable, inactive form.

```smiles
OC/C=C1\O[C@@H]2CC(=O)N2[C@H]1C(=O)O clavulanate
```

Amoxicillin–clavulanate, ampicillin–sulbactam and piperacillin–tazobactam combine a penicillin with such an inhibitor. These classic inhibitors do not act on class C enzymes, on KPC or on the metallo-β-lactamases. Newer inhibitors with other scaffolds, the diazabicyclooctanes avibactam and relebactam and the boronic acid vaborbactam, also inhibit KPC and AmpC, and avibactam inhibits OXA-48. For strains with a metallo-β-lactamase, which hydrolyzes everything except the monobactams, aztreonam is combined with avibactam, which protects it from the serine β-lactamases such strains usually carry as well (the combination was approved in Europe in 2024). Cefiderocol, a cephalosporin linked to a catechol siderophore, is carried into Gram-negative bacteria by their iron transport systems, a "Trojan horse" that bypasses the loss of porins. Resistant isolates have been reported within a few years of the introduction of each of these drugs.

## 15.5. Phenotypic Resistance: Persistence and Tolerance

### 15.5.1. Definitions

Not every survivor of treatment is resistant. A culture of genetically susceptible bacteria exposed to a high concentration of a bactericidal drug is usually not wiped out: a small fraction survives, and when the survivors are regrown, their progeny are as susceptible as the original culture. Joseph Bigger described this in 1944 for staphylococci treated with penicillin and called the survivors *persisters*. A consensus statement (Balaban and colleagues, 2019) fixed the terms now in use.

**Definition 15.5.1 (Tolerance and Persistence)** Resistance is the ability to grow at a high drug concentration and is measured by the MIC. **Antibiotic tolerance** is the ability of a whole population to survive a transient exposure to a bactericidal drug at concentrations above the MIC, without any change of the MIC; it is measured by the *minimum duration for killing* a given fraction of the population (for example $\mathrm{MDK}_{99}$ for $99\,\%$). Persistence is tolerance of a subpopulation: most cells are killed rapidly, while a small fraction of **persisters**, genetically identical to the rest, is killed much more slowly, so that the killing curve is biphasic.

Tolerance appears wherever bacteria grow slowly: in stationary phase, in nutrient-poor tissue, in abscesses and in **biofilms**, whose matrix also slows the penetration of drugs ([7.11.12](/7.11.12)). Tolerance and persistence explain why infections of implanted devices, of the lungs in cystic fibrosis (*P. aeruginosa*) and of tuberculosis granulomas relapse after treatment although the bacteria recovered are fully susceptible.

### 15.5.2. Biphasic Killing

**Formula 15.5.1 (Two-State Model of Persistence)** Let $n$ and $p$ be the numbers of normal cells and of persisters during exposure to a bactericidal drug, killed at rates $k_n \gg k_p$, with switching from normal to persister at rate $a$ and back at rate $b$ (Balaban and colleagues, 2004):

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}n}{\mathrm{d}t} = -(k_n + a)\,n + b\,p, \qquad \frac{\mathrm{d}p}{\mathrm{d}t} = a\,n - (b + k_p)\,p.
$$
If the switching rates are small compared with $k_n$, the total population is approximately

$$
N(t) \approx n_0\,\mathrm{e}^{-k_nt} + p_0\,\mathrm{e}^{-(b + k_p)t}, \tag{15.1}
$$
where $n_0$ and $p_0$ are the numbers of the two kinds of cell when the drug is added.

The first term dominates at first: the population falls steeply, with slope $-k_n$ on a logarithmic plot. Once it has fallen to the level of the persisters, the second term takes over and the curve bends to a much shallower slope, $-(b + k_p)$: persisters die slowly, and mostly after they have woken up and become normal cells. Extrapolating the second phase back to $t = 0$ gives $p_0$, the number of persisters present before treatment, typically $10^{-6}$ to $10^{-4}$ of an exponential culture and up to one per cent in stationary phase. The figure below compares the three responses and shows the test that distinguishes persistence from resistance: survivors that are regrown give the same biphasic curve again.

A: a resistant mutant grows despite the drug; a tolerant population is killed slowly but uniformly, with a long minimum duration for killing 99 % (MDK₉₉, dash-dotted lines); a susceptible population with persisters is killed rapidly at first (dotted: normal cells), then slowly, when only persisters remain (dashed). ### 15.5.3. How Cells Become Persisters

Balaban, Merrin, Chait, Kowalik and Leibler (2004) followed single cells of *E. coli* in microfluidic channels before, during and after treatment with ampicillin and showed that the persisters were cells that had already stopped growing when the drug was added: persistence is a pre-existing phenotypic switch, a form of **bet hedging** ([7.9.17](/7.9.17#sec-17.1.4)). They distinguished type I persisters, formed during passage through stationary phase, from type II persisters, formed continuously during growth. The mutant *hipA7*, isolated by Moyed and Bertrand in 1983, makes a thousand times more persisters; HipA is the toxin of a **toxin–antitoxin system**, a kinase that phosphorylates glutamyl-tRNA synthetase, halts translation and thereby triggers the **stringent response** with its alarmone **(p)ppGpp** ([7.2.10](/7.2.10)). Other routes into dormancy include a low ATP level, which in *S. aureus* predicts which cells survive (Conlon and colleagues, 2016), and stochastic variation in the expression of metabolic genes.

An influential model in which (p)ppGpp acted through about ten type II toxin–antitoxin modules to produce all persisters in *E. coli* has had to be revised: the key mutant strain turned out to carry an unintended lysogenic prophage, a central paper was retracted in 2018, and strains lacking all ten modules form persisters at normal frequencies. The current view is that persistence is a heterogeneous state of low metabolic activity that can be reached by many routes, not the product of one dedicated program.

Persistence also matters for the evolution of resistance. In laboratory evolution with repeated, intermittent treatment, *E. coli* first evolved tolerance (a longer lag, so that cells were dormant while the drug was present), and resistance mutations then arose more readily in the tolerant background, because more cells survived each round to supply them (Levin-Reisman and colleagues, 2017).

## 15.6. Origins and Spread of Resistance Genes

### 15.6.1. Resistance Is Ancient

Resistance genes did not arise in response to medicine. DNA extracted from permafrost sediments about $30\,000$ years old contained genes for resistance to β-lactams, tetracyclines and glycopeptides, among them a *vanHAX*-like operon whose product had the same structure and specificity as the modern enzyme (D'Costa and colleagues, 2011). Bacteria from Lechuguilla Cave in New Mexico, isolated from the surface for millions of years, were resistant to most clinical antibiotics, including synthetic ones (Bhullar and colleagues, 2012). The reason is that producers must protect themselves, so their biosynthetic clusters contain resistance genes (the *vanHAX* homologs of glycopeptide producers, rRNA methyltransferases in macrolide producers), and bacteria living beside them evolved resistance too. The set of all resistance genes in a community, including precursors that could become resistance genes after mobilization or mutation, is its *resistome* (Wright, 2007); soil and the gut hold the largest reservoirs, and metagenomic studies find the same gene families in soil bacteria and in clinical pathogens.

### 15.6.2. Mobilization, Transfer and Clonal Spread

In the late 1950s, during outbreaks of dysentery in Japan, microbiologists found *Shigella* strains resistant at once to sulfonamides, streptomycin, chloramphenicol and tetracycline and showed that the whole set of resistances could be transferred to *E. coli* by contact: they had discovered the **R plasmids** ([7.11.13](/7.11.13)). Their structure shows how multiple resistance accumulates. The early R plasmid R100 carries the tetracycline efflux gene *tetA* in the transposon Tn*10*, a chloramphenicol acetyltransferase gene in Tn*9*, and in the large transposon Tn*21* a sulfonamide resistance gene *sul1* and an aminoglycoside adenylyltransferase gene *aadA1*; most of the rest of the plasmid encodes conjugative transfer.

The genetic elements nest inside one another ([7.11.13](/7.11.13)). **Integrons**, above all those of class 1, capture promoterless **gene cassettes** at a specific site with an integrase and express them from a common promoter; more than a hundred resistance cassettes are known. Integrons sit on **transposons**, which move them between replicons, and these on conjugative **plasmids** and **integrative and conjugative elements**, which move them between cells. Insertion sequences such as IS*Ecp1* mobilize chromosomal genes of environmental bacteria and supply strong promoters, which is how the CTX-M enzymes left *Kluyvera*. Transfer crosses species and genus boundaries: *vanA* moved from enterococci to *S. aureus*, and the same *bla*$_{\mathrm{NDM-1}}$ gene is found in *E. coli*, *K. pneumoniae* and *A. baumannii*. Natural **transformation** spreads resistance among bacteria that take up DNA: penicillin resistance in *S. pneumoniae* and *N. gonorrhoeae* is due to mosaic PBP genes, in which segments from related commensal species have replaced parts of the original gene, lowering the affinity of the enzyme step by step.

Not all spread is by gene transfer. Much of the clinical burden comes from the *clonal expansion* of a few successful lineages that combine resistance with efficient transmission: *E. coli* sequence type ST131 with CTX-M-15, which has become a leading cause of urinary and bloodstream infections worldwide; *K. pneumoniae* ST258 with KPC; MRSA USA300; and the Beijing lineage of *M. tuberculosis*. Whole-genome sequencing of isolates, interpreted with phylogenetic methods ([7.8.6](/7.8.6)), distinguishes such transmission of a clone from independent acquisition of a gene, and has become a standard tool in hospital outbreak investigation ([7.11.16](/7.11.16)).

### 15.6.3. Fitness Costs, Compensation and Co-Selection

Resistance usually has a price. An altered target works less well (resistant RNA polymerases and ribosomes transcribe and translate more slowly), efflux pumps and resistance enzymes consume energy, and plasmids impose a burden. In a drug-free environment the resistant strain is therefore outcompeted, with a selection coefficient of a few per cent per generation; this is why resistance should decline when use of a drug stops, and in some countries it has. But the decline is often slow, for three reasons.

- *Compensatory evolution.* Secondary mutations elsewhere restore fitness without restoring susceptibility. In rifampicin-resistant *M. tuberculosis*, mutations in *rpoA* and *rpoC* compensate for the defective *rpoB*, and such strains are as transmissible as susceptible ones; compensated resistance is effectively irreversible ([7.10.5](/7.10.5)).
- *Co-selection.* Resistance genes travel together on one plasmid or integron, so the use of any one of the drugs selects for all of them. Genes for resistance to heavy metals (mercury, copper, zinc) and to biocides lie on the same elements, so metals in feed and disinfectants in cleaning agents select for antibiotic resistance as well.
- *Low cost to begin with.* Some resistance mechanisms are almost free. Among the possible *rpoB* mutations, those found in patients are the ones with the smallest cost, and the frequency distribution of mutations in clinical isolates is shaped as much by their fitness as by their effect on the MIC.

### 15.6.4. Selection Below the MIC and the Mutant Selection Window

Selection for resistance does not need a concentration that inhibits the wild type. Writing $\psi(c)$ for the net growth rate as a function of concentration ([7.11.14](/7.11.14#sec-14.3.4)), a resistant mutant is favoured whenever $\psi_{\mathrm{mut}}(c) > \psi_{\mathrm{wt}}(c)$. Without drug the mutant grows more slowly by its fitness cost; as the concentration rises, the wild type is slowed more, and the two curves cross.

**Definition 15.6.1 (Minimal Selective Concentration and Mutant Selection Window)** The *minimal selective concentration* (MSC) is the lowest concentration at which a resistant mutant has a higher net growth rate than the susceptible wild type; it lies below the MIC of the wild type. The *mutant prevention concentration* (MPC) is the lowest concentration that prevents the growth of the least susceptible single-step mutant present in a large population. The range between the MIC of the wild type and the MPC is the *mutant selection window*, in which the wild type is inhibited and resistant mutants are not (Drlica and Zhao, 2007).

Two conclusions follow. First, the very low antibiotic concentrations found in sewage, rivers, soil treated with manure and the tissues of treated animals, far below the MIC, are enough to enrich resistant bacteria: minimal selective concentrations measured for *E. coli* and *Salmonella* lie in the range of micrograms to nanograms per litre for several drugs (Gullberg and colleagues, 2011). Second, dosing matters: a regimen whose concentration stays in the window for long periods, because the dose is too low or doses are missed, selects resistance, whereas a regimen that keeps the concentration above the MPC, or a combination in which each drug covers the mutants of the other, does not. In spatially structured environments resistance can also evolve stepwise, as on the giant plate with bands of increasing drug concentration on which *E. coli* reached a thousandfold resistance in about ten days ([7.10.5](/7.10.5), [7.6.41](/7.6.41#sec-41.5.3)).

How long treatment should last has been re-examined in the same light. The traditional advice to "complete the course" was meant to prevent relapse and the selection of resistance in the pathogen; but for many infections the main reservoir under selection is the patient's own microbiota, and the longer the exposure, the stronger the selection there. Randomized trials have shown shorter courses to be as effective as longer ones for community-acquired pneumonia, urinary tract infections and several other conditions, and the advice now is to use the shortest course that is supported by evidence, which for tuberculosis is still months.

### 15.6.5. One Health: Agriculture and the Environment

Most antibiotics by mass are not used in human medicine. A large fraction of global production goes to food animals, historically as growth promoters at low doses in feed, a practice banned in the European Union in 2006 and restricted in the United States from 2017, and still widely as prophylaxis in intensive production. Fish farming adds antibiotics directly to water. Resistant bacteria and resistance genes reach people through food, direct contact and the environment: *mcr-1* and the fluoroquinolone resistance of *Campylobacter* are clear examples, and livestock-associated MRSA (clonal complex 398) colonizes pig farmers. Antibiotics and resistant bacteria also enter sewage from hospitals and households, and the effluent of antibiotic factories can contain milligrams per litre, far above any selective concentration. Because the same genes circulate among humans, animals and the environment, surveillance and control are organized under the *One Health* approach, which treats the three compartments as one system; wastewater surveillance has become a practical way to monitor resistance in a whole population ([7.11.30](/7.11.30)).

## 15.7. Responses

### 15.7.1. Using the Drugs We Have

*Antimicrobial stewardship* is the organized effort to improve how antibiotics are prescribed: treating only bacterial infections (a large share of prescriptions are written for viral respiratory illnesses), choosing a narrow-spectrum drug once the pathogen is known, using the right dose and route, and stopping as soon as the evidence allows. Stewardship programs in hospitals, with audit and feedback, restricted access to reserve drugs and pharmacist review, reduce consumption without worsening outcomes. The WHO AWaRe classification helps: the Access group (such as amoxicillin) should account for most use, the Watch group (fluoroquinolones, third-generation cephalosporins, macrolides) is restricted, and the Reserve group (colistin, linezolid, ceftazidime–avibactam) is for last-resort use.

*Infection prevention* removes the need for treatment: hand hygiene, isolation and decolonization of carriers, sterile technique and care of catheters in hospitals; clean water and sanitation in the community. *Vaccines* reduce resistance twice over, by preventing infections that would be treated and by removing the carriage of resistant strains: after the conjugate pneumococcal vaccines were introduced, infections with penicillin-resistant pneumococci of the vaccine serotypes fell sharply, and vaccination against influenza and rotavirus reduces the antibiotic prescriptions written for those illnesses. *Rapid diagnostics* let narrow treatment start sooner: molecular detection of resistance genes (*mecA*, *rpoB* mutations of *M. tuberculosis*), rapid phenotypic susceptibility testing from positive blood cultures and point-of-care tests that distinguish bacterial from viral infection ([7.11.16](/7.11.16)).

### 15.7.2. Finding New Drugs

The pipeline is thin, for the economic reasons discussed in [7.11.14](/7.11.14#sec-14.1.3), and most drugs that reach the market are new members of old classes. Four directions are being pursued.

- *Reviving old scaffolds.* New β-lactam–inhibitor combinations, new aminoglycosides (plazomicin), new tetracyclines (eravacycline) and new oxazolidinones are the main source of drugs against resistant Gram-negative bacteria today (Section 15.4).
- *Culturing the uncultured.* Most soil bacteria do not grow on laboratory media ([7.11.8](/7.11.8#sec-8.6.2)), and their biosynthetic clusters are an untapped source. The *iChip* grows single cells in diffusion chambers placed back into soil, so that the natural environment supplies what the medium lacks; of the isolates obtained in this way, *Eleftheria terrae* yielded *teixobactin*, reported in 2015, a depsipeptide that binds the pyrophosphate–sugar part of lipid II and of the wall teichoic acid precursor lipid III. Because its targets are not proteins, resistance was not obtained in the laboratory; the compound is active against Gram-positive bacteria only and is still preclinical. Mining genomes and metagenomes for biosynthetic gene clusters and expressing them in a host is the same idea pursued computationally, and machine-learning screens of chemical libraries have produced candidates such as halicin and abaucin.
- *Non-traditional approaches.* **Phage therapy**, almost abandoned in the West after antibiotics arrived but continued in Georgia and Russia, is being tried again in individual cases and in trials, often against biofilm infections of implants ([7.11.23](/7.11.23)). Monoclonal antibodies, antivirulence drugs that disarm pathogens instead of killing them (inhibitors of toxins, of quorum sensing or of secretion systems) and microbiota-based therapies, such as fecal transplantation against recurrent *Clostridioides difficile* infection ([7.11.32](/7.11.32)), reduce the selection pressure because they do not kill bacteria indiscriminately.
- *Treatments designed with evolution in mind.* Combinations, cycling or mixing of drugs in a hospital, and sequences chosen to exploit *collateral sensitivity*, in which resistance to one drug makes a strain more susceptible to another (aminoglycoside resistance from reduced membrane potential, for instance, often increases susceptibility to other classes), are being tested; their benefit in practice is still uncertain.

Finally, the economic problem is being addressed directly by "pull" incentives that pay for availability rather than volume, such as the subscription contracts for reserve antibiotics introduced in the United Kingdom in 2022. The resistance that this chapter describes is not an accident of carelessness alone; it is the predictable response of large, rapidly reproducing populations to strong selection, and it can be slowed but not abolished.

## Summary

| Mechanism | Examples | Genetic basis |
|---|---|---|
| Inactivation of the drug | **β-lactamases** (Definition 15.3.1: Ambler classes A–D, **ESBLs**, carbapenemases KPC, NDM, OXA-48); aminoglycoside-modifying enzymes; Tet(X) | mostly acquired, on plasmids, transposons and integrons |
| Target alteration | PBP2a of **MRSA** (*mecA* on SCC*mec*); Erm methylation of 23S rRNA; *vanA* (D-Ala–D-Lac); *gyrA*/*parC* and *rpoB* mutations; lipid A modification (*mcr-1*, PmrAB) | mutation or acquisition |
| Target protection | Tet(M), Tet(O); Qnr | acquired |
| Reduced uptake, efflux | porin loss (OprD, OmpK35/36); **efflux pumps** (Definition 15.3.2), RND pumps such as AcrAB–TolC | mutation of regulators; intrinsic and acquired |
| Bypass, overproduction | *sul* and *dfr* genes; overproduced DHFR, InhA | acquired or promoter mutation |

- **Antimicrobial resistance** (Definition 15.1.1) caused an estimated 1.27 million deaths directly in 2019 and was associated with 4.95 million. **MDR**, XDR and PDR are defined by the number of antimicrobial categories to which an isolate is non-susceptible (Definition 15.1.2); the ESKAPE organisms and the WHO priority list name the pathogens of greatest concern.
- **Intrinsic resistance** (Definition 15.2.1) follows from the envelope and physiology of a species; acquired resistance comes from mutation ($10^{-6}$ to $10^{-9}$ per cell per generation) or from **horizontal gene transfer**.
- **Antibiotic tolerance** and persistence (Definition 15.5.1) are phenotypic: killing is biphasic (Formula 15.5.1, Eq. (15.1)), **persisters** are dormant cells that pre-exist the drug, and regrown survivors are as susceptible as before. Tolerance is a stepping stone to resistance.
- Resistance genes are ancient, derived from producers and their neighbours; the *resistome* of soil and gut communities is mobilized by insertion sequences, **integrons** and **transposons** onto **R plasmids** and **integrative and conjugative elements**, and spreads both by transfer and by clonal expansion (*E. coli* ST131, *K. pneumoniae* ST258, MRSA USA300).
- Resistance usually carries a fitness cost, which **compensatory mutations** erase, and co-selection by other drugs, metals and biocides maintains it. Selection begins below the MIC, at the *minimal selective concentration*, and is strongest inside the mutant selection window between the MIC and the mutant prevention concentration (Definition 15.6.1).
- The responses are stewardship, infection prevention, vaccines and rapid diagnostics; β-lactamase inhibitors and modified scaffolds; new sources of compounds (the iChip and *teixobactin*, genome mining); and non-traditional therapies (**phage therapy**, antivirulence and microbiota-based approaches), together with incentives that decouple the reward for a new antibiotic from the volume sold.

## Questions

1. A *K. pneumoniae* isolate from a patient is non-susceptible to all penicillins, cephalosporins, carbapenems, fluoroquinolones and aminoglycosides, but susceptible to colistin and tigecycline. Classify it using Definition 15.1.2 and explain what further information you would want before calling it "pandrug-resistant".

   **Answer** It is non-susceptible to at least one agent in more than three categories, hence MDR, and it is susceptible to only two categories, hence XDR. PDR requires non-susceptibility to every agent of every category defined for the species, so one would have to test the complete list of agents, including those rarely used (fosfomycin, chloramphenicol, trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole and newer combinations such as ceftazidime–avibactam), with a method validated for each; a claim of PDR based on a routine panel alone is not justified.

2. Why does vancomycin resistance of the *vanA* type require three enzymes, and why is the operon inducible?

   **Answer** The end of the precursor must be changed from D-Ala–D-Ala to D-Ala–D-Lac, and all three functions are needed: VanH makes D-lactate from pyruvate, VanA ligates D-Ala to D-lactate instead of to D-Ala, and VanX hydrolyzes the normal D-Ala–D-Ala made by the host ligase, so that no susceptible precursor remains. Replacing an amide NH by an ester oxygen removes a hydrogen bond to vancomycin and adds a repulsive interaction, reducing affinity about a thousandfold. Because the depsipeptide precursor cross-links less efficiently and the extra enzymes cost resources, the phenotype has a fitness cost; the two-component system VanS–VanR expresses it only in the presence of the drug.

3. Explain how a Gram-negative bacterium can become resistant to a carbapenem without acquiring a carbapenemase, and why the combination of mechanisms works better than any one alone.

   **Answer** It can lose the porin through which the drug enters (OprD in *P. aeruginosa*, OmpK35/36 in *K. pneumoniae*), raise the expression of an RND efflux pump by mutating its repressor, and produce a β-lactamase with weak carbapenemase activity, such as AmpC or an ESBL. The mechanisms act in series: the influx through the remaining porins is slow, so the pump and the enzyme, each too weak alone, can keep the periplasmic concentration below the level needed to inhibit the PBPs. The MIC rises multiplicatively rather than additively.

4. In a time–kill experiment, a culture of $10^9$ cells per millilitre falls to $10^4$ per millilitre in two hours and then to $3\times10^3$ over the next six hours. What do you conclude, and which two experiments would distinguish persistence from resistance?

   **Answer** The curve is biphasic, which suggests a persister subpopulation: extrapolating the slow second phase back to time zero gives about $10^4$ per millilitre, that is, a persister fraction of about $10^{-5}$. To confirm it, (i) regrow the survivors without drug and repeat the experiment: persisters give the same biphasic curve and the same MIC, whereas resistant mutants give a higher MIC and no killing; (ii) measure the MIC of the survivors directly, which is unchanged for persisters.

5. A patient takes an antibiotic at half the recommended dose. Using Definition 15.6.1 and the figure in Section 15.6.4, explain why this is worse than taking none at all, and why high doses of a single drug are not a general solution.

   **Answer** At half the dose the concentration spends most of each interval between the MIC of the wild type and the mutant prevention concentration, that is, inside the mutant selection window: the susceptible population is suppressed, which removes competition, while any resistant mutant present grows, so the mutant is enriched exactly as in an experiment designed to select it. Taking no drug leaves the wild type in place, and since resistance usually has a fitness cost, the mutant does not spread. High doses keep the concentration above the mutant prevention concentration and prevent single-step mutants, but they are limited by toxicity, cannot reach sanctuary sites such as the interior of granulomas or biofilms, do not touch persisters, and do nothing against resistance acquired as a complete gene by horizontal transfer; hence combinations, shorter courses and the other measures of Section 15.7.
