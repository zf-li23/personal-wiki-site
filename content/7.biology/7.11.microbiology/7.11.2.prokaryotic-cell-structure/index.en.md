A bacterium or archaeon has no internal compartments comparable to those of a eukaryotic cell, so its boundary does most of the work that organelles do elsewhere. The cytoplasmic membrane is at once the permeability barrier, the site of energy conservation and the anchor of transport and sensing; the cell wall outside it holds the cell against its own osmotic pressure and gives it its shape; and in many bacteria an outer membrane adds a second barrier. These layers differ between the two prokaryotic domains in ways that reveal their deep divergence, and between groups of bacteria in ways that the Gram stain, the oldest and still the most used diagnostic test of bacteriology, makes visible. This chapter treats the shape of prokaryotic cells and their envelope from the membrane outward; the structures outside the wall, the inclusions, endospores and motility follow in [7.11.3](/7.11.3), and transport across the membrane in [7.11.4](/7.11.4).

The general comparison of prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells, the prokaryotic cytoskeleton and the three domains are in [7.3.1](/7.3.1#sec-1.4); the chemistry of membrane lipids in [7.1.12](/7.1.12), of **peptidoglycan** and **lipopolysaccharide** in [7.1.10](/7.1.10#sec-10.3.5); the general structure of membranes in [7.1.13](/7.1.13). Here we take the microbiological view: how these structures differ among bacteria and archaea, how they are detected and why they matter for identification and for antibiotics.

## 2.1. Cell Morphology

### 2.1.1. Shapes and Arrangements

Most prokaryotic cells have one of a few shapes. A *coccus* (plural cocci) is spherical or ovoid; a *rod* (or bacillus) is cylindrical; a *vibrio* is a curved rod; a *spirillum* is a rigid helix that swims with external flagella; a *spirochete* is a thin, flexible helix whose flagella lie inside the cell envelope ([7.11.3](/7.11.3)). Other forms include filaments of cells (many cyanobacteria and actinomycetes), cells with appendages (*prosthecae*) or stalks (*Caulobacter*, *Hyphomicrobium*, which reproduces by budding from the tip of a hypha), pleomorphic cells without a fixed shape (the wall-less **mycoplasmas**) and even flat square cells (the archaeon *Haloquadratum walsbyi* of salt ponds).

Cocci that stay together after division form arrangements that reflect the planes of successive divisions: pairs (diplococci, *Neisseria*), chains when division always occurs in parallel planes (*Streptococcus*), tetrads after division in two perpendicular planes (*Micrococcus*), cubical packets of eight after division in three perpendicular planes (*Sarcina*) and irregular grape-like clusters when the planes vary (*Staphylococcus*). Rods occur singly, in chains (*Bacillus*) or, in the corynebacteria, in V shapes and palisades produced by a snapping type of division.

Shape is maintained by the peptidoglycan sacculus, which is synthesized under the direction of cytoskeletal proteins: the actin homolog **MreB** guides insertion of new wall material along the side of rods (cells lacking it become round), the tubulin homolog **FtsZ** forms the division ring, and in *Caulobacter crescentus* the intermediate-filament-like crescentin bends the cell into a crescent ([7.3.1](/7.3.1#sec-1.4.1), [7.11.8](/7.11.8)). Shape is selected because it affects survival: a high surface-to-volume ratio speeds nutrient uptake ([7.11.1](/7.11.1#sec-1.9.2)), elongated and helical cells swim more efficiently in viscous media, filaments and stalks help cells reach or stay in favorable positions, and some shapes resist grazing by protists.

Morphology, however, says little about phylogeny. Rods and cocci occur in many unrelated phyla of Bacteria and in the Archaea, and close relatives can differ in shape; classification rests on sequences ([7.11.16](/7.11.16)). Shape remains useful in identification in combination with the Gram reaction (2.5).

### 2.1.2. Size

The sizes of prokaryotes, from about $0.2\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ to the centimeter-long cells of *Thiomargarita magnifica*, and the importance of the surface-to-volume ratio were discussed in [7.11.1](/7.11.1#sec-1.9). Cells near the lower limit, with volumes of about $0.01\,\mu\mathrm{m}^3$, are common among uncultured lineages, notably the **candidate phyla radiation**, whose small genomes lack many biosynthetic pathways and whose members probably live attached to other cells ([7.11.18](/7.11.18)). They pass through the $0.2\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ filters that are routinely used to sterilize solutions, so filtration through such filters does not remove every cell.

## 2.2. The Cytoplasmic Membrane

### 2.2.1. The Bacterial Membrane

The cytoplasmic membrane of bacteria is a **lipid bilayer** about $6$–$8\,\mathrm{nm}$ thick with embedded and attached proteins, organized as described by the **fluid mosaic model** ([7.1.13](/7.1.13)). Its phospholipids are glycerophospholipids: two fatty acids joined by ester bonds to positions 1 and 2 of *sn*-glycerol 3-phosphate, and a polar head group on the phosphate. In *E. coli* about three quarters of the lipid is phosphatidylethanolamine, the rest mainly phosphatidylglycerol and cardiolipin; Gram-positive bacteria have more phosphatidylglycerol and its lysyl derivative. The fatty acids are $\mathrm{C_{14}}$–$\mathrm{C_{18}}$, straight or branched (iso and anteiso chains in many Gram-positive bacteria), saturated or monounsaturated; cells adjust the mixture to keep the membrane fluid at the growth temperature ([7.11.9](/7.11.9)).

Bacteria do not make **sterols** (a few acquire them: mycoplasmas take up cholesterol from the host), but many make *hopanoids*, pentacyclic triterpenoids formed by cyclization of squalene without oxygen that pack among the fatty acids and stiffen the membrane as sterols do in eukaryotes. Hopanoids are so resistant to decay that their derivatives (hopanes) survive in sediments for hundreds of millions of years and serve as molecular fossils of bacteria. Membrane proteins are either integral, spanning the bilayer, or peripheral, attached to its surface; many peripheral proteins are lipoproteins anchored by a lipid tail.

### 2.2.2. Archaeal Membranes

The membrane lipids of Archaea are chemically distinct ([7.1.12](/7.1.12#sec-12.4.3)). Their hydrophobic chains are not fatty acids but isoprenoids, built from $\mathrm{C_5}$ isoprene units: the $\mathrm{C_{20}}$ phytanyl chain or the $\mathrm{C_{40}}$ biphytanyl chain. The chains are linked to glycerol by ether bonds, and the glycerol has the opposite stereochemistry, *sn*-glycerol 1-phosphate. Two classes predominate:

| Lipid | Structure | Membrane |
|---|---|---|
| Glycerol diethers (archaeol) | Two phytanyl chains ether-linked to one glycerol, as in a phospholipid | Bilayer, about $4\,\mathrm{nm}$ thick |
| Glycerol dialkyl glycerol tetraethers (caldarchaeol and relatives) | Two biphytanyl chains, each ether-linked at both ends to a glycerol, so that one molecule spans the membrane with a polar head on each side | Monolayer, or a mixture of monolayer and bilayer regions |
| Crenarchaeol | A tetraether with a cyclohexane ring and cyclopentane rings, characteristic of the ammonia-oxidizing archaea | Monolayer |

Compound 1 is a bacterial phosphatidylethanolamine (palmitoyl and oleoyl chains on *sn*-glycerol 3-phosphate, joined by ester bonds); compound 2 is the corresponding archaeal lipid, archaetidylethanolamine (two phytanyl chains on *sn*-glycerol 1-phosphate, joined by ether bonds; stereochemistry not drawn).

Ether bonds resist hydrolysis at high temperature and low pH far better than esters; the methyl branches pack tightly and make the membrane much less permeable to protons and ions; and a monolayer of membrane-spanning tetraethers cannot peel apart into two leaflets at high temperature. Tetraethers are therefore common in hyperthermophiles and acidophiles, and many species add cyclopentane rings to their chains as temperature rises; the number of rings in tetraethers of marine archaea preserved in sediments is used as a paleothermometer (the $\mathrm{TEX}_{86}$ index). The phospholipid and archaeal lipid pathways use nonhomologous enzymes to make the two enantiomers of glycerol phosphate, a difference called the "lipid divide", whose bearing on the nature of the last common ancestor and on the origin of eukaryotes (which have bacterial-type lipids) is discussed in [7.10.27](/7.10.27) and [7.10.28](/7.10.28).

### 2.2.3. Functions of the Membrane

The cytoplasmic membrane has three main functions:

- *Permeability barrier.* Water, gases and small hydrophobic molecules cross the bilayer; ions and polar molecules do not, so the cell can concentrate nutrients a thousandfold or more above their external concentration. Uptake is the work of specific transport proteins ([7.11.4](/7.11.4)).
- *Protein anchor.* The membrane carries the proteins of transport, of the respiratory and photosynthetic electron-transport chains, of the synthesis of lipids, wall and secreted proteins, and the chemoreceptors of chemotaxis ([7.11.3](/7.11.3)).
- *Energy conservation.* Respiration and photosynthesis pump protons out of the cell, creating a **proton-motive force** that drives ATP synthesis, transport and flagellar rotation ([7.1.23](/7.1.23), [7.11.5](/7.11.5)). In eukaryotes these functions belong to the inner membrane of mitochondria and the thylakoids of chloroplasts.

A few prokaryotes extend the membrane inward to increase its area: the thylakoids of cyanobacteria and the chromatophores of purple bacteria carry the photosynthetic apparatus, the nitrifying bacteria and methanotrophs have stacked internal membranes, the anammox bacteria a membrane-bounded anammoxosome, and magnetotactic bacteria form each **magnetosome** in an invagination of the membrane ([7.11.3](/7.11.3)). The mesosome of older textbooks is a fixation artifact ([7.3.1](/7.3.1#sec-1.4.1)).

## 2.3. Peptidoglycan

### 2.3.1. Structure

Solutes in the cytoplasm give the cell an internal osmotic pressure of the order of $2\,\mathrm{atm}$ ($0.2\,\mathrm{MPa}$) in *E. coli*, and higher values are usually estimated for Gram-positive bacteria. A membrane alone would burst; almost all Bacteria and Archaea have a cell wall that resists this turgor and fixes the cell's shape. The wall of Bacteria is built of peptidoglycan (murein; [Definition 10.3.4 of 7.1.10](/7.1.10#def-10.3.4)), found in no other domain.

The repeating unit, a *glycan tetrapeptide*, consists of N-acetylglucosamine (NAG) and N-acetylmuramic acid (NAM) joined by a β(1→4) bond, with a peptide attached to the lactyl group of NAM. The peptide is synthesized as a pentapeptide, typically L-Ala–D-Glu–$X$–D-Ala–D-Ala, in which $X$ (position 3) is *meso*-diaminopimelic acid (DAP) in most Gram-negative bacteria and in *Bacillus*, and L-lysine in most Gram-positive cocci. The glutamate is linked through its γ-carboxyl group, and D-amino acids, absent from proteins, make the polymer resistant to ordinary proteases. Glycan strands are joined into a net by transpeptidation: the free amino group of the position-3 residue of one peptide attacks the D-Ala–D-Ala bond of a peptide on a neighboring strand, forming a bond to D-Ala in position 4 and releasing the terminal D-Ala. The cross-link is either

- *direct*, between DAP and D-Ala, as in *E. coli* and most Gram-negative bacteria; or
- through an *interbridge* of other amino acids, as the pentaglycine bridge from the ε-amino group of lysine in *Staphylococcus aureus*.

More than a hundred chemical types of peptidoglycan differ in the position-3 amino acid and in the bridge, and these differences were once used in classification. The whole wall is a single covalent, mesh-like molecule, the *sacculus*, enclosing the cell; cryo-electron tomography shows that in *E. coli* the glycan strands run roughly around the circumference of the rod, like hoops of a barrel. How the sacculus grows by inserting new strands without opening holes is described in [7.11.8](/7.11.8).

![The structure of peptidoglycan. B: direct cross-link between DAP and D-Ala of neighboring strands (*E. coli*). C: cross-link through a pentaglycine interbridge from L-Lys to D-Ala (*Staphylococcus aureus*). The sites cut by lysozyme (the NAM–NAG glycosidic bond) and blocked by penicillin (formation of the cross-link by transpeptidases) are marked](./plots/peptidoglycan.py)

Because peptidoglycan is unique to bacteria and essential to them, it is the target of the most successful antibiotics and of natural defenses. Lysozyme, an enzyme of tears, saliva and egg white, hydrolyzes the glycosidic bond between NAM and NAG; penicillins and other β-lactams inhibit the transpeptidases (penicillin-binding proteins) that form the cross-links, so that growing cells lyse; vancomycin binds the D-Ala–D-Ala end of the precursor ([7.11.14](/7.11.14)). Two groups long thought to lack peptidoglycan, the chlamydiae and the planctomycetes, were found in the 2010s to make a thin layer of it.

### 2.3.2. The Gram-Positive Cell Wall

In Gram-positive bacteria the wall is thick, about $20$–$80\,\mathrm{nm}$, and consists of many layers of peptidoglycan, which can make up most of the wall's mass. Threaded through it are acidic polymers.

**Definition 2.3.1 (Teichoic Acid)** A **teichoic acid** is an anionic polymer of glycerol phosphate or ribitol phosphate units, usually substituted with D-alanine or sugars, found in the cell walls of Gram-positive bacteria. *Wall teichoic acids* are covalently linked to NAM of peptidoglycan; *lipoteichoic acids* are anchored to glycolipids of the cytoplasmic membrane.

Teichoic acids give the Gram-positive surface its strong negative charge; they bind $\ce{Mg^2+}$ and $\ce{Ca^2+}$, regulate the activity of wall-degrading enzymes, take part in cell division and adhesion, and are recognized by the host's innate immune system. The D-alanine substituents reduce the negative charge and with it the binding of cationic antimicrobial peptides. Many surface proteins of Gram-positive bacteria are covalently attached to peptidoglycan by transpeptidases called sortases, which recognize a sequence motif (LPXTG) near their C terminus.

### 2.3.3. The Gram-Negative Cell Envelope

The envelope of Gram-negative bacteria has three layers: the cytoplasmic (inner) membrane, a thin peptidoglycan layer of about $2$–$7\,\mathrm{nm}$ (one or a few layers, only about a tenth of the envelope's mass), and an outer membrane.

**Definition 2.3.2 (Outer Membrane and Periplasm)** The **outer membrane** of Gram-negative bacteria is an asymmetric lipid bilayer outside the peptidoglycan layer whose inner leaflet consists of phospholipids and whose outer leaflet consists mainly of lipopolysaccharide. The **periplasm** is the compartment between the cytoplasmic membrane and the outer membrane, containing the peptidoglycan layer and a gel-like solution of proteins.

Lipopolysaccharide ([Definition 10.4.5 of 7.1.10](/7.1.10#def-10.4.5)) has three parts:

| Part | Structure | Properties |
|---|---|---|
| Lipid A | A disaccharide of glucosamine phosphates carrying six or seven fatty acids (in *E. coli* $\mathrm{C_{12}}$–$\mathrm{C_{14}}$, including 3-hydroxymyristic acid) by ester and amide bonds | Anchors LPS in the membrane; the toxic component, **endotoxin**, recognized by the TLR4 receptor of animals ([7.11.33](/7.11.33)) |
| Core polysaccharide | Ketodeoxyoctonate (KDO), heptoses, glucose, galactose and N-acetylglucosamine; phosphorylated | Fairly constant within a genus; phosphates bridged by $\ce{Mg^2+}$ and $\ce{Ca^2+}$ stabilize the outer leaflet |
| O-specific polysaccharide (O antigen) | Repeating units of three to five sugars, often unusual ones, in chains of variable length | Highly variable among strains: defines serotypes (*E. coli* O157); protects against complement; absent in "rough" mutants |

The outer membrane is permeable to small hydrophilic molecules through *porins*, trimeric β-barrel proteins that form water-filled channels; the general porins of *E. coli* (OmpF, OmpC) pass molecules up to about $600\,\mathrm{Da}$, while specific channels admit particular solutes. The densely packed, cross-bridged LPS makes the outer leaflet a poor solvent for hydrophobic compounds, so the outer membrane excludes detergents, bile salts, many dyes and many antibiotics, such as vancomycin, which is too large to pass the porins; this is a major reason why Gram-negative bacteria are intrinsically resistant to drugs that kill Gram-positive bacteria ([7.11.15](/7.11.15)). The outer membrane is tied to the peptidoglycan by Braun's lipoprotein (Lpp), one of the most abundant proteins of *E. coli*, whose lipid end lies in the inner leaflet of the outer membrane and whose C terminus is covalently linked to DAP of peptidoglycan.

The periplasm is about $15\,\mathrm{nm}$ wide in *E. coli*. It contains hydrolytic enzymes that begin the breakdown of polymers, binding proteins that deliver substrates to ABC transporters ([7.11.4](/7.11.4)), the ligand-binding domains of chemoreceptors, enzymes of peptidoglycan synthesis and, in some species, cytochromes of respiratory chains ([7.11.6](/7.11.6)). Proteins destined for the outside must cross both membranes, which requires the specialized secretion systems of Gram-negative bacteria ([7.11.34](/7.11.34)).

### 2.3.4. Acid-Fast and Wall-Less Bacteria

The Mycobacteriales (*Mycobacterium*, *Corynebacterium*, *Nocardia* and relatives) have a Gram-positive-type peptidoglycan layer that is covalently linked to arabinogalactan, a branched polysaccharide, whose ends are esterified with **mycolic acids**, very long ($\mathrm{C_{60}}$–$\mathrm{C_{90}}$ in mycobacteria) branched hydroxy fatty acids. The mycolic acids form the inner leaflet of an outer lipid layer, the mycomembrane, whose outer leaflet contains free glycolipids. This waxy envelope makes the cells hydrophobic, slow to take up nutrients and stains, resistant to drying, disinfectants and many antibiotics, and acid-fast (2.5.3). It is treated with the actinomycetes in [7.11.18](/7.11.18) and with tuberculosis in [7.11.34](/7.11.34).

A few bacteria have no wall. The mycoplasmas (class Mollicutes, which evolved from Gram-positive ancestors by loss of the wall) are protected by a tough membrane containing sterols taken from their host; they are pleomorphic, pass through bacteriological filters and are insensitive to penicillin and lysozyme. Wall-less cells can also be made in the laboratory. Lysozyme or penicillin in an isotonic medium (for example $0.5\,\mathrm{M}$ sucrose) converts a Gram-positive cell into a spherical **protoplast**, bounded only by its membrane, and a Gram-negative cell, whose outer membrane must first be made permeable with EDTA, into a *spheroplast* that retains remnants of the outer membrane; both lyse in dilute media. *L-forms* are wall-deficient variants of normally walled bacteria that can proliferate without a wall; *Bacillus subtilis* L-forms divide independently of FtsZ by blebbing and extrusion of the membrane (Errington and colleagues, 2009), a possible model of how cells divided before walls evolved.

## 2.4. Cell Walls of Archaea

No archaeon has peptidoglycan; consequently archaea are naturally resistant to lysozyme and to the β-lactams. Their walls are of several kinds:

**Definition 2.4.1 (Pseudomurein)** **Pseudomurein** (pseudopeptidoglycan) is the cell-wall polymer of some methanogenic archaea (Methanobacteriales and *Methanopyrus*): chains of alternating N-acetylglucosamine and N-acetyltalosaminuronic acid joined by β(1→3) bonds and cross-linked through peptides of L-amino acids.

Pseudomurein resembles peptidoglycan in its architecture but not in its chemistry: N-acetyltalosaminuronic acid replaces NAM, the β(1→3) bond is not cut by lysozyme, and the peptides contain only L-amino acids. It evolved independently, a case of convergence. Other archaea have walls of polysaccharide (methanochondroitin in *Methanosarcina*, a sulfated heteropolysaccharide in the extreme halophile *Halococcus*) or of glycoprotein. The commonest archaeal wall, however, is a protein lattice.

**Definition 2.4.2 (S-Layer)** An **S-layer** (surface layer) is a two-dimensional paracrystalline array of identical protein or glycoprotein subunits that forms the outermost layer of the cell envelope, with oblique, square or hexagonal symmetry (two, three, four or six subunits per unit cell).

S-layers occur in all major lineages of Archaea and in many Bacteria, Gram-positive and Gram-negative, where they lie outside the peptidoglycan or the outer membrane (*Clostridioides difficile*, *Bacillus anthracis*, *Caulobacter crescentus*). In many archaea, such as *Sulfolobus* and *Methanocaldococcus*, the S-layer is the only wall: its subunits are anchored in the membrane by stalks or lipid anchors and enclose a space resembling the periplasm. S-layers maintain shape and resist osmotic pressure, act as molecular sieves with pores of a few nanometers, protect against predators and phages and mediate adhesion; because they self-assemble on surfaces, they are also used as templates in nanotechnology. A few archaea have an outer membrane: *Ignicoccus hospitalis* has an outer cellular membrane enclosing a wide intermembrane compartment in which its energy metabolism takes place.

## 2.5. Staining and the Gram Reaction

### 2.5.1. Simple Stains

Most cells are nearly transparent in bright-field microscopy. For staining, a drop of culture is spread on a slide, dried and fixed (by brief heating or with methanol), which kills and attaches the cells. Basic dyes such as methylene blue, crystal violet and safranin are cations that bind the negatively charged surface and nucleic acids of cells; a single dye (a *simple stain*) shows size, shape and arrangement. *Differential stains* use two dyes and a decolorizing step to separate cells into groups.

### 2.5.2. The Gram Stain

**Definition 2.5.1 (Gram Stain)** The **Gram stain** (Hans Christian Gram, 1884) is a differential stain in which fixed cells are stained with crystal violet, treated with iodine, briefly decolorized with ethanol or acetone–ethanol and counterstained with safranin. **Gram-positive bacteria** retain the crystal violet–iodine complex and appear purple; **gram-negative bacteria** are decolorized and appear pink or red from the counterstain.

![The four steps of the Gram stain for a Gram-positive coccus and a Gram-negative rod. 1: crystal violet (about one minute) stains all cells purple. 2: iodine (about one minute) forms an insoluble crystal violet–iodine complex in the cells. 3: ethanol (a few seconds) dehydrates the thick peptidoglycan of the Gram-positive cell, which shrinks and traps the complex; it dissolves the outer membrane of the Gram-negative cell and extracts the complex through the thin peptidoglycan, leaving the cell colorless. 4: safranin (about one minute) stains the colorless cells pink; Gram-positive cells stay purple](./plots/gram-stain.py)

The mechanism lies in the wall. Crystal violet and iodine enter all cells and form a large, insoluble complex in the cytoplasm. Ethanol dehydrates the thick, highly cross-linked peptidoglycan of Gram-positive cells, closing its pores so that the complex cannot escape. In Gram-negative cells ethanol dissolves lipid from the outer membrane, and the thin peptidoglycan layer cannot hold back the complex, which is washed out. The reaction therefore reports the thickness and integrity of the wall, not its chemistry. Several cautions follow:

- *Age and damage.* Gram-positive cells from old cultures, or with walls damaged by antibiotics, often stain Gram-negative; reliable results require young, actively growing cultures and timed decolorization (overdecolorizing makes Gram-positive cells look negative, underdecolorizing the reverse). Some species stain unevenly (*Gram-variable*).
- *Wall-less and atypical cells.* Mycoplasmas, which lack a wall, stain Gram-negative; mycobacteria stain weakly and irregularly because their waxy envelope excludes the dyes.
- *Phylogeny.* Most Gram-positive bacteria belong to two phyla, the Bacillota (formerly Firmicutes) and the Actinomycetota (formerly Actinobacteria), and most Gram-negative bacteria to the many phyla with an outer membrane; but some Bacillota, such as the Negativicutes (*Veillonella*, *Selenomonas*), have an outer membrane with LPS and stain Gram-negative. The terms *monoderm* (one membrane) and *diderm* (two membranes) describe the envelope more precisely ([7.11.18](/7.11.18)).
- *Archaea.* Archaea stain either way depending on their wall (methanogens with pseudomurein stain positive), so the Gram reaction carries no information about their relationships.

### 2.5.3. Acid-Fast and Other Stains

**Definition 2.5.2 (Acid-Fast Stain)** The **acid-fast stain** (Ziehl–Neelsen method) stains cells with carbol fuchsin, a phenol solution of basic fuchsin, with heating, then treats them with acid alcohol (about $3\,\%$ $\ce{HCl}$ in $95\,\%$ ethanol) and counterstains with methylene blue. Cells with mycolic acid–rich envelopes retain the red dye and are called *acid-fast*; other cells are decolorized and appear blue.

Heat drives the phenolic dye into the waxy envelope, which then holds it against acid alcohol. The stain identifies *Mycobacterium* (tuberculosis, leprosy) and, with a weaker acid, *Nocardia*; in sputum smears it remains a rapid first test for tuberculosis, alongside the more sensitive fluorescent auramine stain and molecular tests ([7.11.35](/7.11.35)). The Kinyoun variant uses a more concentrated carbol fuchsin without heat.

Other stains show particular structures. In a *negative stain* the background is darkened with India ink or nigrosin, which do not penetrate cells, so that capsules appear as clear halos ([7.11.3](/7.11.3)). The *endospore stain* (Schaeffer–Fulton) drives malachite green into spores with steam and counterstains vegetative cells with safranin. Flagella, too thin to see, are thickened with a mordant before staining. Fluorescent stains and probes are treated in [7.11.1](/7.11.1#sec-1.9.3) and [7.11.16](/7.11.16).

The resistance entries are tendencies, not rules: ampicillin, for example, crosses porins and kills many Gram-negative bacteria, and some Gram-positive bacteria resist penicillin by enzymes ([7.11.15](/7.11.15)).

## Summary

- Prokaryotic cells are cocci, rods, vibrios, spirilla, spirochetes, filaments, stalked, budding or pleomorphic; arrangements of cocci reflect division planes. Shape is set by the peptidoglycan sacculus under the control of MreB, FtsZ and other cytoskeletal proteins, has adaptive value, but is not a phylogenetic marker.
- The bacterial membrane is a bilayer of fatty acids ester-linked to glycerol 3-phosphate, often stiffened by hopanoids; the archaeal membrane is built of isoprenoid chains ether-linked to glycerol 1-phosphate, as diether bilayers or tetraether monolayers, which resist heat, acid and proton leakage. The membrane is a permeability barrier, a protein anchor and the site of energy conservation.
- Peptidoglycan is a single molecule of NAG–NAM strands cross-linked by peptides containing D-amino acids, either directly (DAP–D-Ala) or through interbridges (pentaglycine in *S. aureus*); it is the target of lysozyme and β-lactams.
- Gram-positive walls are thick peptidoglycan with teichoic acids (Definition 2.3.1); Gram-negative envelopes have a thin peptidoglycan layer in the periplasm and an outer membrane with LPS (lipid A, core, O antigen), porins and Braun's lipoprotein (Definition 2.3.2). Mycobacteria add an arabinogalactan–mycolic acid mycomembrane; mycoplasmas have no wall; protoplasts, spheroplasts and L-forms are wall-less derivatives.
- Archaea lack peptidoglycan; their walls are pseudomurein (Definition 2.4.1), polysaccharides, glycoproteins or, most often, S-layers (Definition 2.4.2), which also occur in many bacteria.
- The Gram stain (Definition 2.5.1) separates bacteria by the ability of a thick, dehydrated wall to retain the crystal violet–iodine complex; it fails for old cultures, wall-less cells, mycobacteria and archaea, and only roughly follows phylogeny. The acid-fast stain (Definition 2.5.2) detects mycolic acid–rich envelopes.

## Questions

1. A Gram-positive bacterium is treated with lysozyme in water, and another sample in $0.5\,\mathrm{M}$ sucrose. Predict the results, and explain why a Gram-negative bacterium is unaffected by lysozyme alone.

   **Answer** In water the cells lyse: once the wall is digested, water enters the hypertonic cytoplasm and bursts the membrane. In isotonic sucrose the cells survive as spherical protoplasts, because there is no net osmotic inflow. In a Gram-negative bacterium the outer membrane prevents lysozyme from reaching the peptidoglycan; EDTA, which removes the divalent cations bridging LPS, makes the outer membrane permeable and allows spheroplast formation.

2. Why would a membrane of tetraether lipids be advantageous at $95\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ and pH 2, and what features of archaeal lipids contribute?

   **Answer** At high temperature a bilayer becomes too fluid and leaky and may separate into leaflets; at low pH ester bonds hydrolyze. Ether bonds are chemically stable; membrane-spanning tetraethers, each covalently joining the two surfaces, cannot separate into leaflets; the methyl-branched isoprenoid chains and their cyclopentane rings pack tightly and reduce the permeability to protons, which is critical for maintaining a proton-motive force and a near-neutral cytoplasm.

3. A student Gram-stains a 72-hour culture of *Bacillus subtilis* and reports pink rods. Give two possible explanations and how to check them.

   **Answer** Old cultures of Gram-positive bacteria have autolysed or damaged walls that no longer retain the crystal violet–iodine complex; repeat with an exponentially growing culture of 12–18 hours. Overdecolorization (too long in ethanol) also removes the complex from Gram-positive cells; repeat with a control slide carrying a known Gram-positive (*Staphylococcus aureus*) and a known Gram-negative (*Escherichia coli*) organism and timed decolorization.

4. Penicillin inhibits peptidoglycan cross-linking, yet *Mycoplasma pneumoniae* and the archaeon *Methanobacterium* are both insensitive to it. Explain each case.

   **Answer** *M. pneumoniae* has no cell wall, so there is no peptidoglycan synthesis to inhibit; its sterol-containing membrane holds the cell together. *Methanobacterium* has a pseudomurein wall built from different sugars and L-amino acids and synthesized by unrelated enzymes, so it has no penicillin-binding transpeptidases that act on D-Ala–D-Ala.

5. Why is vancomycin, which binds the D-Ala–D-Ala ends of peptidoglycan precursors, effective against Gram-positive but not most Gram-negative bacteria, although both make peptidoglycan with the same precursor?

   **Answer** Vancomycin is a large glycopeptide (about $1.45\,\mathrm{kDa}$), above the exclusion limit of the porins (about $600\,\mathrm{Da}$), and too hydrophilic to cross the LPS leaflet of the outer membrane. It therefore cannot reach the periplasm where Gram-negative peptidoglycan is assembled, whereas in Gram-positive bacteria the precursors are exposed on the outer surface of the cytoplasmic membrane, directly accessible to the drug.
