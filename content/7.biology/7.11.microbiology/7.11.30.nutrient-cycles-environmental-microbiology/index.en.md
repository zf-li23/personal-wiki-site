The metabolic diversity of [7.11.5](/7.11.5)–[7.11.7](/7.11.7) has a global consequence: microorganisms catalyze most of the oxidations and reductions by which the elements of living matter move between the atmosphere, the oceans, the rocks and the biosphere. Without them, organic matter would accumulate undecomposed, fixed nitrogen would not return to the atmosphere and sulfur would remain locked in minerals. This chapter treats the main cycles as systems of pools and fluxes, with the reactions, the guilds that carry them out and the orders of magnitude of the global fluxes; the biochemistry of the reactions is in [7.11.6](/7.11.6) and [7.11.7](/7.11.7), the methods used to measure them in [7.11.29](/7.11.29). The second half turns to the built environment: how humans use microbial cycles to treat wastewater, purify drinking water, clean up pollution and recover metals, and how unwanted microbial activity corrodes and destroys materials.

## 30.1. Biogeochemical Cycles

**Definition 30.1.1 (Biogeochemical Cycle)** A **biogeochemical cycle** is the set of biological, chemical and physical transformations and transport processes by which an element moves between its reservoirs (the atmosphere, the hydrosphere, the lithosphere and the biosphere) and between its chemical forms, usually different oxidation states.

Most steps of the cycles of carbon, nitrogen, sulfur, iron and manganese are redox reactions, and the organisms that catalyze them gain energy or obtain building blocks by doing so. A cycle is therefore a chain of metabolisms in which the waste product of one guild is the resource of another: the oxygen of oxygenic phototrophs is the acceptor of aerobic respiration, the sulfide of sulfate reducers is the donor of sulfur oxidizers, and so on. Because one reaction often couples two cycles (sulfate reduction oxidizes organic carbon; denitrification can be driven by sulfide or iron), the cycles are linked.

A reservoir is characterized by its size $M$ and the fluxes $F$ in and out of it. At steady state the residence time $\tau = M/F$ measures how long an atom stays in the reservoir on average: about four years for carbon in the atmosphere against photosynthesis and respiration, but millions of years for carbon in carbonate rocks, which hold more than $99\,\%$ of the Earth's carbon but exchange it only by weathering and burial. Biology works on the small, fast reservoirs; geology controls the large, slow ones. In the ocean, phytoplankton take up carbon, nitrogen and phosphorus in an almost constant ratio, $\mathrm{C}:\mathrm{N}:\mathrm{P} \approx 106 : 16 : 1$ by atoms (the Redfield ratio), which is why the limitation of production by one element affects the cycles of all the others.

## 30.2. The Carbon Cycle

### 30.2.1. Fixation, Decomposition and the Pools of Carbon

**Definition 30.2.1 (Carbon Cycle)** The **carbon cycle** is the biogeochemical cycle of carbon, in which $\ce{CO2}$ is fixed into organic matter by phototrophs and chemolithotrophs and returned to $\ce{CO2}$ by respiration, fermentation and, in anoxic habitats, by methanogenesis followed by methane oxidation, with exchanges between the atmosphere, the ocean, soils, sediments and rocks.

Organic matter is made only by autotrophs, overwhelmingly by oxygenic photosynthesis, $\ce{CO2 + H2O -> (CH2O) + O2}$; global net primary production is about $105\,\mathrm{Pg\,C}$ per year ($1\,\mathrm{Pg} = 10^{15}\,\mathrm{g}$), roughly half on land, mainly by plants, and half in the ocean, almost entirely by microbial phytoplankton (Field et al. 1998). Chemolithoautotrophs contribute little to global production, but dominate in dark habitats. The reverse reaction, respiration $\ce{(CH2O) + O2 -> CO2 + H2O}$, is carried out by all organisms, but most of the decomposition of dead organic matter is microbial, and microbial decomposition is the largest single source of $\ce{CO2}$ to the atmosphere. Plant polymers differ greatly in how fast they are degraded: sugars and proteins in days, cellulose and hemicellulose in months, lignin, degraded mainly by fungi with oxidative enzymes, in years ([7.11.21](/7.11.21#sec-21.6.1)). The residues, transformed by microbes and bound to minerals, form humus, the dark, chemically heterogeneous organic matter of soils, part of which persists for centuries to millennia.

Soils hold about $1500\,\mathrm{Pg}$ of organic carbon in the top meter, roughly twice as much as the atmosphere, and the northern permafrost region holds about $1300\,\mathrm{Pg}$ more, frozen and thus protected from decomposition. As permafrost thaws, part of this carbon becomes available to microorganisms, and its release as $\ce{CO2}$ and methane is a positive feedback on warming whose size is one of the main uncertainties of climate projections; estimates for this century are of the order of $5$–$15\,\%$ of the permafrost carbon (Schuur et al. 2015).

**Definition 30.2.2 (Biological Carbon Pump)** The **biological carbon pump** is the transport of organic carbon fixed by phytoplankton from the surface ocean to the deep ocean and the sediments, as sinking particles (dead cells, aggregates called marine snow, the fecal pellets of zooplankton) and by the mixing and active transport of dissolved and particulate organic matter.

Most of the sinking organic matter is respired by bacteria on the way down (the microbial loop: [7.11.29](/7.11.29#sec-29.9.2)); only a few percent of surface production reaches the deep sea and less than one percent is buried in sediments, but this small fraction, accumulated over geological time, is the source of fossil fuels and of the oxygen in the atmosphere. Together with the physical dissolution of $\ce{CO2}$ in seawater, the ocean takes up about a quarter of the $\ce{CO2}$ emitted by humans each year.

### 30.2.2. The Anaerobic Food Chain

In anoxic habitats, wetlands, rice paddies, lake and marine sediments, landfills, sewage digesters and the rumen, no single organism can mineralize organic polymers completely. Degradation proceeds through a chain of guilds ([7.11.7](/7.11.7#sec-7.5.2)), each using the products of the one before.

![The anaerobic food chain by which organic polymers are mineralized to methane and CO₂ (schematic). (1) Hydrolytic bacteria break polysaccharides, proteins, lipids and nucleic acids into monomers with extracellular enzymes. (2) Primary fermenters convert the monomers to acetate, H₂ and CO₂, and to more reduced products (propionate, butyrate, lactate, alcohols). (3) Syntrophs oxidize these products to acetate and H₂, which is possible only while hydrogen-consuming partners keep the H₂ partial pressure very low (interspecies H₂ transfer, dashed); acetogens make acetate from H₂ and CO₂. (4) Methanogens form CH₄ from acetate (acetoclastic, about two thirds of the methane in freshwater sediments and digesters) and from H₂ + CO₂ (hydrogenotrophic, about one third). Where sulfate is available, as in marine sediments, sulfate reducers oxidize the same intermediates completely to CO₂ and outcompete the methanogens. Methane that diffuses upward is largely oxidized by anaerobic and aerobic methanotrophs before it reaches the atmosphere.](./plots/anaerobic-food-chain.py)

The central step is the oxidation of fatty acids and alcohols by syntrophs. Because these reactions are endergonic at standard conditions, they proceed only when hydrogen-consuming organisms keep the $\ce{H2}$ partial pressure around $10^{-4}$–$10^{-5}\,\mathrm{bar}$ (**syntrophy** and **interspecies hydrogen transfer**: [7.11.7](/7.11.7#sec-7.5.1)); if the methanogens are inhibited, for example by acid or oxygen, the fatty acids accumulate and the whole chain stops, a failure familiar to the operators of biogas plants. Which guild ends the chain depends on the electron acceptors available: in freshwater, with little sulfate, methanogenesis dominates; in marine sediments, where seawater supplies about $28\,\mathrm{mM}$ sulfate, sulfate reducers oxidize up to about half of the organic carbon reaching coastal sediments, and methane is formed only below the depth at which sulfate is exhausted.

### 30.2.3. Methane

Methane is a greenhouse gas with about 80 times the warming effect of $\ce{CO2}$ per unit mass over 20 years and about 30 times over 100 years; its atmospheric concentration has risen from about $0.7\,\mathrm{ppm}$ before industrialization to about $1.9\,\mathrm{ppm}$ today. About two thirds of global emissions are produced by methanogenic archaea, in natural wetlands, in the rumen of cattle and sheep, in rice paddies and in landfills; the rest comes from fossil fuels and fires.

The methane that escapes to the atmosphere is only part of what is produced. Most of the methane formed in marine sediments, by most estimates more than $90\,\%$, is oxidized anaerobically by consortia of ANME archaea and sulfate reducers in the sulfate–methane transition zone before it reaches the water (**methanotrophs** and anaerobic methane oxidation: [7.11.7](/7.11.7#sec-7.4.4)), and aerobic methanotrophs at the oxic surfaces of wetland soils, lake sediments and rice paddy soils consume a large share of the methane rising from below. These "microbial methane filters" keep marine sediments, which produce large amounts of methane, a minor source to the atmosphere. Methane hydrates in continental margin sediments and below permafrost, ice-like solids of methane and water, are estimated to hold several hundred to several thousand Pg of methane carbon; they are stable only at low temperature and high pressure.

## 30.3. The Nitrogen Cycle

### 30.3.1. Transformations

The **nitrogen cycle** ([7.1.27](/7.1.27)) consists of a small number of microbial transformations between oxidation states $-3$ ($\ce{NH4+}$, organic N) and $+5$ ($\ce{NO3-}$); their biochemistry is in [7.11.6](/7.11.6#sec-6.6) and [7.11.7](/7.11.7#sec-7.2.4). Dinitrogen, $78\,\%$ of the atmosphere, is the largest reservoir, but its triple bond makes it unavailable to almost all organisms.

- **Nitrogen fixation** by diazotrophic bacteria and archaea reduces $\ce{N2}$ to ammonia with **nitrogenase**, at a cost of at least 16 ATP per $\ce{N2}$.
- Assimilation incorporates ammonium (and, after assimilatory reduction, nitrate) into amino acids and nucleotides.

**Definition 30.3.1 (Ammonification)** **Ammonification** (nitrogen mineralization) is the release of ammonium from organic nitrogen compounds (proteins, amino acids, nucleic acids, urea) during their microbial decomposition.

- **Nitrification** by ammonia-oxidizing bacteria and archaea and by nitrite-oxidizing bacteria, or completely by comammox *Nitrospira*, converts ammonium to nitrate under oxic conditions. Nitrate is mobile in soil and leaches to groundwater, whereas ammonium is held by clay particles; nitrification inhibitors added to fertilizers slow this loss.
- **Denitrification** reduces nitrate to $\ce{N2O}$ and $\ce{N2}$, and **anammox** oxidizes ammonium with nitrite to $\ce{N2}$; both return fixed nitrogen to the atmosphere. Anammox accounts for a large part, in some regions most, of the $\ce{N2}$ produced in oxygen minimum zones and marine sediments. DNRA, the reduction of nitrate to ammonium, retains the nitrogen instead.

### 30.3.2. The Human Alteration of the Nitrogen Cycle

Fixed nitrogen limits the production of most terrestrial ecosystems and much of the ocean. Since the industrial synthesis of ammonia from $\ce{N2}$ and $\ce{H2}$ at high temperature and pressure over an iron catalyst (the Haber–Bosch process, introduced in 1913), humans fix about as much nitrogen as all natural processes together, mainly as fertilizer (figure above, panel B); roughly half of the nitrogen in the bodies of people today has passed through a Haber–Bosch plant. Only part of the fertilizer nitrogen is taken up by crops; the rest is nitrified, leached as nitrate into groundwater, rivers and coastal seas, volatilized as ammonia, or denitrified.

The incomplete reduction of nitrate and the side reactions of nitrification release nitrous oxide, $\ce{N2O}$. It is a greenhouse gas about 270 times as effective as $\ce{CO2}$ per unit mass over 100 years, and since chlorofluorocarbons were phased out it is the largest ozone-depleting substance emitted (Ravishankara et al. 2009). Fertilized agricultural soils are the largest anthropogenic source. Whether a denitrifier releases $\ce{N2O}$ or $\ce{N2}$ depends on whether it has, and expresses, the $\ce{N2O}$ reductase NosZ, which is inhibited by oxygen and low $\mathrm{pH}$; liming of acid soils and microorganisms that reduce $\ce{N2O}$ without producing it are being studied as ways to lower emissions.

### 30.3.3. Eutrophication

**Definition 30.3.2 (Eutrophication)** **Eutrophication** is the enrichment of a body of water with nutrients, above all nitrogen and phosphorus, that leads to excessive growth of algae and cyanobacteria, and, when the biomass is decomposed, to oxygen depletion of the bottom water.

Fertilizer runoff and sewage turn lakes and coastal seas eutrophic. Cyanobacterial blooms of *Microcystis*, *Planktothrix* or *Dolichospermum* color lakes green, some produce toxins (microcystins) that poison drinking water and livestock, and the decomposition of the bloom consumes the oxygen of the hypolimnion ([7.11.29](/7.11.29#sec-29.8)). In coastal seas, nutrient enrichment favors harmful algal blooms, including the red tides of **dinoflagellates**, some of which produce toxins that accumulate in shellfish ([7.11.20](/7.11.20#sec-20.4.2)), and hypoxic "dead zones" such as that in the Gulf of Mexico at the outflow of the Mississippi, whose size each summer follows the nitrogen load of the river. Phosphorus is usually the limiting nutrient in fresh water, as whole-lake experiments in Canada showed in the 1970s (a lake divided by a curtain, with carbon and nitrogen added to both halves and phosphorus to one, bloomed only on the side that received phosphorus), whereas nitrogen limits more often in the sea.

Recovery is slow, because a lake that has turned turbid tends to stay so even after the external loading is reduced. When the hypolimnion becomes anoxic, iron(III) in the sediment is reduced and the phosphate bound to it is released into the water, so the lake fertilizes itself. A minimal model captures this feedback. Let $P$ be the phosphorus in the water, $L$ the external loading, $s$ the rate constant of loss by outflow and sedimentation, and let recycling from the sediment rise steeply with $P$:

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}P}{\mathrm{d}t} = L - sP + r\,\frac{P^q}{m^q + P^q},
$$
where $r$ is the maximum recycling rate, $m$ the phosphorus level at which recycling is half-maximal and $q$ the steepness of the switch (Carpenter et al. 1999). For steep switching the equilibria $L = sP - rP^q/(m^q + P^q)$ form an S-shaped curve in the $(L, P)$ plane, with two stable branches separated by an unstable one; this is the fold structure of bistable systems analyzed in [7.6.33](/7.6.33).

![Hysteresis in lake eutrophication (minimal phosphorus model, after Carpenter et al. 1999; dimensionless parameters s = r = m = 1, q = 8). A: equilibrium phosphorus in the water against external loading L: stable branches (solid) and unstable branch (dashed). Between the two folds (shaded, about L = 0.39 to 0.66) a clear and a turbid state are both stable. B: a simulated history in which the loading rises slowly to 0.85 and is then reduced again: the lake flips to the turbid state only after L passes the upper fold and returns to the clear state only when L falls below the lower fold, far below the loading at which it flipped.](./plots/lake-eutrophication.py)

The model explains why restoration often requires reducing the phosphorus loading far below the level at which the lake turned eutrophic, or measures that act on the internal loading directly, such as aerating the hypolimnion, removing sediment or precipitating phosphate with aluminum or iron salts.

## 30.4. The Sulfur Cycle

**Definition 30.4.1 (Sulfur Cycle)** The **sulfur cycle** is the biogeochemical cycle of sulfur between its oxidation states $-2$ (sulfide, organic thiols), $0$ (elemental sulfur), $+2$ (thiosulfate) and $+6$ (sulfate), driven by the reduction of sulfate by sulfate-reducing microorganisms and the oxidation of reduced sulfur compounds by chemolithotrophic and phototrophic bacteria.

Sulfate is abundant in seawater ($28\,\mathrm{mM}$) and scarce in most fresh water; sulfur in rocks is mainly in pyrite ($\ce{FeS2}$) and gypsum ($\ce{CaSO4 * 2H2O}$).

![The sulfur cycle by oxidation state, and acid mine drainage. A: dissimilatory sulfate reduction by sulfate-reducing bacteria and archaea produces H₂S; chemolithotrophs (Beggiatoa, Thiobacillus, Acidithiobacillus, cable bacteria) and anoxygenic phototrophs (purple and green sulfur bacteria) oxidize sulfide to elemental sulfur and sulfate; S⁰ can be reduced back to sulfide; thiosulfate is disproportionated to sulfate and sulfide (dashed); sulfate is assimilated into cysteine and methionine, and sulfide released from organic sulfur by desulfurylation. Marine algae make dimethylsulfoniopropionate (DMSP), from which bacteria and algae release dimethyl sulfide (DMS) to the atmosphere. B: pyrite oxidation: initiation by O₂ (1) releases Fe²⁺ and acid; acidophilic iron oxidizers reoxidize Fe²⁺ (2); Fe³⁺ oxidizes pyrite rapidly (3), also without O₂, regenerating Fe²⁺, so that reactions 2 and 3 form a self-propagating cycle that releases 16 H⁺ per FeS₂.](./plots/sulfur-cycle-amd.py)

**Dissimilatory sulfate reduction** ([7.11.7](/7.11.7#sec-7.3.3)) is the main anaerobic respiration of marine sediments. Its product, sulfide, is toxic to most organisms, reacts with iron to form black iron sulfides and pyrite, and diffuses up to the oxic surface, where sulfur oxidizers reoxidize most of it, often coupled to the reduction of oxygen or nitrate over centimeters by cable bacteria. The small fraction buried as pyrite links the sulfur cycle to the carbon cycle: each sulfate reduced and buried as sulfide corresponds to organic carbon oxidized and oxygen left in the atmosphere, and over geological time the burial of pyrite and organic carbon has controlled atmospheric oxygen. The sulfur isotope signature of sedimentary sulfides ([7.11.29](/7.11.29#sec-29.6.3)) records the activity of sulfate reducers back to about 3.5 billion years.

Dimethyl sulfide is the main volatile sulfur compound produced by living organisms and the largest natural biogenic sulfur flux to the atmosphere. Marine algae (especially coccolithophores and dinoflagellates) accumulate dimethylsulfoniopropionate as an osmolyte; bacteria such as the Roseobacter clade either demethylate it, using its sulfur, or cleave it to DMS. In the atmosphere DMS is oxidized to sulfate aerosols that can act as cloud condensation nuclei; whether this forms a climate feedback, as proposed in 1987, remains debated.

**Definition 30.4.2 (Acid Mine Drainage)** **Acid mine drainage** (AMD) is acidic water, often below $\mathrm{pH}$ 3 and rich in sulfuric acid, iron and other dissolved metals, produced when sulfide minerals, mainly pyrite, exposed to air and water by mining are oxidized chemically and by acidophilic iron- and sulfur-oxidizing microorganisms.

The oxidation of pyrite proceeds through two coupled reactions (figure above, panel B). It is started by oxygen,

$$
\ce{FeS2 + 7/2 O2 + H2O -> Fe^2+ + 2 SO4^2- + 2 H+},
$$
but the dominant oxidant is ferric iron:

$$
\ce{FeS2 + 14 Fe^3+ + 8 H2O -> 15 Fe^2+ + 2 SO4^2- + 16 H+}.
$$
The ferrous iron produced must be reoxidized, $\ce{Fe^2+ + 1/4 O2 + H+ -> Fe^3+ + 1/2 H2O}$. Below $\mathrm{pH}$ about 3.5 this reaction is very slow abiotically, and acidophilic iron oxidizers, *Acidithiobacillus ferrooxidans*, *Leptospirillum ferrooxidans* and the archaeon *Ferroplasma*, accelerate it by several orders of magnitude, so that the rate of pyrite dissolution is controlled by microbial iron oxidation ([7.11.7](/7.11.7#sec-7.2.3)). Where the acid water reaches streams it kills most life, and iron(III) hydroxides precipitate as orange-brown deposits ("yellow boy"). In the Richmond mine at Iron Mountain, California, drainage of $\mathrm{pH}$ near 0 is dominated by *Leptospirillum* and *Ferroplasma*. Prevention aims at keeping oxygen and water away from the sulfides (flooding, covering, sealing); treatment neutralizes the water with lime or passes it through constructed wetlands in which sulfate reducers precipitate the metals as sulfides.

## 30.5. Iron and Manganese

Iron and manganese each cycle between a reduced, soluble form ($\ce{Fe^2+}$, $\ce{Mn^2+}$) and an oxidized, insoluble one (iron(III) oxyhydroxides, manganese(IV) oxides). At neutral $\mathrm{pH}$, $\ce{Fe^2+}$ is oxidized abiotically by oxygen within minutes, so neutrophilic iron oxidizers must live where it is supplied from below and oxygen is low: microaerophilic *Gallionella* and *Leptothrix* in groundwater seeps and pipes, where they leave twisted stalks and sheaths of iron oxides, and the marine Zetaproteobacteria (*Mariprofundus*) at hydrothermal iron mats. Iron(II) is also oxidized anaerobically, by nitrate-reducing bacteria and by anoxygenic photoferrotrophs ([7.11.6](/7.11.6#sec-6.2.6)). At acidic $\mathrm{pH}$, where $\ce{Fe^2+}$ is stable in air, acidophiles take over (30.4).

In anoxic sediments and soils, iron(III) and manganese(IV) oxides are respired by **dissimilatory metal reduction** ([7.11.7](/7.11.7#sec-7.3.4)) by *Geobacter*, *Shewanella* and many others, which transfer electrons to the insoluble minerals by **extracellular electron transfer**, through outer-membrane cytochromes, conductive filaments or soluble electron shuttles; humic substances in soil act as such shuttles. Iron reduction is a major pathway of organic matter oxidation in iron-rich sediments and, because iron(III) oxides bind phosphate and trace metals, it releases them when the oxides dissolve (30.3.3).

Before oxygenic photosynthesis, the ocean was anoxic and rich in dissolved iron(II). Its oxidation, by oxygen from cyanobacteria and possibly by photoferrotrophs, precipitated the banded iron formations, the world's main iron ores, deposited mainly between about 3.8 and 1.8 billion years ago, and their history is tied to the rise of oxygen in the **Great Oxidation Event** ([7.10.27](/7.10.27#sec-27.5.3)). Today the opposite problem prevails: in about a third of the surface ocean, the high-nutrient, low-chlorophyll regions such as the Southern Ocean, phytoplankton growth is limited by iron, as ship-borne iron fertilization experiments since the 1990s have shown by inducing blooms; marine bacteria compete for iron with **siderophores** ([7.11.4](/7.11.4#sec-4.3.5)).

## 30.6. Phosphorus, Calcium and Silicon

Phosphorus has no significant gaseous form; it is released from rocks (apatite, a calcium phosphate) only by weathering, and its cycle is a one-way flow from land to ocean sediments, closed only by geological uplift over millions of years. Microorganisms mineralize organic phosphorus with phosphatases, solubilize mineral phosphate with organic acids, accumulate polyphosphate (**metachromatic granules**, [7.11.3](/7.11.3#sec-3.2.1)) and, in phosphate-poor ocean waters, use phosphonates and replace phospholipids in their membranes with sulfolipids. Because phosphorus is scarce, it often limits production in fresh water and is the target of nutrient removal in wastewater treatment (30.9.4). Mined phosphate rock, the source of fertilizer phosphorus, is a finite resource concentrated in a few countries, which makes the recovery of phosphorus from wastewater of growing interest.

Calcium and silicon cycle largely through the skeletons of marine microorganisms. Coccolithophores, such as *Emiliania huxleyi*, cover themselves with plates of calcium carbonate (coccoliths), and **foraminifera** build calcareous shells; their sinking remains form chalk and limestone and remove carbon from the ocean over geological time. **Diatoms** make cell walls of hydrated silica and dominate the marine silicon cycle ([7.11.20](/7.11.20#sec-20.5.1)); where silicate is exhausted, diatom blooms end. Ocean acidification by anthropogenic $\ce{CO2}$, which has lowered the $\mathrm{pH}$ of surface seawater by about 0.1 since preindustrial times, reduces the saturation of seawater with calcium carbonate and so hinders calcification.

## 30.7. Mercury and Other Metals

Mercury reaches the environment from volcanoes, coal combustion and gold mining, mostly as the vapor of metallic mercury, $\mathrm{Hg^0}$, which is oxidized in the atmosphere and deposited as $\ce{Hg^2+}$. In anoxic sediments and wetlands, microorganisms carrying the genes *hgcA* and *hgcB* (a corrinoid protein and a ferredoxin; Parks et al. 2013) methylate $\ce{Hg^2+}$ to methylmercury, $\ce{CH3Hg+}$, which is the main toxic form: it is lipid-soluble, crosses membranes, is retained in tissues and so accumulates up the food chain (biomagnification), reaching in predatory fish concentrations about a million times those in the water. The methylators are mainly sulfate-reducing and iron-reducing bacteria (largely Thermodesulfobacteriota, the former Deltaproteobacteria), methanogens and some fermenting Bacillota. The poisoning of the population of Minamata in Japan in the 1950s by methylmercury in fish, from the effluent of a chemical plant, made the danger known.

Many bacteria resist mercury with the genes of the *mer* operon, usually located on plasmids and transposons (such as Tn*21* and Tn*501*), which often also carry antibiotic resistance genes ([7.11.13](/7.11.13)). MerT and MerP take up $\ce{Hg^2+}$; the mercuric reductase MerA reduces it with NADPH to volatile $\mathrm{Hg^0}$, which diffuses out of the cell; the organomercurial lyase MerB cleaves methylmercury and other organomercurials to $\ce{Hg^2+}$ for MerA; and the regulator MerR represses the operon in the absence of mercury and activates it when $\ce{Hg^2+}$ binds. Similar detoxification systems exist for arsenic (reduction of arsenate and efflux of arsenite, or methylation), cadmium, copper and other metals.

## 30.8. Microorganisms and Global Change

Human activity has changed the major cycles faster than at any time in recorded history: the $\ce{CO2}$ concentration of the atmosphere has risen from about $280\,\mathrm{ppm}$ before industrialization to over $420\,\mathrm{ppm}$, methane has more than doubled and $\ce{N2O}$ has risen by about a quarter; together they have warmed the Earth's surface by about $1.2\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$. Microorganisms are both sources and sinks of the three gases, and their activity responds to temperature, moisture and nutrients:

- Warming accelerates the decomposition of soil organic matter and of thawing permafrost, releasing $\ce{CO2}$ and methane (30.2.1); in wetlands, methanogenesis is more sensitive to temperature than methane oxidation, so warmer wetlands are expected to emit more methane.
- Fertilization and the intensification of agriculture increase emissions of $\ce{N2O}$ from soils (30.3.2) and of methane from rice and livestock.
- The warming ocean holds less oxygen and mixes less: oxygen minimum zones expand, increasing nitrogen loss and $\ce{N2O}$ production, and stratification reduces the nutrient supply to surface phytoplankton.
- Microbial processes also offer ways to reduce emissions: methanotrophs in landfill covers, inhibitors of ruminal methanogenesis, $\ce{N2O}$-reducing bacteria in soils, and the conversion of waste to biogas instead of methane release.

How much these feedbacks will amplify warming is uncertain, because the responses of whole communities are not simple sums of the responses of their members; a consensus statement of microbiologists has argued that microorganisms must be included explicitly in climate research and policy (Cavicchioli et al. 2019).

## 30.9. Wastewater Treatment

### 30.9.1. Biochemical Oxygen Demand

Domestic sewage and industrial wastewater contain organic matter, nitrogen, phosphorus and pathogens. Discharged untreated, they deplete the oxygen of rivers and lakes ([7.11.29](/7.11.29#sec-29.8)), cause eutrophication and spread disease. Treatment uses microorganisms to remove the organic matter and nutrients and is judged by how much oxygen demand it removes.

**Definition 30.9.1 (Biochemical Oxygen Demand)** The **biochemical oxygen demand** (BOD) is the amount of dissolved oxygen consumed by microorganisms in oxidizing the biodegradable organic (and oxidizable inorganic) matter in a water sample, measured in a sealed bottle in the dark, usually over 5 days at $20\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ ($\mathrm{BOD}_5$), in milligrams of $\ce{O2}$ per liter.

Domestic sewage has a $\mathrm{BOD}_5$ of about $200\,\mathrm{mg\,L^{-1}}$, effluents of dairies and food industries can exceed $1000\,\mathrm{mg\,L^{-1}}$, and treated effluent typically has $10$–$30\,\mathrm{mg\,L^{-1}}$ after secondary treatment and less after further treatment. The chemical oxygen demand (COD), measured by oxidation with dichromate in acid, includes nondegradable organic matter and is obtained in hours; total organic carbon (TOC) and dissolved oxygen (DO) are other routine measures of water quality.

### 30.9.2. Primary, Secondary and Tertiary Treatment

Treatment proceeds in stages. Primary treatment removes coarse and settleable solids by screens, grit chambers and sedimentation tanks, about a third of the BOD. Secondary treatment removes most of the dissolved and colloidal organic matter biologically, by aerobic oxidation to $\ce{CO2}$ and conversion into microbial biomass that can be settled out. Tertiary (advanced) treatment removes nitrogen and phosphorus (30.9.4), and disinfection by chlorine, ultraviolet light or ozone reduces the number of pathogens before discharge. The sludge produced is digested anaerobically (30.9.5).

**Definition 30.9.2 (Activated Sludge)** **Activated sludge** is the flocculent microbial biomass, bacteria embedded with protists in a matrix of extracellular polymers, that is kept suspended in an aerated tank to oxidize the organic matter of wastewater, separated from the treated water by settling in a clarifier and largely returned to the tank.

The flocs, formed by bacteria such as *Zoogloea* and many others with their extracellular polysaccharides, adsorb particles and colloids and take up the dissolved organic matter; ciliates and other protists graze on free bacteria and clarify the effluent. Wastewater stays in the aeration tank for a few hours (the hydraulic retention time), but the sludge is recycled and stays much longer. When filamentous bacteria (such as *Microthrix parvicella* and *Sphaerotilus natans*) overgrow the floc formers, the sludge settles poorly and escapes with the effluent, a failure called sludge bulking. In fixed-film processes, trickling filters and rotating biological contactors, the same organisms grow as **biofilms** on stones or plastic supports over which the wastewater flows. Newer processes select dense granules of $1$–$2\,\mathrm{mm}$ (aerobic granular sludge), which settle ten times faster than flocs and contain aerobic nitrifiers in their outer layer and denitrifiers in their anoxic interior, so that one reactor removes organic matter and nitrogen together.

### 30.9.3. The Sludge Age

An activated sludge plant is a chemostat ([7.6.39](/7.6.39#sec-39.1.3), [7.11.8](/7.11.8#sec-8.5.1)) with biomass recycle. The recycle uncouples the time the biomass spends in the system from the time the water spends there, and this is what lets slowly growing organisms stay in the plant.

**Definition 30.9.3 (Solids Retention Time)** The **solids retention time** (SRT, sludge age) of a biological treatment plant is the average time biomass stays in the system: the total biomass in the reactor divided by the biomass removed per day in the waste sludge and the effluent. The hydraulic retention time (HRT) is the reactor volume divided by the flow of wastewater.

At steady state the biomass of a population must grow as fast as it is removed. With growth on a limiting substrate at the Monod rate $\mu = \mu_{\max}S/(K_{\mathrm{s}} + S)$ and a decay rate $b$ for endogenous respiration and death, the net growth rate must equal the inverse of the SRT, $\theta_X$:
$$
\frac{\mu_{\max}S}{K_{\mathrm{s}} + S} - b = \frac{1}{\theta_X}.
$$

**Formula 30.9.1 (Effluent Concentration and Minimum SRT)** The steady-state substrate concentration in a completely mixed activated sludge reactor depends only on the SRT and the kinetic parameters of the organisms, not on the influent concentration or the HRT:
$$
S = K_{\mathrm{s}}\,\frac{1 + b\,\theta_X}{\theta_X(\mu_{\max} - b) - 1}.
$$
The organisms are washed out when $S$ reaches the influent concentration $S_0$, that is below the minimum SRT $\theta_X^{\min} = \bigl(\mu_{\max}S_0/(K_{\mathrm{s}} + S_0) - b\bigr)^{-1}$.

B, C: steady-state effluent concentration against SRT from Formula 30.9.1 at 20 °C (solid) and 10 °C (dashed), with typical design kinetic parameters: heterotrophs on BOD (μmax = 6 d⁻¹, Ks = 20 mg L⁻¹, b = 0.12 d⁻¹; influent 200 mg L⁻¹) and ammonia-oxidizing bacteria on ammonium (μmax = 0.9 d⁻¹, Ks = 0.5 mg N L⁻¹, b = 0.17 d⁻¹; influent 35 mg N L⁻¹); temperature corrections μmax × 1.07^(T−20), b × 1.04^(T−20). **Example 30.9.1** For ammonia-oxidizing bacteria at $20\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ ($\mu_{\max} = 0.9\,\mathrm{d^{-1}}$, $K_{\mathrm{s}} = 0.5\,\mathrm{mg\,N\,L^{-1}}$, $b = 0.17\,\mathrm{d^{-1}}$), an SRT of $5\,\mathrm{d}$ gives
$$
S = 0.5 \times \frac{1 + 0.17\times5}{5\times(0.9 - 0.17) - 1} = 0.5 \times \frac{1.85}{2.65} \approx 0.35\,\mathrm{mg\,N\,L^{-1}}.
$$
At $10\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$, $\mu_{\max}$ falls to about $0.46\,\mathrm{d^{-1}}$ and $b$ to about $0.11\,\mathrm{d^{-1}}$, and the same SRT gives $S \approx 1.1\,\mathrm{mg\,N\,L^{-1}}$; at $3\,\mathrm{d}$ the nitrifiers are lost altogether. Plants that must nitrify in winter therefore run at SRTs of 10–20 days, with correspondingly large tanks, whereas the removal of BOD alone needs only a few days.

The model explains several practical rules: the SRT, set by the rate of sludge wasting, is the main control variable of a plant; nitrification fails first when the temperature falls or the SRT is shortened; and organisms with low growth rates, such as nitrifiers and anammox bacteria, need long sludge ages or biofilms and granules that retain them. It also shows why the effluent concentration is independent of the influent concentration as long as the organisms are not washed out: a higher load produces more biomass, not a higher effluent concentration.

### 30.9.4. Removal of Nitrogen and Phosphorus

Nitrogen is removed biologically by combining nitrification with denitrification. In a common design, wastewater first enters an anoxic tank, where denitrifiers oxidize its organic matter with nitrate recycled from the aerated tank that follows, in which nitrifiers convert ammonium to nitrate; the nitrogen leaves as $\ce{N2}$. For wastewater rich in ammonium and poor in organic carbon, such as the liquor from sludge digesters, processes based on **anammox** are used: about half of the ammonium is oxidized to nitrite (partial nitritation), and anammox bacteria convert the remaining ammonium with the nitrite to $\ce{N2}$, saving about $60\,\%$ of the aeration energy and all external organic carbon; the first full-scale anammox reactor began operating in Rotterdam in 2002.

**Definition 30.9.4 (Enhanced Biological Phosphorus Removal)** **Enhanced biological phosphorus removal** (EBPR) removes phosphate from wastewater by cycling activated sludge between an anaerobic and an aerobic zone, which selects polyphosphate-accumulating organisms: under anaerobic conditions they take up short-chain fatty acids and store them as polyhydroxyalkanoates, using energy from the hydrolysis of stored polyphosphate and releasing phosphate; under aerobic conditions they oxidize the stored carbon, grow and take up more phosphate than they released, storing it as polyphosphate, and the phosphorus leaves the plant with the waste sludge.

The main polyphosphate-accumulating organisms are the uncultured *Candidatus* Accumulibacter phosphatis (Rhodocyclaceae, formerly classed with the Betaproteobacteria) and actinomycetes of the genus *Tetrasphaera*; their competitors for the fatty acids, glycogen-accumulating organisms that do not store phosphate, can cause the process to fail. Phosphorus can also be removed chemically, by precipitation with iron or aluminum salts. Many pharmaceuticals, hormones and other micropollutants pass through conventional plants only partly degraded, and some plants now add treatment with ozone or activated carbon to remove them.

### 30.9.5. Anaerobic Digestion

**Definition 30.9.5 (Anaerobic Digestion)** **Anaerobic digestion** is the microbial degradation of organic matter, such as sewage sludge, manure, food waste or concentrated industrial wastewater, by the anaerobic food chain in a closed reactor, producing biogas (about $55$–$70\,\%$ methane and $30$–$45\,\%$ $\ce{CO2}$) and a stabilized residue (digestate).

Digesters run the steps of the anaerobic food chain (30.2.2), hydrolysis, acidogenesis, acetogenesis and methanogenesis (older texts combine the middle two into one acid-forming stage and speak of three stages), usually at about $35\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ (mesophilic) or $55\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ (thermophilic, faster and with better killing of pathogens), with retention times of two to four weeks for sludge. The methanogens grow slowest and are the most sensitive to acid, ammonia and toxic compounds; an overload of degradable substrate produces fatty acids faster than they can be consumed, the $\mathrm{pH}$ falls and gas production stops. High-rate reactors for dissolved industrial wastes, such as the upflow anaerobic sludge blanket (UASB) reactor, retain the slow-growing organisms as dense granules through which the wastewater flows upward, so that the SRT greatly exceeds the HRT, the same principle as in 30.9.3. The biogas is burned to heat the digester and produce electricity, so that a large treatment plant can cover much of its own energy demand.

## 30.10. Drinking Water

Drinking water is taken from groundwater, which is often naturally filtered and of good quality, or from rivers and lakes, which must be treated. A conventional treatment plant combines physical, chemical and biological steps:

1. Coagulation and flocculation: aluminum or iron salts are added and the water is stirred gently, so that particles, microorganisms and colored organic matter aggregate into flocs.
2. Sedimentation, and filtration through sand or activated carbon, which removes the remaining particles; in slow sand filters, the biofilm on the sand surface degrades organic matter and traps microorganisms.
3. Disinfection, most commonly by chlorination (chlorine gas or hypochlorite), sometimes by chloramine (chlorine combined with ammonia, which persists longer in the pipes), ozone or ultraviolet light; a residual of disinfectant is maintained in the distribution system.

Each step removes a fraction of the pathogens; the combination ("multiple barriers") makes the water safe even if one step fails. Not all pathogens are equally sensitive: the oocysts of *Cryptosporidium* resist chlorination and must be removed by filtration or inactivated by ultraviolet light; in 1993, the failure of filtration at a treatment plant in Milwaukee caused the largest documented waterborne outbreak in the United States, with more than 400 000 people ill. Chlorination can also produce disinfection by-products (trihalomethanes) from natural organic matter, a reason for removing organic matter before disinfection.

**Definition 30.10.1 (Indicator Organism)** An **indicator organism** is a microorganism whose presence in water or food indicates fecal contamination and thus the possible presence of intestinal pathogens; it should be present in large numbers in feces, absent from unpolluted water, survive in water at least as long as the pathogens, and be easy to detect.

Pathogens are diverse and often rare, so water is not tested for them routinely but for indicators. The classical indicators are the coliform bacteria (gram-negative, non-spore-forming rods that ferment lactose with acid and gas within 48 hours at $35\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$, *Escherichia coli* and related Enterobacterales), of which the thermotolerant ("fecal") coliforms grow at $44.5\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$. Because some coliforms live in soil and water, *E. coli* itself is now the preferred indicator of fecal contamination; drinking water standards such as those of the World Health Organization require that no *E. coli* be detectable in any $100\,\mathrm{mL}$ sample. Enterococci are used for recreational and marine waters, where they survive longer than *E. coli*. The indicators are counted by membrane filtration, in which $100\,\mathrm{mL}$ of water is filtered and the filter incubated on a selective, differential medium, by the **most probable number** method ([7.11.8](/7.11.8#sec-8.6.3)), or by enzyme substrate tests in which coliforms cleave a chromogenic galactoside (β-galactosidase) and *E. coli* a fluorogenic glucuronide (β-glucuronidase). Indicators are imperfect: viruses and protozoan cysts survive disinfection better than *E. coli*, so their absence does not guarantee the absence of all pathogens.

Within the distribution system, most bacteria live in **biofilms** on pipe walls, where they are protected from the disinfectant residual. Such biofilms, and the warm water of buildings, can harbor opportunistic pathogens such as *Legionella pneumophila* ([7.11.17](/7.11.17#sec-17.5.4)), nontuberculous mycobacteria and *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*; *Legionella* multiplies inside free-living amoebae, which protect it, and infection occurs by inhaling aerosols from showers and cooling towers. Keeping hot water above about $55\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ and avoiding stagnant pipes are the main preventive measures.

## 30.11. Bioremediation

**Definition 30.11.1 (Bioremediation)** **Bioremediation** is the use of microorganisms, or less often plants, to remove, degrade or immobilize pollutants in soil, sediment and water, either by stimulating the activity of native organisms (biostimulation, for example by adding nutrients, electron acceptors or donors) or by adding organisms with the required activity (bioaugmentation).

Most organic pollutants of natural origin are degradable in principle; whether they are degraded depends on the conditions. Crude oil is a mixture of thousands of hydrocarbons that many bacteria and fungi oxidize aerobically ([7.11.7](/7.11.7#sec-7.5.3)), but in the sea their growth is limited by nitrogen and phosphorus. After the grounding of the tanker *Exxon Valdez* in Alaska in 1989, fertilizer applied to oiled beaches stimulated native hydrocarbon degraders and accelerated the cleanup. After the blowout of the Deepwater Horizon well in the Gulf of Mexico in 2010, a plume of dissolved hydrocarbons and gas at about $1100\,\mathrm{m}$ depth was rapidly colonized by blooms of gammaproteobacteria (Oceanospirillales, then *Colwellia* and *Cycloclasticus*), which consumed much of the propane, ethane and lighter hydrocarbons within weeks; specialists such as *Alcanivorax borkumensis* bloom on surface oil.

Synthetic compounds new to nature are harder to degrade.

**Definition 30.11.2 (Xenobiotic)** A **xenobiotic** is a synthetic chemical compound not produced by living organisms, such as a pesticide, a polychlorinated biphenyl (PCB), a chlorinated solvent, an explosive or a synthetic polymer.

Xenobiotics structurally close to natural compounds are often degraded, but many, especially those with several halogen substituents, persist for years; DDT and PCBs are examples. Some are degraded only fortuitously.

**Definition 30.11.3 (Cometabolism)** **Cometabolism** is the transformation of a compound by an enzyme of an organism that does not gain energy or carbon from it, because the enzyme, induced for another substrate, has a broad specificity; for example, methane monooxygenase of methanotrophs oxidizes trichloroethylene while the cells grow on methane.

Chlorinated ethenes, the solvents tetrachloroethylene (PCE) and trichloroethylene (TCE), are among the most common groundwater contaminants. In anoxic aquifers they are dechlorinated stepwise, $\text{PCE} \to \text{TCE} \to \textit{cis}\text{-dichloroethylene} \to \text{vinyl chloride} \to \text{ethene}$, each step replacing a chlorine by hydrogen; the bacteria that carry out these steps conserve energy by organohalide respiration ([7.11.7](/7.11.7#sec-7.3.5)), using $\ce{H2}$ from fermentation as electron donor. Only *Dehalococcoides mccartyi* completes the reduction to harmless ethene; the accumulation of the carcinogen vinyl chloride where it is absent is the main risk, and adding cultures containing *Dehalococcoides* together with an electron donor is a widely used bioaugmentation.

Metals and radionuclides cannot be destroyed, but their mobility depends on their oxidation state, so microorganisms can immobilize them. Soluble uranium(VI) in groundwater is reduced to insoluble uranium(IV) as the mineral uraninite ($\ce{UO2}$) by metal-reducing bacteria such as *Geobacter*; in field experiments at a former uranium mill site in Colorado, acetate injected into the aquifer stimulated *Geobacter* and lowered uranium in the groundwater, until iron(III) was exhausted and sulfate reducers took over. Toxic chromate, chromium(VI), is reduced to less soluble chromium(III) in the same way. Immobilization lasts only as long as the reducing conditions do.

Synthetic plastics are the largest class of persistent xenobiotics. Polyethylene, polypropylene and polystyrene, with carbon–carbon backbones, are degraded extremely slowly if at all. Polyesters are more accessible: the bacterium *Ideonella sakaiensis*, isolated from a bottle recycling site, hydrolyzes poly(ethylene terephthalate) with the enzymes PETase and MHETase and grows on the products (Yoshida et al. 2016), and engineered PET-hydrolyzing enzymes are being developed for enzymatic recycling. Biodegradable plastics made by bacteria, such as polyhydroxyalkanoates, are described in [7.11.3](/7.11.3). Microbial tests also detect pollution: the Ames test measures mutagenicity ([7.11.13](/7.11.13#sec-13.4)), and the decrease of the light emitted by the luminescent marine bacterium *Aliivibrio fischeri* is a rapid general test of toxicity ([7.11.17](/7.11.17#sec-17.5.3)).

## 30.12. Biomining, Corrosion and Microbial Electrochemistry

### 30.12.1. Bioleaching

**Definition 30.12.1 (Bioleaching)** **Bioleaching** (microbial leaching, biomining) is the extraction of metals from low-grade sulfide ores by acidophilic iron- and sulfur-oxidizing microorganisms, which oxidize the sulfide minerals directly or regenerate the ferric iron that oxidizes them, releasing the metals into an acidic solution from which they are recovered.

Bioleaching uses the same chemistry as acid mine drainage (30.4), deliberately. Low-grade copper ore is piled in large heaps or dumps and irrigated with dilute sulfuric acid; *Acidithiobacillus ferrooxidans* and *Leptospirillum*, and in the hotter interior of the heap moderately thermophilic and thermoacidophilic bacteria and archaea (*Sulfobacillus*, *Acidianus*, *Sulfolobus*), regenerate the ferric iron that oxidizes the copper sulfides, for example covellite:
$$
\ce{CuS + 8 Fe^3+ + 4 H2O -> Cu^2+ + 8 Fe^2+ + SO4^2- + 8 H+}.
$$
The copper-rich solution is collected, and copper is recovered by solvent extraction and electrowinning or, in older operations, by cementation on scrap iron, $\ce{Cu^2+ + Fe -> Cu + Fe^2+}$. About a quarter of the world's copper has been obtained this way (Madigan et al. 2019). In biooxidation of refractory gold ores, in stirred tank reactors, the microorganisms dissolve the pyrite and arsenopyrite in which fine gold particles are locked, so that the gold can afterwards be extracted with cyanide; uranium, cobalt, nickel and zinc are also recovered by bioleaching.

### 30.12.2. Microbially Influenced Corrosion and Biodeterioration

Microbial activity accelerates the electrochemical **corrosion** of iron and steel in pipelines, ship hulls, offshore platforms, storage tanks and water pipes, causing losses of billions of dollars a year. Sulfate-reducing bacteria are the main culprits in anoxic environments: their sulfide reacts with iron to form iron sulfides, which promote further corrosion, and some sulfate reducers and methanogens take electrons directly from metallic iron by extracellular electron uptake, oxidizing it faster than abiotic corrosion would. Under biofilms, differences in oxygen concentration between covered and exposed areas create local electrochemical cells. In concrete sewer pipes, sulfide released by sulfate reducers in the wastewater escapes into the headspace, where sulfur-oxidizing bacteria (*Acidithiobacillus thiooxidans*) on the moist crown of the pipe oxidize it to sulfuric acid, which dissolves the concrete (crown corrosion). More generally, the destruction of materials by microorganisms is called biodeterioration: molds grow on damp textiles, leather and paper, wood-decaying fungi degrade the cellulose and lignin of timber ([7.11.21](/7.11.21#sec-21.6.1)), and stone buildings and monuments are attacked by biofilms of cyanobacteria, algae, fungi and lichens that produce acids and chelating compounds and penetrate the stone.

### 30.12.3. Microbial Fuel Cells

Bacteria capable of **extracellular electron transfer** ([7.11.7](/7.11.7#sec-7.3.4)) can use an electrode as electron acceptor. In a microbial fuel cell, a biofilm of *Geobacter* or other exoelectrogens on the anode oxidizes organic matter and transfers the electrons to the anode; they flow through an external circuit to the cathode, where oxygen is reduced, generating a current. The power densities achieved, of the order of watts per square meter of electrode, are too low for large-scale electricity production, but microbial fuel cells power sensors on the seafloor (sediment fuel cells), serve as biosensors for BOD and toxicity, and, in related microbial electrolysis cells and electrosynthesis, use electricity to drive the production of hydrogen, methane or acetate from wastewater or $\ce{CO2}$.

## Summary

- Biogeochemical cycles (Definition 30.1.1) are chains of metabolisms between reservoirs; small, fast biological reservoirs sit on large, slow geological ones; residence time $\tau = M/F$; the Redfield ratio $106:16:1$ couples C, N and P.

| Cycle | Key microbial processes | Global significance |
|---|---|---|
| Carbon (Definition 30.2.1) | photosynthesis, decomposition, anaerobic food chain, methanogenesis, methanotrophy | NPP about 105 Pg C yr⁻¹; soil and permafrost carbon feedback; biological pump (Definition 30.2.2); about two thirds of methane emissions are microbial, and microbial filters consume most methane formed in sediments |
| Nitrogen | fixation, ammonification (Definition 30.3.1), nitrification, denitrification, anammox, DNRA | human fixation (Haber–Bosch, crops, combustion) about equals natural fixation; N₂O; eutrophication (Definition 30.3.2) with hysteresis |
| Sulfur (Definition 30.4.1) | sulfate reduction, sulfide and sulfur oxidation, disproportionation, DMS release | sulfide–pyrite burial couples S, C and O₂; acid mine drainage (Definition 30.4.2) |
| Iron, manganese | neutrophilic and acidophilic oxidation, dissimilatory reduction | banded iron formations; iron limitation of the ocean; phosphate release from sediments |
| P, Ca, Si | mineralization, polyphosphate; coccoliths, foraminifera, diatom frustules | P limits fresh water; calcification and ocean acidification |
| Mercury | methylation (*hgcAB*), *mer* reduction and demethylation | biomagnification of methylmercury |

- Microorganisms are sources and sinks of CO₂, CH₄ and N₂O, and their responses to warming, fertilization and deoxygenation are feedbacks on global change.
- Wastewater treatment reduces BOD (Definition 30.9.1) with activated sludge (Definition 30.9.2) or biofilms; the solids retention time (Definition 30.9.3) determines effluent quality and the retention of slow growers such as nitrifiers (Formula 30.9.1, Example 30.9.1); nitrogen is removed by nitrification–denitrification or anammox, phosphorus by EBPR (Definition 30.9.4); sludge is converted to biogas by anaerobic digestion (Definition 30.9.5).
- Drinking water is protected by multiple barriers and monitored with indicator organisms (Definition 30.10.1), above all *E. coli*.
- Bioremediation (Definition 30.11.1) degrades oil and many xenobiotics (Definition 30.11.2), sometimes only by cometabolism (Definition 30.11.3); *Dehalococcoides* dechlorinates solvents to ethene; metals are immobilized by reduction. Bioleaching (Definition 30.12.1) recovers copper and gold; sulfate and sulfur bacteria corrode metals and concrete; exoelectrogens power microbial fuel cells.

## Questions

1. Why is most of the methane produced in marine sediments not emitted to the atmosphere, whereas wetlands and rice paddies are major sources?

   **Answer** In marine sediments seawater sulfate allows sulfate reducers to outcompete methanogens near the surface, so methane forms only deeper, and as it diffuses up through the sulfate–methane transition zone ANME–sulfate reducer consortia oxidize most of it anaerobically; aerobic methanotrophs remove much of the rest. Freshwater wetlands and paddies have little sulfate, so methanogenesis is the terminal process near the surface, the anaerobic filter is weak, and methane escapes by diffusion, ebullition and through the aerenchyma of plants, bypassing the thin oxic layer.

2. Compare nitrification and denitrification: organisms, conditions, and their effect on the nitrogen available to plants. Why do fertilized soils emit $\ce{N2O}$?

   **Answer** Nitrification is aerobic chemolithotrophic oxidation of ammonium to nitrate by ammonia-oxidizing bacteria and archaea and nitrite oxidizers (or comammox); it converts retained ammonium to mobile nitrate but keeps nitrogen in fixed form. Denitrification is anaerobic respiration of nitrate by facultative heterotrophs to $\ce{N2O}$ and $\ce{N2}$ and removes fixed nitrogen. Fertilized soils supply ammonium and nitrate; $\ce{N2O}$ is a by-product of nitrification (hydroxylamine oxidation, nitrifier denitrification) and an intermediate of denitrification that escapes when NosZ is absent or inhibited by oxygen or low $\mathrm{pH}$.

3. Write the reactions of acid mine drainage and explain why microorganisms control its rate even though ferric iron, not the microbes, oxidizes most of the pyrite.

   **Answer** $\ce{FeS2 + 7/2 O2 + H2O -> Fe^2+ + 2 SO4^2- + 2 H+}$ starts the process; $\ce{FeS2 + 14 Fe^3+ + 8 H2O -> 15 Fe^2+ + 2 SO4^2- + 16 H+}$ is fast; it consumes ferric iron, which must be regenerated by $\ce{Fe^2+ + 1/4 O2 + H+ -> Fe^3+ + 1/2 H2O}$. Below $\mathrm{pH}$ 3.5 this last reaction is very slow abiotically, so the rate of the whole cycle is set by acidophilic iron oxidizers (*Acidithiobacillus*, *Leptospirillum*, *Ferroplasma*), which accelerate it by orders of magnitude.

4. Using Formula 30.9.1, explain why an activated sludge plant that removes BOD well in summer may stop nitrifying in winter, and what the operator can do.

   **Answer** Heterotrophs grow fast and wash out only at SRTs well below a day, whereas nitrifiers grow slowly and their washout SRT roughly doubles from 20 to 10 °C (about 1.4 to 3 days in Example 30.9.1); at an SRT adequate in summer, the winter effluent ammonium rises sharply or nitrifiers are lost. The operator increases the SRT by wasting less sludge (keeping more biomass, which may require more tank volume or aeration) or retains nitrifiers on biofilm carriers.

5. Why does reducing the phosphorus input to a eutrophic lake often fail to restore clear water quickly, according to the model of 30.3.3?

   **Answer** Anoxic bottom water releases phosphate from iron(III)-bound sediment phosphorus, a positive feedback that makes recycling rise steeply with the phosphorus level. The equilibrium curve then has two stable branches, and a lake on the turbid branch stays there until the loading falls below the lower fold, far below the loading at which it turned turbid (hysteresis); restoration therefore needs a large reduction of external loading or direct action on internal loading (aeration, phosphate precipitation, sediment removal).
