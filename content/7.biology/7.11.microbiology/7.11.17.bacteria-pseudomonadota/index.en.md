The chapters on cell structure, metabolism and growth described what bacteria can do; this chapter and the next ask who does it. Diversity is surveyed here by phylogeny, phylum by phylum and class by class, because the phylogenetic tree is the only classification that keeps related organisms together (7.11.16), and the functions met in earlier chapters (anoxygenic photosynthesis, chemolithotrophy, nitrogen fixation, endospores, predation) are then placed on the tree where they occur. The phylum Pseudomonadota, formerly called Proteobacteria, comes first because it is the largest and most varied group of cultured bacteria: it contains *Escherichia coli* and most of the Gram-negative pathogens, the rhizobia of legume nodules, purple phototrophs, chemolithotrophs of the sulfur, iron and nitrogen cycles, and the relatives of the mitochondrion. Until recently it also contained the Delta- and Epsilonproteobacteria, which genome-based taxonomy has made phyla of their own; they are treated at the end of the chapter. Gram-positive bacteria and the remaining phyla follow in [7.11.18](/7.11.18), the archaea in [7.11.19](/7.11.19). The early history of bacterial lineages, the root of the tree and the role of lateral gene transfer are treated in [7.10.27](/7.10.27), and the classification and naming of prokaryotes in [7.11.16](/7.11.16); both are used here and not repeated.

## 17.1. Organizing Bacterial Diversity

### 17.1.1. Two Axes: Phylogeny and Function

A bacterium can be described by its place on the tree and by what it does, and the two descriptions do not coincide. Many physiological groups that once served as taxa are spread across several phyla: sulfate reducers occur among the Thermodesulfobacteriota and the Bacillota, anoxygenic phototrophs in at least six phyla ([7.11.6](/7.11.6)), nitrogen fixers in most of them, and the ability to oxidize ammonia in two classes of Pseudomonadota, in the Nitrospirota and in the archaea. Conversely, a single phylum can contain phototrophs, chemolithotrophs and pathogens. Functional traits that depend on a few genes, such as a rhodopsin, a nitrogenase or a set of genes for sulfur oxidation, move between lineages by **horizontal gene transfer** and are lost when not needed; traits that depend on many interacting genes and on the architecture of the cell, such as the Gram-positive wall, the outer membrane or the endospore, are inherited vertically and mark whole lineages.

The chapters on diversity therefore follow the tree, and for each group give four kinds of information: cell form and envelope; energy metabolism and relation to oxygen; habitat; and the members that matter for other parts of microbiology or for people. The physiology itself is explained where it was first introduced and only linked here.

### 17.1.2. How Many Phyla?

The Earth holds about $10^{30}$ bacterial and archaeal cells, most of them in five habitats: the open ocean, the upper oceanic sediment, the deep oceanic subsurface, soil and the deep continental subsurface (Flemming and Wuertz 2019). How many lineages they represent depends on how one looks. Sequencing of 16S rRNA genes from the environment revealed more than 80 bacterial phyla, more than half of them without a single cultured member, and genome-resolved metagenomics has since raised the number of candidate phyla to more than a hundred, many of them in the **candidate phyla radiation** ([7.11.18](/7.11.18#sec-18.1.2)). Cultured species are concentrated in a few phyla: more than 90 % of the characterized species of bacteria belong to four, the Pseudomonadota, **Actinomycetota**, **Bacillota** and **Bacteroidota**, while some phyla that are abundant in nature, such as the Acidobacteriota of soil, have few species in culture. The difference between the number of sequences and the number of cultures is a measure of how much of a group's diversity escapes the laboratory (the **great plate count anomaly**, [7.11.4](/7.11.4#sec-4.5.4)).

## 17.2. The Phylum Pseudomonadota

### 17.2.1. General Features

**Definition 17.2.1 (Pseudomonadota)** The **Pseudomonadota** (formerly Proteobacteria; type genus *Pseudomonas*) are a phylum of Gram-negative bacteria with an outer membrane containing lipopolysaccharide, comprising the classes Alphaproteobacteria and Gammaproteobacteria (with the former Betaproteobacteria) and several smaller classes. It is the largest phylum of cultured bacteria, with about a third of all described species.

The former name Proteobacteria referred to Proteus, the Greek sea god who could take any shape, because the phylum includes every form and nearly every kind of metabolism found in bacteria. Its members are rods, cocci, vibrios, spirilla, filaments, stalked and budding cells; they are chemoorganotrophs, chemolithotrophs and phototrophs; aerobes, **facultative anaerobes**, **microaerophiles** and strict anaerobes. What unites them is a Gram-negative envelope with an **outer membrane** and a common ancestry, established by 16S rRNA in the 1980s and confirmed by genome trees. Nearly all model bacteria of molecular biology except *Bacillus subtilis* belong here, and so do most Gram-negative pathogens of humans, animals and plants.

The classes were defined on 16S rRNA trees and named with Greek letters. Their main members and lifestyles are:

| Class | Typical lifestyles | Representative genera |
|---|---|---|
| Alphaproteobacteria | oligotrophs, phototrophs, methylotrophs, nitrogen-fixing plant symbionts, obligate intracellular parasites | *Rhodobacter*, *Sinorhizobium*, *Agrobacterium*, *Caulobacter*, *Wolbachia*, *Pelagibacter* |
| Betaproteobacteria (Burkholderiales and related orders) | aerobic heterotrophs and chemolithotrophs of soil and water, a few pathogens | *Nitrosomonas*, *Neisseria*, *Bordetella*, *Burkholderia*, *Sphaerotilus* |
| Gammaproteobacteria | the largest class: enteric bacteria, pseudomonads, vibrios, sulfur bacteria, methanotrophs, many pathogens | *Escherichia*, *Salmonella*, *Pseudomonas*, *Vibrio*, *Legionella*, *Chromatium* |
| Zetaproteobacteria | microaerophilic iron oxidizers of marine iron mats | *Mariprofundus* |
| Acidithiobacillia | acidophilic sulfur and iron oxidizers | *Acidithiobacillus* |

### 17.2.2. The Former Delta- and Epsilonproteobacteria

Two further classes, the Deltaproteobacteria (sulfate reducers, myxobacteria and the predator *Bdellovibrio*) and the Epsilonproteobacteria (*Campylobacter*, *Helicobacter* and many vent bacteria), were included in the Proteobacteria because of their 16S sequences. Phylogenies of concatenated proteins did not support this: the "Deltaproteobacteria" fell apart into several unrelated lineages, and neither they nor the Epsilonproteobacteria are the sister groups of the remaining classes. Between 2017 and 2020, Waite and colleagues and the Genome Taxonomy Database (GTDB, [7.11.16](/7.11.16#sec-16.6)) therefore raised them to phyla, and these were given names with the ending -ota ([7.11.16](/7.11.16#sec-16.7.3)): the sulfate reducers and their relatives became the Thermodesulfobacteriota (GTDB name Desulfobacterota), the myxobacteria the Myxococcota, *Bdellovibrio* and relatives the Bdellovibrionota, and the Epsilonproteobacteria the Campylobacterota. GTDB went one step further and placed the former Betaproteobacteria, as the order Burkholderiales, inside the Gammaproteobacteria; the List of Prokaryotic Names with Standing in Nomenclature still lists Betaproteobacteria as a class, and the traditional name remains in common use. The groups are treated below in the traditional order, but with their current names.

## 17.3. Alphaproteobacteria

The Alphaproteobacteria are the second largest class. Many of their members are oligotrophs of nutrient-poor waters and soils, many live in close association with eukaryotes, and their lineage gave rise to the mitochondrion ([7.10.28](/7.10.28#sec-28.2.1)).

### 17.3.1. Phototrophs, Methylotrophs and Acetic Acid Bacteria

The purple nonsulfur bacteria (*Rhodobacter*, *Rhodopseudomonas*, *Rhodospirillum*) carry out **anoxygenic photosynthesis** with a type II reaction center when light is available and no oxygen is present, and grow as aerobic heterotrophs in the dark; *Rhodobacter sphaeroides* and *R. capsulatus* are the classic organisms for the study of the bacterial reaction center ([7.11.6](/7.11.6#sec-6.2.2)). Aerobic anoxygenic phototrophs (*Roseobacter* and its relatives) are common in the surface ocean and use bacteriochlorophyll only as a supplement to respiration ([7.11.6](/7.11.6#sec-6.2.5)). Type II **methanotrophs** (*Methylosinus*, *Methylocystis*) and the pink **methylotrophs** *Methylobacterium* on plant leaves assimilate one-carbon compounds by the serine pathway ([7.11.7](/7.11.7#sec-7.4.3)), and *Nitrobacter* oxidizes nitrite to nitrate ([7.11.7](/7.11.7#sec-7.2.4)).

The acetic acid bacteria (*Acetobacter*, *Gluconobacter*) are obligate aerobes that oxidize ethanol incompletely to acetic acid, an incomplete oxidation used to make vinegar; *Gluconobacter* oxidizes glucose to gluconic acid, and *Komagataeibacter xylinus* secretes cellulose fibrils, the "bacterial cellulose" of kombucha mats and wound dressings. *Zymomonas mobilis*, from fermenting plant sap, ferments glucose to ethanol by the **Entner–Doudoroff pathway** ([7.11.5](/7.11.5#sec-5.2.2)), with a yield of only one ATP per glucose but very high rates.

### 17.3.2. Plant Symbionts and Pathogens: Rhizobia and *Agrobacterium*

The **rhizobia** are soil bacteria that form nitrogen-fixing root nodules on legumes. Most belong to the order Hyphomicrobiales (formerly Rhizobiales) of the Alphaproteobacteria (*Rhizobium leguminosarum*, *Sinorhizobium meliloti*, *Bradyrhizobium japonicum*, *Mesorhizobium loti*), but some Betaproteobacteria (*Paraburkholderia*, *Cupriavidus*) also nodulate legumes, having acquired the nodulation and nitrogen-fixation genes by horizontal transfer; "rhizobium" is therefore a functional, not a phylogenetic, category. Rhizobia have large genomes, often split into a chromosome and megaplasmids or "symbiotic islands" that carry the *nod* and *nif* genes and can be transferred between strains. Host ranges vary: *R. leguminosarum* bv. *trifolii* nodulates only clovers, while *Sinorhizobium fredii* NGR234 nodulates more than a hundred legume genera. The exchange of flavonoids and Nod factors, the infection thread, the differentiation of **bacteroids** inside the **root nodule** and the protection of nitrogenase from oxygen are treated in [7.11.31](/7.11.31) and [7.11.6](/7.11.6#sec-6.6.3).

*Agrobacterium tumefaciens*, a close relative of the rhizobia, causes *crown gall*, tumors at the base of the stems of many dicots. It senses phenolic compounds released by wounded plant tissue through the two-component system VirA–VirG, cuts out a segment, the T-DNA, of its *Ti plasmid* (tumor-inducing plasmid, a large plasmid that also carries the *vir* genes for the transfer), and transfers it as a single strand with the attached protein VirD2 into the plant cell through a **type IV secretion system**; the T-DNA integrates into a plant chromosome and directs the synthesis of plant hormones, which cause the tumor, and of opines, amino acid and sugar derivatives that only the bacterium can use. It is a natural genetic engineer, and disarmed Ti plasmids, with the tumor genes replaced by genes of interest, are the standard vectors for making transgenic plants.

Other members of the order are pathogens of animals: *Brucella* (brucellosis, a zoonosis transmitted through milk and contact with livestock) and *Bartonella* (cat-scratch disease and trench fever), both facultatively intracellular.

### 17.3.3. Obligate Intracellular Bacteria: Rickettsias and *Wolbachia*

The order Rickettsiales consists of bacteria that grow only inside eukaryotic cells. The **rickettsias** are small rods transmitted by arthropods that cause typhus (*Rickettsia prowazekii*, carried by body lice) and the spotted fevers (*R. rickettsii*, the agent of Rocky Mountain spotted fever, carried by ticks). They enter endothelial cells by phagocytosis, escape from the phagosome into the cytoplasm, and grow there; the spotted fever rickettsias polymerize host actin at one pole, as *Listeria* does ([7.11.18](/7.11.18#sec-18.2.3)), and are pushed into neighboring cells. Their genomes are small (about $1.1\,\mathrm{Mb}$ in *R. prowazekii*) and still decaying, with many pseudogenes; they have lost the pathways for amino acid and nucleotide synthesis and glycolysis, import ATP from the host in exchange for ADP through an ATP/ADP translocase, and are "energy parasites" when the host can supply ATP. *Orientia tsutsugamushi* (scrub typhus), *Ehrlichia* and *Anaplasma* are related tick- and mite-borne pathogens. *Coxiella burnetii* (Q fever), once grouped with the rickettsias, is a gammaproteobacterium.

*Wolbachia* lives in the cytoplasm of the germ line of an estimated 40–65 % of insect species, and in spiders, mites, crustaceans and filarial nematodes. It is transmitted through the egg and manipulates the reproduction of its hosts in favor of infected females: by killing male embryos, by feminizing genetic males, by inducing parthenogenesis in wasps, or most often by **cytoplasmic incompatibility** ([Definition 11.6.3](/7.4.11#def-11.6.3)), in which embryos die when an infected male fertilizes an uninfected female. In filarial nematodes it is a mutualist on which the worms depend, so that the antibiotic doxycycline is used against onchocerciasis and lymphatic filariasis. In *Aedes aegypti* the strain *w*Mel blocks the replication of dengue and other viruses, and releases of infected mosquitoes, which spread through the population by cytoplasmic incompatibility, reduced the incidence of dengue by about three quarters in a cluster-randomized trial in Yogyakarta, Indonesia (Utarini and colleagues 2021).

The small genomes of these intracellular bacteria, and the resemblance of the ATP/ADP translocase and the genes for respiration to those of mitochondria, once suggested that the mitochondrion descends from a rickettsia-like parasite; with models that correct for the compositional bias of reduced genomes the question is open ([7.10.28](/7.10.28#sec-28.2.1)), and genome reduction in endosymbionts is discussed in [7.10.18](/7.10.18#sec-18.8.3).

### 17.3.4. Stalked, Budding and Magnetotactic Bacteria

Several Alphaproteobacteria of nutrient-poor waters have appendages or unusual modes of division. *Caulobacter crescentus* divides asymmetrically into a stalked cell, attached to surfaces by an adhesive holdfast at the end of its stalk (a prostheca, an extension of the cell envelope), and a flagellated swarmer cell that must differentiate before it can replicate; its cell cycle is a model of bacterial development ([7.11.12](/7.11.12)). The stalk increases the surface for nutrient uptake in dilute water, and the holdfast is one of the strongest biological adhesives known. *Hyphomicrobium* grows a hypha from the mother cell and forms a bud at its tip, which develops a flagellum and swims away; it is a methylotroph of soils and freshwater.

Magnetotactic bacteria such as *Magnetospirillum magneticum* and *M. gryphiswaldense* are microaerophilic spirilla of the oxic–anoxic interface of sediments and stratified waters. They synthesize **magnetosomes**, membrane-bounded crystals of magnetite ($\ce{Fe3O4}$) arranged in a chain along a filament of the actin-like protein MamK, which give the cell a permanent magnetic moment; the cell is aligned by the geomagnetic field like a compass needle, and because the field is inclined, swimming along it leads up or down toward the preferred oxygen concentration (**magnetotaxis**, [7.11.3](/7.11.3#sec-3.5.2)). The physics of the alignment is treated in [7.6.19](/7.6.19#sec-19.2). Magnetotactic bacteria are not a phylogenetic group: they occur in at least five phyla, including the Thermodesulfobacteriota, whose members make greigite ($\ce{Fe3S4}$).

### 17.3.5. *Pelagibacter*: the Most Abundant Heterotroph

The SAR11 clade (order Pelagibacterales), represented in culture by "*Candidatus* Pelagibacter ubique", is perhaps the most abundant group of organisms in the oceans, about a quarter to a third of the bacterial cells in the surface water. Its cells are tiny crescents about $0.2 \times 0.5\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ ([7.11.1](/7.11.1#sec-1.9.1)) with a streamlined genome of about $1.3\,\mathrm{Mb}$, no pseudogenes and very short intergenic spacers, an extreme example of genome reduction in a free-living organism: it lacks some biosynthetic pathways (it needs reduced sulfur compounds and certain vitamin precursors from other organisms), has few regulatory genes, and lives on dissolved organic compounds at nanomolar concentrations, supplemented by light energy from a **proteorhodopsin** ([7.11.6](/7.11.6#sec-6.4)). Streamlining is thought to result from selection for economy in an environment of very low nutrients and huge population sizes, in which even small savings are visible to selection.

## 17.4. Betaproteobacteria

The Betaproteobacteria are aerobic or facultatively anaerobic chemoorganotrophs and chemolithotrophs, common in freshwater, soil and wastewater, where nutrients come and go.

- Ammonia oxidizers: *Nitrosomonas europaea* and *Nitrosospira* oxidize ammonia to nitrite by ammonia monooxygenase and hydroxylamine oxidoreductase, the first step of **nitrification** ([7.11.7](/7.11.7#sec-7.2.4)). They are obligate chemolithoautotrophs with stacks of internal membranes; in many soils and in the ocean they are outnumbered by ammonia-oxidizing archaea ([7.11.19](/7.11.19#sec-19.6.2)).
- Hydrogen and sulfur oxidizers: *Cupriavidus necator* (formerly *Ralstonia eutropha*) grows on $\ce{H2}$ and $\ce{CO2}$ and stores large amounts of **polyhydroxybutyrate**, which made it the classic organism for bioplastic production ([7.11.7](/7.11.7#sec-7.2.1)); *Thiobacillus denitrificans* oxidizes sulfur compounds with nitrate as the acceptor.
- Sheathed bacteria: *Sphaerotilus natans* and *Leptothrix* grow as chains of rods inside a tube of protein and polysaccharide, from which swarmer cells escape. *Sphaerotilus* thrives in polluted streams and in activated sludge, where its filaments cause "bulking", a sludge that does not settle; *Leptothrix* deposits iron and manganese oxides in its sheath. *Zoogloea* forms the slimy flocs that make activated sludge work ([7.11.30](/7.11.30)).
- Pathogens: *Neisseria gonorrhoeae* (gonorrhea) and *N. meningitidis* (meningococcal meningitis) are aerobic, oxidase-positive diplococci that colonize mucous membranes; they are naturally competent for transformation, which, together with phase and antigenic variation of their pili and outer membrane proteins, makes them masters of immune evasion ([7.11.34](/7.11.34)). *Bordetella pertussis* causes whooping cough with an AB toxin. The *Burkholderia cepacia* complex comprises metabolically versatile soil bacteria that infect the lungs of patients with cystic fibrosis, and *B. pseudomallei* causes melioidosis in Southeast Asia and northern Australia; *Ralstonia solanacearum* causes bacterial wilt of tomatoes and potatoes.

## 17.5. Gammaproteobacteria

### 17.5.1. Enteric Bacteria

The enteric bacteria are the best-studied bacteria of all, because they include *E. coli* and many pathogens of the intestinal tract.

**Definition 17.5.1 (Enteric Bacteria)** The **enteric bacteria** (order Enterobacterales) are Gram-negative, non-sporulating, straight rods that are facultative anaerobes, oxidase-negative and catalase-positive, are motile by peritrichous flagella or nonmotile, reduce nitrate to nitrite, and ferment glucose to acids by **mixed-acid fermentation** or **butanediol fermentation**.

The two fermentation patterns ([7.11.5](/7.11.5#sec-5.3.4)) divide the group in the diagnostic laboratory. *Escherichia*, *Salmonella*, *Shigella*, *Proteus* and *Yersinia* make a mixture of lactate, acetate, succinate, formate (or $\ce{H2}$ and $\ce{CO2}$) and ethanol, which lowers the pH of a glucose broth below 4.4 (positive methyl red test); *Enterobacter*, *Klebsiella* and *Serratia* convert much of the pyruvate to the neutral product 2,3-butanediol through acetoin, which is detected by the Voges–Proskauer test, and produce more gas. Further tests (lactose fermentation on MacConkey agar, $\ce{H2S}$, urease, citrate, indole) and today MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry identify the genera ([7.11.16](/7.11.16#sec-16.8)).

| Genus | Distinctive features | Significance |
|---|---|---|
| *Escherichia* | lactose fermenter, mixed acid | commensal of the gut; model organism; pathotypes cause diarrhea, urinary tract infections, sepsis and the hemolytic uremic syndrome (O157:H7) |
| *Shigella* | non-motile, lactose negative | dysentery; genomically a set of lineages within *E. coli*, kept as a genus for medical reasons |
| *Salmonella* | $\ce{H2S}$, usually lactose negative | *S. enterica* serovars cause gastroenteritis and typhoid fever |
| *Klebsiella* | capsule, butanediol, non-motile | pneumonia and hospital infections; carbapenem-resistant strains ([7.11.15](/7.11.15)) |
| *Enterobacter*, *Serratia* | butanediol; *Serratia* makes the red pigment prodigiosin | opportunistic infections |
| *Proteus* | urease, swarming on agar | urinary tract infections and stones |
| *Yersinia* | grows at $4\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ | *Y. pestis* (plague, a recent clone of *Y. pseudotuberculosis*), *Y. enterocolitica* |
| *Pectobacterium*, *Erwinia* | pectinases | soft rots and blights of plants |

In 2016 the order was divided into several families on genomic evidence (Enterobacteriaceae for *Escherichia*, *Salmonella*, *Klebsiella* and *Enterobacter*; Yersiniaceae for *Yersinia* and *Serratia*; Morganellaceae for *Proteus*; Pectobacteriaceae and Erwiniaceae for the plant pathogens), but the traditional term "enterics" covers all of them. Close relatives of the enterics include *Haemophilus influenzae* (order Pasteurellales), the first organism whose complete genome was sequenced (1995), and the endosymbionts of insects such as *Buchnera aphidicola* in aphids, whose genomes have shrunk below $0.7\,\mathrm{Mb}$.

### 17.5.2. Pseudomonads

The pseudomonads are Gram-negative, polarly flagellated, strictly respiratory rods that are oxidase-positive and can use a very wide range of organic compounds as sources of carbon and energy; many grow on aromatic compounds, hydrocarbons or pesticides, and *Pseudomonas putida* is used in bioremediation and as a chassis for industrial biotechnology. They do not ferment sugars and grow anaerobically only by **denitrification** or, in *P. aeruginosa*, by fermentation of arginine. *P. fluorescens* and *P. putida* colonize plant roots and suppress fungal pathogens; *P. syringae* injects effectors into plant cells by a **type III secretion system**, and its ice-nucleation protein, which lets water freeze at about $-2\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$, damages frost-sensitive plants. *Azotobacter*, a free-living nitrogen fixer with very high respiration rates that protect its nitrogenase ([7.11.6](/7.11.6#sec-6.6.3)), belongs to the same family.

*Pseudomonas aeruginosa* is the best-known member and a major **opportunistic pathogen**. Its genome of about $6.3\,\mathrm{Mb}$ has an unusually large fraction of regulatory genes, which lets it adjust to environments from soil and water to the lungs of patients with cystic fibrosis, burns, catheters and ventilators. Its outer membrane has a low permeability and it carries several multidrug **efflux pumps**, which together give it a high **intrinsic resistance** to many antibiotics ([7.11.15](/7.11.15)); it forms **biofilms** and controls its virulence factors (elastase, exotoxin A, rhamnolipids and the blue-green phenazine pigment pyocyanin) by layered **quorum sensing** circuits ([7.11.11](/7.11.11#sec-11.7.3)). *Acinetobacter baumannii*, a related member of the order Pseudomonadales (family Moraxellaceae), is one of the most drug-resistant hospital pathogens.

On surfaces, *P. aeruginosa* moves by **twitching motility**: **type IV pili** extended from one pole attach to the surface and retract, pulling the cell forward ([7.11.3](/7.11.3#sec-3.4.3)). In a flowing liquid, as on the wall of a catheter or a vessel, cells move against the flow (Shen and colleagues 2012). The explanation is mechanical. Attached by its pili at the leading pole, the cell is a rod pinned at one end; the drag of the flow on the rest of the body turns the free trailing pole downstream, like a weathervane, so that the leading pole points upstream, and the pili then pull the cell in that direction.

**Formula 17.5.1 (Upstream Twitching)** Let $\varphi$ be the angle of the cell axis (from the trailing to the leading pole) measured from the direction of flow. A cell that moves at speed $v$ along its axis, while the torque of the flow turns its axis toward the upstream direction $\varphi = \pi$ at rate $\omega$ and rotational diffusion with coefficient $D_{\mathrm{r}}$ perturbs it, obeys

$$
\mathrm{d}\varphi = -\omega \sin(\varphi - \pi)\,\mathrm{d}t + \sqrt{2D_{\mathrm{r}}}\,\mathrm{d}W_t, \qquad
\frac{\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{r}}{\mathrm{d}t} = v\,(\cos\varphi, \sin\varphi),
$$
where $W_t$ is a Wiener process. For $D_{\mathrm{r}} \ll \omega$, $\varphi$ stays close to $\pi$ and the cell drifts upstream at a speed close to $v$.

![Upstream twitching of *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*. A: type IV pili extend from the leading pole, attach to the surface and retract, pulling the cell forward; with the leading pole anchored, the drag of the flow turns the trailing pole downstream, so that the leading pole comes to point upstream. B: trajectories of 25 cells over 15 min in the model of Formula 17.5.1 ($v = 0.3\,\mu\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, $\omega = 0.02\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$, $D_{\mathrm{r}} = 0.005\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$; illustrative parameters), all starting at the origin with random orientations: cells that start facing downstream turn around within a minute or two, and all end upstream.](./plots/upstream-twitching.py)

Upstream migration allows *P. aeruginosa* to colonize vessels, catheters and the upstream ends of flow systems that would be out of reach of passive dispersal, and is one reason why its biofilms spread against the flow in medical devices.

### 17.5.3. Vibrios

The Vibrionales are curved or straight rods with polar flagella, facultative anaerobes of marine and estuarine waters that ferment sugars by the mixed-acid pathway and are oxidase-positive, which distinguishes them from the enterics. Most require $\ce{Na+}$ for growth. They are unusual among bacteria in having two chromosomes. *Vibrio cholerae* causes cholera: its toxin genes are carried by a filamentous phage (CTXφ) integrated into its chromosome ([7.11.33](/7.11.33)), and it lives between outbreaks in brackish water, often attached to the chitin of copepods. *V. parahaemolyticus* (gastroenteritis from seafood) and *V. vulnificus* (wound infections and sepsis) are other pathogens.

Bioluminescent vibrios live free in the sea, on dead fish and in the light organs of fish and squid. *Vibrio fischeri* (reclassified as *Aliivibrio fischeri* in 2007; both names are in use) colonizes the light organ of the Hawaiian bobtail squid *Euprymna scolopes*, which acquires it anew from seawater in every generation: mucus, ciliary currents and antimicrobial factors of the juvenile light organ select *V. fischeri* from a bacterioplankton in which it makes up less than 0.1 % of the cells, and within hours each crypt of the organ is colonized by one or a few cells, which grow to high density and switch on light. The squid uses the light to erase its shadow when it hunts by moonlight (counterillumination). Light is made by luciferase, which oxidizes reduced flavin mononucleotide together with a long-chain aldehyde, encoded in the *luxCDABEG* operon and switched on only at high cell density by **quorum sensing** through the LuxI–LuxR system ([7.11.11](/7.11.11#sec-11.7.2)), the system in which quorum sensing was discovered. The host–symbiont interaction is treated further in [7.11.31](/7.11.31). Luminescent bacteria are also used to test the toxicity of water samples, because toxic substances lower their light output.

### 17.5.4. *Legionella* and Other Gammaproteobacterial Pathogens

*Legionella pneumophila* lives inside free-living amoebae in natural and man-made waters (cooling towers, hot-water systems, whirlpools) and, when inhaled in aerosols, infects alveolar macrophages in the same way, using a type IV secretion system (Dot/Icm) to inject several hundred effector proteins that convert the phagosome into a vacuole derived from the endoplasmic reticulum; it causes Legionnaires' disease, a pneumonia named after an outbreak at a convention of the American Legion in 1976. *Coxiella burnetii* (Q fever) also replicates in a modified vacuole, and *Francisella tularensis* causes tularemia. The life inside host cells is treated in [7.11.34](/7.11.34).

### 17.5.5. Sulfur Bacteria and Methanotrophs

The purple sulfur bacteria (*Chromatium*, *Thiocapsa*, *Allochromatium*) are anoxygenic phototrophs that use hydrogen sulfide as electron donor and store the elemental sulfur they produce as globules inside the cell; they bloom in the anoxic, sulfide-rich, illuminated layers of lakes and lagoons and in microbial mats ([7.11.6](/7.11.6#sec-6.2.6)).

Colorless sulfur bacteria oxidize sulfide and sulfur chemolithotrophically ([7.11.7](/7.11.7#sec-7.2.2)). *Beggiatoa* forms gliding filaments that move up and down in the gradient between oxygen above and sulfide below; it was in studying *Beggiatoa* that Winogradsky conceived of chemolithotrophy ([7.11.1](/7.11.1#sec-1.5)). Several large relatives store nitrate in a central vacuole as an electron acceptor, which lets them oxidize sulfide far from oxygen: *Thioploca* forms bundles of filaments inside sheaths in the sediments off the coast of Chile, gliding down to sulfide and up to nitrate; *Thiomargarita namibiensis*, whose cells reach $0.75\,\mathrm{mm}$, and the centimeter-long filamentous single cells of "*Ca.* Thiomargarita magnifica" are among the largest bacteria known ([7.11.1](/7.11.1#sec-1.9.1)). *Achromatium* accumulates calcite as well as sulfur. Sulfur-oxidizing gammaproteobacteria are also the chemosynthetic symbionts of tube worms (*Riftia*) and clams at hydrothermal vents ([7.11.31](/7.11.31)).

The type I **methanotrophs** (*Methylococcus*, *Methylomonas*) assimilate formaldehyde by the ribulose monophosphate pathway and have stacks of internal membranes ([7.11.7](/7.11.7#sec-7.4.3)); together with the type II methanotrophs of the Alphaproteobacteria they consume much of the methane produced in wetlands and sediments before it reaches the atmosphere.

## 17.6. Phyla Formerly in the Proteobacteria

### 17.6.1. Thermodesulfobacteriota: Sulfate and Metal Reducers

The Thermodesulfobacteriota (GTDB: Desulfobacterota) contain most of the Gram-negative **sulfate-reducing bacteria** ([7.11.7](/7.11.7#sec-7.3.3)), strict anaerobes of marine sediments, where sulfate is abundant, and of anoxic freshwater, gut and oil-field habitats. They fall into two physiological groups: incomplete oxidizers such as *Desulfovibrio*, which oxidize lactate or $\ce{H2}$ and excrete acetate, and complete oxidizers such as *Desulfobacter* and *Desulfococcus*, which oxidize acetate to $\ce{CO2}$. *Desulfovibrio* contributes to the corrosion of iron pipes in anoxic soils and to the souring of oil wells by sulfide. Sulfate reduction is not confined to this phylum: the spore-forming *Desulfotomaculum* belongs to the Bacillota, and *Archaeoglobus* is an archaeon ([7.11.19](/7.11.19#sec-19.5.3)). The class Thermodesulfobacteria, thermophilic sulfate reducers of hot springs with ether-linked (though not isoprenoid) membrane lipids, gave the phylum its valid name.

*Geobacter* (*G. metallireducens*, *G. sulfurreducens*) and *Desulfuromonas* oxidize acetate with ferric iron or elemental sulfur as acceptor. *Geobacter* transfers electrons to insoluble iron oxides outside the cell through outer-membrane cytochromes and conductive filaments ("nanowires"), a form of **extracellular electron transfer** that also lets it generate current in microbial fuel cells and pass electrons directly to methanogens ([7.11.7](/7.11.7#sec-7.3.4)). The cable bacteria (*Electrothrix*, *Electronema*, family Desulfobulbaceae), described in 2012, take this further: filaments of thousands of cells, up to several centimeters long, span the sediment from the sulfidic zone to the oxic surface and conduct electrons along periplasmic fibers from sulfide oxidation at one end to oxygen reduction at the other, so that a single multicellular organism carries out a redox reaction over a centimeter.

### 17.6.2. Myxococcota

The *myxobacteria* are aerobic soil bacteria that glide in swarms over surfaces, kill and lyse other microorganisms with secreted enzymes and antibiotics, and, when starved, aggregate by the hundred thousand into **fruiting bodies** in which cells differentiate into resistant myxospores. Their two motility systems, the signaling during development and the evolutionary problem of cooperation are treated in [7.11.12](/7.11.12). *Myxococcus xanthus* is the model species. The myxobacteria have some of the largest bacterial genomes (about $9\,\mathrm{Mb}$ in *M. xanthus* and up to about $13$–$15\,\mathrm{Mb}$ in *Sorangium cellulosum*), rich in gene clusters for **secondary metabolites**; the epothilones of *Sorangium*, which stabilize microtubules as paclitaxel does, led to the anticancer drug ixabepilone. Their fruiting bodies, often colored by carotenoids, range from simple mounds (*Myxococcus*) to branched, tree-like structures with sporangioles (*Stigmatella*, *Chondromyces*).

### 17.6.3. Bdellovibrionota: Bacteria That Eat Bacteria

*Bdellovibrio bacteriovorus*, discovered by Stolp and Petzold in 1962 in a search for phages of plant pathogens, is a small, comma-shaped, very fast-swimming bacterium (about $0.3 \times 1\,\mu\mathrm{m}$) that preys on other Gram-negative bacteria. Its life cycle has two phases. In the free-swimming attack phase, the predator collides with a prey cell, attaches by its non-flagellated pole using type IV pili, opens a pore in the outer membrane and the peptidoglycan, and enters the **periplasm**, resealing the hole behind it. The prey cell dies and rounds up into a *bdelloplast*, because the predator modifies its peptidoglycan. In the growth phase, the predator degrades the prey's macromolecules, imports the products, and grows into a long filament without dividing; when the prey is exhausted, the filament divides at several sites at once into an odd or even number of progeny (non-binary fission), usually three to six from an *E. coli* cell, which grow flagella and are released when the prey lyses, about three to four hours after attack. Related predators live in the sea (*Bacteriovorax*, now in a separate order) and in soil.

The kinetics of predation can be described like those of phage infection ([7.11.22](/7.11.22)), with an encounter rate, a latent period and a burst size:

**Formula 17.6.1 (Predation by *Bdellovibrio*)** Let $N$ be the density of free prey, $B$ of bdelloplasts and $P$ of free predators. With mass-action encounters at rate constant $k$, a growth phase of mean duration $\tau$, $Y$ progeny per prey and a death rate $m$ of free predators, and prey that do not grow,

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}N}{\mathrm{d}t} = -kNP, \qquad
\frac{\mathrm{d}B}{\mathrm{d}t} = kNP - \frac{B}{\tau}, \qquad
\frac{\mathrm{d}P}{\mathrm{d}t} = \frac{Y B}{\tau} - kNP - mP.
$$

A: an attack-phase cell swims to a Gram-negative prey cell, attaches by its non-flagellated pole, enters the periplasm and turns the prey into a rounded bdelloplast (dashed line: the prey's cytoplasmic membrane); it grows into a filament, which divides at several sites at once, and the progeny develop flagella and are released when the prey lyses, about 3–4 h after attack. B: Formula 17.6.1 for $10^8$ prey cells per mL suspended in buffer, with three initial ratios of predator to prey (illustrative parameters $k = 2\times10^{-8}\,\mathrm{mL\,h^{-1}}$, $\tau = 3.5\,\mathrm{h}$, $Y = 4$, $m = 0.02\,\mathrm{h^{-1}}$). The model shows why predation is effective only above a threshold: the rate of attack per prey cell is $kP$, so a small inoculum of predators needs several cycles of amplification before it can reduce the prey, and at low prey density the encounter rate falls so low that predators starve and prey survive; this is one reason why prey and predator coexist in nature. Because *Bdellovibrio* kills antibiotic-resistant bacteria as readily as sensitive ones and resistance to it does not arise by single mutations, it has been explored as a "living antibiotic", in the same spirit as phage therapy ([7.11.23](/7.11.23)).

### 17.6.4. Campylobacterota

The Campylobacterota (formerly Epsilonproteobacteria) are curved or helical, microaerophilic, polarly flagellated bacteria. Two genera are major human pathogens. *Campylobacter jejuni*, carried in the intestines of poultry and other animals, is one of the most frequent bacterial causes of diarrhea worldwide, and an occasional trigger of the autoimmune Guillain–Barré syndrome through molecular mimicry between its lipooligosaccharides and nerve gangliosides. *Helicobacter pylori* colonizes the mucus layer of the human stomach in about half of the world's population, usually from childhood and for life; it survives the acid by making large amounts of urease, which splits urea into ammonia and $\ce{CO2}$, swims through mucus with a tuft of sheathed flagella, and attaches to the gastric epithelium. Most carriers have no symptoms, but it causes chronic gastritis and peptic ulcers and is the main cause of gastric adenocarcinoma and gastric MALT lymphoma. Barry Marshall and Robin Warren, who cultured it in 1982 and showed its role in ulcers against the prevailing belief that the stomach was sterile, received the Nobel Prize in 2005; eradication with antibiotics now cures most ulcers. Its detection by the urea breath test and stool antigen tests is described in [7.11.16](/7.11.16#sec-16.8).

The same phylum dominates the microbial communities of deep-sea hydrothermal vents and sulfidic caves. *Sulfurimonas*, *Sulfurovum* and *Nautilia* oxidize hydrogen or reduced sulfur compounds with oxygen or nitrate and fix $\ce{CO2}$ by the **reverse citric acid cycle** ([7.11.6](/7.11.6#sec-6.5.2)); they colonize chimney walls, form mats, and live as episymbionts on vent animals such as the Pompeii worm and the shrimp *Rimicaris*.

## Summary

- Diversity is organized by phylogeny, with function placed on the tree; physiological groups (sulfate reducers, phototrophs, nitrogen fixers, ammonia oxidizers, magnetotactic bacteria) are often polyphyletic because their key genes move by horizontal transfer. More than 90 % of cultured bacterial species belong to four phyla; many phyla are known only from sequences.
- The **Pseudomonadota** (Definition 17.2.1, formerly Proteobacteria) are Gram-negative, metabolically and morphologically the most diverse phylum, with about a third of the described species. The former Delta- and Epsilonproteobacteria are now separate phyla.

## Questions

1. Why is "rhizobium" a functional rather than a phylogenetic category, while "Gram-negative bacterium with an outer membrane" corresponds well to phylogenetic groups?

   **Answer** Nodulation and nitrogen fixation depend on clusters of *nod*, *nif* and *fix* genes carried on plasmids or symbiotic islands, which are transferred horizontally; they have spread among Alphaproteobacteria and into Betaproteobacteria (*Paraburkholderia*, *Cupriavidus*), so legume symbionts are polyphyletic. The outer membrane requires many interacting systems (LPS synthesis and transport, the Bam and Lol machineries, periplasmic peptidoglycan), which cannot be acquired as a unit; it is inherited vertically and marks whole lineages, although it has been lost in some (the Gram-positive phyla) and is present in a few lineages of Bacillota.

2. A Gram-negative, oxidase-negative rod from a stool sample ferments glucose with gas, gives a positive methyl red and a negative Voges–Proskauer test, and does not ferment lactose. Which group does it belong to, and which two genera are most likely? What would distinguish them?

   **Answer** It is an enteric bacterium (Definition 17.5.1) with a mixed-acid fermentation. Non-fermentation of lactose points to *Salmonella* or *Shigella* rather than *E. coli*. *Salmonella* is usually motile and produces $\ce{H2S}$ (black colonies on media with iron salts); *Shigella* is non-motile, $\ce{H2S}$-negative and does not produce gas from glucose (most species), so the gas here favors *Salmonella*; serotyping or MALDI-TOF would confirm it.

3. In Formula 17.6.1, what is the initial rate at which a prey cell is attacked, and why does a predator population fail to control prey that are present at low density? Which assumption of the model would you change for prey that grow?

   **Answer** Each prey cell is attacked at rate $kP$; the predators increase only if the yield from attacks exceeds their death, roughly $kN\,(Y - 1) > m$ (each attack removes one free predator and returns $Y$ after a delay). At low prey density $kN$ is small, the predators die faster than they reproduce, and the prey persist at a refuge density. For growing prey one adds a growth term, $\mathrm{d}N/\mathrm{d}t = rN(1 - N/K) - kNP$, and the model becomes a predator–prey system with a delay that can oscillate or settle to coexistence.

4. Explain, with Formula 17.5.1, why a twitching cell in a flow moves upstream, and why a cell that is attached by the middle of its body, or that has pili at both poles, would not.

   **Answer** The flow exerts a torque about the pivot at the attached leading pole that turns the trailing end downstream; the axis therefore relaxes to $\varphi = \pi$ (leading pole upstream) at rate $\omega$, and pili pull the cell along its axis, giving a drift $v\cos\varphi \approx -v$ in the flow direction. If the cell were pinned in the middle, the drag on the two halves would give no net torque and the orientation would diffuse at random; with pili active at both poles, the direction of pulling would change at random and the mean drift would vanish.

5. *Helicobacter pylori* and *Sulfurimonas* belong to the same phylum. What do their physiologies have in common, and what does their joint classification illustrate about the relation between phylogeny and ecology?

   **Answer** Both are curved or helical, polarly flagellated microaerophiles that thrive at gradients: *H. pylori* in the mucus layer between the gastric lumen and the epithelium, *Sulfurimonas* between oxygen and reduced sulfur compounds at vents; both use $\ce{H2}$ as an electron donor (*H. pylori* has a hydrogenase that oxidizes $\ce{H2}$ from other gut bacteria). Their classification shows that a phylum is a lineage, not an ecological guild: descendants of one ancestor have diversified into hosts and hydrothermal habitats while retaining a shared cell plan and some metabolic traits.
