A bacteriophage is a virus of a bacterium, and by any numerical measure the phages are the dominant biological entities on Earth. They are also the organisms in which most of the general principles of the previous chapter were discovered, and in which molecular biology itself grew up: the phage was to genetics what *Drosophila* was to heredity. In the last two decades they have returned to the centre of microbiology for two further reasons — they are being developed again as therapeutic agents against resistant bacteria, and the search for the mechanisms bacteria use to resist them has turned out to be a search for the origins of innate immunity.

This chapter treats phages as biological objects in their own right: their history and their ecology, their life cycles, the detailed molecular biography of one of them, the quantitative experiments that phages made possible, the machinery with which they break out of a cell, and the arms race between phages and their hosts. The general properties of viruses, the plaque assay and the one-step growth curve are those of [7.11.22](/7.11.22); the use of phages as tools of genetic analysis — transduction, interrupted mating, fine-structure mapping — belongs to [7.4.10](/7.4.10), and the genetic switch that decides between lysis and lysogeny in phage λ is dissected in [7.2.11](/7.2.11#sec-11.5). None of these is repeated. What is added here is what a phage does to a bacterium, and what bacteria do about it.

## 23.1. Phages in the History of Biology

### 23.1.1. Discovery

Frederick Twort in 1915 described a "glassy transformation" of micrococcal colonies that could be passaged in series through filtrates, and suggested that the agent might be a virus. Félix d'Hérelle, working in 1917 on the filtered stools of convalescent dysentery patients, found an agent that lysed *Shigella* cultures, recognized that it multiplied at the expense of the bacteria, named it *bacteriophage* — the eater of bacteria — and devised the plaque assay to count it. D'Hérelle saw at once what the agent could be used for, and spent the rest of his career on it.

### 23.1.2. The Phage Group

Phages became the model system of molecular genetics because they are the simplest self-replicating objects that can be handled quantitatively, and because a single afternoon yields a result that bacterial genetics needs days for. From the late 1930s Max Delbrück, Salvador Luria and Alfred Hershey — the "phage group" — restricted themselves by agreement to a few phages of *E. coli*, the T series, so that results would be comparable. The list of what followed is a list of the foundations of the discipline:

- the one-step growth experiment, which made virus replication a measurable cycle (Ellis and Delbrück, 1939; Section 23.5);
- the fluctuation test, which showed that mutations to phage resistance arise before selection and not in response to it (Luria and Delbrück, 1943; [7.4.12](/7.4.12));
- the demonstration that DNA is the genetic material (Hershey and Chase, 1952; [7.11.22](/7.11.22#sec-22.2.1));
- fine-structure genetics, with the *rII* locus of T4 mapped to the resolution of the nucleotide and the triplet nature of the code established in it (Benzer; Crick and Brenner, 1961; [7.4.10](/7.4.10#sec-10.6));
- the discovery of mRNA, of the suppression of nonsense mutations, and of restriction and modification ([7.2.1](/7.2.1));
- the λ repressor, the operator and the first complete molecular description of a developmental decision ([7.2.11](/7.2.11#sec-11.5));
- the enzymes of genetic engineering — phage T4 DNA ligase and polynucleotide kinase, T7 and SP6 RNA polymerases, λ as the first cloning vector ([7.2.22](/7.2.22));
- **phage display**, in which a peptide or antibody library is fused to a coat protein of filamentous phage M13 so that genotype and phenotype travel in the same particle (Smith, 1985; Nobel Prize 2018), now a standard route to therapeutic antibodies; and
- CRISPR–Cas, which was recognized as an antiphage immune system before it became a genome-editing tool ([7.2.24](/7.2.24#sec-24.4), Section 23.8.4).

### 23.1.3. Phage Therapy

D'Hérelle treated dysentery with phage in 1919 and went on to use phages against cholera and plague; the Eliava Institute in Tbilisi, founded with him in 1923, has produced therapeutic phage preparations ever since. In the West the practice was abandoned in the 1940s, for reasons that were partly scientific — uncontrolled trials, preparations that contained no active phage, no understanding of host range or of neutralizing antibodies — and partly because antibiotics arrived and worked against everything. Interest returned with resistance ([7.11.15](/7.11.15)).

**Definition 23.1.1 (Phage Therapy)** **Phage therapy** is the therapeutic use of lytic bacteriophages to kill a bacterial pathogen in an infected host. Its characteristic features follow from the biology of the agent: it is narrow-spectrum, it replicates at the site of infection as long as the host bacterium is present, and it is itself subject to bacterial resistance and to the patient's immune response.

The comparison with antibiotics is instructive in both directions.

| | Antibiotic | Therapeutic phage |
|---|---|---|
| Spectrum | Broad to moderate; collateral damage to the microbiota | Often strain-specific; the microbiota is spared, but the pathogen must be identified and the phage matched to it |
| Pharmacokinetics | Dose decays; concentration set by dosing | Self-amplifying where the host is, cleared where it is not; "auto-dosing" but hard to model |
| Resistance | Arises and spreads, often by horizontal transfer | Arises readily by receptor mutation, but is often costly, and cocktails or phage–antibiotic combinations can exploit the trade-off |
| Biofilms | Penetration poor; dormant cells tolerant | Depolymerases degrade the matrix; dormant cells still a problem |
| Development and regulation | A defined chemical entity | A replicating biological agent, variable between batches; regulatory frameworks designed for drugs fit badly |
| Immunity | Rarely relevant | Neutralizing antibodies limit repeated courses; endotoxin from lysed Gram-negative cells must be removed from the preparation |

Modern practice is therefore not the empirical phage of 1920. A clinical case begins with isolation of the patient's strain, screening of phage banks for lytic phages active against it, purification to remove endotoxin, and exclusion of temperate phages, which could lysogenize the pathogen and carry toxin or resistance genes. The best-documented case is that of a patient with a disseminated multidrug-resistant *Acinetobacter baumannii* infection treated in 2016 with personalized intravenous phage cocktails assembled by several laboratories within weeks, who recovered (Schooley et al., 2017); in 2019 a cystic fibrosis patient with disseminated *Mycobacterium abscessus* was treated with a three-phage cocktail in which two members had been genetically engineered to be strictly lytic (Dedrick et al., 2019). Randomized trials have been harder: the Phagoburn trial of phage for burn-wound *Pseudomonas aeruginosa* infection was underpowered and used a preparation whose titre had fallen during storage. As of the mid-2020s phage therapy is an established compassionate-use option in several countries, with controlled trials in progress; its most likely niches are chronic biofilm infections of implants and of the lung, where antibiotics perform worst.

A related product avoids the complications of a replicating agent: purified phage lysins, the **endolysin**s of Section 23.6, applied from outside to lyse Gram-positive bacteria, which have no outer membrane to protect their peptidoglycan.

## 23.2. Phages in Nature

### 23.2.1. Abundance

Phages are the most numerous biological entities on Earth: of the order of $10^{31}$ particles, roughly $200$ million tonnes of material. Concentrations are fairly uniform in seawater, $10^5$–$10^7$ particles per millilitre, and far more variable in soil, $10^3$–$10^9$ per gram depending on the soil type. The ratio of virus particles to microbial cells averages about 10 but varies with cell density and habitat.

Two consequences matter for the rest of microbiology. First, phages kill a substantial fraction of marine bacteria every day, releasing their contents as dissolved organic matter rather than passing them up the food chain — the viral shunt, treated with marine microbial ecology in [7.11.29](/7.11.29). Second, phage predation is one of the strongest selective forces acting on bacteria, and phage genomes are a major vehicle of horizontal gene transfer, both by transduction ([7.4.10](/7.4.10#sec-10.5), [7.11.13](/7.11.13#sec-13.6)) and by the genes that prophages carry.

### 23.2.2. The Phages of the Human Gut

The human gut contains a dense and individual phage community, dominated by tailed phages and by the small ssDNA *Microviridae*, most of them still unclassified. Its composition is highly personal, largely stable over years in an adult, and changes rapidly in early life; altered gut viromes have been reported in inflammatory bowel disease. The most prevalent single member known is crAssphage, discovered in 2014 by cross-assembly of faecal metagenomes rather than by isolation, present in most human populations sampled and infecting *Bacteroides*. A large fraction of gut phages appear to be temperate, which fits the high host density and the stable conditions; their effects on the bacterial community and on the host are the subject of [7.11.32](/7.11.32).

### 23.2.3. Morphotypes and Genomes

![The morphotypes and life cycles of bacteriophages. A: the main shapes, drawn to scale. Tailed phages with double-stranded DNA — a myovirus with a contractile tail (T4), a siphovirus with a long flexible non-contractile tail (λ, T5), a podovirus with a short tail (T7, φ29) — make up most isolates; the tailless icosahedral phages include the ssDNA microvirus φX174 and the dsRNA cystovirus φ6, which is enveloped; the filamentous inovirus M13 carries circular ssDNA and is extruded without lysis. Morphology is a description, not a taxon: the families once defined by tail type were abolished in 2022 ([7.11.22](/7.11.22#sec-22.3.2)). B: the two life cycles. A virulent phage can only replicate and lyse. A temperate phage chooses: it may enter the lytic cycle, or establish lysogeny by integrating as a prophage (λ) or persisting as a plasmid (P1), in which state it is replicated with the host, confers immunity to superinfection and may change the phenotype of its host by lysogenic conversion; damage to host DNA induces the SOS response and the prophage excises and goes lytic.](./plots/phage-types-cycles.py)

Most phages isolated by plating are tailed phages with double-stranded DNA, and they fall into three morphological kinds that are still named after the abolished families: *myoviruses* with a long contractile tail, *siphoviruses* with a long flexible non-contractile tail, and *podoviruses* with a short tail. Three further groups are important out of proportion to their numbers: the small icosahedral tailless phages with single-stranded DNA (*Microviridae*, φX174) or with single-stranded RNA (*Leviviricetes*, MS2 and Qβ), the filamentous phages with circular ssDNA (M13, f1, fd, and CTXφ of *Vibrio cholerae*), and the enveloped, segmented dsRNA cystovirus φ6, the standard laboratory model for a segmented RNA virus. Genomes run from $3.6\,\mathrm{kb}$ (MS2, four genes) through the $48.5\,\mathrm{kb}$ of λ and the $169\,\mathrm{kb}$ of T4 to the "jumbo" phages above $200\,\mathrm{kb}$, some of which build a proteinaceous nucleus-like compartment around their replicating DNA (Section 23.8.6).

## 23.3. Virulent and Temperate Phages

**Definition 23.3.1 (Virulent Phage)** A **virulent phage** can follow only the lytic cycle: after infection it replicates, assembles progeny and lyses its host. Phages T4 and T7 are virulent. A **temperate phage** can in addition establish **lysogeny**, in which its genome persists in the cell as a **prophage** — integrated into the chromosome (λ) or maintained as a plasmid (P1) — replicating with the host and expressing almost no genes.

A lysogen is immune to superinfection by the same phage, because the repressor that silences the prophage also silences any incoming genome of the same kind. Induction, by anything that damages host DNA and triggers the SOS response, restores lytic growth. The molecular switch is the subject of [7.2.11](/7.2.11#sec-11.5); its ecological logic is the subject of Section 23.7.

**Definition 23.3.2 (Lysogenic Conversion)** **Lysogenic conversion** is the acquisition by a lysogen of a new phenotype encoded by prophage genes that are expressed during lysogeny. It is a major route by which bacteria acquire virulence factors.

The examples are the classic ones of medical microbiology, and they are the reason that a therapeutic phage must be strictly lytic: diphtheria toxin is encoded by the prophage β of *Corynebacterium diphtheriae*; cholera toxin by the filamentous phage CTXφ of *Vibrio cholerae*; Shiga toxin by lambdoid prophages of *E. coli* O157:H7, whose release is driven by prophage induction, which is why fluoroquinolones are avoided in that infection; botulinum toxin type C and the erythrogenic toxins of *Streptococcus pyogenes* likewise. Prophages also supply enzymes of lipopolysaccharide modification that change serotype, superinfection-exclusion systems, and in many genomes a large fraction of the strain-specific accessory genes ([7.11.13](/7.11.13#sec-13.1.4)). Prophages are not passive passengers: they are among the commonest mobile elements in bacterial genomes, and a substantial part of what distinguishes one strain of a species from another.

## 23.4. Bacteriophage T4

![Bacteriophage T4: structure, adsorption and injection. A: the virion — a prolate icosahedral head containing 169 kbp of linear dsDNA, a neck and collar, a contractile sheath around a rigid central tube, a hexagonal baseplate, six long tail fibres and six short tail fibres. B–E: the steps of entry into a Gram-negative cell. B: a long tail fibre makes reversible contact with the host receptor — the lipopolysaccharide core or the outer-membrane porin OmpC — and the virion walks over the surface by repeated binding and release of different fibres. C: when two or three long fibres are bound, a signal reaches the baseplate, which changes from hexagonal to a star conformation and releases the short tail fibres; these bind irreversibly. D: the sheath contracts to about half its length, driving the central tube through the outer membrane, while a lysozyme-like domain of the tail digests a local opening in the peptidoglycan. E: the DNA passes through the tube into the cytoplasm — a process taking some minutes, since the DNA is packed in the head under tens of atmospheres of pressure — and the empty capsid remains outside. F: other phages solve the same problem differently: a siphovirus delivers a tail-tip complex that forms a channel; the podovirus φ29 has a tail knob just long enough to cross the wall of a Gram-positive host; T7 ejects internal core proteins that build a conduit across the periplasm; the tailless φX174 uses its protein H for the same purpose.](./plots/t4-structure.py)

Phage T4 infects *E. coli*, has been studied since the 1940s, and remains the most completely described virus of any kind. Its $169\,\mathrm{kbp}$ genome encodes about 300 gene products, of which some 60 are structural.

### 23.4.1. Adsorption and Host Range

T4 recognizes its host with the receptor-binding proteins at the tips of its six long tail fibres, which bind the outer core of the lipopolysaccharide or the porin OmpC. Adsorption is a two-stage process. The long fibres bind reversibly, and because each contact is weak and transient the particle effectively walks over the cell surface, sampling it until it finds a suitable site; when two or three fibres are engaged, a conformational signal reaches the baseplate, which opens and releases the six short tail fibres. These bind irreversibly, and the virion is committed.

Receptors used by phages are whatever is abundant and exposed on the surface: lipopolysaccharide and outer-membrane proteins in Gram-negative hosts (the maltoporin LamB for λ, the vitamin $\mathrm{B}_{12}$ transporter BtuB for BF23, the iron transporter FhuA for T1 and T5, the efflux pump TolC), peptidoglycan and the wall teichoic acids in Gram-positive hosts, and the appendages — flagella for the phage χ, which slides down the rotating filament, and conjugative pili for the filamentous phages and the RNA phages, which attach to the side or the tip. A phage that uses the F pilus is thereby restricted to cells carrying the F plasmid ([7.11.13](/7.11.13#sec-13.7)).

The receptor-binding protein is the most variable part of a phage genome, and the fibre tip in particular is a hot spot of recombination and diversification; the **host range** of a phage is correspondingly evolvable, and can be extended in the laboratory by selection in a few passages. Broadening it usually costs performance on the original host.

### 23.4.2. Penetration

The contractile tail of T4 is a nanomachine. The sheath, a helical array of gp18 subunits around the rigid central tube, is assembled in a metastable, extended state; the conformational change of the baseplate releases it, and the sheath contracts to roughly half its length, driving the tube through the outer membrane. A lysozyme-like activity associated with the tail opens a local hole in the peptidoglycan, and the tube reaches the cytoplasmic membrane. The DNA then leaves the head — driven at first by the internal pressure of the packaged DNA, which is of the order of tens of atmospheres, and completed with the help of the membrane potential and of host factors — while the capsid stays outside.

Other phages reach the cytoplasm by other routes, because the obstacle differs with the envelope and the tail available. A siphovirus with a long non-contractile tail delivers, from its tail-tip complex, proteins that insert into the membranes and form a channel. The podovirus φ29 has a short tail with a knob that degrades peptidoglycan — long enough to cross the wall of its Gram-positive host *Bacillus subtilis*. T7's tail is too short to span a Gram-negative envelope, so the phage ejects internal core proteins that assemble a conduit across the periplasm; the tailless φX174 uses its pilot protein H in the same way. In every case the genome, and sometimes a few proteins with it, is what enters; the shell is left behind. This is the fundamental difference from animal virus entry ([7.11.22](/7.11.22#sec-22.5.2)), and it is what made the Hershey–Chase experiment possible.

### 23.4.3. The Temporal Programme

![The programme of a T4 infection. A: the timetable at 37 °C. Host transcription and DNA synthesis stop within a minute; T4 early, middle and late transcripts appear in sequence; phage DNA replication begins at about 4–5 min; the first complete virion is assembled at about 12 min, which ends the eclipse period; heads, tails and fibres accumulate and are joined; and the cell lyses at about 25–30 min, releasing of the order of 100 particles. B: how the switch is made. Early promoters resemble host promoters and are read by host RNA polymerase with σ⁷⁰; the phage proteins Alt (injected with the DNA) and ModA ADP-ribosylate the α subunits and AsiA binds σ⁷⁰, which stops the enzyme recognizing host promoters. Middle promoters need MotA together with the modified σ⁷⁰. Late promoters have only a −10 element and are read by a new phage-encoded σ factor, gp55, with gp33; the sliding clamp gp45, loaded at nicks and gaps in the replicating DNA, acts as a mobile transcriptional enhancer, so that late genes are transcribed only from DNA that is being replicated. The genome itself is modified: 5-hydroxymethylcytosine replaces cytosine and is glucosylated, which protects it from the host's restriction enzymes, while a phage nuclease specific for ordinary cytosine destroys the host chromosome. C: concatemers and headful packaging — recombination joins replicated genomes end to end, and the terminase cuts a headful, slightly more than one genome length, so the progeny are circularly permuted and terminally redundant; the packaging motor works at up to 2000 bp s⁻¹ against the pressure of the DNA already inside.](./plots/t4-program.py)

An infection must do incompatible things in sequence — first dismantle the host's programme and replicate, then build particles — and it has only one RNA polymerase to work with, the host's. T4 solves the problem by modifying that polymerase in stages, a classic of transcriptional regulation ([7.2.10](/7.2.10)).

*Immediate early and delayed early* transcription begins within seconds, from promoters that resemble host promoters and are read by the host enzyme with σ⁷⁰. Among the first products are proteins that shut the host down: the injected Alt protein and the early ModA ADP-ribosylate the α subunits of RNA polymerase, and AsiA binds σ⁷⁰ and inactivates its recognition of the −35 element, so that host promoters are no longer used. Early proteins also include nucleases that degrade the host chromosome into nucleotides for the phage's own use, the enzymes that make and glucosylate 5-hydroxymethylcytosine, and the eight-protein T4 replisome.

*Middle* transcription, from about two minutes, uses the activator MotA together with the AsiA-modified polymerase at promoters with a distinctive "MotA box".

*Late* transcription, from about ten minutes, requires a genuine phage σ factor, gp55, with the accessory protein gp33; late promoters have only a −10 element. The elegant part is the coupling to replication: the clamp gp45, loaded by its clamp loader at the nicks and gaps that abound in replicating T4 DNA, slides along the DNA and acts as a mobile enhancer that activates the gp55 holoenzyme. Only replicating DNA is therefore competent for late transcription, so structural proteins are made only when there are genomes to package into them.

### 23.4.4. Replication and Packaging

T4 DNA is replicated first from origins and later, predominantly, by recombination-dependent replication, in which the $3'$ end of a recombination intermediate primes synthesis. The result is a branched *concatemer*, several genome lengths long, not discrete molecules. Packaging solves this by measuring rather than by reading: the terminase gp17/gp16 cuts the concatemer, the packaging motor pumps DNA into a preassembled prohead until it is full, and the terminase then cuts again. Because a head holds slightly more than one genome length, each progeny genome is *terminally redundant* ($3$–$6\,\mathrm{kbp}$ repeated at the two ends) and the population of genomes is *circularly permuted*: every genome contains the same genes, but each begins at a different point. Circular permutation was deduced genetically before it was seen physically, and it explains why the T4 genetic map is circular although its DNA is linear.

The packaging motor is the strongest known molecular motor, generating forces above $50\,\mathrm{pN}$ and packaging up to $2000$ base pairs per second against the growing internal pressure; its mechanics, measured by optical tweezers on the related phage φ29, are treated in [7.6.28](/7.6.28#sec-28.5.4).

### 23.4.5. Assembly

Head, tail and tail fibres are built along three independent pathways and joined at the end — an architecture that Edgar and Wood established in the 1960s by mixing extracts of cells infected with different conditional lethal mutants, the first "assembly *in vitro*" of a biological structure.

The head begins as a membrane-associated initiation complex around the portal protein gp20; the scaffolding proteins gp21, gp22 and gp67 and the major capsid protein gp23 assemble around it into a prohead. The scaffolding protease gp21 then activates itself and digests the scaffold, the amino termini of the capsid proteins are cleaved, the peptides are expelled, and the shell expands and thins, becoming more angular. The $169\,\mathrm{kbp}$ genome is pumped in in about five minutes, the motor leaves, the neck proteins seal the portal, and the accessory proteins Hoc and Soc decorate the outside. The tail is built from the baseplate up: six wedges assemble around a central hub to make the baseplate, the tube polymerizes on it, the sheath polymerizes around the tube, and a terminator caps it. Head and tail then join spontaneously, and the six long fibres, assembled separately, attach last. Each step depends on the completion of the preceding one, so that an unfinished structure cannot be sealed, and the chaperonins GroEL/GroES of the host are needed for the folding of some capsid and fibre proteins.

## 23.5. Counting Phages and Resolving the Cycle

### 23.5.1. The Plaque Assay

The **plaque** assay of [7.11.22](/7.11.22#sec-22.6.2) is in its original form a phage method: a dilution of the phage suspension is mixed with host bacteria in molten soft agar and poured over a nutrient plate; the bacteria grow into a turbid lawn, and each infectious particle produces a clear zone 1–2 mm across. The count gives the titre in PFU/ml. Plating efficiencies for phages are high, often above $50\,\%$, so plaque counts come close to particle counts — one reason phages are such convenient quantitative material. Plaque morphology reports on the phage's biology: a turbid plaque indicates a temperate phage, because lysogens grow within it; small plaques indicate a long latent period, a small burst or a poor diffusion through the agar.

### 23.5.2. One-Step Growth, Single Bursts and Premature Lysis

![Resolving a single cycle of phage growth, simulated from one model: each infected cell accumulates particles from the end of the eclipse period (12 min) and lyses at a time drawn from a normal distribution (mean 25 min, standard deviation 3 min) with a burst of 100. A: the one-step growth experiment. Phage is adsorbed and the culture then diluted, so released particles cannot reinfect. The number of plaque-forming units measured in the culture stays constant through the latent period — each infected cell gives one plaque, however many phages it contains — and rises during the rise period to a plateau a hundredfold higher: the burst size is the ratio of the two. B: the premature-lysis experiment of Doermann. Samples are lysed artificially before the cells burst. No infectious phage is found for the first 12 min — the eclipse period, the time needed to replicate the genome and assemble the first complete particle — after which the intracellular count rises linearly, as though particles came off an assembly line, while the DNA pool (dotted) has been rising since about 5 min; free phage in the medium (dashed) appears only when the cells lyse. C: the single-burst experiment. An infected culture at low multiplicity is distributed into 400 small tubes with on average 0.6 infected cells each, and every tube is plated after lysis. The distribution of yields is wide, which shows that the burst size is a property of a single cell; its mean over occupied tubes (130 here) overestimates the burst size (100) because some tubes contain more than one infected cell — the correction Ellis and Delbrück made when they reported 59 phages per tube and 48 per burst.](./plots/one-step-growth.py)

Ellis and Delbrück's 1939 experiment is the origin of the one-step growth curve and is worth following in its own terms. Phage T4 was added to a growing culture of *E. coli*; after ten minutes for adsorption the whole culture was diluted ten-thousandfold, so that collisions between released phage and uninfected cells became negligible; samples were taken at intervals and plated. The count stayed constant for 25 min, rose between 25 and 45 min, and then levelled off at a hundred times the original value. The interpretation is the one given in [7.11.22](/7.11.22#sec-22.6.6): during the **latent period** each infected cell is one infective centre and gives one plaque regardless of how many phages it contains; when the cells lyse, each contributes its burst; and the ratio of plateau to initial count is the **burst size**.

Two refinements complete the picture.

In the *single-burst experiment*, the diluted culture is distributed into many small tubes, with on average rather less than one infected cell per tube, so that by Poisson statistics most occupied tubes contain exactly one. Plating each tube separately after lysis shows what a single cell produces. The yields are widely scattered — a few particles in some tubes, hundreds in others — so the burst size is a distribution, not a constant, and the bulk value is its mean. Ellis and Delbrück obtained a mean of 59 phages per occupied tube, 48 after correcting for tubes with two infected cells.

In the *premature lysis experiment* (Doermann), samples are lysed artificially — with chloroform, or by a superinfecting phage — before the cells would have burst, and the lysate is assayed. For the first twelve minutes nothing infectious is found at all: this is the **eclipse period**, and its existence is the direct demonstration that the infecting particle is disassembled and that progeny particles are built from parts rather than copied. After that the intracellular count rises linearly, which says that particles are completed one at a time rather than all at once, and that assembly, not replication, is rate-limiting at the end. Measuring phage DNA instead of infectivity shows a pool of DNA accumulating from about five minutes, well before the first particle exists.

### 23.5.3. A Model of the Curve

The curve in panel A of the figure is produced by a model with three parameters, which is all that the experiment determines: an eclipse period $\tau_{\mathrm{e}}$, a lysis time $\tau$ distributed about a mean $\bar\tau$ with standard deviation $\sigma_\tau$, and a burst size $\beta$. If $F(t)$ is the cumulative distribution of lysis times, the free phage per infected cell is

$$
P(t) = \beta\,F(t) , \tag{23.1}
$$

so the rise period reflects nothing but the spread of lysis times: a sharp distribution gives a step, and a broad one gives the gradual rise actually observed. Unlysed cells still count as one infective centre each, so the measured titre is $N_0[\,1 - F(t) + \beta F(t)\,]$, which starts at $N_0$ and ends at $\beta N_0$. The intracellular curve measured by premature lysis is $\beta\,(t - \tau_{\mathrm{e}})/(\bar\tau - \tau_{\mathrm{e}})$ per unfinished cell, the assembly line of the previous paragraph. These three parameters — latent period, burst size and the variance of lysis time — are what natural selection acts on in a phage, and Section 23.9 shows how they enter the population dynamics.

## 23.6. Lysis

A phage that lyses a Gram-negative host must breach three barriers — the cytoplasmic membrane, the peptidoglycan and the outer membrane — and it must do so at a time of its own choosing, because lysing early wastes the cell and lysing late wastes time. The λ paradigm uses one protein per barrier plus a clock.

![The lysis of the host cell. A: the λ-type pathway in three steps, on cross-sections of the Gram-negative envelope. During late gene expression the endolysin accumulates harmlessly in the cytoplasm while the holin accumulates in the inner membrane. At a genetically programmed moment the holin triggers: the molecules coalesce into rafts and open micron-scale holes, the proton-motive force collapses, and the endolysin escapes into the periplasm, where it hydrolyses the peptidoglycan. Degradation of the wall then frees the spanins — an inner-membrane subunit and an outer-membrane lipoprotein that span the periplasm — to fuse the inner and outer membranes, removing the last barrier. The whole sequence takes seconds. B: the clock. The holin gene has two translational starts; the upstream one gives the antiholin, which carries an extra positively charged residue that keeps its first transmembrane domain out of the bilayer and binds holin in an inactive heterodimer. Triggering occurs when the free holin reaches a threshold concentration, and because depolarization then releases the antiholin's blocked helix into the membrane, the number of hole-competent molecules abruptly rises — an all-or-none switch. Collapsing the membrane potential artificially, with cyanide or a protonophore, triggers lysis at once, which is how the mechanism was found. C: the pinholin–SAR endolysin pathway of phage 21. The endolysin is exported by the Sec system and tethered to the membrane in an inactive form; the pinholin makes small heptameric channels that depolarize the membrane without letting protein through; depolarization releases the SAR endolysin, which refolds into its active form in the periplasm.](./plots/lysis.py)

### 23.6.1. The Holin Clock

**Definition 23.6.1 (Holin)** A **holin** is a small phage-encoded membrane protein that accumulates harmlessly in the cytoplasmic membrane during late gene expression and then, at a genetically determined time, abruptly oligomerizes to form holes that permeabilize the membrane, collapse the proton-motive force and release the endolysin to the peptidoglycan. The holin sets the time of lysis and therefore the length of the latent period.

Holins are the purest biological timers known: the time of triggering is encoded in the protein's sequence, is reproducible to within a minute or two, and can be shifted by single missense mutations, which lengthen or shorten the latent period without changing anything else. The current model is that holin molecules accumulate as dimers, and that above a critical surface concentration they condense into two-dimensional rafts, which locally depolarize the membrane; depolarization drives a conformational change that opens micron-scale holes, large enough for a folded enzyme to pass. The clock is therefore a concentration threshold, not a timer in the chemical sense, which is why anything that depresses the proton-motive force — cyanide, a protonophore, even abrupt anaerobiosis caused by stopping the shaker — triggers lysis immediately. That observation, the "premature lysis" of the 1940s phage group, is now the main evidence for the mechanism.

The timer has a built-in brake. The λ holin gene has two translational start codons in the same frame; the upstream start adds two residues, one of them positively charged, which prevents the first transmembrane helix from inserting and converts the product S107 into an *antiholin* that binds S105 and holds it inactive. The ratio of the two products, about 2:1, is fixed by a stem-loop in the mRNA. At the moment of triggering the depolarization releases the antiholin's blocked helix into the membrane, so the number of hole-forming molecules abruptly triples: the switch is made sharp by converting the inhibitor into an activator at the threshold.

### 23.6.2. Endolysin and Spanin

**Definition 23.6.2 (Endolysin)** An **endolysin** is a phage-encoded peptidoglycan hydrolase that degrades the cell wall from within at the end of infection. Endolysins fall into five enzymatic classes by the bond they cut — glycosidases (muramidases and glucosaminidases), lytic transglycosylases, amidases and endopeptidases — and in phages of Gram-positive hosts they typically have a separate cell-wall-binding domain.

With the peptidoglycan gone, a Gram-positive cell bursts. A Gram-negative cell does not: the outer membrane holds. The third component, the *spanin*, solves this. The λ spanin is a two-component complex of an inner-membrane protein (Rz) and an outer-membrane lipoprotein (Rz1) that reach across the periplasm and are held apart by the intact peptidoglycan; once the wall is degraded they are free to oligomerize and to fuse the two membranes, which abolishes the outer membrane as a topological barrier. Deleting the spanin genes gives cells that round up into spherical, unlysed forms after the endolysin has worked — the clearest demonstration of what each component does.

### 23.6.3. Variations

The *pinholin–SAR endolysin* pathway of phage 21 reverses the logic. Its endolysin is exported by the Sec pathway ([7.11.34](/7.11.34)) and held in the periplasm in an inactive form, tethered to the membrane by a signal-anchor-release (SAR) domain. Its pinholin makes only small heptameric channels, too small for a protein to pass but sufficient to depolarize the membrane; depolarization releases the SAR domain from the bilayer, the endolysin refolds into its active conformation, and lysis follows. The timing device is the same; what differs is where the enzyme waits.

The small RNA and ssDNA phages have no room for a three-gene lysis cassette and use *single-gene lysis* instead: one small protein that inhibits an enzyme of peptidoglycan synthesis, so that the growing cell lyses itself. Protein E of φX174 inhibits the translocase MraY, and protein A2 of the RNA phage Qβ inhibits MurA; both act like a protein antibiotic on a specific step of the pathway of [7.11.8](/7.11.8#sec-8.3), and both kill only growing cells.

### 23.6.4. Why Timing Matters

The latent period set by the holin is an optimization problem. Lysing early releases fewer particles but starts the next round sooner; lysing late yields more per cell but risks the cell dying first and wastes the interval during which the particles sit inside. The optimum depends on host density: when hosts are abundant, a short latent period wins, because finding the next host is fast; when hosts are scarce, a long latent period and a large burst win. Phages do in fact evolve latent periods in the predicted direction in serial transfer experiments, and holin mutations are the usual route. The same reasoning, applied to a temperate phage, becomes the lysis–lysogeny decision.

## 23.7. The Ecology of the Lysis–Lysogeny Decision

The molecular switch of phage λ — the mutual repression of CI and Cro, the role of CII and its protease FtsH, the bistability and its induction by RecA — is described in [7.2.11](/7.2.11#sec-11.5) and will not be repeated. What concerns microbiology is what the switch is *for*.

Two inputs dominate. **Multiplicity of infection**: each infecting genome contributes copies of *cII* and *cIII*, so several genomes in one cell raise CII above the threshold for lysogeny; a high MOI means that hosts are scarce relative to phages, exactly the condition in which killing the host would be a poor investment. *Host physiology*: starved cells, in which proteases are active and biosynthesis slow, favour lysogeny, while a well-fed cell capable of supporting a large burst favours lysis. The decision is thus an estimate of whether the environment can support a lytic cycle, computed with the molecules at hand.

The decision is also noisy, in the technical sense: genetically identical phages infecting genetically identical cells under the same conditions do not all make the same choice, and single-cell experiments show that the outcome depends on the number of genomes, the size of the cell and the fluctuations of a few molecules ([7.6.32](/7.6.32), [7.9.17](/7.9.17)). A mixed strategy is itself adaptive when the future is uncertain, exactly as in the bet-hedging argument for sporulation ([7.11.12](/7.11.12#sec-12.4)).

Some phages communicate rather than estimate. Phages of the SPbeta group infecting *Bacillus* encode a small peptide, arbitrium, which is secreted by the infected cell, accumulates in the medium as infections proceed and is taken up by newly infected cells, where it binds a receptor that suppresses lysis; a high peptide concentration therefore reports that many hosts have already been consumed, and biases later infections towards lysogeny (Erez et al., 2017). It is a quorum-sensing system operated by a virus ([7.11.11](/7.11.11)).

At the level of communities, these decisions produce two contrasting regimes. In the *kill-the-winner* picture, lytic phages crop whichever bacterial population grows fastest, so phage predation maintains diversity by preventing any one host from dominating. In the *piggyback-the-winner* picture, lysogeny increases where host densities are high, because the temperate phage does better by replicating with a thriving host than by killing it. Both patterns are seen in marine and gut data, and reconciling them is an active question of microbial ecology ([7.11.29](/7.11.29)).

## 23.8. The Arms Race

Phage predation is strong enough that bacteria devote a few per cent of their genomes to defence, and phages devote a comparable share to counter-defence. The systems are layered: they act at adsorption, at entry, at the incoming genome, and — when all else fails — on the infected cell itself.

| Level | Bacterial defence | Phage counter-measure |
|---|---|---|
| Adsorption | Loss, alteration or masking of the receptor; capsule and O-antigen; **phase variation** of surface structures; outer-membrane vesicles as decoys | Mutation of the receptor-binding protein; depolymerases in the tail spikes that digest capsule and biofilm matrix; use of an alternative receptor |
| Entry | Superinfection-exclusion proteins of a resident prophage (Imm and Sp of T4) that block translocation of the DNA | Infect a cell without that prophage; evolve resistance to the exclusion protein |
| The incoming genome | **Restriction–modification system**s; BREX and DISARM, which block replication of the phage DNA instead of cutting it; CRISPR–Cas, which cuts sequences recorded from past infections | Hypermodified bases (5-hydroxymethylcytosine, glucosylated, in T4); the host's own methyltransferase or one of the phage's own; restriction inhibitors (Ocr of T7, Dar of P1, Stp of T4); depletion of recognition sites; escape mutations in the protospacer or PAM; **anti-CRISPR protein**s; a nucleus-like shell around the replicating DNA |
| The infected cell | **Abortive infection**: toxin–antitoxin modules, Rex, Lit, CBASS, Thoeris, retrons, type III CRISPR and Cas13 — sensors that kill or arrest the cell before progeny are finished, saving the clone | Inhibitors of the sensors; nucleotide "sponges" and hydrolases that absorb or destroy the cyclic signalling molecules of CBASS and Thoeris; faster replication than the sensor can act |

### 23.8.1. Preventing Adsorption

The simplest resistance is to remove the receptor, and in the laboratory this is how resistant mutants almost always arise. It is rarely free: losing LamB costs growth on maltose, losing OmpF or OmpC alters permeability, losing a pilus or a flagellum costs conjugation or motility ([7.11.3](/7.11.3)). The cost is exactly what makes phage–antibiotic combinations attractive, when phage resistance forces a change in an efflux pump or an outer-membrane protein that also restores antibiotic sensitivity.

Masking the receptor is cheaper than losing it: capsules and extracellular polysaccharide bury the surface, O-antigen chains extend beyond outer-membrane proteins, and proteins can be made that cover the binding site. **Phase variation** ([7.11.34](/7.11.34)) makes the defence stochastic — a fraction of the population expresses the receptor at any time, so the population as a whole is never wiped out and never completely resistant. Outer-membrane vesicles act as decoys that adsorb phages away from cells.

Phages answer in kind: receptor-binding proteins mutate rapidly, many tailed phages carry polysaccharide depolymerases in their tail spikes that drill through capsule and biofilm matrix, and some can switch between alternative receptors.

### 23.8.2. Blocking Entry

A resident prophage often protects its host from related phages at the step of DNA translocation rather than adsorption — *superinfection exclusion*. T4 itself encodes two such proteins: Imm, which blocks the translocation of DNA from a superinfecting T-even phage across the membrane, and Sp, which inhibits the degradation of the peptidoglycan by the superinfecting phage's tail lysozyme, so that its DNA is trapped in the periplasm. From the first phage's point of view this protects the investment it has already made in the cell.

### 23.8.3. Restriction–Modification and Its Evasion

A **restriction–modification system** ([7.2.1](/7.2.1), [7.2.22](/7.2.22)) discriminates self from non-self chemically: a methyltransferase marks the host's own recognition sites, and an endonuclease cleaves DNA in which those sites are unmethylated. Type I enzymes are multi-subunit complexes that cut at a distance; type II enzymes — the tools of genetic engineering — cut within or near the site; type III cut near asymmetric sites that must occur in a particular relative orientation; and type IV cut *modified* DNA instead, which is a defence against phages that evade the first three by modifying their own bases.

Phage counter-measures are as diverse as the systems themselves, and T4 illustrates most of them.

- *Modify the DNA so it is not a substrate.* T4 replaces cytosine with 5-hydroxymethylcytosine and then glucosylates it; the resulting DNA is cut by no known *E. coli* restriction enzyme. Other phages incorporate other hypermodified bases.
- *Borrow or carry a methyltransferase.* A phage genome that survives one cycle is methylated by the host enzyme and is protected in the next; some phages encode their own methyltransferase or, like λ with Ral, stimulate the host's.
- *Inhibit the enzyme.* T7 injects Ocr, a protein whose surface charge distribution mimics a bent B-DNA duplex and which binds and sequesters the type I enzyme EcoKI; T4 Stp perturbs the restriction–modification complex; P1 co-injects the Dar proteins, which bind the DNA and physically mask the sites.
- *Have fewer sites.* Phage genomes are statistically depleted in the recognition sequences of the common restriction enzymes of their hosts, and T7 and T3 carry their EcoRII and EcoP15I sites in orientations or spacings that the enzymes cannot cut.

The escalation continues: *E. coli* strains with the Gmr system (GmrS–GmrD) specifically cleave glucosylated hydroxymethylcytosine DNA, so T4-like phages acquired the internal protein IPI, injected with the DNA, which inhibits Gmr; some hosts then fused GmrS to GmrD, making IPI ineffective. Reciprocal escalation at a single locus, documented step by step, is the clearest molecular picture of an arms race in all of biology.

### 23.8.4. CRISPR–Cas

![CRISPR–Cas as an immune system. A: the locus — *cas* genes, a leader and an array of identical repeats (grey triangles) separated by spacers acquired from past invaders, newest at the leader end, so the array is a dated record of infections. Below it, adaptation: Cas1–Cas2 excises a protospacer from the invading genome, choosing it next to a protospacer-adjacent motif (PAM), and integrates it at the leader-proximal end; in type I systems a partial match that fails to be cut primes rapid acquisition of further spacers. B: expression and interference. The array is transcribed as a pre-crRNA and processed into individual crRNAs, each carrying one spacer, which assemble with Cas proteins into surveillance complexes; the complex scans DNA for a PAM, then for complementarity beginning at the seed region next to it, and a match opens an R loop and licenses cleavage. The spacer inside the array is flanked by repeats, not by a PAM, so the cell never cuts its own locus. C: the two classes and six types, with the effector and what it does. D: the phage counter-measures — escape mutations in the seed or the PAM, anti-CRISPR proteins, phage-encoded CRISPR systems, and the nucleus-like compartment of jumbo phages.](./plots/crispr.py)

CRISPR–Cas is adaptive immunity: it records past infections and uses the record to recognize them again. The discovery that the spacers in these arrays match phage and plasmid sequences (2005), and the demonstration in *Streptococcus thermophilus* that a culture surviving a phage challenge has acquired new spacers from that phage and is resistant to it (Barrangou et al., 2007), established the function; its conversion into a genome-editing tool is treated in [7.2.24](/7.2.24#sec-24.4) and is not the subject here.

The system works in three stages.

*Adaptation.* Cas1 and Cas2, the only proteins common to all types, excise a short piece of the invader's genome — a **protospacer** — and integrate it as a new spacer at the leader-proximal end of the array. Selection of the protospacer is not random: it must lie next to a short **protospacer adjacent motif** (PAM), typically two to five nucleotides. Because new spacers are added at one end, the array is a dated record of the cell's encounters, which makes CRISPR loci useful for strain typing and for tracing phage–host interactions in nature.

*Expression.* The array is transcribed as a long pre-crRNA and cut into individual crRNAs, each containing one spacer and part of a repeat, which assemble with Cas proteins into surveillance complexes.

*Interference.* The complex scans DNA for a PAM; only then does it test for base pairing, starting in the *seed* region immediately adjacent to the PAM. A match unwinds the duplex into an R loop and licenses cleavage. The requirement for a PAM solves the self/non-self problem: the spacer within the array is flanked by repeats, not by a PAM, so the cell never cleaves its own locus.

The systems are classified into two classes and six types. Class 1 uses multi-subunit effector complexes: type I (Cascade, which recruits the nuclease–helicase Cas3 to degrade the target processively), type III (Csm and Cmr, which bind nascent transcripts and cleave both the RNA and the DNA being transcribed, and which also synthesize cyclic oligoadenylate second messengers that switch on indiscriminate RNases — an abortive-infection-like response) and type IV. Class 2 uses a single large protein: type II (Cas9, dual-RNA-guided, the basis of genome editing), type V (Cas12, which also shows collateral single-stranded DNase activity) and type VI (Cas13, which targets RNA and, once activated, cleaves RNA indiscriminately, arresting the cell). Roughly $40\,\%$ of bacterial and $85\,\%$ of archaeal genomes carry a CRISPR–Cas system.

Phages evade it at every stage.

- *Escape mutants.* A single substitution in the seed region or in the PAM abolishes interference. Type I systems counter this with *priming*: a partial match, which does not permit cleavage, strongly stimulates acquisition of further spacers from the same genome, so the host updates its memory faster than the phage can escape it.
- **Definition 23.8.1 (Anti-CRISPR Protein)** An **anti-CRISPR protein** is a small phage-encoded protein that inhibits a CRISPR–Cas system, by blocking DNA binding by the surveillance complex, by preventing the recruitment or the activity of the nuclease, by mimicking DNA, or by degrading the signalling nucleotides of type III systems. The first examples were found in prophages of *Pseudomonas aeruginosa* (Bondy-Denomy et al., 2013); dozens of families are now known, with no sequence similarity to one another, and they are widely used as off-switches for Cas9 in genome editing.
- *Phage-encoded CRISPR.* Some phages of *Vibrio cholerae* carry their own CRISPR–Cas system whose spacers match a phage-inducible chromosomal island in the host that would otherwise block the infection: the phage uses adaptive immunity against the host's defence.
- *Compartmentalization.* Jumbo phages of the φKZ group build a proteinaceous shell around their replicating DNA, which excludes DNA-targeting nucleases, including Cas9 and restriction enzymes, while allowing mRNA out.

### 23.8.5. Abortive Infection and Suicide

**Definition 23.8.2 (Abortive Infection)** **Abortive infection** (Abi) is a defence in which an infected cell, having failed to block the infection, kills itself or arrests its own metabolism before progeny phages are completed. The cell is lost, but the phage is not amplified, and the surrounding clone — genetically identical, and therefore sharing the alleles responsible — is protected. It is a form of altruistic suicide, and it is selected at the level of the clone rather than of the individual.

The mechanisms share an architecture: a sensor that detects an infection-specific event, and an effector that destroys something essential.

- *Classical Abi systems.* The Rex system of a λ lysogen detects a protein–DNA intermediate of a superinfecting phage; RexA activates RexB, an ion channel that depolarizes the membrane. Lit of *E. coli* is activated by the T4 capsid peptide Gol and cleaves the elongation factor EF-Tu, stopping translation. AbiZ of *Lactococcus* accelerates lysis so that it occurs before the particles are assembled.
- **Toxin–antitoxin system**s ([7.11.13](/7.11.13#sec-13.2.3)) serve as Abi modules when the infection destabilizes the labile antitoxin or when the toxin is specifically activated by a phage protein; the freed toxin cleaves mRNA, blocks translation or depletes NAD⁺.
- *CBASS* (cyclic-oligonucleotide-based antiphage signalling system), present in over $10\,\%$ of sequenced bacterial genomes, uses a cGAS-like nucleotidyltransferase that is activated by infection and synthesizes a cyclic di- or trinucleotide, which binds an effector — a phospholipase, a nuclease, or a pore-forming protein — that kills the cell.
- *Thoeris* uses a TIR-domain protein that, on sensing infection, produces a cyclic ADP-ribose isomer; this second messenger activates ThsA, which destroys the cell's NAD⁺.
- *Retrons* are tripartite modules of a reverse transcriptase, a non-coding RNA that it copies into a multicopy single-stranded DNA, and an effector; the RT–msDNA complex appears to guard a host process, and releases the effector when a phage protein disturbs it.

### 23.8.6. The Pan-Immune System

Until about 2018 the known defences were restriction–modification, CRISPR–Cas and a scattering of Abi systems. They were then discovered to be only the visible part of a much larger repertoire, found by a simple and powerful heuristic: defence genes cluster in *defence islands*, so genes of unknown function located in those islands are candidate defence systems, and can be tested directly by cloning them into a naive host and challenging it with phages. Dozens of new systems have been characterized by this route — BREX and DISARM, which use methylation to mark self but block the replication of phage DNA rather than cleaving it; Gabija, Hachiman, Septu, Lamassu, Zorya, Wadjet, Shedu and others, with nuclease, membrane-depolarizing or chromosome-segregating effectors; Pycsar, which signals with cyclic pyrimidine mononucleotides; prokaryotic Argonaute proteins, which use short DNA guides; prokaryotic viperins, which make chain-terminating nucleotide analogues that poison phage transcription; bacterial gasdermins, which form pores; and Avs proteins, which are STAND NTPases that directly recognize conserved phage proteins such as the portal or the terminase.

Two generalizations have come out of this work.

- *Defence is distributed and mobile.* An individual genome carries only a handful of systems, but a species carries dozens across its **pangenome** ([7.11.13](/7.11.13#sec-13.1.4)), and defence genes move readily on prophages, plasmids, integrative elements and phage satellites. Bernheim and Sorek proposed the term *pan-immune system*: immunity is a property of the population, held in a shared and circulating pool, with each cell drawing a sample from it. This is a direct parallel to the diversity of antigen receptors within an animal's lymphocyte population, generated in a quite different way.
- *Innate immunity is older than animals.* The homologies are not vague: the cGAS–STING pathway of animals is descended from CBASS, the TIR domains that signal in plant and animal immunity work in Thoeris, STAND NLR receptors have the architecture of Avs proteins, viperin and gasdermin have bacterial ancestors, and argonautes are shared. The eukaryotic innate immune system is in substantial part an inheritance from the bacterial arms race ([7.11.27](/7.11.27)).

## 23.9. Phage and Host as a Population

![Phage and bacteria in a chemostat, from the model of Levin, Stewart and Chao (1977): a limiting resource flows in at concentration s₀ and is diluted at rate D; sensitive bacteria grow on it with Monod kinetics; phages adsorb at rate δ and, after a latent period τ, release β progeny, which enters as a delayed term (Eq. (23.2); μmax = 0.7 h⁻¹, Ks = 0.5 μg ml⁻¹, yield 2 × 10⁶ cells per μg, s₀ = 10 μg ml⁻¹, D = 0.2 h⁻¹, δ = 1.5 × 10⁻⁹ ml h⁻¹, β = 40, τ = 0.5 h). A: the sensitive host and the phage settle into bounded oscillations about a coexistence state in which the host is held about fivefold below the density it would reach alone (dashed line), with a period set by the latent period and the growth rate. B: the resource, which now accumulates to several μg ml⁻¹ instead of the 0.2 μg ml⁻¹ that the host alone would leave: control has passed from the resource to the predator. C: a resistant mutant, which has lost the receptor and pays for it with a 15 % lower maximum growth rate, is introduced at t = 50 h. It sweeps while the phage is abundant, the sensitive population and the phage both fall, and because resistance is costly the sensitive type recovers when phages become scarce, so that all three persist. D: the phage's growth rate (each curve normalized to its own maximum) against its latent period, when a longer latent period buys a larger burst. The optimum is short when hosts are abundant and long when they are scarce.](./plots/phage-chemostat.py)

The quantitative study of phage–bacterium dynamics began with the chemostat experiments and model of Levin, Stewart and Chao (1977), which remain the standard framework. With a limiting resource $s$, sensitive bacteria $N$, free phage $P$, dilution rate $D$, Monod growth $\mu(s) = \mu_{\max} s/(K_{\mathrm{s}} + s)$, adsorption rate constant $\delta$, latent period $\tau$ and burst size $\beta$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\mathrm{d}s}{\mathrm{d}t} &= D(s_0 - s) - \frac{\mu(s)N}{Y} ,\\
\frac{\mathrm{d}N}{\mathrm{d}t} &= \mu(s)N - \delta NP - DN ,\\
\frac{\mathrm{d}P}{\mathrm{d}t} &= \beta\,\delta\,N(t-\tau)P(t-\tau)\,\mathrm{e}^{-D\tau} - \delta NP - DP .
\end{aligned} \tag{23.2}
$$

The chemostat equations themselves are those of [7.6.39](/7.6.39) and [7.11.8](/7.11.8#sec-8.5); what the phage adds is a predator term with a delay. Three results are worth stating.

*Coexistence rather than extinction.* The phage term drives the host density down to the value at which phage production balances washout, $N^* \approx D/[\delta(\beta\mathrm{e}^{-D\tau} - 1)]$, far below the resource-limited density; the resource then accumulates almost to $s_0$, the signature of top-down control. The delay makes the approach oscillatory, with a period of the order of the latent period plus a generation time, and the oscillations damp in a well-mixed chemostat but persist in structured environments.

*Resistance changes the regime, and its cost preserves diversity.* Receptor mutants arise at the usual rates ([7.4.12](/7.4.12)) and sweep while the phage is abundant. If resistance were free, the phage would be lost and the system would return to resource limitation; because it is not free, the sensitive strain persists as a minority, the phage persists on it, and the community settles into a coexistence of sensitive and resistant hosts with phage — the simplest model of the diversity that phage predation maintains in natural communities.

*Phage life-history parameters are under selection.* The model makes the trade-off of Section 23.6.4 explicit: increasing $\beta$ requires increasing $\tau$, and the combination that maximizes the phage's growth rate depends on $N$. Serial-transfer experiments confirm that latent periods evolve downwards at high host density and upwards at low density, and that phage and host can be propagated in a continuing arms race (escalating resistance and counter-resistance) or in fluctuating selection (cycling through receptor variants), with the outcome depending on the mutational supply and on the cost of each step.

Two caveats keep this from being the whole story. Real communities are spatially structured, so that encounters are local and refuges exist; and most natural phages are temperate, so that the "predator" can also become part of the host genome, which neither the Lotka–Volterra picture nor the model above represents. These are the themes of microbial ecology in [7.11.29](/7.11.29).

## Summary

- Phages are the most abundant biological entities on Earth ($\sim 10^{31}$ particles; $10^5$–$10^7$ per ml of seawater; a virus-to-cell ratio of about 10), the source of most of the foundational experiments of molecular biology, and the subject of **phage therapy** (Definition 23.1.1), now returning for resistant and biofilm infections in narrow-spectrum, personalized form.
- Most isolates are tailed dsDNA phages (myo-, sipho- and podovirus morphologies, which are descriptions and not taxa); filamentous, small icosahedral ssDNA, ssRNA and enveloped dsRNA phages are important exceptions. **Virulent phage**s only lyse; temperate phages can lysogenize, and **lysogenic conversion** (Definition 23.3.2) gives their hosts new properties, including the toxins of diphtheria, cholera and *E. coli* O157:H7.
- T4 binds lipopolysaccharide or OmpC with its long tail fibres in a reversible, searching phase, commits through the short fibres, contracts its sheath and injects its genome; other morphotypes build channels in other ways. Its infection runs on a timetable enforced by progressive modification of the host RNA polymerase (ADP-ribosylation, AsiA, MotA, the late σ factor gp55 with the sliding clamp gp45 as a mobile enhancer), on a genome made of glucosylated hydroxymethylcytosine, and on headful packaging from concatemers, which makes the genomes circularly permuted and terminally redundant.
- The one-step growth, single-burst and premature-lysis experiments resolve the cycle into **eclipse period**, **latent period**, rise and **burst size**, show that burst size is a cell-level random variable, and show that particles are assembled one at a time; a three-parameter model (Eq. (23.1)) reproduces the curves.
- Lysis of a Gram-negative host needs three functions and a clock: the **holin** (Definition 23.6.1) accumulates and triggers at a genetically set time, collapsing the proton-motive force; the **endolysin** (Definition 23.6.2) degrades the peptidoglycan; the spanin fuses the inner and outer membranes. An antiholin made from a second translational start sharpens the switch. The pinholin–SAR endolysin pathway and the single-gene lysis of small phages are variations on the same logic, and the time of lysis is a life-history trait under selection.
- The lysis–lysogeny decision integrates multiplicity of infection and host physiology, is stochastic, and in some *Bacillus* phages is communicated by the arbitrium peptide; at the community level it appears as kill-the-winner and piggyback-the-winner dynamics.
- Bacterial defence is layered — loss or masking of receptors, superinfection exclusion, **restriction–modification system**s and their relatives, CRISPR–Cas adaptive immunity (adaptation, expression, interference, with PAM and seed providing specificity and self/non-self discrimination), and **abortive infection** (Definition 23.8.2) by toxin–antitoxin modules, CBASS, Thoeris and retrons — and each is met by counter-measures: base modification, methyltransferases, restriction inhibitors such as Ocr, escape mutations, **anti-CRISPR protein**s (Definition 23.8.1), phage-encoded CRISPR, and nucleus-like compartments. Defence genes cluster in defence islands and move between genomes, so immunity is a property of the pangenome — the pan-immune system — and several of its components are the ancestors of eukaryotic innate immunity (cGAS–STING, TIR, STAND NLR, viperin, gasdermin).
- In a chemostat (Eq. (23.2)) phage predation holds the host far below its resource-limited density, produces damped oscillations with a period set by the latent period, and selects resistant mutants whose cost permits coexistence of sensitive hosts, resistant hosts and phage.

## Questions

1. A therapeutic phage preparation is screened against a patient's *Klebsiella pneumoniae* isolate. One candidate gives large clear plaques, another gives small turbid plaques, and a third gives clear plaques only on a plate also containing the patient's isolate grown overnight in the presence of the phage. Which would you take forward, and what further checks are essential before use?

   **Answer** The clear-plaque phage is the candidate: turbid plaques indicate a temperate phage, which would lysogenize rather than kill the pathogen and could carry or mobilize toxin and resistance genes. The third observation describes a phage that plates on an already-exposed culture, which suggests it is being propagated on a resistant or lysogenized derivative and is unreliable. Before use one must sequence the chosen phage's genome to exclude integrase, repressor, toxin and antibiotic-resistance genes (strictly lytic by genome, not only by plaque morphology), confirm activity against the isolate in liquid culture and at the relevant cell densities, purify the preparation to remove endotoxin released by lysis, determine the titre and its stability in storage, and ideally assemble a cocktail of phages using different receptors, so that a single receptor mutation does not confer resistance to the whole preparation.

2. A holin missense mutant of phage λ lyses its host at 60 min instead of 50 min. Predict the effect on burst size and on the phage's fitness at high and at low host density, and explain how a 10 min change in lysis time can result from a single amino acid substitution.

   **Answer** Particles continue to accumulate at a roughly constant rate during the latent period, so a later lysis gives a larger burst — perhaps 20 % more. Fitness, however, is the rate of increase, not the yield per cell: at high host density the time to find the next host is short, so the generation time of the phage is dominated by the latent period and the earlier-lysing wild type wins; at low host density the search dominates, the extra ten minutes cost little, and the larger burst wins. The timing is set by the concentration threshold at which holin molecules condense into rafts and open holes, so any substitution that changes the protein's propensity to oligomerize, its stability or its insertion into the bilayer shifts the threshold and hence the time at which the accumulating protein reaches it; the clock is a concentration, not a chemical timer, which is why single substitutions move it by many minutes and why collapsing the membrane potential triggers lysis at once.

3. A *Streptococcus thermophilus* culture is challenged with a lytic phage, and survivors are isolated. Half of them have acquired a new CRISPR spacer; the rest have not. How would you determine the basis of resistance in each group, and what would you expect if you then challenged both groups with a phage carrying a point mutation in the protospacer used by the first group?

   **Answer** For the first group, sequence the CRISPR array to confirm the new spacer and check that it matches the phage, and delete or inactivate the *cas* genes: if resistance disappears, it is CRISPR-mediated. For the second group, test adsorption of the phage to the cells — a failure of adsorption indicates a receptor or surface-polysaccharide mutation, which can be confirmed by sequencing the relevant genes and by the usual cost (altered surface, slower growth). The mutant phage with a changed protospacer should escape the CRISPR-based resistance, plating with near wild-type efficiency on that group; but in a type I or II system a partial match also primes rapid acquisition of further spacers, so resistant clones reappear quickly. The receptor mutants will remain resistant to the mutant phage, since their defence is upstream of the genome altogether; the mutant phage escapes only if it has also altered its receptor-binding protein.

4. CBASS, Thoeris and toxin–antitoxin systems kill the infected cell. Explain how such a system can be maintained by selection, and what features of bacterial population structure are necessary for it. What would you predict about the distribution of abortive-infection systems between a well-mixed planktonic population and a biofilm?

   **Answer** The dying cell gains nothing; the alleles do. If a cell that commits suicide is surrounded by relatives carrying the same system, its death prevents a burst of perhaps a hundred phages that would otherwise infect them, so the inclusive fitness of the allele increases even though the individual's is zero — kin selection, requiring that relatedness among the neighbours of an infected cell be high and that the phage spread locally. Clonal growth from a single cell, limited dispersal and spatial structure all supply this. A biofilm or a colony, where neighbours are clonemates and diffusion is short-range, is therefore favourable to abortive infection, whereas in a well-mixed, dilute planktonic population the released phages would mostly infect unrelated cells and suicide would be a pure loss, favouring instead defences that save the individual (receptor loss, restriction, CRISPR). One would predict abortive-infection systems to be commoner in surface-associated and spatially structured lineages, and the prediction is testable by comparing genomes across lifestyles.

5. Using Eq. (23.2), explain why a phage cannot drive its host to extinction in a chemostat, and what changes if the host can become resistant at a cost.

   **Answer** Phage replication requires encounters, at a rate $\delta NP$ proportional to the host density; as $N$ falls the phage's own production falls with it, and at $N^* \approx D/[\delta(\beta \mathrm{e}^{-D\tau} - 1)]$ production only balances washout, so the phage cannot be maintained below that host density and the host is released from predation whenever it drops lower. The system therefore converges on a steady state (through damped oscillations caused by the latent-period delay) rather than on extinction; stochastic extinction is possible in small or spatially restricted populations, but not in the deterministic well-mixed model. With a resistant mutant, the phage loses its substrate: the resistant type sweeps while phage are abundant. If resistance were free the phage would be washed out. Because it costs growth rate, the sensitive type competes better for the resource whenever phage are scarce, and the three populations settle into a coexistence in which the phage persists on a minority sensitive subpopulation — resistance cost converts what would be a competitive exclusion into stable diversity.
