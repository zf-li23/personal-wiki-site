Archaea were first recognized in the 1970s among organisms of extreme habitats: methanogens of anoxic mud, extreme halophiles of salt lakes, and hyperthermophiles of boiling springs. This shaped the image of the domain as a collection of extremophiles. Molecular surveys of the environment have since shown that archaea are everywhere: they are among the most abundant cells in the deep ocean, numerous in soils and sediments, and present in the human gut, and metagenomes have revealed whole phyla that have never been cultured, among them the Asgard archaea, the closest prokaryotic relatives of eukaryotes. This chapter surveys the domain along its phylogeny. The comparison of archaeal, bacterial and eukaryotic cells is in [7.3.1](/7.3.1#sec-1.4), the archaeal membrane and cell walls in [7.11.2](/7.11.2#sec-2.2.2) and [7.11.2](/7.11.2#sec-2.4), the archaellum in [7.11.3](/7.11.3#sec-3.4.2), methanogenesis and anaerobic methane oxidation in [7.11.7](/7.11.7#sec-7.4.2), the adaptations to heat and salt in [7.11.9](/7.11.9), and the debate over three domains or two, LUCA and the origin of eukaryotes in [7.10.27](/7.10.27#sec-27.4) and [7.10.28](/7.10.28#sec-28.3.1); those are linked here, not repeated.

## 19.1. The Archaea as a Domain

### 19.1.1. Discovery

In 1977 Carl Woese and George Fox compared catalogs of oligonucleotides of small-subunit rRNA and found that methanogens differed from other prokaryotes as much as from eukaryotes ([7.11.1](/7.11.1#sec-1.7)). Other evidence soon supported the distinction: methanogens, halophiles and the acidophilic thermophiles *Sulfolobus* and *Thermoplasma* shared ether-linked isoprenoid lipids, lacked muramic acid in their walls, and had RNA polymerases of many subunits, resembling those of eukaryotes. Woese and colleagues named the group "archaebacteria" and in 1990 made it the domain Archaea of the **three-domain system** ([7.10.27](/7.10.27#sec-27.4)). The name, from the Greek for "ancient", reflected the belief that the habitats of these organisms resembled those of the early Earth; it describes neither their age, which is that of the bacteria, nor their ecology.

### 19.1.2. What Archaea Share with Bacteria and with Eukaryotes

| Character | Bacteria | Archaea | Eukaryotes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nucleus, membrane-bound organelles | absent | absent | present |
| Chromosome | usually one, circular | usually one, circular | several, linear |
| Operons, polycistronic mRNA | yes | yes | rare |
| Membrane lipids | fatty acids ester-linked to glycerol 3-phosphate | **isoprenoid** chains ether-linked to glycerol 1-phosphate; tetraether monolayers in many | as in bacteria |
| Cell wall | peptidoglycan (muramic acid) in nearly all | no peptidoglycan; **S-layer** in most, **pseudomurein** in some methanogens, other polymers, or none | none, cellulose or chitin |
| RNA polymerase | one, five subunits ($\alpha_2\beta\beta'\omega$) | one, about twelve subunits, resembling Pol II | three (Pol I–III) |
| Promoter recognition | σ factors | **TATA-binding protein** and TFB (TFIIB homolog) | TBP and TFIIB with many other factors |
| DNA replication proteins | DnaA, DnaB, Pol III | Orc1/Cdc6, MCM helicase, PCNA, family B and D polymerases | Orc, MCM, PCNA, family B polymerases |
| Histones | absent (other nucleoid proteins) | in many (histone-fold dimers that form extended "hypernucleosomes") | nucleosomes of octamers |
| Translation | formylmethionine initiator; Shine–Dalgarno | methionine initiator; Shine–Dalgarno; eukaryote-like initiation and elongation factors | methionine initiator; cap and scanning |
| Ribosomes | 70S | 70S, with archaea-specific proteins shared with eukaryotes | 80S |
| Introns | rare (group I, II) | in tRNA and rRNA genes, spliced by a specific endonuclease | spliceosomal, common |
| Energy metabolism unique to the domain | chlorophyll-based photosynthesis | **methanogenesis** | none |
| Pathogens of humans | many | none known | some |

The pattern, with the cell plan and metabolism of a prokaryote and the information-processing machinery of a eukaryote, is what the three-domain tree explained by making archaea and eukaryotes sister groups, and what the two-domain tree explains by placing eukaryotes within the archaea ([7.10.27](/7.10.27#sec-27.4)). The lipids are unique: their glycerol backbone has the opposite stereochemistry from that of bacteria and eukaryotes, which suggests that the two kinds of membranes arose separately after the divergence of the two primary lineages ([7.11.2](/7.11.2#sec-2.2.2)).

## 19.2. The Phylogeny of Archaea

### 19.2.1. Four Supergroups

Early rRNA trees divided the archaea into two phyla, the Euryarchaeota (methanogens, halophiles, *Thermoplasma*, *Thermococcus*, *Archaeoglobus*) and the Crenarchaeota (sulfur-dependent hyperthermophiles). Since the 2000s, environmental genomics has added so many lineages that the domain is now divided into four large groups:

- the former Euryarchaeota, split in GTDB into several phyla, chiefly the Methanobacteriota, the Halobacteriota and the Thermoplasmatota (the names differ between GTDB and the List of Prokaryotic Names with Standing in Nomenclature and are still being settled);
- the TACK superphylum, named for its first members (Thaumarchaeota, Aigarchaeota, Crenarchaeota, Korarchaeota), treated in GTDB as the single phylum Thermoproteota, whose classes include the hyperthermophilic Thermoprotei (the former Crenarchaeota), the ammonia-oxidizing Nitrososphaeria (the former Thaumarchaeota, validly named as the phylum **Nitrososphaerota** in 2021), the Korarchaeia and the Bathyarchaeia;
- the Asgard archaea, the sister group of eukaryotes (Section 19.8);
- the DPANN archaea, small cells with reduced genomes (Section 19.7).

![Major lineages of Archaea (cladogram; branch lengths have no meaning, unresolved relationships are drawn as polytomies, and the position of the root is debated; here it is drawn between the DPANN archaea and the rest). The former Euryarchaeota (orange) comprise the GTDB phyla Methanobacteriota, Halobacteriota and Thermoplasmatota; the TACK archaea (green), the GTDB phylum Thermoproteota, include the hyperthermophilic Thermoprotei, the ammonia-oxidizing Nitrososphaeria, the Korarchaeia and the Bathyarchaeia; eukaryotes (blue) branch within or beside the Asgard archaea (purple).](./plots/archaeal-tree.py)

Physiology maps onto this tree only loosely. Methanogenesis, once thought to be confined to the Euryarchaeota, has been inferred from genomes of Bathyarchaeia and other TACK lineages, which carry methyl-coenzyme M reductase genes, and may be ancestral to a large part of the domain; hyperthermophily occurs in nearly all the major groups and is probably ancestral as well; aerobic respiration, acquired by horizontal transfer from bacteria, occurs in the haloarchaea, *Sulfolobus* and the ammonia oxidizers.

### 19.2.2. From Cultures to Genomes

A larger share of archaeal than of bacterial diversity is known only from DNA. Most DPANN and Asgard lineages and many TACK lineages have no cultured member, and are named as *Candidatus* taxa or under the SeqCode ([7.11.16](/7.11.16#sec-16.7.2)). Their biology is therefore inferred from genomes, and claims about their metabolism or cell biology are hypotheses until they are cultured, a caution that the first cultivation of an Asgard archaeon in 2020 both confirmed and corrected (Section 19.8).

## 19.3. Extremely Halophilic Archaea

The **extreme halophiles** of the class Halobacteria (the haloarchaea; *Halobacterium*, *Haloferax*, *Haloarcula*, *Haloquadratum*, *Natronomonas*) need at least $1.5\,\mathrm{M}$ NaCl to grow and most grow best at $2$–$4\,\mathrm{M}$, near saturation ([7.11.9](/7.11.9#sec-9.3)). They live in salt lakes such as the Great Salt Lake and the Dead Sea, in solar salterns, whose crystallizer ponds they color red with carotenoids (bacterioruberins), and in salted foods; the natronoarchaea (*Natronomonas*, *Natronobacterium*) of soda lakes are also alkaliphiles, growing at pH 9–11. Most are aerobic chemoorganotrophs that use amino acids or organic acids; some respire nitrate or fumarate.

They maintain osmotic balance by the salt-in strategy ([7.11.9](/7.11.9#sec-9.3.3)). Rather than synthesizing **compatible solutes**, they pump $\ce{K+}$ into the cytoplasm until its concentration exceeds that of $\ce{Na+}$ outside. The whole proteome is adapted to this: cytoplasmic proteins are rich in acidic amino acids and poor in hydrophobic residues and lysine, require high $\ce{K+}$ for their structure and activity, and denature at low salt, and the ribosomes need high $\ce{KCl}$. The cell wall, a glycoprotein S-layer rich in acidic residues, is stabilized by $\ce{Na+}$ bound to its carboxyl groups; when the salt is diluted, its negative charges repel each other, and the cell lyses.

![The salt-in strategy of *Halobacterium salinarum*. A: ion concentrations in the medium and in the cells (data from Christian and Waltho 1962, as tabulated by Madigan et al. 2019): the cells exclude $\ce{Na+}$ (1.4 M inside, 4 M outside) and accumulate $\ce{K+}$ (4.6 M inside, 0.03 M outside), with $\ce{Cl-}$ as the main counter-ion. B: schematic. $\ce{K+}$ is taken up and $\ce{Na+}$ exported; the glycoprotein S-layer is stabilized by $\ce{Na+}$ outside, while cytoplasmic proteins are acidic and require high $\ce{K+}$; bacteriorhodopsin (BR), made when oxygen is scarce, pumps protons out in the light, and ATP synthase uses the proton gradient to make ATP.](./plots/halophile-ions.py)

Because oxygen is poorly soluble in brine, haloarchaea often run short of it. *Halobacterium salinarum* then synthesizes **bacteriorhodopsin**, which forms the purple membrane and pumps protons out of the cell with light energy, a form of phototrophy without chlorophyll and without $\ce{CO2}$ fixation ([7.11.6](/7.11.6#sec-6.4)); halorhodopsin pumps chloride inward, and sensory rhodopsins steer phototaxis. Many haloarchaea float to the light with **gas vesicles** ([7.11.3](/7.11.3#sec-3.2.2)). *Haloquadratum walsbyi*, among the most abundant organisms of saturated brines, has flat, square cells about $2\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ across and $0.1$–$0.5\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ thick, which maximizes the surface for uptake and light capture. *Haloferax volcanii*, from the Dead Sea, is one of the two main genetic models of the domain (with *Sulfolobus*). Haloarchaea descend from methanogens; their ancestor acquired around a thousand genes from bacteria, among them those for aerobic respiration, which transformed an anaerobic autotroph into an aerobic heterotroph ([7.10.27](/7.10.27#sec-27.6.2)).

## 19.4. Methanogenic Archaea

**Methanogens** are strict anaerobes that make methane as the end product of their energy metabolism ([Definition 7.4.3](/7.11.7#def-7.4.3)). They are the most numerous cultured archaea and very diverse in form and wall chemistry:

| Order | Representative genera | Cell form and wall | Substrates for methanogenesis |
|---|---|---|---|
| Methanobacteriales | *Methanobacterium*, *Methanobrevibacter*, *Methanothermobacter*, *Methanosphaera* | rods and short rods; pseudomurein | $\ce{H2 + CO2}$, formate; $\ce{H2}$ + methanol (*Methanosphaera*) |
| Methanococcales | *Methanococcus*, *Methanocaldococcus* | irregular cocci; S-layer | $\ce{H2 + CO2}$, formate |
| Methanomicrobiales | *Methanospirillum*, *Methanogenium* | spirilla in a protein sheath, irregular cocci; S-layer | $\ce{H2 + CO2}$, formate, alcohols |
| Methanosarcinales | *Methanosarcina*, *Methanothrix* (formerly *Methanosaeta*) | packets of cocci with a methanochondroitin matrix; filaments in a sheath | acetate, methanol, methylamines, $\ce{H2 + CO2}$ |
| Methanopyrales | *Methanopyrus* | rods; pseudomurein | $\ce{H2 + CO2}$ |
| Methanocellales, Methanomassiliicoccales | *Methanocella*, *Methanomassiliicoccus* | rods, cocci | $\ce{H2 + CO2}$; $\ce{H2}$ + methyl compounds |

The table summarizes Madigan et al. (2019). Methanogens live wherever organic matter decomposes without oxygen, nitrate, iron(III) or sulfate: freshwater sediments, wetlands and rice paddies, anaerobic digesters and landfills, and the digestive tracts of animals. In the rumen, *Methanobrevibacter ruminantium* and relatives remove the $\ce{H2}$ produced by fermenting bacteria, which keeps fermentation running (interspecies hydrogen transfer, [7.11.7](/7.11.7#sec-7.5.1)) and makes ruminants a major source of atmospheric methane ([7.11.31](/7.11.31)); in the human colon, *Methanobrevibacter smithii* is the dominant archaeon. *Methanocaldococcus jannaschii*, a hyperthermophile from a deep-sea vent, was in 1996 the first archaeon whose genome was sequenced, which showed at a glance how many of its information-processing genes resembled eukaryotic ones and how many of its genes were of unknown function. *Methanosarcina*, with the widest substrate range and some of the largest archaeal genomes, is the model for the genetics and biochemistry of methanogenesis, and the organism in which the 22nd genetically encoded amino acid, pyrrolysine, was found, in its methylamine methyltransferases.

## 19.5. Thermoplasmatota, Thermococci and Archaeoglobi

### 19.5.1. *Thermoplasma*, *Ferroplasma* and *Picrophilus*

The order Thermoplasmatales contains extreme **acidophiles**. *Thermoplasma* lacks a cell wall altogether, like a mycoplasma, and stabilizes its membrane with tetraether lipids and glycoproteins; it was isolated from self-heating coal refuse piles and from solfataras, grows optimally at about $55\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ and pH 2, and is a facultative anaerobe that respires sulfur when oxygen is absent. *Ferroplasma*, also wall-less, oxidizes ferrous iron in acid mine drainage at pH near 0 ([7.11.30](/7.11.30)). *Picrophilus* holds the record for acidophily: it grows optimally at pH 0.7 and still below pH 0, at $60\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$, keeping its cytoplasm near pH 4.6 by a membrane that is nearly impermeable to protons. The same phylum contains the marine group II archaea (Poseidoniales), uncultured photoheterotrophs with proteorhodopsin that are abundant in surface waters.

### 19.5.2. *Thermococcus* and *Pyrococcus*

The Thermococcales are hyperthermophilic, strictly anaerobic heterotrophs of hydrothermal vents that ferment peptides and sugars and dispose of the electrons by reducing elemental sulfur to $\ce{H2S}$ or, without sulfur, protons to $\ce{H2}$ with a membrane-bound hydrogenase that pumps ions. *Pyrococcus furiosus* ("rushing fireball") grows optimally at $100\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ with a doubling time of about $37\,\mathrm{min}$; its proofreading DNA polymerase (Pfu) is used for high-fidelity PCR, and *Thermococcus kodakarensis* is a genetic model for hyperthermophiles. They use modified glycolytic pathways with ADP-dependent kinases and ferredoxin-dependent oxidoreductases ([7.11.5](/7.11.5#sec-5.2.5)).

### 19.5.3. *Archaeoglobus* and the ANME Archaea

*Archaeoglobus fulgidus*, from hot marine sediments and vents, is an archaeal sulfate reducer: it reduces sulfate to sulfide at up to about $90\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$, with the same enzymes (ATP sulfurylase, APS reductase, dissimilatory sulfite reductase) as sulfate-reducing bacteria, from which it acquired them by horizontal transfer, together with several coenzymes otherwise found in methanogens, from which it descends; it produces a trace of methane. Its relative *Ferroglobus placidus* oxidizes $\ce{Fe^2+}$ anaerobically with nitrate, a metabolism that could have deposited iron oxides before the atmosphere contained oxygen.

The anaerobic methanotrophic archaea (ANME-1, -2 and -3; ANME-2 and -3 belong to the Methanosarcinales, ANME-1 forms a separate order) oxidize methane by running the methanogenic pathway in reverse, in consortia with sulfate-reducing bacteria or, in "*Candidatus* Methanoperedens", with nitrate or metal oxides as acceptor; the anaerobic oxidation of methane that they carry out consumes most of the methane formed in marine sediments ([7.11.7](/7.11.7#sec-7.4.4)). They have not been obtained in pure culture.

## 19.6. Thermoproteota (TACK Archaea)

### 19.6.1. Hyperthermophilic Crenarchaea

The class Thermoprotei (the former Crenarchaeota) contains most of the archaea with the highest growth temperatures. Two kinds of habitat dominate. Terrestrial hot springs and solfataras are rich in sulfur and often acidic, because sulfide is oxidized to sulfuric acid: here live aerobic and facultatively aerobic sulfur oxidizers and heterotrophs such as *Sulfolobus* (*S. acidocaldarius*, *Saccharolobus solfataricus*), lobed cocci that grow at about $75\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ and pH 2–3, oxidize $\ce{H2S}$ or $\ce{S0}$ to sulfuric acid or respire sugars, and fix $\ce{CO2}$ by the 3-hydroxypropionate/4-hydroxybutyrate cycle ([7.11.6](/7.11.6#sec-6.5.4)). *Sulfolobus* is the best-studied crenarchaeon: its cell division uses homologs of the eukaryotic ESCRT-III proteins, its chromosome has three origins of replication, and it is attacked by an extraordinary range of viruses with unusual shapes ([7.11.26](/7.11.26)). *Thermoproteus* and *Pyrobaculum* are rods of neutral to slightly acidic springs that respire with sulfur, and *Pyrobaculum* grows up to about $102\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$.

Submarine hydrothermal vents, at neutral pH and under pressure, support the most heat-tolerant members, the Desulfurococcales. *Pyrodictium* forms disk-shaped cells joined in a network by hollow protein tubules (cannulae), which connect the periplasmic spaces of the cells; *Pyrolobus fumarii*, from the walls of black smokers, grows from $90$ to $113\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ and is so dependent on heat that it does not grow below $90\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$; it is a chemolithoautotroph that oxidizes $\ce{H2}$ with nitrate, thiosulfate or low concentrations of oxygen. *Ignicoccus hospitalis* ("hospitable fireball") has an unusual envelope with an outer membrane enclosing a wide periplasm in which the ATP synthase sits, and it is the host of *Nanoarchaeum* (Section 19.7).

### 19.6.2. Ammonia-Oxidizing Archaea

**Definition 19.6.1 (Nitrososphaerota)** The **Nitrososphaerota** (formerly Thaumarchaeota; in GTDB the class Nitrososphaeria of the Thermoproteota) are archaea that, as far as is known from cultures, are aerobic chemolithoautotrophs which oxidize ammonia to nitrite with an archaeal ammonia monooxygenase and fix $\ce{CO2}$ by a variant of the 3-hydroxypropionate/4-hydroxybutyrate cycle.

Their abundance was discovered before their metabolism. In the 1990s, archaeal 16S sequences were found in oxic seawater, far from any extreme habitat, and fluorescent probes later showed that these "marine group I" archaea make up about a fifth of the picoplankton cells of the ocean, and up to about 40 % in the deep ocean. Metagenomes then revealed archaeal genes for ammonia monooxygenase, and in 2005 *Nitrosopumilus maritimus* was isolated from a marine aquarium and shown to grow by ammonia oxidation. The terrestrial *Nitrososphaera viennensis* also uses urea. In many soils, especially acidic ones, archaeal *amoA* genes outnumber those of ammonia-oxidizing bacteria, sometimes by orders of magnitude, so that the first step of **nitrification** ([7.11.7](/7.11.7#sec-7.2.4)) is carried out by archaea and bacteria together.

The success of these archaea in the open ocean is explained by their extraordinary affinity for ammonia.

**Formula 19.6.1 (Michaelis–Menten Kinetics of Ammonia Oxidation)** The rate $v$ of ammonia oxidation at substrate concentration $S$ is

$$
v = \frac{v_{\max} S}{K_{\mathrm{m}} + S},
$$
where $K_{\mathrm{m}}$, the half-saturation constant, is the concentration at which the rate is half maximal. For *N. maritimus*, $K_{\mathrm{m}} \approx 0.13\,\mu\mathrm{M}$ total ammonium (Martens-Habbena and colleagues 2009); for ammonia-oxidizing bacteria, values are typically tens to hundreds of micromolar.

![Substrate affinity of ammonia oxidizers (Formula 19.6.1). Curves of the relative rate against total ammonium for *Nitrosopumilus maritimus* ($K_{\mathrm{m}} = 0.13\,\mu\mathrm{M}$) and for a representative ammonia-oxidizing bacterium ($K_{\mathrm{m}} = 50\,\mu\mathrm{M}$; published values for bacteria range from about 10 to several hundred micromolar); dots mark $K_{\mathrm{m}}$. The shaded band marks ammonium concentrations typical of the open ocean, where the archaeon works at a substantial fraction of its maximal rate and the bacterium at well under 1 %.](./plots/aoa-affinity.py)

In the open ocean ammonium is present at tens to hundreds of nanomolar, a range in which the archaeon oxidizes ammonia near half its maximal rate while the bacteria are almost idle; this, and their low energy requirement per cell, explains why ammonia-oxidizing archaea control nitrification in the sea, whereas bacteria dominate in fertilized soils and wastewater with high ammonium. Ammonia-oxidizing archaea are also a source of nitrous oxide and produce characteristic tetraether lipids (crenarchaeol) that are preserved in sediments and used to reconstruct past sea-surface temperatures (the TEX$_{86}$ index), because the number of cyclopentane rings in the lipids increases with growth temperature ([7.11.2](/7.11.2#sec-2.2.2)).

### 19.6.3. Korarchaeia and Bathyarchaeia

*Korarchaeum cryptofilum*, an ultrathin filament from a hot spring in Yellowstone, known from an enrichment culture, is a heterotroph that ferments peptides and lacks many biosynthetic pathways. The Bathyarchaeia, known from genomes, are among the most abundant archaea in marine and freshwater sediments, where they appear to degrade proteins, aromatic compounds and lignin and to fix carbon by the Wood–Ljungdahl pathway; some lineages carry genes for methanogenesis.

## 19.7. DPANN Archaea

**Definition 19.7.1 (DPANN)** The **DPANN** archaea (named for Diapherotrites, Parvarchaeota, Aenigmarchaeota, Nanoarchaeota and Nanohaloarchaeota, the first lineages recognized) are a large group of archaea, divided in GTDB into several phyla, with very small cells and genomes (often below $1\,\mathrm{Mb}$) that lack many biosynthetic and energy-conserving pathways; those that have been studied live attached to and depend on other archaea.

The first and best known is *Nanoarchaeum equitans*, discovered in 2002 in a hydrothermal vent off Iceland. Its cells are about $0.4\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ across, about 1 % of the volume of an *E. coli* cell, and its genome of $0.49\,\mathrm{Mb}$ is one of the smallest of any cellular organism and one of the most gene-dense, with more than 95 % coding sequence. It contains genes for replication, transcription, translation and repair, but lacks those for the synthesis of amino acids, nucleotides, cofactors and lipids, for glycolysis and probably for a complete ATP synthase. It grows only attached to the surface of its host, the crenarchaeon *Ignicoccus hospitalis*, up to ten or more cells per host, and obtains lipids, amino acids and probably energy through the contact sites; at high numbers it slows the growth of its host, so the relationship is parasitic or at best commensal. Other DPANN archaea have been grown with hosts from very different groups: *Nanohaloarchaeota* with haloarchaea, the acidophilic Micrarchaeota with Thermoplasmatales in acid mine drainage. *Altiarchaeum*, an abundant archaeon of cold sulfidic springs, is not a dwarf; it forms biofilms in which cells are connected by hooked appendages (hami).

The DPANN and the bacterial candidate phyla radiation ([7.11.18](/7.11.18#sec-18.1.2)) show the same pattern in the two domains: a large group of small, metabolically dependent cells, known mostly from genomes. Whether the DPANN branch at the root of the archaea, as drawn in trees such as the one above, or are a derived group whose fast-evolving genomes are attracted to the base of the tree, is debated.

## 19.8. Asgard Archaea

**Definition 19.8.1 (Asgard Archaea)** The **Asgard archaea** (GTDB phylum Asgardarchaeota; Lokiarchaeia, Thorarchaeia, Odinarchaeia, Heimdallarchaeia and other classes) are archaea whose genomes encode many eukaryotic signature proteins, and from which eukaryotes descend or with which they share a most recent common ancestor.

The first lineage, the Lokiarchaeota, was discovered in 2015 by Spang and colleagues in a metagenome from marine sediments near the hydrothermal field Loki's Castle on the Arctic Mid-Ocean Ridge; with the related lineages found soon after, the group was named after the realm of the Norse gods (Zaremba-Niedzwiedzka and colleagues 2017). Their genomes encode homologs of eukaryotic actins and the actin-binding proteins profilin and gelsolin, ESCRT proteins of membrane remodeling, small GTPases of the Ras family, components of the ubiquitin system and of vesicle trafficking; the significance of these genes for the origin of the eukaryotic cell and of the nucleus is treated in [7.10.28](/7.10.28#sec-28.3.1).

In 2020 Imachi and colleagues reported the first cultured Asgard archaeon, "*Candidatus* Prometheoarchaeum syntrophicum" strain MK-D1, after a cultivation of more than ten years from deep-sea sediment. It grows extremely slowly (doubling time two to three weeks) on amino acids and peptides only in co-culture with a sulfate-reducing bacterium and a methanogen that remove its $\ce{H2}$ and formate, a syntrophic way of life ([7.11.7](/7.11.7#sec-7.5)). Its small coccoid cells (about $0.5\,\mu\mathrm{m}$) extend long, branching protrusions and have no internal membrane compartments. This observation suggested how a host archaeon could have entangled a bacterial partner, the future mitochondrion, with its protrusions. A second species cultured later, *Lokiarchaeum ossiferum*, was shown by cryo-electron tomography to have an actin cytoskeleton within such protrusions. Asgard archaea are found in anoxic sediments worldwide, and their diversity, with some lineages known only from a few genomes, is being explored rapidly.

## 19.9. Life at High Temperature

Most **hyperthermophiles** are archaea, and archaea hold the records for growth temperature: the highest at which growth has been shown is $122\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$, by *Methanopyrus kandleri* strain 116 in pressurized culture (Takai and colleagues 2008), and the highest for a bacterium is about $95\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ (*Aquifex*, [7.11.18](/7.11.18#sec-18.8.2)). Among energy metabolisms, phototrophy stops at about $73\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$, organotrophy at about $110\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$, and chemolithotrophy with $\ce{H2}$ reaches the highest values.

The upper limit is probably set by the stability of small molecules rather than of macromolecules. Proteins, nucleic acids and membranes can be stabilized by evolution ([7.11.9](/7.11.9#sec-9.1.3)): proteins by more ionic interactions and tighter hydrophobic cores, ribosomal RNAs by GC-rich stems and modified nucleotides, membranes by tetraether lipids whose cyclopentane rings increase with temperature, DNA by reverse gyrase, which introduces positive supercoils, by archaeal histones and other DNA-binding proteins, and by high concentrations of $\ce{K+}$ and of compatible solutes such as di-*myo*-inositol phosphate and, in *Methanopyrus*, cyclic 2,3-diphosphoglycerate. Reverse gyrase is the only protein found in all hyperthermophiles and in no mesophile, and the hyperthermophilic bacteria appear to have acquired it from archaea by horizontal transfer. ATP and NAD, however, hydrolyze within minutes at $150\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$, and water from vents above about $150\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ contains no detectable DNA or protein, which suggests a limit for life as we know it between $122$ and about $150\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$.

Hyperthermophiles occupy short, deep branches in rRNA trees, many of them oxidize $\ce{H2}$ and fix $\ce{CO2}$ with simple metal-dependent enzymes, and $\ce{H2}$ and $\ce{CO2}$ were abundant at hydrothermal vents on the early Earth; these observations led to the hypothesis that life began, or at least the last common ancestor lived, in hot, hydrogen-rich environments. Phylogenetic reconstructions of ancestral proteins support a thermophilic ancestor of the archaea, while the case for a hyperthermophilic LUCA is disputed; the hypotheses about the origin of life are discussed in [7.10.27](/7.10.27#sec-27.2).

## 19.10. Archaea beyond Extreme Habitats

Most archaea do not live in extreme environments. Ammonia oxidizers are among the most abundant cells of the deep ocean and of soils (Section 19.6.2); marine group II archaea live in surface waters; Bathyarchaeia, Asgard and ANME archaea are abundant in sediments, which hold a large part of the Earth's microbial biomass; methanogens are central to anoxic carbon cycling in wetlands, rice paddies and animal guts. Archaea live on and in the human body: *Methanobrevibacter smithii* in the gut of many people, other methanogens in dental plaque, and ammonia-oxidizing archaea on the skin ([7.11.32](/7.11.32)). No archaeon is known to be a pathogen, perhaps because the molecular interactions that pathogens use to manipulate host cells evolved mostly in bacteria, although methanogens in periodontal pockets may support bacteria that cause disease by consuming their $\ce{H2}$. Archaea also host their own viruses, which differ strikingly from bacterial and eukaryotic viruses in shape ([7.11.26](/7.11.26)).

## Summary

- Archaea (Section 19.1) combine a prokaryotic cell plan and metabolism with eukaryote-like information processing (RNA polymerase, TBP and TFB, MCM and PCNA, histones, translation factors) and have unique features: isoprenoid ether lipids on glycerol 1-phosphate, walls without peptidoglycan, methanogenesis; no pathogens are known.
- The domain comprises the former Euryarchaeota (Methanobacteriota, Halobacteriota, Thermoplasmatota), the TACK archaea (Thermoproteota, including the Nitrososphaerota, Definition 19.6.1), the DPANN archaea (Definition 19.7.1) and the Asgard archaea (Definition 19.8.1), the sister group of eukaryotes. Much of the diversity is known only from genomes.

| Methanogens | *Methanobrevibacter*, *Methanococcus*, *Methanosarcina*, *Methanothrix*, *Methanopyrus* | strict anaerobes; $\ce{CH4}$ from $\ce{H2 + CO2}$, acetate or methyl compounds; varied walls | sediments, wetlands, rumen and gut, digesters, vents |

- Growth has been shown up to $122\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ (*Methanopyrus*); macromolecules are stabilized by sequence, solutes, histones, reverse gyrase and tetraether lipids, and the limit is probably set near $150\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ by the instability of small molecules such as ATP.

## Questions

1. Which features of an organism would you test to decide whether a newly isolated prokaryote is an archaeon, without sequencing its genome? Which single test would be most decisive?

   **Answer** Lipids: ether-linked isoprenoids (detected by chromatography and mass spectrometry) instead of ester-linked fatty acids; the absence of muramic acid and of sensitivity to penicillin and lysozyme; resistance to antibiotics that act on the bacterial ribosome and RNA polymerase (streptomycin, chloramphenicol, rifampicin) combined with sensitivity to some that act on eukaryotic ones; and, for methanogens, $\mathrm{F}_{420}$ fluorescence and methane production. The lipid analysis is the most decisive, because ether-linked isoprenoid lipids on glycerol 1-phosphate are unique to archaea (bacteria with ether lipids, such as *Thermodesulfobacterium*, have non-isoprenoid chains).

2. Explain why a haloarchaeon lyses when transferred to dilute medium, whereas *E. coli* would survive a similar transfer from 0.5 M salt to water.

   **Answer** The S-layer of the haloarchaeon is a highly acidic glycoprotein whose negative charges are neutralized by bound $\ce{Na+}$; when the salt is removed, the charges repel each other and the wall disintegrates, and the cytoplasm, which holds about 4–5 M $\ce{K+}$, takes up water and bursts the membrane; its proteins also unfold without high $\ce{K+}$. *E. coli* has a peptidoglycan sac that withstands turgor, uses compatible solutes that it can release quickly through mechanosensitive channels, and has proteins that work at low ionic strength.

3. Use Formula 19.6.1 to compare the rates at which *N. maritimus* ($K_{\mathrm{m}} = 0.13\,\mu\mathrm{M}$) and a bacterium with $K_{\mathrm{m}} = 50\,\mu\mathrm{M}$ oxidize ammonia at $0.05\,\mu\mathrm{M}$, relative to their maximal rates. What does this imply for their distribution?

   **Answer** For the archaeon, $v/v_{\max} = 0.05/(0.13 + 0.05) \approx 0.28$; for the bacterium, $0.05/50.05 \approx 0.001$. At oceanic concentrations the archaeon works at more than a quarter of its maximal rate, the bacterium at a tenth of a per cent, so archaea dominate nitrification in the open ocean and in other ammonium-poor habitats, while bacteria, with higher maximal rates, can compete where ammonium is abundant (fertilized soils, wastewater).

4. *Nanoarchaeum equitans* and *Mycoplasma genitalium* both have genomes of about half a megabase. Compare what they have lost and on what they depend.

   **Answer** Both have kept the genes for replication, transcription, translation and repair and lost most biosynthetic pathways, obtaining amino acids, nucleotides and lipid precursors from a host. *M. genitalium* keeps glycolysis and an ATP synthase and so makes its own ATP, and can be cultured in rich cell-free media with sterols; *N. equitans* lacks glycolysis and probably a complete ATP synthase and must obtain lipids and probably energy through direct contact with its archaeal host, so it grows only attached to *Ignicoccus*. One is a reduced bacterium of animal hosts, the other a reduced archaeon living on another prokaryote.

5. Why is the upper temperature limit of life thought to be set by small molecules rather than by proteins or DNA? Which observation from hydrothermal vents supports this?

   **Answer** Evolution can tune macromolecules: more ionic bonds and packed cores stabilize proteins, histones, positive supercoiling by reverse gyrase and high $\ce{K+}$ protect DNA, and tetraether lipids stabilize membranes; but universal small molecules such as ATP and NAD hydrolyze within minutes at about $150\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$, and no organism could replace all of them. Consistent with this, water emerging from vents above about $150\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ contains no detectable DNA, RNA or protein, whereas cooler fluids do.
