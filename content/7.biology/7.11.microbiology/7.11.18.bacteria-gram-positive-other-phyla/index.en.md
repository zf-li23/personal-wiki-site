The previous chapter followed one phylum, the Pseudomonadota, through its classes. This chapter covers the rest of the bacterial tree. Its first part is the gram-positive bacteria, which fall into two phyla: the Bacillota, with a low G + C content of their DNA, which include the lactic acid bacteria, the endospore formers and the wall-less mycoplasmas; and the Actinomycetota, with a high G + C content, which range from cocci to branching mycelia and include the mycobacteria and the streptomycetes, the source of most antibiotics. The cyanobacteria, the only bacteria with oxygenic photosynthesis and the ancestors of plastids, follow. The second part surveys the gram-negative phyla outside the Pseudomonadota: the Bacteroidota of the gut and soil, the planctomycetes, verrucomicrobia and chlamydiae, the spirochetes, and several smaller phyla with remarkable properties, from the radiation-resistant *Deinococcus* to hyperthermophiles at the edge of the bacterial domain. As in [7.11.17](/7.11.17), each group is described by form and envelope, metabolism, habitat and significance; the physiology is linked to the chapters where it was explained, and the development of *Bacillus*, *Streptomyces* and heterocystous cyanobacteria to [7.11.12](/7.11.12).

## 18.1. The Bacterial Tree

### 18.1.1. Major Lineages

Trees of concatenated conserved proteins from thousands of genomes ([7.11.16](/7.11.16#sec-16.3.3), [7.10.27](/7.10.27#sec-27.5.1)) group most bacterial phyla into two large clades. The *Terrabacteria* include the two gram-positive phyla, the cyanobacteria, the Chloroflexota and the Deinococcota; most of them are monoderm, with a single membrane and a thick peptidoglycan wall, and the name reflects the inference that their ancestors adapted to life on land, with resistance to desiccation and ultraviolet light. The *Gracilicutes* are diderm, with an outer membrane; they include the Pseudomonadota and the phyla split from it, the spirochetes, and two well-supported superphyla, the FCB group (Fibrobacterota, Chlorobiota, Bacteroidota and relatives) and the PVC group (Planctomycetota, Verrucomicrobiota, Chlamydiota and relatives). Analyses that root the tree place the root between these two clades, but the deep branching order is still uncertain, and the positions of several phyla, such as the hyperthermophilic Aquificota and Thermotogota, once thought to branch first, are not settled.

The distinction between monoderm and diderm envelopes runs deeper than the Gram reaction. Some Bacillota, the Negativicutes (*Veillonella*, *Selenomonas*), have an outer membrane with lipopolysaccharide and stain gram-negative; the outer membrane appears to have been present in the common ancestor of Bacteria and to have been lost several times. The mycoplasmas have lost the wall altogether, and the mycobacteria have built a second, unusual outer layer of their own.

### 18.1.2. The Candidate Phyla Radiation

The **candidate phyla radiation** ([Definition 16.3.2](/7.11.16#def-16.3.2)), treated as the single phylum Patescibacteria in GTDB, was the great surprise of genome-resolved metagenomics. In the tree of Hug and colleagues (2016) it accounted for a large part of bacterial diversity, almost all of it uncultured. Its members have small cells (some about $0.2\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ across, near the lower limit for a cell, [7.11.1](/7.11.1#sec-1.9.1)) and genomes, often below $1\,\mathrm{Mb}$, that lack complete pathways for the synthesis of nucleotides, amino acids and lipids, and usually the citric acid cycle and a respiratory chain; they ferment, and they must obtain building blocks from other organisms. The few that have been grown in co-culture are epibionts attached to the surface of other bacteria: the first, a member of the Saccharibacteria (formerly TM7) from the human mouth, grows on the surface of an actinomycete and depends on it (He and colleagues 2015). CPR bacteria are abundant in groundwater and sediments, and Saccharibacteria are common in the human oral microbiome. Whether the radiation is an ancient group of small-genome bacteria or a lineage that has undergone extreme reduction is debated.

## 18.2. Bacillota (Firmicutes)

### 18.2.1. Overview

The **Bacillota** (formerly Firmicutes, from Latin for "strong skin") are the low G + C gram-positive bacteria: their thick peptidoglycan wall contains **teichoic acids**, and the G + C content of most genomes lies between about 30 and 50 %. They are the only bacteria that form **endospores** ([7.11.3](/7.11.3#sec-3.3)), although many members have lost the ability. Metabolically they range from aerobes (*Bacillus*) through aerotolerant fermenters (lactic acid bacteria) to strict anaerobes (clostridia), and they include the heliobacteria, anoxygenic phototrophs with a type I reaction center ([7.11.6](/7.11.6#sec-6.2.4)), the spore-forming sulfate reducer *Desulfotomaculum*, and the acetogens *Moorella* and *Acetobacterium* ([7.11.7](/7.11.7#sec-7.4.1)). GTDB splits the traditional phylum into several, chiefly the Bacillota in the strict sense (the class Bacilli) and Bacillota_A (most Clostridia); the traditional grouping is kept here.

### 18.2.2. Lactic Acid Bacteria

The lactic acid bacteria (order Lactobacillales) ferment sugars to lactic acid as the main product. They have no cytochromes and no catalase and do not respire (a few can when supplied with heme), yet they tolerate oxygen: they are **aerotolerant anaerobes** ([7.11.9](/7.11.9#sec-9.4.1)). They are also nutritionally fastidious, needing amino acids, vitamins and nucleotide bases, and therefore live where these are plentiful: milk, plant surfaces and fermenting plant material, and the mucosal surfaces of animals. Homofermentative species (*Lactococcus*, *Streptococcus*, *Pediococcus*, many lactobacilli) convert glucose by glycolysis into two lactate; heterofermentative ones (*Leuconostoc*, *Limosilactobacillus*) use the **phosphoketolase pathway** and make lactate, ethanol and $\ce{CO2}$ ([7.11.5](/7.11.5#sec-5.3.2)). The acid they produce lowers the pH below what most spoilage organisms tolerate, which is the basis of food preservation by fermentation: yogurt (*Lactobacillus delbrueckii* subsp. *bulgaricus* with *Streptococcus thermophilus*), cheese (*Lactococcus lactis*), sauerkraut and kimchi (*Leuconostoc*, *Lactiplantibacillus plantarum*), sourdough, silage and fermented sausages. In 2020 the genus *Lactobacillus*, which had grown to more than 250 species of very different relationships, was divided into 25 genera on genomic evidence, so that many familiar names changed (*L. plantarum* became *Lactiplantibacillus plantarum*, *L. casei* became *Lacticaseibacillus casei*).

Streptococci and enterococci are also found on mucosal surfaces, and several are pathogens. They are classified in the laboratory by their hemolysis on blood agar ($\beta$, a clear zone; $\alpha$, a partial greenish zone; $\gamma$, none) and by the Lancefield antigens of the wall: *Streptococcus pyogenes* ($\beta$, group A) causes pharyngitis and skin infections, *S. agalactiae* (group B) neonatal infections, *S. pneumoniae* ($\alpha$) pneumonia, otitis and meningitis, and *Enterococcus faecalis* and *E. faecium* (group D) urinary tract and hospital infections, including vancomycin-resistant strains ([7.11.15](/7.11.15)). *S. pneumoniae* is also of historical importance: its transformation was the experiment that identified DNA as the genetic material ([7.4.10](/7.4.10)). *S. mutans* ferments dietary sucrose in dental plaque and is associated with caries ([7.11.32](/7.11.32)).

### 18.2.3. *Bacillus* and Its Relatives

The order Bacillales contains aerobic or facultatively anaerobic rods and cocci, most of them catalase-positive. *Bacillus subtilis*, a soil bacterium, is the model gram-positive organism; its choice between growth, competence, matrix production and sporulation is a classic system of bacterial cell fate ([7.11.12](/7.11.12#sec-12.2)), and it is used industrially to make enzymes and vitamins and in the Japanese fermented soybean food natto. *Geobacillus stearothermophilus* is a thermophile whose spores are unusually heat resistant, which is why they serve as the biological indicator for steam sterilization ([7.11.10](/7.11.10)). Spores of *Bacillus* and relatives are also used as traceable markers in ecology and food science, because they survive drying and can be detected by sequencing long after application.

The *Bacillus cereus* group illustrates how closely related soil bacteria can differ in their relations with other organisms. *B. cereus* is widespread in soil and foods and causes food poisoning; *B. anthracis*, which is genomically almost a lineage within *B. cereus*, causes anthrax in grazing animals and occasionally in people who handle them, and persists in soil as spores; *B. thuringiensis* produces during sporulation a crystalline protein inclusion that is toxic to the larvae of certain insects after it dissolves in their alkaline gut. In the three cases the differences lie chiefly in large plasmids. Preparations of *B. thuringiensis* (Bt) have been used as insecticides for decades, and its *cry* genes expressed in maize and cotton ("Bt crops") reduce the use of chemical insecticides; because each Cry protein acts on a narrow range of insects that carry the matching gut receptors and is inactive in the acidic vertebrate stomach, the toxicity is highly selective. Resistance in target pests is managed by planting refuges of non-Bt plants.

*Staphylococcus* is a genus of nonmotile, catalase-positive cocci in clusters that tolerate drying and high salt and live on skin and mucous membranes. *S. epidermidis* is a normal resident of skin and the commonest cause of infections of catheters and implants, through its biofilms; *S. aureus*, carried in the nose of about a third of people, causes skin, wound, bone and bloodstream infections, and some strains cause food poisoning. It is distinguished from *S. epidermidis* by the coagulase test, and methicillin-resistant strains (**MRSA**) are among the most important resistant pathogens ([7.11.15](/7.11.15)).

*Listeria monocytogenes* is a small rod that grows at refrigerator temperature ($4\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$) and in high salt, which makes it a hazard of chilled ready-to-eat foods such as soft cheese, deli meats and smoked fish; listeriosis is dangerous mainly in pregnancy, in newborns and in immunocompromised people. It is also a model intracellular bacterium: it moves through the cytoplasm of host cells on a comet tail of host actin assembled by one of its surface proteins, a system that became a tool for studying actin polymerization ([7.3.13](/7.3.13)).

### 18.2.4. Clostridia and Giant Bacteria

The class Clostridia contains strict anaerobes, many of them endospore formers in which the spore swells the mother cell. They ferment sugars, amino acids, purines or cellulose, and make butyrate, butanol, acetone, $\ce{H2}$ and $\ce{CO2}$ ([7.11.5](/7.11.5#sec-5.3.5)); the acetone–butanol–ethanol fermentation of *Clostridium acetobutylicum* was one of the first large-scale industrial fermentations, and cellulolytic clostridia (*Acetivibrio*, formerly *Clostridium thermocellum*) degrade plant material with cellulosomes, multienzyme complexes on the cell surface. Clostridia are abundant in soil, sediments and the intestinal tract, where the butyrate-producing species of the families Lachnospiraceae and Oscillospiraceae are major members of the healthy gut flora ([7.11.32](/7.11.32)). Several species cause disease when their spores germinate in anoxic tissue or in food, among them *C. tetani* (tetanus), *C. botulinum* (botulism), *C. perfringens* (gas gangrene and food poisoning) and *Clostridioides difficile*, which causes colitis after antibiotics have disturbed the gut community ([7.11.32](/7.11.32)); all are prevented or treated by established means (vaccination, hygiene and food processing, [7.11.35](/7.11.35)).

*Epulopiscium fishelsoni* and its relatives, Bacillota of the gut of surgeonfish, are among the largest bacteria known, up to about $80 \times 600\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ ([7.11.1](/7.11.1#sec-1.9.1)). A cell contains tens of thousands of copies of its genome distributed near the cell surface, which keeps the distance between a gene and the cytoplasm it supplies small. Instead of binary fission, it forms several daughter cells inside the mother cell and releases them when the mother lyses, a mode of reproduction related to sporulation: the genes used are homologs of the sporulation genes of *Bacillus*.

### 18.2.5. Mollicutes: the Mycoplasmas

The **mycoplasmas** (class Mollicutes, "soft skin", within the Bacillota) have no cell wall and are bounded only by the cytoplasmic membrane. They are the smallest cells capable of independent growth (about $0.2$–$0.8\,\mu\mathrm{m}$), have among the smallest genomes of any cellular organism (about $0.58$–$1.4\,\mathrm{Mb}$), and most require sterols for their membrane.

Having no wall, they are pleomorphic, pass through filters that retain other bacteria, and are intrinsically resistant to penicillins and other inhibitors of wall synthesis ([7.11.14](/7.11.14#sec-14.4.1)). Their genomes have lost the pathways for amino acids, nucleotides, fatty acids and sterols, which they obtain from the host or the medium; their G + C content is low, and in many of them the codon UGA is read as tryptophan rather than as a stop ([7.2.19](/7.2.19)). They descend from clostridia-like ancestors by extreme genome reduction, not from a primitive wall-less state. Mycoplasmas live in association with animals and plants: *Mycoplasma pneumoniae* causes an atypical pneumonia ("walking pneumonia"), *M. genitalium*, with the smallest genome of a free-living organism ($0.58\,\mathrm{Mb}$, 525 genes), served as the reference organism of the minimal-cell project, its genome being the first to be synthesized chemically (2008), although the minimal synthetic cell JCVI-syn3.0 was eventually built from the faster-growing *Mycoplasma mycoides* ([7.11.1](/7.11.1)), and mycoplasmas are a notorious contaminant of cell cultures, invisible by light microscopy and unaffected by penicillin. The phytoplasmas, relatives transmitted by sap-feeding insects, live in the phloem of plants and cause yellows, witches' brooms and the conversion of flowers into leafy structures; they cannot be grown in pure culture, unlike the related *Spiroplasma*, a helical mycoplasma of insects and plants.

## 18.3. Actinomycetota (Actinobacteria)

### 18.3.1. Overview and the Coryneform Group

The **Actinomycetota** are the high G + C gram-positive bacteria, with genomes often above 60 % and up to more than 70 % G + C. Most are aerobic chemoorganotrophs of soil, where they degrade recalcitrant polymers (cellulose, chitin, lignin fragments, even some pesticides and rubber) and are central to the turnover of organic matter; others live on skin and mucosa or in the gut. In form they range from cocci and rods to branching filaments and true mycelia, and the phylum is conventionally divided by cell form into the coryneform bacteria, the mycobacteria, the **actinomycetes** proper and the bifidobacteria.

The coryneform bacteria are irregular, often club-shaped rods that divide by "snapping", so that the daughter cells remain attached at an angle, giving the V shapes and palisades seen in stained smears. *Corynebacterium glutamicum*, a soil bacterium, is one of the two great workhorses of industrial amino acid production: mutants that no longer regulate their glutamate or lysine pathways excrete these amino acids in enormous amounts, and world production is measured in millions of tonnes a year. *C. diphtheriae* causes diphtheria, now rare where childhood vaccination is maintained. *Cutibacterium acnes* (formerly *Propionibacterium acnes*) lives in the sebaceous follicles of skin ([7.11.32](/7.11.32)). The propionic acid bacteria (*Propionibacterium freudenreichii*) ferment lactate to propionate and $\ce{CO2}$ ([7.11.5](/7.11.5#sec-5.3.6)), giving Swiss-type cheeses their flavor and their holes, and are used industrially to make vitamin B12. *Arthrobacter*, abundant in soil, alternates between rods in growth and cocci in stationary phase and is remarkably resistant to drying and starvation.

### 18.3.2. Mycobacteria

Mycobacteria are slender, rod-shaped Actinomycetota with a unique cell envelope. Outside the peptidoglycan lies a layer of arabinogalactan, to which **mycolic acids** are covalently attached: very long-chain ($\mathrm{C}_{60}$–$\mathrm{C}_{90}$) branched $\beta$-hydroxy fatty acids that, with free lipids, form a second, waxy outer membrane of extremely low permeability ([7.11.2](/7.11.2#sec-2.3.4)). This envelope explains three properties: acid-fastness (once stained with hot carbol fuchsin, the cells resist decolorization with acid alcohol, the Ziehl–Neelsen stain, [7.11.16](/7.11.16#sec-16.8.2)); a high intrinsic resistance to many antibiotics, detergents and drying, so that mycobacteria survive for long periods in dust and water; and very slow growth, since nutrients cross the envelope slowly. *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* has a generation time of about $20\,\mathrm{h}$ and needs weeks to form visible colonies, and *M. leprae*, with about $14\,\mathrm{d}$, has never been grown in pure culture and is propagated in armadillos and mouse footpads. The genome of *M. leprae* is a textbook case of reductive evolution: of about $3.3\,\mathrm{Mb}$, only some 1600 genes are intact and about 1100 are pseudogenes.

*M. tuberculosis* causes tuberculosis, one of the leading infectious causes of death worldwide; its slow growth and impermeable envelope are the reasons why treatment requires several drugs for months ([7.11.14](/7.11.14#sec-14.8.2)), and drug-resistant tuberculosis is a major problem ([7.11.15](/7.11.15)). *M. leprae* causes leprosy. Most other mycobacteria are harmless inhabitants of soil and water (*M. smegmatis*, a fast-growing species, is the laboratory model), although some cause opportunistic infections. Related genera, *Nocardia* and *Rhodococcus*, also have mycolic acids, shorter ones, and are partially acid-fast; *Rhodococcus* species degrade an extraordinary range of organic compounds and are used in bioremediation and biocatalysis.

### 18.3.3. Streptomycetes and Other Filamentous Actinomycetes

**Definition 18.3.1 (Actinomycete)** The **actinomycetes** are filamentous Actinomycetota that grow as a branching mycelium of hyphae and reproduce by spores formed on aerial hyphae. They are aerobic soil organisms; the largest genus is *Streptomyces*.

A spore germinates into a substrate mycelium that grows through the soil and secretes exoenzymes. When nutrients run out, aerial hyphae grow into the air, drawing their material from the lysis of the substrate mycelium, and septate at regular intervals into chains of spores that disperse in air and water ([7.11.12](/7.11.12#sec-12.6)). The spores are not endospores: they are not especially heat resistant, and they serve dispersal and survival of drying. Streptomycetes give soil its earthy smell, through the volatile terpene geosmin, which also taints drinking water and fish; springtails and other soil arthropods are attracted by geosmin and disperse the spores.

The same starvation that triggers aerial growth switches on the synthesis of **secondary metabolites**. About half of the *Streptomyces* strains isolated from soil produce an antibiotic, and streptomycetes are the source of most antibiotics in clinical use, among them streptomycin, tetracyclines, erythromycin (from *Saccharopolyspora*), chloramphenicol, vancomycin and rifamycin (both from *Amycolatopsis*), as well as antifungals, immunosuppressants (rapamycin, tacrolimus) and anticancer agents ([7.11.14](/7.11.14)). The genes for each compound lie in a biosynthetic gene cluster, typically a polyketide synthase or a non-ribosomal peptide synthetase with tailoring enzymes, resistance genes and regulators; a genome of $8$–$10\,\mathrm{Mb}$ carries 20 to 40 such clusters, most of them silent under laboratory conditions, which is why genome mining for "cryptic" clusters has become a route to new drugs. Why so much metabolic effort is spent on these compounds is not settled: at the high concentrations used in medicine they inhibit competitors, which fits their production at the start of sporulation, but at the low concentrations found in soil many of them alter gene expression in other bacteria, which suggests a role in signaling.

Other actinomycetes matter in other ways. *Frankia* fixes nitrogen in root nodules of actinorhizal plants such as alder and *Casuarina*, within vesicles whose multilayered lipid envelope protects nitrogenase from oxygen ([7.11.6](/7.11.6#sec-6.6.3), [7.11.31](/7.11.31)); several genera are important in composting; and the bifidobacteria (*Bifidobacterium*), anaerobic, often Y-shaped rods, dominate the gut of breastfed infants, where they grow on the human milk oligosaccharides that the infant cannot digest, fermenting sugars by a characteristic phosphoketolase pathway, the "bifid shunt" ([7.11.32](/7.11.32)).

## 18.4. Cyanobacteria

**Definition 18.4.1 (Cyanobacteria)** The **cyanobacteria** (phylum Cyanobacteriota, formerly also called blue-green algae) are the only bacteria with oxygenic photosynthesis: they carry photosystems I and II in thylakoid membranes, use water as the electron donor and release $\ce{O2}$, and harvest light mainly with **phycobilisomes** of phycobiliproteins attached to the thylakoids.

Their photosynthesis, the pigments, chromatic acclimation and the carboxysomes in which they concentrate $\ce{CO2}$ are treated in [7.11.6](/7.11.6#sec-6.3); the differentiation of **heterocysts** and **akinetes** in [7.11.12](/7.11.12#sec-12.8); their role in the oxygenation of the atmosphere and the origin of plastids in [7.10.27](/7.10.27#sec-27.5.3) and [7.10.28](/7.10.28#sec-28.2.2). Here the group itself is described.

Cyanobacteria are gram-negative in envelope structure but have an unusually thick peptidoglycan layer. They are morphologically the most diverse bacterial phylum, and the classification of Rippka and colleagues (1979) into five subsections by form and mode of division is still used alongside the genomic taxonomy:

![The five morphological subsections of cyanobacteria (schematic; after Rippka and colleagues 1979). I: unicellular cells dividing by binary fission (*Synechococcus*) or in colonies within a common envelope (*Gloeocapsa*). II: cells that divide by multiple fission into many small baeocytes (*Pleurocapsa*). III: filaments (trichomes) of undifferentiated cells (*Oscillatoria*). IV: filaments with heterocysts, which fix nitrogen, and akinetes, thick-walled resting cells (*Anabaena*, *Nostoc*). V: filaments with true branching and heterocysts (*Fischerella*). Subsections I–III are not natural groups; the heterocystous forms of IV and V together form a clade.](./plots/cyanobacteria.py)

Their habitats are everywhere light and water reach: oceans, lakes, hot springs up to about $73\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ ([7.11.9](/7.11.9#sec-9.1.3)), soil crusts, rock surfaces in deserts and Antarctica, and in symbiosis with fungi (lichens), liverworts, cycads, the water fern *Azolla* and diatoms ([7.11.31](/7.11.31)). Many have **gas vesicles** that adjust their buoyancy in the water column ([7.11.3](/7.11.3#sec-3.2.2)), and filamentous forms glide over surfaces. Motile filament fragments, the hormogonia, are the dispersal stage of *Nostoc* and its relatives and the stage that colonizes plant hosts.

Two marine genera deserve particular mention. *Prochlorococcus*, with cells of about $0.6\,\mu\mathrm{m}$, is probably the most abundant photosynthetic organism on Earth, with on the order of $10^{27}$ cells in the tropical and subtropical oceans; it has no phycobilisomes but divinyl chlorophylls *a* and *b*, and its ecotypes are adapted to high and low light at different depths. *Synechococcus* is its larger, more widely distributed relative. Together with the picoeukaryotes they carry out a large share of marine primary production ([7.11.29](/7.11.29)). Cyanobacteria are also responsible for harmful blooms in eutrophic lakes and reservoirs, where species of *Microcystis*, *Dolichospermum* and *Planktothrix* produce toxins such as microcystins, a water-quality problem discussed with nutrient pollution in [7.11.30](/7.11.30). In primary succession, nitrogen-fixing cyanobacteria are among the first colonists of bare rock and new soils, since they need only light, water, $\ce{CO2}$, $\ce{N2}$ and minerals.

## 18.5. The FCB Group: Bacteroidota and Chlorobiota

The **Bacteroidota** (formerly Bacteroidetes) are gram-negative, non-sporulating rods that specialize in the degradation of polysaccharides, which they accomplish with large numbers of polysaccharide utilization loci: gene clusters encoding surface-binding proteins, enzymes and transporters for one class of polysaccharide. They fall into three main groups by lifestyle. The Bacteroidales are obligate anaerobes of the intestinal tract, where *Bacteroides* and *Prevotella* are among the most abundant genera and break down dietary fiber and host glycans into short-chain fatty acids that the host absorbs ([7.11.32](/7.11.32)); some are opportunistic pathogens of abdominal wounds. The Cytophagales are aerobic soil and freshwater bacteria, long slender rods that glide over surfaces by a mechanism of their own, in which adhesins are moved along a track in the outer membrane; they are important degraders of cellulose and chitin. The Flavobacteriales are aerobic, often yellow-pigmented bacteria of soil and water, numerous in the sea, where they degrade algal polysaccharides after blooms and where many carry proteorhodopsin ([7.11.6](/7.11.6#sec-6.4)); *Flavobacterium psychrophilum* and relatives cause diseases of fish. The sister phylum Fibrobacterota contains *Fibrobacter succinogenes*, one of the main cellulose degraders of the rumen ([7.11.31](/7.11.31)).

The Chlorobiota (green sulfur bacteria, *Chlorobium*, *Chlorobaculum*, *Prosthecochloris*) are strictly anaerobic anoxygenic phototrophs with a type I reaction center and huge light-harvesting **chlorosomes**, which let them grow at the lowest light intensities of any phototroph, in the deep anoxic, sulfide-rich layers of lakes and in the Black Sea at $100\,\mathrm{m}$ ([7.11.6](/7.11.6#sec-6.2.4)). *Chlorobium* fixes $\ce{CO2}$ by the **reverse citric acid cycle**. The phototrophic consortium "*Chlorochromatium aggregatum*", in which green sulfur bacteria surround a motile betaproteobacterium that carries them toward light and sulfide, is one of the most intimate associations known between free-living bacteria.

## 18.6. The PVC Group

### 18.6.1. Planctomycetota

The Planctomycetota have long been studied for their unusual cell plan. They divide by budding rather than binary fission, lack FtsZ, often attach to surfaces by a stalk, and their cytoplasm is divided by invaginations of the cytoplasmic membrane into compartments: the region containing the nucleoid was once called a "nucleus-like" structure and taken as evidence for a link with eukaryotes. Careful electron cryotomography has shown that the compartments are continuous with the periplasm, so that the plan is an elaborated gram-negative envelope rather than a eukaryotic one. Their peptidoglycan was long overlooked, which gave rise to the "mystery of the missing peptidoglycan", until it was detected in 2015 with methods sensitive enough to show the thin layer present.

The anammox bacteria ("*Candidatus* Brocadia", "*Ca.* Kuenenia") are planctomycetes that oxidize ammonium with nitrite to $\ce{N2}$ anaerobically, inside a special compartment, the anammoxosome, whose membrane contains ladderane lipids ([7.11.7](/7.11.7#sec-7.2.5)). They are responsible for a large part of the loss of fixed nitrogen from the ocean and are used in wastewater treatment ([7.11.30](/7.11.30)).

### 18.6.2. Verrucomicrobiota

The Verrucomicrobiota, named for the wart-like prosthecae on their cells, are abundant in soil (often several per cent of the 16S sequences), in freshwater and in the guts of animals. *Akkermansia muciniphila*, which grows on the mucin of the intestinal mucus layer, is a common member of the human gut flora whose abundance correlates with metabolic health ([7.11.32](/7.11.32)). Other members are acidophilic, thermophilic methanotrophs of volcanic soils (*Methylacidiphilum*), which oxidize methane but fix $\ce{CO2}$ by the Calvin cycle ([7.11.7](/7.11.7#sec-7.4.3)), and the endosymbionts of some protists.

### 18.6.3. Chlamydiota

The **chlamydiae** (phylum Chlamydiota) are gram-negative bacteria that grow only inside eukaryotic cells and alternate between two forms: the small, infectious, metabolically inactive elementary body, which spreads between cells, and the larger, metabolically active reticulate body, which divides inside a membrane-bound vacuole, the inclusion.

Their genomes are small (about $1\,\mathrm{Mb}$), they cannot make several metabolites and they import ATP from the host with ATP/ADP translocases, like the rickettsias, although the two groups are unrelated ([7.11.17](/7.11.17#sec-17.3.3)); the resemblance is convergent, a consequence of the intracellular life. They have little or no detectable peptidoglycan in the usual sense, but they do make it transiently at the division septum, which explains their sensitivity to drugs that act on wall synthesis.

![The developmental cycle of *Chlamydia trachomatis* in an epithelial cell (schematic; approximate times after infection). The small, infectious elementary body (about $0.3\,\mu\mathrm{m}$) attaches and is taken into a vacuole; within hours it converts into a larger reticulate body (about $1\,\mu\mathrm{m}$), which divides inside the growing inclusion, a vacuole that does not fuse with lysosomes and from which the bacteria draw nutrients and ATP; from about 24 h the reticulate bodies convert back into elementary bodies, and at 48–72 h these are released when the host cell lyses or the whole inclusion is extruded.](./plots/chlamydia-cycle.py)

*Chlamydia trachomatis* causes trachoma, a scarring infection of the conjunctiva that is still a leading infectious cause of blindness, and the commonest bacterial sexually transmitted infection, which is frequently asymptomatic and can lead to infertility; *Chlamydia psittaci* causes psittacosis, transmitted from birds, and *Chlamydia pneumoniae* a mild pneumonia. Environmental relatives (Parachlamydiaceae) live in free-living amoebae, which is thought to be the ancestral habitat of the group.

## 18.7. Spirochaetota

**Definition 18.7.1 (Spirochete)** A **spirochete** (phylum Spirochaetota) is a slender, helically coiled, gram-negative bacterium whose flagella, the *endoflagella* or axial filaments, lie within the periplasm between the cytoplasmic membrane and the outer sheath; their rotation makes the whole cell flex and rotate, so that it moves by a corkscrew motion.

Because the motor is inside the cell, spirochetes move efficiently through viscous media such as mucus, mud and connective tissue, where bacteria with external flagella slow down. They are $0.1$–$3\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ wide and up to $500\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ long, too thin to be seen well by bright-field microscopy, and are observed by dark-field or phase-contrast methods. Most are free-living anaerobes or facultative anaerobes of mud and water (*Spirochaeta*), or live in the gut of animals; the termite hindgut contains many spirochetes, some of which fix nitrogen and make acetate for the host ([7.11.31](/7.11.31)). *Leptospira*, aerobic and distinguished by hooked ends, lives in water and in the kidneys of rodents and other mammals and causes leptospirosis, a water-borne zoonosis. *Treponema pallidum*, the agent of syphilis, and *Borrelia burgdorferi* and its relatives, transmitted by ticks and the cause of Lyme borreliosis, cannot easily be grown in culture; *T. pallidum* has a reduced genome ($1.14\,\mathrm{Mb}$) that lacks many biosynthetic pathways. *Borrelia* is unusual among bacteria in having a linear chromosome and numerous linear and circular plasmids, and it changes its surface lipoproteins during infection, which helps it persist in the host.

## 18.8. Other Phyla

### 18.8.1. Deinococcota

*Deinococcus radiodurans*, isolated in 1956 from canned meat that had spoiled after irradiation, is the most radiation-resistant organism known: its cells survive doses of several kilogray without loss of viability, where a few gray kill a human and a few hundred gray kill *E. coli*. Survival curves of this kind are described by a multi-target model, in which a cell is killed only when all of $n$ targets have been hit.

**Formula 18.8.1 (Multi-Target Survival)** If a cell contains $n$ targets, each inactivated independently with mean lethal dose $D_0$, the surviving fraction after dose $D$ is

$$
S(D) = 1 - \bigl(1 - \mathrm{e}^{-D/D_0}\bigr)^{n},
$$
which is flat ("shouldered") at low doses and falls exponentially with slope $-1/D_0$ at high doses.

![Survival after gamma irradiation in the multi-target model of Formula 18.8.1, with illustrative parameters chosen to reproduce typical published curves: *Escherichia coli* ($n = 2$, $D_0 = 0.1\,\mathrm{kGy}$), endospores of *Bacillus* ($n = 3$, $D_0 = 0.8\,\mathrm{kGy}$) and *Deinococcus radiodurans* ($n = 100$, $D_0 = 1.6\,\mathrm{kGy}$), whose curve has a long shoulder: viability is unaffected up to several kGy. The dashed line marks about 5 Gy, a dose lethal to most humans.](./plots/radiation-survival.py)

The resistance is not due to a shield or to unusually tough DNA: a dose of $5\,\mathrm{kGy}$ breaks the genome of *D. radiodurans* into more than a hundred fragments, and the cell reassembles it within hours. Three features make this possible. The cell keeps 4 to 10 copies of its genome, so that intact sequences are available as templates for homologous recombination; it reassembles the fragments by extended synthesis-dependent strand annealing followed by RecA-dependent recombination ([7.2.8](/7.2.8)); and it accumulates a very high ratio of manganese to iron, in complexes of $\ce{Mn^2+}$ with orthophosphate and small peptides that scavenge reactive oxygen species and protect proteins, in particular the repair enzymes, from oxidation. Protein protection, rather than DNA protection, now appears to be the decisive factor: what kills most cells after irradiation is not the breaks themselves but the oxidative damage that disables the machinery that would mend them. The same mechanism explains why *Deinococcus* is also extremely resistant to desiccation, which causes similar damage and is probably what it is adapted to.

Its relative *Thermus aquaticus*, isolated by Thomas Brock from a hot spring in Yellowstone, is the source of Taq polymerase, which made automated PCR possible ([7.11.9](/7.11.9#sec-9.1.3)).

### 18.8.2. Hyperthermophilic Phyla: Aquificota and Thermotogota

The Aquificota (*Aquifex*, *Hydrogenobacter*, *Thermocrinis*) are hyperthermophilic chemolithoautotrophs that oxidize $\ce{H2}$, sulfur or thiosulfate with oxygen at low concentration or with nitrate, and fix $\ce{CO2}$ by the reverse citric acid cycle; *Aquifex aeolicus* grows up to $95\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$, the highest known for a bacterium, and *Thermocrinis ruber* forms the pink streamers in the outflow channels of Yellowstone hot springs in which hyperthermophiles were first studied. The Thermotogota (*Thermotoga maritima*, up to $90\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$) are anaerobic fermenters of sugars and polymers, named for the loose outer sheath, the "toga", that balloons over their poles. Both phyla branch deeply in rRNA trees, which once suggested that the last common ancestor of bacteria was a hyperthermophile; genome analyses complicated the picture, because a substantial share of the genes of *T. maritima* are most similar to archaeal genes, evidence of extensive horizontal transfer between hyperthermophilic bacteria and archaea sharing the same habitats ([7.10.27](/7.10.27#sec-27.6.2)). *Thermodesulfobacterium*, a thermophilic sulfate reducer, is unusual among bacteria in having ether-linked membrane lipids, although they are not isoprenoid as in archaea ([7.11.2](/7.11.2#sec-2.2.2)).

### 18.8.3. Chloroflexota, Acidobacteriota, Nitrospirota and Fusobacteriota

The Chloroflexota (green nonsulfur bacteria, filamentous anoxygenic phototrophs) are monoderm filaments that glide; *Chloroflexus aurantiacus* grows photoheterotrophically in hot-spring microbial mats beneath a layer of cyanobacteria, with chlorosomes and a type II reaction center, and fixes $\ce{CO2}$ by the 3-hydroxypropionate bicycle ([7.11.6](/7.11.6#sec-6.5.3)). Most members of the phylum are not phototrophs: *Dehalococcoides* respires chlorinated solvents in groundwater and is used in bioremediation ([7.11.30](/7.11.30)), and uncultured lineages are abundant in sediments and sludge.

The Acidobacteriota are one of the most abundant phyla in soil, often 20 % or more of the 16S sequences, yet few species are in culture: they grow slowly, prefer dilute media and acidic conditions, and are overgrown on rich plates. Those that have been cultured are aerobic or facultatively anaerobic heterotrophs that degrade plant polymers, and one, *Chloracidobacterium thermophilum* from hot springs, is an aerobic phototroph with chlorosomes.

The Nitrospirota contain the nitrite oxidizer *Nitrospira*, more abundant in most soils and wastewater plants than the better-known *Nitrobacter*, and the lineages of *Nitrospira* that carry out **comammox**, the complete oxidation of ammonia to nitrate in one organism ([7.11.7](/7.11.7#sec-7.2.4)); also *Leptospirillum*, an acidophilic iron oxidizer of acid mine drainage, and *Thermodesulfovibrio*, a thermophilic sulfate reducer. The Fusobacteriota are anaerobic, spindle-shaped gram-negative rods that ferment amino acids and peptides; *Fusobacterium nucleatum* is a normal inhabitant of the mouth, common in dental plaque and periodontal disease, and is found in colorectal tumors, where its role is under investigation. Two further small phyla of fermentative anaerobes associated with animal guts, the Synergistota and the Fibrobacterota, complete the list of groups regularly met in surveys of the mammalian microbiome.

## Summary

- The bacterial tree falls into two large clades, the mostly monoderm Terrabacteria (gram-positive phyla, cyanobacteria, Chloroflexota, Deinococcota) and the diderm Gracilicutes (Pseudomonadota and relatives, FCB and PVC groups, spirochetes); the candidate phyla radiation (Definition 16.3.2) is a large group of small, dependent cells known mainly from metagenomes.
- The two gram-positive phyla differ in G + C content and in form: Bacillota (low G + C, endospores, teichoic acids) and Actinomycetota (high G + C, from coryneforms to mycelia). The mycoplasmas are wall-less Bacillota with minimal genomes; the actinomycetes (Definition 18.3.1) are the main source of antibiotics; mycolic acids give mycobacteria their acid-fastness, impermeability and slow growth.
- The cyanobacteria (Definition 18.4.1) are the only oxygenic phototrophic bacteria, with five morphological subsections and differentiated cells (heterocysts, akinetes, hormogonia); *Prochlorococcus* and *Synechococcus* dominate marine picophytoplankton.
- Among the gram-negative phyla: Bacteroidota degrade polysaccharides in the gut, soil and sea; Chlorobiota are extreme low-light phototrophs; Planctomycetota have compartmentalized cells, bud, and include the anammox bacteria; Verrucomicrobiota include *Akkermansia* and acidophilic methanotrophs; chlamydiae alternate elementary and reticulate bodies; spirochetes (Definition 18.7.1) swim with periplasmic flagella.
- *Deinococcus* survives kilogray doses of radiation by multiple genome copies, efficient reassembly of fragments and Mn-based protection of its repair proteins (Formula 18.8.1); Aquificota and Thermotogota are hyperthermophilic bacteria whose genomes record transfer with archaea; Acidobacteriota and Chloroflexota are abundant but largely uncultured; *Nitrospira* performs most nitrite oxidation and, in some lineages, comammox.

## Questions

1. Mycoplasmas and chlamydiae both have small genomes and depend on a host, but one of them can be grown in cell-free media and the other cannot. Explain the difference, and state why neither is affected by penicillin.

   **Answer** Mycoplasmas have lost biosynthetic pathways but keep a complete energy metabolism and transport systems, so a rich medium supplemented with sterols and precursors replaces the host; chlamydiae have in addition lost the ability to make enough ATP and import it from the host cytoplasm, which only a living cell can supply, so they are grown in cell culture. Penicillin inhibits the cross-linking of peptidoglycan; mycoplasmas have no wall at all, and chlamydiae make peptidoglycan only transiently at the septum of the reticulate body, with no wall in the infectious elementary body.

2. Why does a thick, mycolic-acid-rich envelope lead at once to acid-fastness, to slow growth and to a broad intrinsic resistance to antibiotics?

   **Answer** All three follow from the very low permeability of the waxy layer. Once the hot stain has entered, the dye and its complexes cannot be washed out by acid alcohol (acid-fastness); nutrients enter slowly, which limits the growth rate (generation times of hours to days); and hydrophilic drugs cross the envelope so slowly that their intracellular concentration remains below the inhibitory level, which adds to the specific resistance mechanisms.

3. Use Formula 18.8.1 to explain the shoulder of the *Deinococcus* survival curve, and say what the parameter $n$ corresponds to biologically. Why is the protection of proteins rather than of DNA considered decisive?

   **Answer** With $n$ targets, the surviving fraction stays near 1 until the probability that all targets have been hit becomes appreciable, giving a shoulder of width about $D_0\ln n$, after which the curve falls exponentially with slope $-1/D_0$. Here $n$ is a formal measure of how much damage the cell can repair before it dies, reflecting multiple genome copies and efficient reassembly, not literally $n$ separate targets. Irradiation breaks DNA in resistant and sensitive bacteria alike at similar rates, so the difference lies in what happens afterwards: cells whose repair enzymes have been oxidized cannot mend the breaks, and the Mn–phosphate–peptide complexes of *Deinococcus* keep those enzymes active.

4. Subsections I–III of the cyanobacteria are not natural groups, while subsections IV and V form a clade. What does this say about the use of morphology in bacterial classification, and why might heterocyst formation be a better phylogenetic character than cell shape?

   **Answer** Simple shapes (cocci, rods, undifferentiated filaments) arise and are lost readily and are shared by unrelated lineages; classification based on them is therefore artificial, as it also proved to be for the gram-positive bacteria, which are two phyla. Heterocyst formation requires a developmental program of many interacting genes (sensing nitrogen status, a patterning circuit, new wall layers, remodeling of the photosynthetic apparatus), which cannot be acquired or reinvented easily, so its distribution tracks ancestry; it is the kind of complex character that is phylogenetically informative.

5. A soil sample yields, on a rich plate, colonies of *Bacillus*, *Streptomyces* and pseudomonads, although 16S sequencing of the same soil shows that Acidobacteriota are as abundant as any of them. Give two reasons for the discrepancy and one way to reduce it.

   **Answer** The common isolates grow fast on high concentrations of nutrients, whereas many soil bacteria, acidobacteria among them, are slow-growing oligotrophs that are inhibited by rich media (and by the reactive oxygen species formed in them) and overgrown by their neighbors; and many soil organisms depend on signals, siderophores or metabolites supplied by other members of the community, which are absent on a plate. Cultivation improves with dilute media that resemble the habitat, long incubation at the natural pH, and diffusion chambers or the isolation chip, which incubate single cells in the soil itself so that the natural compounds reach them ([7.11.4](/7.11.4#sec-4.5.4)).
