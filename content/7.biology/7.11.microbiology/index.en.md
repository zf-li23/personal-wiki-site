Microbiology is defined by a size and a set of methods rather than by a branch of the tree of life. Its objects, **microorganisms**, are the bacteria, the archaea and the microscopic eukaryotes, together with the **viruses** that depend on cells, and they are studied with the same tools: the microscope, sterile technique, the **pure culture** and the enrichment. Because a single cell can rarely be watched, a microorganism is known through what its populations do, in a culture, in a community or in a host, and much of the subject consists in reading cellular properties back from such collective behavior. Three questions run through it. How does a cell of about a micrometer, without internal compartments, live: how is it bounded, how does it gain energy and carbon, how does it grow, sense and change its genes? Who are the microorganisms, and how are they related, now that the **16S rRNA gene** and genome sequences have placed bacteria, archaea and eukaryotes on one tree? And what do they do in the world: in the cycles of the elements, in the bodies of plants and animals, and as agents of infectious disease?

The chapters follow the usual order of the subject. After its history and scale, the course builds the **prokaryote** cell from the inside out, its membranes and walls, the **peptidoglycan** and the **outer membrane** behind the **Gram stain**, then its surface structures, inclusions, **endospores** and motility. Nutrition and transport lead to energy metabolism, from **fermentation** and respiration to the metabolisms that only prokaryotes have: anoxygenic phototrophy, **nitrogen fixation**, chemolithotrophy, **anaerobic respiration** and **methanogenesis**, all ordered by the redox potentials of electron donors and acceptors. The growth of populations, its dependence on the environment and its control by sterilization and disinfection come next, then regulation, differentiation and the **biofilm**, and bacterial genetics, in which **horizontal gene transfer** makes the genome fluid; antimicrobial drugs and **antimicrobial resistance** apply both. The survey of diversity begins with systematics and identification and goes through the bacterial phyla, the archaea, the protists and algae, and the fungi. Seven chapters on viruses treat the virion and the infectious cycle, the phages and the defenses bacteria raise against them, the molecular biology of DNA viruses, RNA viruses and retroviruses, viral diversity and subviral agents, the interactions of viruses with their hosts, and antiviral drugs and vaccines. The course ends with ecology, its methods and habitats, the nutrient cycles and the symbioses with plants and animals, and with microbes and humans: the human **microbiome**, bacterial pathogenesis from adhesion and toxins to immune evasion and secretion systems, and the epidemiology of infectious disease. The course summary follows these threads across the chapters and collects the quantitative models side by side.

Microbiology draws on almost every earlier biology course. The chemistry of the cell envelope, bioenergetics and photosynthesis come from [biochemistry](/7.1); transcription, operons and CRISPR from [molecular biology](/7.2); the prokaryotic and eukaryotic cell from [cell biology](/7.3); transformation, conjugation and transduction as genetic tools from [genetics](/7.4); the physics of chemotaxis and the growth laws from [biophysics](/7.6); the algorithms for phylogenies and for amplicon and metagenome data from [bioinformatics](/7.8); noise and bet hedging from [systems biology](/7.9); and the tree of life, the origin of the prokaryotes and of eukaryotes and the evolution of resistance from [evolutionary biology](/7.10). The models of growth, killing and epidemics use the exponential laws and Poisson statistics of [probability](/3.1) and the equations of [ordinary differential equations](/2.4). In turn, microbiology is the background of the later courses: the rhizosphere, nodules and plant pathogens of botany and plant physiology, the communities and element cycles of ecology, and above all [immunology](/7.15), which takes up the pathogens, the innate response to viruses and the vaccines met here from the side of the host.

I first studied microbiology during olympiad training, and I think it is the right place to begin a systematic understanding of biological diversity. One reason is that it is not an enormous subject. The microorganisms that have been described and cultured are few compared with the plants and animals that fill the later courses, and the diversity that sequencing has revealed is largely known only as genomes; yet the organisms that are known are closely tied to our lives, as the agents of disease and of fermentation, as the inhabitants of our own bodies and as the chemists of the soil and the sea. The tree of life on which they are placed is the same one that [evolutionary biology](/7.10) explains, and on it almost every branch is microbial, so that the plants, fungi and animals studied later appear as a few late branches of a mostly microbial tree rather than as the whole of life.

The other reason is that microbiology is the part of organismal biology that stays closest to molecular and cellular biology. Many of its terms, the cell envelope, the operon, plasmids and transposons, transformation and conjugation, the replication of viruses, have already been introduced in cell biology and genetics, and a bacterium is understood through its membranes, its metabolism and its genes rather than through an anatomy that has to be learned first. Little knowledge of macroscopic biology is needed to start it, and in exchange it gives the later courses on plants, animals and ecology the organisms and processes, symbionts, pathogens and the cycles of elements, without which they cannot be understood. Of the books, Brock is the classic reference in English and the main line of these pages; the textbook of Deqing Zhou is the one best suited to a first encounter at the olympiad level, and the book of Ping Shen and Xiangdong Chen is the more advanced reference.
