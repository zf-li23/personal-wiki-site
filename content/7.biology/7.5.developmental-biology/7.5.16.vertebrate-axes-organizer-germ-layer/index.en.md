Vertebrate eggs do not lay down a grid of maternal gradients as the *Drosophila* egg does ([7.5.14](/7.5.14)). They have one maternal axis, animal–vegetal, and a single, transient asymmetry that defines the dorsal side; everything else is made by signalling between cells, by induction. This chapter follows the two inductions on which the vertebrate body plan rests. In the blastula, vegetal cells induce the cells above them to become **mesoderm**, more dorsal vegetal cells inducing more dorsal mesoderm. The most dorsal mesoderm becomes a signalling centre, the **Spemann organizer**, which during **gastrulation** patterns the mesoderm along the dorsal–ventral axis, induces the nervous system and organizes the head-to-tail axis. We start with *Xenopus*, where the classical experiments were done, then compare the zebrafish, chick and mouse, whose embryos look very different but use the same signals. The movements of gastrulation and the definitions of the **germ layers** are in [7.5.9](/7.5.9); the theory of morphogens and of activator–inhibitor systems on which the models here rely is in [7.5.11](/7.5.11) and [7.5.12](/7.5.12). The next chapter, [7.5.17](/7.5.17), continues with neural induction, the anterior–posterior axis and left–right asymmetry.

## 16.1. The Dorsal Determinant of the Amphibian Egg

### 16.1.1. Sperm Entry and Cortical Rotation

The *Xenopus* egg is radially symmetric about its animal–vegetal axis. The animal hemisphere is pigmented and contains the nucleus; the vegetal hemisphere is yolky and contains localized maternal mRNAs, among them those of the transcription factor VegT and the TGF-β family member Vg1. The sperm can enter anywhere in the animal hemisphere, and the point of entry becomes the ventral side.

During the first cell cycle, from about 45 to 90 min after fertilization, the cortex, the outer few micrometres of the egg, rotates by about 30° relative to the inner cytoplasm, the vegetal cortex moving towards the side opposite sperm entry. The rotation is driven by kinesin moving along a parallel array of **microtubules** that forms under the vegetal cortex, oriented by the sperm centrosome. In some amphibians it exposes a less pigmented band, the grey crescent, opposite the sperm entry point. Three experiments show that the rotation determines the dorsal side.

- Ultraviolet irradiation of the vegetal pole, or cold or nocodazole, which destroy the microtubule array, block the rotation. The embryos develop without dorsal structures, as radially symmetric ventral tissue ("belly pieces").
- Tilting a UV-irradiated egg by 90° in the gravitational field, which displaces the cytoplasm relative to the cortex as the rotation would, rescues the axis, and the dorsal side forms where the vegetal cytoplasm has moved (Gerhart and colleagues).
- Lithium chloride, which inhibits the kinase GSK3, and deuterium oxide, which stabilizes microtubules, have the opposite effect: they dorsalize the embryo.

The rotation therefore moves something from the vegetal pole to one side. Transplantation of vegetal cortex showed that the activity is in the cortex itself and can be moved to any position.

### 16.1.2. Huluwa and β-Catenin

The molecular target of the rotation is **β-catenin**. In all cells, β-catenin is constantly phosphorylated by GSK3 in the **destruction complex** organized by Axin and degraded ([7.3.19](/7.3.19)); where the complex is inhibited, β-catenin accumulates and enters the nucleus with Tcf/Lef transcription factors. In the *Xenopus* blastula, nuclear β-catenin is found on the dorsal side from the 32-cell stage, and depleting maternal β-catenin mRNA from oocytes abolishes all dorsal structures. Overexpression of components of the **Wnt signaling pathway**, or inhibition of GSK3, on the ventral side induces a second axis.

The localized activity that stabilizes β-catenin was long sought among the Wnt pathway components, and maternal Wnt11 and Dishevelled were candidates. The determinant identified most directly is Huluwa, a maternal transmembrane protein (Yan and colleagues, 2018). Huluwa protein is localized at the vegetal cortex of the egg and shifts dorsally after fertilization. It binds the poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase Tankyrase and promotes the ubiquitination and degradation of Axin; without Axin the destruction complex cannot form and β-catenin is stabilized, independently of Wnt ligands and their receptors. The evidence comes from both directions. Depleting maternal *huluwa* mRNA from *Xenopus* oocytes, which are then fertilized after transfer into a host female, eliminates the organizer and the body axis; ectopic Huluwa on the ventral side induces a complete secondary axis; and in zebrafish, a maternal-effect mutation in *hwa* found in a genetic screen gives embryos without organizer, radially ventralized, which develop no head, notochord or neural tube. The zebrafish mutant *ichabod*, which reduces maternal β-catenin-2, has the same phenotype, as expected if Huluwa acts through β-catenin.

Nuclear β-catenin, together with Tcf, activates the homeobox genes *siamois* and *twin* on the dorsal side. These cooperate with signals from the vegetal cells (16.3) to specify the organizer, and, in the dorsal animal cells, nuclear β-catenin activates *chordin* and *noggin* already before the mid-blastula transition, a region called the blastula Chordin- and Noggin-expressing centre that prepares the dorsal ectoderm for neural induction ([7.5.17](/7.5.17)).

## 16.2. Keeping the Ectoderm Ectoderm

Mesoderm-inducing signals spread from the vegetal hemisphere into the animal cells, and the animal cells must be protected from them to remain ectoderm. Two maternal or early zygotic factors of the animal hemisphere do this.

- Ectodermin, also known as TIF1γ or TRIM33, is a ubiquitin ligase expressed in the animal hemisphere. It monoubiquitinates Smad4, the common partner of the Smads activated by Nodal and BMP receptors, and so limits the response of animal cells to both signals (Dupont and colleagues, 2005, 2009). Depleting it extends mesoderm into the animal cap; overexpressing it in the marginal zone suppresses mesoderm. Its effect on BMP signalling also restrains the ventralizing signal in the ectoderm, which favours neural fate.
- Norrin, a secreted protein of the animal ectoderm, binds and antagonizes Nodal, Activin and BMP directly, and at the same time acts as a ligand of the Wnt receptor Frizzled 4 and its coreceptor LRP, promoting β-catenin signalling in the dorsal ectoderm (Xu and colleagues, 2012). It therefore protects the ectoderm from mesoderm induction and favours the expression of BMP antagonists on the dorsal side.

The default state of the animal cells, in the sense of what they do when left alone, is thus not a passive one: it is maintained by active inhibition of the signals that would change it. The same theme, a default fate protected by antagonists, returns in neural induction ([7.5.17](/7.5.17)).

## 16.3. Mesoderm Induction and the Nieuwkoop Centre

### 16.3.1. Nieuwkoop's Experiments

In the late blastula, the animal cap (above the blastocoel), the marginal zone (around the equator) and the vegetal mass are already specified as prospective ectoderm, mesoderm and endoderm. Nieuwkoop (1969) asked where the mesoderm comes from by cutting axolotl blastulae into these parts. The animal cap cultured alone forms only epidermis (atypical ectoderm), the vegetal mass only endoderm-like tissue; neither forms mesoderm. When an animal cap is combined with a vegetal mass, mesoderm forms, and when the animal cap is labelled, the mesoderm is found to come from the animal cells. Vegetal cells therefore induce mesoderm in cells that would otherwise become ectoderm.

**Definition 16.3.1 (Mesoderm induction)** **Mesoderm induction** is the induction of mesodermal fates in the cells of the marginal zone of the amphibian blastula (and its equivalents in other vertebrates) by signals from the vegetal (endodermal) cells, principally Nodal-related members of the TGF-β family.

The vegetal mass is itself patterned. Combinations of animal caps with dorsal vegetal cells give dorsal mesoderm (notochord and muscle), and with ventral vegetal cells ventral mesoderm (blood and mesenchyme) (Boterenbrood and Nieuwkoop, 1973; Dale and Slack, 1987).

**Definition 16.3.2 (Nieuwkoop centre)** The **Nieuwkoop center** is the group of dorsal vegetal cells of the amphibian blastula, marked by nuclear β-catenin, that induces the overlying marginal zone to become the Spemann organizer; it is itself specified by the maternal dorsal determinants moved by cortical rotation.

The Nieuwkoop centre is defined by its activity, not by its fate: its cells become endoderm. Transplanted to the ventral side of a host blastula, a group of dorsal vegetal cells induces a second organizer and a second axis from host cells, while contributing to neither (Gimlich and Gerhart, 1984).

### 16.3.2. The Signals

The vegetal maternal mRNAs supply the inducing activity. VegT, a T-box transcription factor whose mRNA is localized in the vegetal cortex of the oocyte, is required for both endoderm and mesoderm: embryos from VegT-depleted oocytes form almost no endoderm or mesoderm, and their vegetal cells cannot induce (Zhang and colleagues, 1998). VegT activates the zygotic genes of the Nodal-related ligands (*Xnr1*, *2*, *4*, *5*, *6*, and *derrière*), which signal through Activin-like receptors and Smad2 ([7.3.19](/7.3.19)); Vg1, also maternal and vegetal, adds to the signal. Because nuclear β-catenin on the dorsal side cooperates with VegT at the *Xnr* promoters, the Nodal-related proteins are more abundant dorsally, and the resulting dorsal-high gradient of Smad2 activity across the marginal zone is the main source of mesodermal dorsal–ventral pattern (Agius and colleagues, 2000). Inhibitors confirm the role: a cleaved form of Cerberus that binds only Nodal, or the antagonist Lefty, abolishes mesoderm.

### 16.3.3. The Animal-Cap Assay and Thresholds

The animal cap is an ideal test tube for inducing signals: it is competent to respond to them until the early gastrula, and without signals it forms epidermis. Adding a protein to the culture medium, or injecting its mRNA, and scoring the tissues formed is the animal-cap assay. Activin, a TGF-β family member that acts through the same receptors as the Nodal-related proteins, was the first inducer identified this way. Its effect is concentration-dependent: at low concentrations caps form ventral mesoderm (mesenchyme, blood), at higher ones muscle, and at the highest notochord and organizer markers such as *goosecoid*. In explants, a 1.5-fold change in concentration is enough to change muscle to notochord (Green and Smith, 1990). Beads releasing Activin placed among dissociated cap cells induce *goosecoid* close to the bead and *brachyury* further away, which shows that the protein acts at a distance in a graded way.

**Formula 16.3.1 (Receptor occupancy thresholds)** Let a cell carry $R_{\mathrm{T}}$ receptors that bind the ligand at concentration $c$ with dissociation constant $K_{\mathrm{D}}$. At equilibrium the number of occupied receptors is $R(c) = R_{\mathrm{T}}c/(c + K_{\mathrm{D}})$, and a gene that responds when $R \ge R_i$ is switched on above

$$
c_i = K_{\mathrm{D}}\,\frac{R_i}{R_{\mathrm{T}} - R_i} .
\tag{16.1}
$$

**Example 16.3.1** Dyson and Gurdon (1998) estimated that *Xenopus* animal-cap cells have about 5000 Activin receptors, and that occupancy of about 100 switches on *brachyury* (Xbra, a general mesoderm gene) and about 300 switches on *goosecoid* (dorsal mesoderm). By Eq. (16.1) the corresponding concentrations are $c_1 = K_{\mathrm{D}} \times 100/4900 = 0.020\,K_{\mathrm{D}}$ and $c_2 = K_{\mathrm{D}} \times 300/4700 = 0.064\,K_{\mathrm{D}}$, a ratio of 3.1. At such low occupancies the cell counts receptors almost linearly with concentration, and a threefold change in concentration separates the two fates. The cells responded to the absolute number of occupied receptors, not to the fraction: caps with fewer receptors (made by reducing their expression) required proportionally more Activin for the same response.

A threshold on a graded input gives graded boundaries unless the targets reinforce the decision. *goosecoid* and *brachyury* repress each other, and their mutual repression sharpens the boundary between organizer and general mesoderm.

**Formula 16.3.2 (Mutual repression sharpens a threshold)** Let $s_1 = R/R_1$ and $s_2 = R/R_2$ be the occupancy relative to the two thresholds, $h(u) = u^m/(1 + u^m)$, and $B$, $G$ the levels of Brachyury and Goosecoid. Then

$$
\dot B = \frac{h(s_1)}{1 + (G/K_G)^4} - B, \qquad \dot G = \frac{h(s_2)}{1 + (B/K_B)^2} - G .
\tag{16.2}
$$

With strong repression of $B$ by $G$ ($K_G$ small), cells above the second threshold switch $B$ off completely, and the two genes come to occupy exclusive domains with a sharp border (figure).

The response of a cell also depends on its neighbours. Gurdon (1988) found that a cell taken from the prospective muscle of a gastrula and placed singly among animal-cap cells does not differentiate as muscle, whereas a group of a hundred or more does: the **community effect**, a signal exchanged among like cells that maintains their common fate ([7.3.24](/7.3.24)). In the mesoderm, FGF signalling and Brachyury maintain each other in a positive feedback loop that is part of this effect.

## 16.4. Nodal and Lefty: A Self-Limiting Signal

Nodal-related genes are induced by Nodal signalling itself, so the Nodal signal is self-enhancing; and it also induces its own antagonists, Lefty1 and Lefty2, which bind Nodal and its coreceptor and block signalling. This is the structure of an activator–inhibitor system ([7.5.12](/7.5.12)), and measurements in the zebrafish blastoderm supported the further condition of such systems, that the inhibitor spread further than the activator. Müller, Schier and colleagues (2012) measured diffusion of fluorescent fusion proteins in living embryos by **FRAP** and found effective diffusion coefficients of $0.7\,\mathrm{\mu m^2\,s^{-1}}$ for Cyclops, $3.2$ for Squint (the two zebrafish Nodals) and $11$ and $19$ for Lefty1 and Lefty2, with clearance half-lives of the order of 1–2 h.

**Formula 16.4.1 (Nodal–Lefty model)** On a line $x \ge 0$ from the blastoderm margin, let $N$ and $L$ be the concentrations of Nodal and Lefty and $S = N/(1 + L/K_L)$ the effective Nodal signal. With a maternal source $\rho(x)$ at the margin and a cooperative response $f(S) = S^h/(K^h + S^h)$,

$$
N_t = D_N N_{xx} - k_N N + \rho(x) + \sigma_N f(S), \qquad L_t = D_L L_{xx} - k_L L + \sigma_L f(S) .
\tag{16.3}
$$

Here the source stands for the yolk syncytial layer and the marginal cells, where maternal Eomesodermin, Huluwa/β-catenin and Nodal itself activate *nodal* genes; $D_N < D_L$.

Without Lefty, the self-enhancing term would spread Nodal production through the whole blastoderm, the front of a bistable system advancing like a wave ([7.5.12](/7.5.12)); with Lefty, induced wherever Nodal signals and spreading further, the spread is halted. In the simulation the signal stays above the half-maximal level only within about 90 µm of the margin, about six cell diameters, the width of the domain that forms mesoderm and endoderm in the zebrafish. Experiments agree with the qualitative prediction. In *lefty1*; *lefty2* double mutant zebrafish, Nodal signalling expands, mesoderm and endoderm are produced in excess and the embryos die, and a small-molecule inhibitor of the Nodal receptor applied for a short time rescues them, which shows that the essential role of Lefty is to limit the duration and range of signalling rather than to create a pattern (Rogers and colleagues, 2017). Nodal–Lefty is thus not a Turing system that makes a periodic pattern from noise, but an activator–inhibitor pair anchored to a source, whose function is to make a self-enhancing signal self-limiting. The same pair, in the left lateral plate, breaks left–right symmetry ([7.5.17](/7.5.17)).

## 16.5. The Spemann Organizer

### 16.5.1. The Graft

In 1924 Hans Spemann and Hilde Mangold grafted the dorsal lip of the blastopore of an early gastrula of the lightly pigmented newt *Triturus cristatus* into the ventral side of a gastrula of the darker *Triturus taeniatus*. The host developed a second body axis, with a second neural tube, notochord and somites, sometimes a nearly complete second embryo joined to the first. The difference in pigmentation allowed them to follow the graft: the notochord of the secondary axis and part of its somites were derived from the graft, but its neural tube, most of its somites and its gut lining were host tissue.

**Definition 16.5.1 (Spemann organizer)** The **Spemann organizer** is the dorsal mesoderm of the amphibian early gastrula, at the dorsal lip of the blastopore, which when grafted to the ventral side of another embryo self-differentiates into dorsal axial mesoderm and induces the surrounding host cells to form a complete, patterned secondary body axis; by extension, the equivalent region in other vertebrates (the shield of fish, Hensen's node of birds, the node of mammals).

Two properties are separated by the experiment. The organizer differentiates according to its origin (self-differentiation), into prechordal mesoderm, notochord and the pharyngeal endoderm; and it instructs its neighbours (induction), converting ventral mesoderm into somites and ventral ectoderm into neural tube. The organizer is also patterned in time: the dorsal lip of an early gastrula, whose cells will involute first and lie most anteriorly, induces heads when grafted, whereas the lip of a late gastrula induces trunks and tails. The early organizer is therefore a head organizer, the later a trunk organizer ([7.5.17](/7.5.17)).

![The organizer graft of Spemann and Mangold (1924). B: cross-section of the twinned embryo that results. The primary axis (top) is entirely host tissue. In the secondary axis (bottom), the notochord and part of a somite derive from the graft (dashed outlines), while the neural tube, most of the somites and the gut are host tissue: the graft self-differentiated into dorsal axial mesoderm and induced the neighbouring host cells to form axial structures](./plots/organizer-graft.py)

**Example 16.5.1 (Experiment and reasoning: is the organizer instructive?)** A graft that produces a second axis could, in principle, act permissively, by releasing host cells from an inhibition that is lifted elsewhere, or instructively, by giving them new information. Four results bear on this. First, the secondary axis is made largely of host cells that would otherwise have formed belly tissue, so the graft changed their fate. Second, the induced structures are arranged in register with the graft (the neural tube above the grafted notochord, somites beside it), so the information is spatial. Third, the effect is graded with distance: host mesoderm next to the graft forms somites, further away lateral mesoderm. Fourth, the organizer molecules turned out to be inhibitors (16.5.2), so in molecular terms the instruction consists in creating a region where ventral signals are absent, an instructive effect achieved by a permissive mechanism. The example shows why the distinction between instructive and permissive signals is always relative to a defined target.

### 16.5.2. The Molecules of the Organizer

The organizer was dissected molecularly from about 1990, by screening for genes expressed in the dorsal lip and testing their mRNAs in two assays: rescue of the axis of UV-ventralized embryos, and induction of a second axis on the ventral side.

| Class | Molecule | Activity |
|---|---|---|
| Transcription factors | Goosecoid, Lim1, Xnot, Otx2, Siamois, Twin | specify organizer and its derivatives; Goosecoid injected ventrally induces a partial secondary axis |
| BMP antagonists | Chordin, Noggin, Follistatin | bind BMPs and prevent receptor binding; dorsalize ventral mesoderm, induce neural tissue from ectoderm |
| Multifunctional antagonist | Cerberus | binds BMP, Nodal and Wnt; induces ectopic heads |
| Wnt antagonists | Dickkopf-1, Frzb, Tiki | block Wnt8 and other Wnts; required for head formation |
| BMP | ADMP | a BMP expressed in the organizer and repressed by BMP signalling (16.6) |

The organizer works mainly by secreting antagonists of the ventral signals, BMP4 (with BMP2 and BMP7) and Wnt8, which are expressed throughout the marginal zone except where the organizer represses them. Noggin mRNA rescued the axis of UV-irradiated embryos (Smith and Harland, 1992), and the simultaneous depletion of Chordin, Noggin and Follistatin by antisense morpholinos abolishes the neural plate and the dorsal mesoderm while leaving the organizer itself in place (Khokha and colleagues, 2005). Conversely, a dominant-negative BMP receptor expressed on the ventral side induces a second axis without any organizer cells. The organizer's induction is therefore a negative one: it makes a dorsal region by removing ventral signals from it.

## 16.6. The BMP Gradient and Its Scaling

### 16.6.1. A Shuttle with the Opposite Polarity

The BMP antagonists create a gradient of BMP signalling across the marginal zone and the ectoderm, high ventrally and low dorsally, read as phosphorylated Smad1/5/8 in the nuclei. The mesoderm is patterned by it into notochord (lowest BMP), somites, intermediate mesoderm and lateral plate and blood (highest), and the ectoderm into neural plate, neural plate border and epidermis ([7.5.17](/7.5.17)). The gradient is formed by a version of the shuttle that concentrates Dpp in the fly ([7.5.14](/7.5.14#sec-14.6)): Chordin, produced dorsally, binds BMP, the complex diffuses, and the metalloprotease Xolloid (Tolloid) cleaves Chordin and releases BMP, which therefore accumulates ventrally, at the side opposite the source of the antagonist. Sizzled, a secreted Frizzled-related protein made ventrally, inhibits Tolloid, and Crossveinless-2 and Twisted gastrulation modulate binding. The fly and the frog use the same molecules (Dpp/BMP4, Sog/Chordin, Tolloid) with the same logic and opposite orientations relative to the nerve cord.

### 16.6.2. Regulation and Expansion–Repression

Amphibian embryos regulate. When a blastula is bisected along the plane that separates dorsal and ventral halves, the dorsal half, containing the organizer, forms a small but well-proportioned embryo, as Spemann had already found; the BMP gradient in the half embryo is compressed to fit its smaller size. A gradient formed by a fixed source with fixed decay length cannot do this: its length scale $\lambda$ does not depend on the size of the tissue ([7.5.11](/7.5.11)). Ben-Zvi, Shilo, Barkai and colleagues proposed a general mechanism, which they called expansion–repression.

**Formula 16.6.1 (Expansion–repression)** Let $M$ be the morphogen (BMP activity) on a field $0 \le x \le L$, entering at $x = 0$ with flux $j_0$, and $E$ an expander that reduces the degradation of $M$. Let $E$ be produced only where $M$ is below a threshold $T$, and be stable and diffusible:

$$
M_t = D_M M_{xx} - \frac{k\,M}{1 + E/K_E}, \qquad E_t = D_E E_{xx} - k_E E + \frac{\eta}{1 + (M/T)^n}, \qquad -D_M M_x(0) = j_0 ,
\tag{16.4}
$$

with $k_E$ small and $D_E$ large.

**Proposition 16.6.1 (Scaling)** In the limit $k_E \to 0$ and $D_E$ large (so that $E$ is nearly uniform), a steady state of Eq. (16.4) requires $M(L) \approx T$, and the decay length of $M$ is then

$$
\lambda_{\mathrm{eff}} = \sqrt{\frac{D_M (1 + E/K_E)}{k}} \approx \frac{L}{\ln\bigl(M(0)/T\bigr)},
\tag{16.5}
$$

proportional to $L$: the gradient scales with the size of the field.

**Proof** (sketch) With $k_E = 0$, $E$ can stop growing only if its production $\eta/(1 + (M/T)^n)$ vanishes, approximately, everywhere, that is if $M \gtrsim T$ throughout the field; since $M$ decreases from the source, this means $M(L) \gtrsim T$. As long as $M(L) < T$, $E$ grows, reduces the degradation of $M$ and lengthens its profile, which raises $M(L)$. Production of $E$ therefore stops when $M(L)$ has risen to about $T$. With $E$ uniform, $M$ has the exponential profile $M(x) \approx M(0)\mathrm{e}^{-x/\lambda_{\mathrm{eff}}}$ with $\lambda_{\mathrm{eff}}$ as in Eq. (16.5), and $M(L) = T$ gives $\lambda_{\mathrm{eff}} = L/\ln(M(0)/T)$. $M(0) = j_0\lambda_{\mathrm{eff}}/D_M$ depends on $\lambda_{\mathrm{eff}}$ only logarithmically through the denominator, so $\lambda_{\mathrm{eff}}$ is close to proportional to $L$. $\square$

The mechanism needs two properties of the expander: it must be repressed by the morphogen, and it must be long-lived, so that it integrates the state of the whole field. In *Xenopus*, two proteins have been proposed in this role. ADMP, a BMP made in the organizer and repressed by BMP signalling, is shuttled ventrally by Chordin (Ben-Zvi and colleagues, 2008; Reversade and De Robertis, 2005, showed that when BMP2, 4 and 7 are depleted, ADMP still patterns the embryo, and when ADMP is also depleted the whole embryo becomes neural). Sizzled, repressed by BMP in the opposite sense and long-lived, stabilizes Chordin and has the properties of an expander for the Chordin gradient (Inomata, Haraguchi and Sasai, 2013).

A: with a stable expander, made only where the morphogen is below a threshold, the profiles plotted against relative position x/L nearly coincide: the expander accumulates until the morphogen at the far end has fallen to the threshold, which adjusts the decay length to the field. ## 16.7. The Organizer in Other Vertebrates

### 16.7.1. Zebrafish: The Shield

The zebrafish blastoderm sits on a large yolk cell; at its margin lie the yolk syncytial layer (YSL), formed by nuclei that entered the yolk, and the marginal blastomeres. At the onset of gastrulation, a thickening of the dorsal margin, the embryonic shield, forms; grafted to the ventral margin, it induces a secondary axis (Shih and Fraser, 1996): it is the organizer. Its specification uses the same components as in *Xenopus*. Maternal Huluwa stabilizes β-catenin dorsally (16.1); the β-catenin targets *bozozok* (*dharma*) and *chordin* are activated on the dorsal side. The Nodal genes *squint* and *cyclops* are expressed in the YSL and the margin; their transcription is activated by three inputs, maternal Eomesodermin A (a T-box transcription factor, the functional counterpart of VegT), dorsal β-catenin, and Nodal signalling itself, so that a maternal prepattern is amplified by autoregulation. Lefty1 and Lefty2 limit the domain (16.4).

The mutants make the logic explicit:

- *squint*; *cyclops* double mutants, and maternal-zygotic mutants of the Nodal coreceptor *one-eyed pinhead* (*MZoep*), lack endoderm and most mesoderm (no notochord, prechordal plate or most somites). Yet they form a nervous system, with neural plate and brain regions, lacking only the ventral midline. Neural induction therefore does not require the Nodal-induced organizer mesoderm: dorsal BMP antagonists induced directly by β-catenin, and the lower level of BMP on the dorsal side, suffice ([7.5.17](/7.5.17)).
- Mutants in BMP signalling are dorsalized: *swirl* (*bmp2b*), *snailhouse* (*bmp7*) and the receptor *lost-a-fin*; mutants in its antagonism are ventralized: *chordino* (*chordin*). Their phenotypes are graded, with the neural plate expanded or reduced, as expected for a morphogen gradient read at several thresholds.

### 16.7.2. Chick: The Posterior Marginal Zone and Hensen's Node

The chick embryo starts as a disc on top of the yolk, the blastoderm, with a translucent central area pellucida and a peripheral area opaca. Its posterior side is determined by gravity: as the egg rotates during its passage through the shell gland, the side of the blastoderm that is uppermost becomes posterior (Kochav and Eyal-Giladi, 1971). The posterior marginal zone (PMZ), at the border of the two areas, and Koller's sickle above it, express Vg1 (the chick GDF1/3) and Wnt8c, which together induce *Nodal* in the overlying epiblast and initiate the primitive streak ([7.5.9](/7.5.9)). Each of the two signals is required, and ectopic Vg1 in the marginal zone induces an ectopic streak and axis, but only if Wnt signalling is also present. Under the epiblast, the hypoblast secretes Cerberus, which blocks Nodal and prevents the streak from forming until the hypoblast has been displaced anteriorly by the endoblast; removing the hypoblast allows several streaks to form (Bertocchini and Stern, 2002). The hypoblast thus ensures that only one streak, one axis, forms in a regulative embryo in which every part of the margin is competent.

At the anterior end of the full-length streak lies Hensen's node, which expresses *chordin*, *noggin* and *goosecoid* and, grafted into the area opaca of another embryo, induces a secondary axis from host cells (Waddington, 1933). Its derivatives, like those of the amphibian organizer, are the notochord, the prechordal mesoderm and the floor plate of the neural tube.

### 16.7.3. Mouse: Visceral Endoderm and the Node

The mouse embryo at implantation is a cup, the egg cylinder, of epiblast covered by visceral endoderm, with the extra-embryonic ectoderm at its open end ([7.5.5](/7.5.5), [7.5.9](/7.5.9)). Its first visible asymmetry is in the endoderm: at about E5.5 a group of cells at the distal tip, the distal visceral endoderm (DVE), expressing *Hhex*, *Cer1* (a Cerberus), *Lefty1* and *Dkk1*, migrates to one side within about a day and becomes the anterior visceral endoderm (AVE). The AVE secretes Nodal and Wnt antagonists into the adjacent epiblast; on the opposite side, Nodal from the epiblast, Wnt3 and BMP4 from the extra-embryonic ectoderm induce the primitive streak at about E6.5 (*Wnt3* mutants form no streak). The AVE thus defines anterior and, by restriction, posterior.

At the anterior end of the streak, the node forms at about E7.5. Grafted to another embryo, it induces a secondary axis with neural tube and somites but without the most anterior forebrain (Beddington, 1994). Forebrain induction requires the AVE: mouse embryos lacking *Otx2*, *Lim1* or *Hhex* in the visceral endoderm alone, made by chimeras in which the epiblast is wild type, lack the forebrain. The activity that a single amphibian organizer carries is thus divided in the mouse between two tissues, a head organizer (the AVE, with the anterior mesendoderm) and a trunk organizer (the node), an arrangement that also exists, less separated in time, in the chick (hypoblast and node).

## Questions

1. A *Xenopus* egg is irradiated with UV light at the vegetal pole before cortical rotation. (a) What is the phenotype? (b) Name three ways of rescuing it, and say what each shows about the mechanism.

   **Answer** (a) Radially symmetric ventral embryos without notochord, somites or neural tube, because the microtubule array that drives cortical rotation is destroyed. (b) Tilting the egg 90° (gravity-driven displacement of the cytoplasm) rescues an axis on the side to which the vegetal cytoplasm has moved: the rotation acts by moving determinants, not by the rotation itself. Injecting a stabilizer of β-catenin signalling (mRNA for β-catenin, Huluwa or a dominant-negative GSK3, or lithium) on one side restores an axis there: the determinants act through β-catenin. Transplanting dorsal vegetal cells (a Nieuwkoop centre) from a normal embryo restores the axis: the determinant's output is the inducing activity of the dorsal vegetal cells.

2. In Nieuwkoop's recombination experiment, how do we know that the mesoderm arises from animal-cap cells and not from vegetal cells?

   **Answer** One of the two partners is labelled (by a lineage tracer such as fluorescent dextran or, in Nieuwkoop's experiments, by species-specific differences in pigmentation or nuclei), and the mesodermal tissues of the recombinant are found to contain the label of the animal cap. Since animal caps alone never form mesoderm, the vegetal partner must have induced it.

3. Using Eq. (16.1) with $R_{\mathrm{T}} = 5\,000$ receptors and thresholds of 100 and 300 occupied receptors, compute $c_1/c_2$. How does the ratio change if a cell has only 2 500 receptors, and what does that predict for the response of such cells?

   **Answer** $c_1/c_2 = [100/4900]/[300/4700] = 0.0204/0.0638 = 0.32$, a factor of 3.1. With 2 500 receptors, $c_1 = 100/2400 = 0.0417\,K_{\mathrm{D}}$ and $c_2 = 300/2200 = 0.136\,K_{\mathrm{D}}$: both thresholds roughly double, while their ratio becomes 3.3. Cells with fewer receptors need about twice the Activin concentration for each response, because the cell counts occupied receptors, not the fraction occupied.

4. Explain why Example 16.5.1 calls the organizer's effect "instructive in outcome, permissive in mechanism". Give the molecular experiment that best demonstrates the permissive mechanism.

   **Answer** The graft changes the fate of host cells and imposes a spatial pattern on them (instructive in outcome), but it does so by secreting antagonists that remove ventral signals (BMP, Wnt) from their surroundings, so that cells adopt the fate they take in the absence of those signals (permissive in mechanism). The best demonstration is that a dominant-negative BMP receptor expressed on the ventral side, with no organizer cells at all, induces a secondary axis, and that depletion of BMP2, 4, 7 and ADMP converts the whole ectoderm to neural tissue.

5. In the Nodal–Lefty model, what happens if (a) Lefty diffuses as slowly as Nodal, (b) Lefty is not induced by Nodal but expressed uniformly? Relate (b) to the role of feedback.

   **Answer** (a) With equal diffusion, Lefty accumulates where Nodal is, rather than ahead of it: the inhibition is local, so the Nodal domain is less sharply confined, and depending on parameters signalling is either suppressed near the source or spreads further before Lefty catches up; the range is no longer limited efficiently by a long-range inhibitor. (b) A uniform Lefty level lowers signalling everywhere by the same factor; it can prevent spreading only if it is high enough to weaken signalling at the margin as well. Induction by Nodal makes the inhibition proportional to the activity it must limit (negative feedback), so that a stronger or longer signal produces more inhibitor, which is what makes the range robust to the strength of the source.

6. A *Xenopus* blastula is cut in half along a plane that separates dorsal from ventral. Predict the fate of each half and explain the result for the dorsal half with Proposition 16.6.1.

   **Answer** The ventral half, lacking the organizer and the dorsal determinants, forms a ball of ventral tissues (a "belly piece"). The dorsal half forms a small embryo with all body parts in normal proportion. With an expansion–repression mechanism, the expander (ADMP or Sizzled) accumulates in the half embryo until the BMP activity at its ventral edge reaches the threshold, which compresses the decay length in proportion to the smaller size, $\lambda_{\mathrm{eff}} \approx L/\ln(M_0/T)$, so that the pattern of fates scales.

7. Zebrafish *MZoep* embryos lack nearly all mesoderm and endoderm but form a nervous system. What does this imply about the source of neural-inducing signals, and which experiment would test the implication?

   **Answer** It implies that neural induction does not require the organizer mesoderm induced by Nodal; the inducing activity must come from Nodal-independent sources: β-catenin-dependent expression of BMP antagonists (Chordin via Bozozok) in the dorsal blastoderm and the ectoderm itself, and FGF. Test: remove β-catenin signalling as well (maternal *hwa* or *ichabod* combined with *MZoep*), or deplete Chordin in *MZoep*; if neural tissue is then lost or reduced, the Nodal-independent, β-catenin-dependent antagonists are the neural inducers. Conversely, restoring BMP signalling dorsally (expressing a constitutively active BMP receptor) should convert the remaining neural tissue to epidermis.

8. In the chick, removing the hypoblast leads to multiple primitive streaks. Explain this with the roles of Vg1, Nodal and Cerberus, and say why it implies that the whole marginal zone is competent to form a streak.

   **Answer** Vg1 and Wnt8c in the posterior marginal zone induce Nodal in the epiblast, and Nodal induces the streak; Cerberus from the hypoblast blocks Nodal and delays streak formation until the hypoblast has moved away from the posterior. Without the hypoblast, Nodal signalling is unopposed, and wherever Vg1-like inducing activity reaches a threshold in the marginal zone, a streak can form. That several form shows that many regions of the margin can initiate a streak; normally only the one with the highest Vg1 activity, at the posterior, wins, and the hypoblast and the first streak (which produces its own antagonists, such as Lefty) suppress the others.

9. A mouse node is grafted into the lateral region of a host embryo of the same stage. What structures form, which do not, and why? How would you test whether the missing structures depend on the anterior visceral endoderm?

   **Answer** A secondary neural tube with trunk character, notochord derived from the graft and somites from host mesoderm form, but no forebrain, because forebrain induction requires the anterior signals (Nodal and Wnt antagonists) of the AVE and anterior mesendoderm, which the graft does not provide. Test: co-graft the node with AVE tissue (or anterior mesendoderm), or make chimeras in which the visceral endoderm is mutant for *Otx2* or *Hhex* but the epiblast is wild type; loss of the forebrain in such chimeras, and its induction by AVE plus node, show that the AVE is required.

10. (Quantitative) For the expansion–repression model, suppose $M(0)/T = 20$ in a field of length $L = 1\,\mathrm{mm}$. What is $\lambda_{\mathrm{eff}}$? If the field is halved and $M(0)$ falls by 30 %, what is the new $\lambda_{\mathrm{eff}}$, and how well does the pattern scale?

    **Answer** $\lambda_{\mathrm{eff}} = L/\ln 20 = 1/3.0 = 0.33\,\mathrm{mm}$. For $L = 0.5\,\mathrm{mm}$ and $M(0)/T = 14$, $\lambda_{\mathrm{eff}} = 0.5/\ln 14 = 0.5/2.64 = 0.19\,\mathrm{mm}$. The ratio $\lambda/L$ changes from $0.33$ to $0.38$, by about 13 %, while $L$ changed by a factor of 2: the pattern scales to within the variation of the logarithm of the source amplitude, a weak dependence.
