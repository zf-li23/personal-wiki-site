The **Spemann organizer** of [7.5.16](/7.5.16) does more than pattern the mesoderm. When grafted, it induces a second nervous system from ectoderm that would have become epidermis, and the nervous system it induces is complete, with forebrain at one end and spinal cord at the other. This chapter treats the three axes that are completed during and after **gastrulation**. First, the ectoderm is divided into neural and epidermal parts by the BMP gradient set up by the organizer; the classical experiments and their molecular interpretation, the "default model", are examined together with the evidence that it is incomplete. Second, the neural plate and the paraxial mesoderm acquire anterior–posterior identities from a combination of anterior antagonists and posterior signals, and these identities are encoded by the *Hox* genes, whose clustered organization and **Hox colinearity** we met in *Drosophila* ([7.5.15](/7.5.15)). Third, the left–right axis, the last to be specified, is set by a leftward fluid flow driven by cilia at the node, amplified by an activator–inhibitor system of the kind discussed in [7.5.12](/7.5.12) and [7.5.16](/7.5.16). The later development of the neural tube is in [7.5.18](/7.5.18), that of somites and the vertebral column in [7.5.23](/7.5.23), and the morphogenesis of the asymmetric heart in [7.5.28](/7.5.28).

## 17.1. Neural Induction

### 17.1.1. The Default Model

**Definition 17.1.1 (Neural induction)** **Neural induction** is the process by which cells of the embryonic ectoderm are directed towards neural rather than epidermal fates, in vertebrates by signals from the organizer and its derivatives during gastrulation.

The organizer graft of Spemann and Mangold showed that neural tissue can be induced, and for decades the inducer was sought as an activating signal. A series of experiments on *Xenopus* animal caps reversed the logic.

1. An intact animal cap cultured alone forms epidermis.
2. If the cells of the cap are dissociated in a calcium-free medium, kept apart for several hours and then reaggregated, they form neural tissue (Grunz and Tacke, 1989; Godsave and Slack, 1989). Dissociation should, if anything, remove signals, so the ectoderm must contain a signal that suppresses neural fate and that is diluted when the cells are separated.
3. That signal is BMP: BMP4 protein added to dissociated cells restores epidermis (Wilson and Hemmati-Brivanlou, 1995), and expression in intact caps of a truncated, dominant-negative BMP receptor, or of the organizer's BMP antagonists Noggin, Chordin or Follistatin, makes them neural.
4. BMP4 is expressed throughout the ectoderm of the blastula and is cleared from the dorsal side during gastrulation, where the antagonists act.

**Definition 17.1.2 (The default model)** The **default model** of neural induction holds that ectoderm cells adopt a neural fate unless they receive BMP signalling, which instructs them to become epidermis; the organizer induces the nervous system by secreting BMP antagonists that block this signal (Hemmati-Brivanlou and Melton, 1997).

![The experiments behind the default model, in *Xenopus* animal caps. An intact cap forms epidermis, because BMP4 made by its cells acts on them. Dissociated cells, from which secreted BMP is diluted away, form neural tissue when reaggregated. Dissociated cells given BMP4 protein form epidermis again. An intact cap expressing the BMP antagonists Noggin or Chordin, or a dominant-negative BMP receptor (dnBMPR), forms neural tissue. BMP signalling keeps the ectoderm epidermal; neural is the fate it adopts when BMP signalling is blocked](./plots/neural-induction.py)

The model explains the action of the organizer, the *Drosophila*–vertebrate homology of Dpp/Sog and BMP/Chordin ([7.5.14](/7.5.14)), and the striking result that depleting BMP2, BMP4, BMP7 and ADMP in *Xenopus* converts almost the entire ectoderm into neural tissue ([7.5.16](/7.5.16)). The ectoderm is subdivided by the level of BMP: high BMP gives epidermis, intermediate levels the neural plate border, from which the **neural crest** and the cranial **placodes** arise ([7.5.20](/7.5.20), [7.5.22](/7.5.22)), and low BMP the neural plate.

### 17.1.2. FGF, Wnt and the Limits of the Default Model

In the chick the default model is not sufficient. BMP antagonists such as Chordin, applied to the epiblast outside the normal neural region, do not induce neural tissue, and the early steps of neural induction begin before gastrulation and require FGF (Streit and colleagues, 2000; Wilson and colleagues, 2000). Wnt signalling, conversely, blocks the response of the epiblast to FGF and directs it towards epidermis, so that FGF acting where Wnt is absent initiates neural development, and BMP inhibition is needed later to stabilize it. In *Xenopus* too, FGF contributes, and the two pathways interact at the level of the Smad proteins: MAP kinase activated by FGF phosphorylates the linker region of Smad1 and inhibits it, and GSK3, active where Wnt signalling is absent, adds further phosphorylations that target Smad1 for degradation (Pera and colleagues, 2003; Fuentealba and colleagues, 2007). The BMP signal of a cell is therefore integrated with FGF and Wnt at one molecule, and the "default" state of the ectoderm is itself defined by the balance of the three pathways.

**Example 17.1.1 (Experiment and reasoning: a nervous system without an organizer?)** Zebrafish embryos lacking both Nodal genes (*squint*; *cyclops*), or the Nodal coreceptor maternally and zygotically (*MZoep*), have essentially no mesoderm or endoderm, and no organizer-derived notochord or prechordal plate ([7.5.16](/7.5.16)). They nevertheless form a neural plate and a brain with forebrain, midbrain and hindbrain, lacking mainly the ventral structures that depend on signals from axial mesoderm. Three inferences follow. (1) The mesodermal organizer is not required for neural induction as such. (2) The neural-inducing activity must come from tissue that forms without Nodal: in the fish, dorsal β-catenin, stabilized by maternal Huluwa, activates BMP antagonists such as Chordin in the dorsal blastoderm directly, and the ectoderm itself expresses inhibitors. (3) The test is to remove this source as well: in embryos lacking both Nodal signalling and dorsal β-catenin, the neural plate is lost, and in those, inhibiting BMP signalling by other means restores neural tissue. The experiment separates the inductive role of the organizer from its other roles: making axial mesoderm, which later patterns the ventral neural tube ([7.5.18](/7.5.18)).

## 17.2. Anterior–Posterior Patterning

### 17.2.1. Activation and Transformation

The neural plate induced by BMP antagonists alone expresses anterior markers (*Otx2*, forebrain genes): Noggin-treated animal caps make forebrain-like tissue but no hindbrain or spinal cord. Posterior neural tissue requires additional signals. Two models were proposed long before the molecules were known. Mangold (1933) found that the early dorsal lip induces heads and the late lip trunks and tails, and proposed regionally specific inducers. Nieuwkoop (1952) inserted folds of competent ectoderm vertically into the neural plate of host embryos: the distal tips of the folds, far from the mesoderm, formed forebrain, and the proximal parts, close to the host mesoderm, formed more posterior structures.

**Definition 17.2.1 (Activation–transformation model)** The **activation–transformation model** (Nieuwkoop) holds that neural induction proceeds in two steps: an activating signal induces neural tissue of anterior (forebrain) character, and a transforming signal, graded from posterior to anterior and emitted by the mesoderm, converts it to progressively more posterior identities (midbrain, hindbrain, spinal cord).

The molecular data support a version of this model. Noggin together with FGF induces posterior neural markers in animal caps (Lamb and Harland, 1995); Wnt8, FGF and retinoic acid (RA) each posteriorize neural tissue in a dose-dependent way; and an endogenous gradient of nuclear β-catenin, high posteriorly, exists in the *Xenopus* neural plate (Kiecker and Niehrs, 2001).

### 17.2.2. Head Inducers

The head requires the inhibition of Wnt as well as BMP. Cerberus, a secreted protein of the anterior endomesoderm that binds Nodal, BMP and Wnt, induces ectopic heads when expressed in ventral vegetal cells (Bouwmeester and colleagues, 1996). Dickkopf-1 (Dkk1), which binds the Wnt coreceptor LRP6, together with a BMP antagonist induces complete secondary heads, and antibodies against it reduce the head (Glinka and colleagues, 1998); mouse embryos lacking *Dkk1* lack the structures anterior to the midbrain (Mukhopadhyay and colleagues, 2001). Frzb, a soluble Wnt-binding protein, and Tiki, a protease that inactivates Wnt, add to the effect. Glinka, Niehrs and colleagues summarized the results in a two-inhibitor model: trunk is formed where BMP is inhibited, and head where BMP and Wnt are both inhibited. In birds and mammals the head-protecting antagonists come from the hypoblast and the anterior visceral endoderm ([7.5.16](/7.5.16)), so that the forebrain is protected from posteriorizing signals from the earliest stages.

### 17.2.3. Posteriorizing Signals and a Source–Sink Gradient

Three posterior signals act on the neural plate: Wnts and FGFs from the posterior mesoderm and the region around the primitive streak or blastopore, and retinoic acid. RA is a small lipophilic molecule derived from vitamin A; it is synthesized by retinaldehyde dehydrogenase 2 (Raldh2, encoded by *Aldh1a2*) in the paraxial mesoderm of the trunk and degraded by cytochrome P450 enzymes of the Cyp26 family, expressed in the anterior neural plate and in the tailbud. It acts through nuclear receptors (RAR–RXR) that bind response elements in target genes, among them the 3′ *Hox* genes of the hindbrain ([7.5.18](/7.5.18)). Raldh2-deficient mice lack the posterior hindbrain identities and die early, and excess RA during gastrulation posteriorizes the hindbrain and reduces the forebrain, the basis of the teratogenicity of isotretinoin ([7.5.38](/7.5.38)).

**Formula 17.2.1 (A source–sink gradient)** Let $c(x)$ be the RA concentration along the axis, with synthesis $s(x)$ in the paraxial mesoderm and position-dependent degradation $k(x)$, large where Cyp26 is expressed and small elsewhere:

$$
D\,c'' - k(x)\,c + s(x) = 0, \qquad c'(0) = c'(L) = 0 .
\tag{17.1}
$$

In a region of constant $k$ the solution is a combination of $\mathrm{e}^{\pm x/\lambda}$ with local decay length $\lambda(x) = \sqrt{D/k(x)}$ (plus $s/k$ inside the source): the profile is a plateau over the source and falls off steeply into the sinks, with decay length $\sqrt{D/k_{\mathrm{sink}}}$, much shorter than in tissue without Cyp26. A gradient formed this way is set by the positions of the source and the sinks, not by the diffusion length alone, and the RA level at a given point is buffered: since Cyp26a1 is itself induced by RA, raising the synthesis raises the degradation, which keeps the boundary of the forebrain domain in place (White and colleagues, 2007).

The RA gradient was visualized in living zebrafish embryos with a genetically encoded fluorescent sensor, and has the predicted shape, high in the trunk and falling towards the head and the tail (Shimozono and colleagues, 2013).

**Example 17.2.1 (Two gradients specify four territories)** Combine the RA profile of Eq. (17.1), with source in the paraxial mesoderm and sinks in the anterior neural plate and the tailbud, with a Wnt/FGF gradient decaying exponentially from the posterior end. A nucleus that reads both with fixed thresholds can distinguish four territories: Wnt/FGF and RA both low (forebrain), Wnt/FGF intermediate and RA low (midbrain), RA high (hindbrain and anterior spinal cord), Wnt/FGF high (posterior spinal cord, near the tailbud). The figure shows the outcome of a numerical solution. Two independent gradients give more distinct territories than one, and their combination makes the readout less sensitive to the absolute level of either; in the embryo the boundaries are then sharpened and maintained by cross-repressing transcription factors, *Otx2* anterior and *Gbx2* posterior of the future midbrain–hindbrain boundary, in the same way as the gap genes of the fly ([7.5.15](/7.5.15)).

### 17.2.4. The Posterior Body from a Stem-Cell Zone

The anterior neural plate is induced in a single step during gastrulation, but the spinal cord and the trunk and tail mesoderm are made progressively. Near the node and the anterior primitive streak, in the caudal lateral epiblast and later in the tailbud, lies a population of neuromesodermal progenitors that coexpress the neural gene *Sox2* and the mesodermal gene *Brachyury* (*T*). Clonal analysis in the mouse showed that single cells of this population give rise to both spinal cord and paraxial mesoderm (Tzouanacou and colleagues, 2009). Wnt and FGF keep the cells in the progenitor state and bias them towards mesoderm, and the transcription factor Tbx6, which represses *Sox2*, is required for the mesodermal choice: mice lacking *Tbx6* form ectopic neural tubes in place of the paraxial mesoderm, three neural tubes in the posterior trunk. As the axis extends, the progenitors leaving the zone at later times acquire more posterior identities, the temporal colinearity of the *Hox* genes (17.3) converting time into position.

## 17.3. The Vertebrate Hox Genes

### 17.3.1. Four Clusters, Thirteen Paralogous Groups

Mammals have four clusters of **Hox genes**, *Hoxa* to *Hoxd* (on human chromosomes 7, 17, 12 and 2), each about 100–150 kb long, with 39 genes in all. The genes fall into 13 paralogous groups by sequence similarity: group 1 corresponds to *labial*, groups 2, 4 and 5 to *proboscipedia*, *Deformed* and *Sex combs reduced*, groups 6–8 to the central *Antennapedia*-like genes, and groups 9–13 to *Abdominal-B*. No cluster has all 13: each lost some members after the two rounds of genome duplication early in vertebrate evolution that produced four clusters from one, and teleost fish, after a further duplication, have seven. Within each cluster the genes have the same orientation, and the **homeodomain** sequences of paralogues are more similar to each other than to their neighbours in the same cluster.

The clusters obey colinearity in two senses. Genes at the 3′ end are expressed with more anterior anterior limits in the neural tube and paraxial mesoderm (spatial colinearity), and are activated earlier, in the posterior epiblast during gastrulation (temporal colinearity); in the mouse, *Hox1* genes begin to be expressed at about E7.0 and *Hox13* genes only in the tailbud. Activation is accompanied by the progressive removal of the repressive Polycomb mark H3K27me3 from the 3′ to the 5′ end of the cluster (Soshnikova and Duboule, 2009), and, in the *Hoxa* cluster, by a sequence of enhancers that respond successively to Wnt, to the homeodomain factor Cdx2 and to signals of the late axis. Because cells that leave the progenitor zone keep the *Hox* combination they had when they left, the temporal sequence is converted into the anterior–posterior sequence of identities (17.2.4). Moving a 3′ gene to the 5′ end of the cluster delays its activation and shifts its domain posteriorly, so in vertebrates, unlike *Drosophila*, colinearity depends on the position of a gene within an intact cluster.

### 17.3.2. The Hox Code of the Vertebral Column

Each vertebra is made by the paraxial mesoderm of a pair of somites ([7.5.23](/7.5.23)), and its identity (cervical, thoracic with ribs, lumbar without ribs, sacral fused to the pelvis) is predicted by the combination of *Hox* genes expressed there: a *Hox* code (Kessel and Gruss, 1991). Loss of function causes **homeotic transformations**, usually towards anterior identities.

- Mice lacking *Hoxc8* have an extra pair of ribs on the first lumbar vertebra and other thoracic vertebrae transformed towards the identity of their anterior neighbours (Le Mouellic and colleagues, 1992).
- The paralogous genes are partly redundant, and their roles appear only when all members of a group are removed. Mice lacking *Hoxa10*, *Hoxc10* and *Hoxd10* have no lumbar vertebrae: ribs grow from every vertebra posterior to the thorax, so *Hox10* genes repress rib formation. Mice lacking all three *Hox11* genes have no sacrum: the sacral vertebrae take a lumbar identity (Wellik and Capecchi, 2003).
- RA given at the time of *Hox* activation shifts the expression boundaries and the vertebral identities, and mice lacking RA receptors show the reverse transformations.

The vertebrate *Hox* code is thus organized by groups rather than single genes, with redundancy inside each group, and with a degree of posterior prevalence: in many tissues the most posterior gene expressed dominates. *Hox* genes also pattern the hindbrain rhombomeres ([7.5.18](/7.5.18)) and, in a second phase of colinear expression, the limbs ([7.5.32](/7.5.32)). Human mutations are rare and affect the latter structures: *HOXD13* mutations cause synpolydactyly, *HOXA13* mutations the hand–foot–genital syndrome, and *HOXA1* mutations a syndrome of hindbrain malformation, deafness and abnormal eye movements.

## 17.4. Left–Right Asymmetry

### 17.4.1. Situs and Its Disorders

Vertebrates are bilaterally symmetric on the outside but not inside. In humans the heart points to the left, the stomach and spleen lie on the left, most of the liver on the right, the right lung has three lobes and the left two, the gut coils in a fixed sense, and even the brain is lateralized.

**Definition 17.4.1 (Situs)** The arrangement of the asymmetric organs relative to the left–right axis is the **situs**. The normal arrangement is situs solitus; its complete mirror image is **situs inversus** (situs inversus totalis); any other arrangement, with the organs neither normally nor completely reversed, is **heterotaxy**, often in the form of isomerism, in which both sides take the morphology of one side: right isomerism (both lungs trilobed, no spleen, asplenia syndrome) or left isomerism (both lungs bilobed, several spleens, polysplenia syndrome).

Situs inversus totalis occurs in about 1 in 10 000 people and is usually without symptoms, because the organs remain in correct relation to each other. Heterotaxy, of similar frequency at birth, is serious: the relations between the heart chambers and the great vessels are disturbed, it accounts for about 3 % of congenital heart disease and often requires surgery. Many affected genes are those of the pathway described below, among them *ZIC3* (X-linked heterotaxy), *NODAL*, *CFC1* (Cryptic) and *LEFTY2*, and, frequently, genes for ciliary motility.

### 17.4.2. Four Steps

Left–right asymmetry is established after the other two axes, and it is defined relative to them: a left side can be specified only once anterior and dorsal are known. In all vertebrates studied it develops in four steps (Hamada and colleagues, 2002).

1. Symmetry is broken in a transient left–right organizer at the end of gastrulation: the node in mouse, Kupffer's vesicle in zebrafish, the gastrocoel roof plate in *Xenopus*, Hensen's node in the chick.
2. A left-sided signal is transferred from the organizer to the left lateral plate mesoderm.
3. In the left lateral plate the signal is amplified and maintained: *Nodal*, its inhibitor *Lefty2* and the transcription factor *Pitx2* are expressed there and not on the right, while *Lefty1* in the midline prevents spreading to the right.
4. Organs develop asymmetrically under the control of Pitx2 and other factors: the heart tube loops to the right, the gut rotates, the lungs form different numbers of lobes ([7.5.28](/7.5.28), [7.5.30](/7.5.30)).

### 17.4.3. Breaking Symmetry with Cilia

The mouse node at E7.5–E8 is a shallow pit on the ventral surface of the embryo, about 50 µm across, whose 200–300 cells each bear a single motile **cilium** about 3–5 µm long. These cilia have the 9+0 arrangement of microtubule doublets typical of primary cilia but carry axonemal **dyneins** and rotate, clockwise when viewed from their tip (from the ventral side), at about 10 Hz. Their rotation drives a leftward flow of the fluid in the pit (Nonaka and colleagues, 1998). Four experiments link the flow to asymmetry.

- Mice lacking the kinesin subunit Kif3b have no node cilia and no flow, and their asymmetry is randomized: half have normal situs, half reversed or heterotaxy.
- In *iv* mutants, which lack the left–right dynein Dnah11, the node cilia are immotile, there is no flow, and situs is random.
- Embryos cultured under an imposed rightward flow of medium strong enough to override the cilia develop reversed situs; an imposed leftward flow rescues the asymmetry of *iv* mutants (Nonaka and colleagues, 2002).
- Humans with primary ciliary dyskinesia, a group of disorders of ciliary motility (mutations in *DNAH5*, *DNAI1* and many other genes), have chronic respiratory infections, infertility and, in about half of cases, situs inversus (Kartagener syndrome): the asymmetry is randomized, not reversed, exactly as in *iv* mice. This is a **ciliopathy** of motile cilia; ciliopathies of the **primary cilium** affect Hedgehog signalling instead ([7.3.14](/7.3.14)).

How does a rotation produce a directed flow? A cilium rotating upright would only swirl the fluid. The node cilia are tilted towards the posterior, by about 40° (Nonaka and colleagues, 2005; Okada and colleagues, 2005), and the tilt combined with the presence of the cell surface breaks the symmetry between the two halves of the stroke. In the half of each rotation in which the cilium is far from the surface it moves to the left; in the other half it is close to the surface and moves to the right. Near a no-slip wall the fluid is held back by the wall, so the rightward half stroke moves less fluid than the leftward one.

**Formula 17.4.1 (Pumping by a tilted rotating cilium)** Let a cilium of length $\ell$ rotate with angular velocity $\omega$ on a cone of half-angle $\psi$ whose axis is tilted posteriorly by $\theta$ from the normal to the cell surface. A point force $F$ parallel to a plane no-slip wall at height $h$ above it transports fluid at a volume rate proportional to $Fh/\mu$ ($\mu$ the viscosity; Blake, 1971), and the force exerted by the cilium on the fluid is proportional to its velocity. The net transport along the left–right axis $x$ is therefore proportional to the time average of $v_x h$ over one rotation. With the tip at height $h(t) = \ell(\cos\psi\cos\theta + \sin\psi\sin\theta\sin\omega t)$ and velocity $v_x(t) = -\ell\omega\sin\psi\sin\omega t$,

$$
\langle v_x h \rangle = -\tfrac12\,\ell^2\omega\,\sin^2\psi\,\sin\theta ,
\tag{17.2}
$$

which vanishes for an upright cilium ($\theta = 0$), grows with the tilt, and is limited by the condition that the cilium not strike the surface, $\theta + \psi \le 90°$.

**Proof** The tip position is $\boldsymbol{r}(t) = \ell[\cos\psi\,\boldsymbol{n} + \sin\psi(\cos\omega t\,\boldsymbol{e}_1 + \sin\omega t\,\boldsymbol{e}_2)]$, where $\boldsymbol{n} = (0, \sin\theta, \cos\theta)$ is the rotation axis in coordinates (left–right $x$, anterior–posterior $y$, height $z$), $\boldsymbol{e}_1 = (1, 0, 0)$ and $\boldsymbol{e}_2 = \boldsymbol{n}\times\boldsymbol{e}_1 = (0, \cos\theta, -\sin\theta)$ up to the sense of rotation. Then $x(t) = \ell\sin\psi\cos\omega t$, $v_x = -\ell\omega\sin\psi\sin\omega t$, and $z(t) = \ell[\cos\psi\cos\theta \mp \sin\psi\sin\theta\sin\omega t]$, the sign depending on the sense of rotation relative to the tilt. The product $v_x z$ contains a term $-\ell^2\omega\sin\psi\cos\psi\cos\theta\sin\omega t$, which averages to zero, and a term $\pm\ell^2\omega\sin^2\psi\sin\theta\sin^2\omega t$, whose average is $\pm\tfrac12\ell^2\omega\sin^2\psi\sin\theta$. For clockwise rotation seen from the tip and a posterior tilt, the high half of the stroke moves to the left, which gives Eq. (17.2) with the leftward sign. $\square$

The model makes three predictions that experiments confirmed: the direction of flow is fixed by the sense of rotation (set by the chirality of the dynein–microtubule machinery) and the direction of tilt (set by the posterior positioning of the basal body, which depends on planar cell polarity); cilia with the wrong tilt pump little; and reversing the tilt would reverse the flow. Hydrodynamic simulations of many cilia (Cartwright, Piro and Tuval, 2004; Smith, Blake and Gaffney, 2008) confirm the result quantitatively. The chirality of the molecules is thus converted into a left–right difference of the embryo by three geometric facts: the anterior–posterior and dorsal–ventral axes already exist, the cilia rotate in one sense, and the wall breaks the symmetry of the stroke.

How the flow is sensed is debated. One proposal is that the flow carries a morphogen, or membrane-bound vesicles containing signalling molecules, to the left side of the node. The other is that the perinodal crown cells sense the flow mechanically with immotile primary cilia containing the cation channel polycystin-2 (Pkd2), so that only the cilia on the left are bent in the appropriate direction (the two-cilia model, McGrath and colleagues, 2003). Recent work favours the mechanosensory model: immotile crown-cell cilia respond to the bending caused by flow, and the response depends on Pkd2 (Katoh and colleagues, 2023). The output is known: on the left side of the node the flow causes degradation of the mRNA of *Dand5* (also called *Cerl2*), a Nodal antagonist expressed in the crown cells, and so increases Nodal activity on the left.

Other vertebrates use the same principle with different organs. In zebrafish, the dorsal forerunner cells form Kupffer's vesicle, a fluid-filled sac of ciliated cells in the tailbud, in which the cilia drive a counterclockwise flow (seen dorsally); in *Xenopus*, the ciliated gastrocoel roof plate in the roof of the archenteron produces a leftward flow. The chick, and apparently the pig, have no motile cilia at the node; there, cells around Hensen's node move leftward and displace the domains of *Shh* (left) and *Fgf8* (right) asymmetrically (Gros and colleagues, 2009), and asymmetric activity of an H⁺/K⁺-ATPase and membrane potential have been implicated at earlier stages. The cilia-driven flow is therefore one solution, widespread but not universal, to the problem of converting molecular chirality into a left–right signal.

### 17.4.4. Amplification by Nodal and Lefty

The small left–right difference at the node is amplified in the lateral plate mesoderm into all-or-none expression of *Nodal* on the left. Nodal (with its partner Gdf1) is transferred from the node to the left lateral plate, where it induces its own expression and that of *Lefty2* and *Pitx2*; Lefty2 is a feedback inhibitor that diffuses further than Nodal ([7.5.16](/7.5.16)); Lefty1, expressed in the midline floor plate and notochord, forms a barrier that keeps Nodal from crossing to the right. Mice lacking *Lefty1* express *Nodal* on both sides and develop left isomerism (Meno and colleagues, 1998). Nakamura, Hamada and colleagues (2006) showed by experiment and modelling that the system works by self-enhancement and lateral inhibition (SALI): even when the input from the node is made only slightly unequal, the output is fully lateralized.

**Formula 17.4.2 (Self-enhancement and lateral inhibition)** Along a line $x \in [-1, 1]$ from the right to the left lateral plate, with the midline at $x = 0$, let $N$ and $L$ be Nodal and Lefty, $S = N^2/(K^2 + N^2)\cdot 1/(1 + (L/K_L)^2)$ the signal, $\chi(x)$ the indicator of the lateral plate and $\mu(x)$ that of the midline. With a transient input $u(x, t)$ from the node,

$$
N_t = D_N N_{xx} + \chi(x)\bigl[\alpha S + u(x, t)\bigr] - N, \qquad L_t = D_L L_{xx} + \beta\chi(x) S + \gamma\mu(x) - \delta L, \qquad D_L > D_N .
\tag{17.3}
$$

The figure shows the three consequences. A 10 % stronger input on the left produces full *Nodal* expression on the left and none on the right: the left side, slightly ahead, makes Lefty that reaches the right and suppresses its self-enhancement before it starts. Without the midline source of Lefty, Nodal is expressed on both sides. With exactly equal inputs, the outcome is symmetric; in a real embryo, noise then decides at random, which is the randomization seen when flow is absent.

Pitx2, a homeodomain transcription factor (its *Pitx2c* isoform in the lateral plate), executes much of the asymmetric morphogenesis: ectopic Pitx2 on the right randomizes heart looping and gut coiling in *Xenopus* and chick, and in mice lacking it the lungs show right isomerism. In the heart, the second heart field and the asymmetric growth of the tube are affected ([7.5.28](/7.5.28)).

### 17.4.5. Invertebrates

The chirality of a snail shell is determined by the mother's genotype (a maternal effect, [7.4.20](/7.4.20)) and becomes visible at the third cleavage, when the four micromeres are rotated clockwise (dextral) or anticlockwise (sinistral) relative to the macromeres in the spiral cleavage of the embryo ([7.5.6](/7.5.6)). In the pond snail *Lymnaea stagnalis* the gene responsible encodes a formin, a regulator of actin; embryos lacking it after genome editing cleave sinistrally and develop into sinistral snails (Abe and Kuroda, 2019). In dextral snails *nodal* and *Pitx* are expressed on the right side of the embryo, and inhibiting Nodal signalling makes the shell straight rather than coiled (Grande and Patel, 2009). Molecular chirality, amplified by a Nodal–Pitx module, thus underlies asymmetry in molluscs as in vertebrates, although the side on which Nodal acts differs. In the ascidian *Ciona*, the embryo rotates within the chorion at the neurula stage, and contact of its left side with the chorion triggers *nodal* and *Pitx2* expression in the left epidermis (Nishide and colleagues, 2012). In sea urchins *nodal* is expressed on the right. The use of Nodal and Pitx2 in left–right patterning is very old in the deuterostome and lophotrochozoan lineages, but the side, and the way the initial bias is produced, vary.

## Questions

1. In the dissociation experiment of 17.1.1, why is it essential that the cells be kept apart for several hours before reaggregation, and what control shows that the neural fate is not caused by the calcium-free medium itself?

   **Answer** BMP4 is secreted continuously; only if the cells remain dispersed long enough, while still competent, is its local concentration around each cell reduced below the level that maintains epidermis. Brief dissociation followed by immediate reaggregation gives epidermis. The control is to add BMP4 protein to dissociated cells in the same medium: they form epidermis, which shows that the medium does not cause neural development and that the absence of BMP is what matters.

2. Explain why the default model, if strictly true, would predict that BMP antagonists alone should induce neural tissue in the chick epiblast outside the neural plate, and how the failure of this prediction led to the role of FGF and Wnt.

   **Answer** If neural were simply the fate of ectoderm without BMP, any cell deprived of BMP would become neural. Chordin applied outside the normal neural region does not induce neural markers, so BMP inhibition is not sufficient there. Further experiments showed that cells of the epiblast must first receive FGF, before gastrulation, and that Wnt in the lateral epiblast makes them unresponsive to FGF and directs them towards epidermis; BMP inhibition then stabilizes a neural fate already initiated. The neural "default" holds only for cells that already have the appropriate FGF and low Wnt history, as in the *Xenopus* animal cap.

3. Nieuwkoop's folds formed forebrain at their tips and posterior structures near their base. Interpret this result in terms of a posteriorizing gradient, and predict the result of the experiment in an embryo in which Wnt signalling has been blocked throughout the neural plate.

   **Answer** The whole fold was activated (made neural, anterior by default), and the transforming signal from the host mesoderm decreased with distance from it, so the proximal parts, close to the mesoderm, received more and became posterior, the distal parts little and remained forebrain. With Wnt signalling blocked (for example by Dkk1 or Frzb throughout the plate), the transforming signal would be weakened: the folds, and the host neural plate itself, would form more anterior tissue, with forebrain extended posteriorly and hindbrain and spinal cord reduced.

4. For the source–sink model of Formula 17.2.1, take $D = 1\,\mathrm{\mu m^2\,s^{-1}}$, $k = 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$ outside the sinks and $k = 10^{-2}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$ in the Cyp26 domain. Compare the decay lengths, and explain why the Cyp26 sink makes the anterior boundary of the RA domain sharp.

   **Answer** Outside the sinks, $\lambda = \sqrt{1/10^{-4}} = 100\,\mathrm{\mu m}$; in the Cyp26 domain, $\lambda = \sqrt{1/10^{-2}} = 10\,\mathrm{\mu m}$, about one cell diameter. RA that diffuses from the trunk into the anterior neural plate is degraded within one or two cell diameters of the edge of the Cyp26 domain, so the concentration drops steeply there and the boundary of RA-responsive cells coincides with the edge of the sink rather than with a slowly decaying tail of the gradient.

5. Why do mice lacking a single *Hox10* gene have only mild defects, whereas mice lacking all three *Hox10* genes lack the lumbar region? What does this imply about how the vertebrate *Hox* code should be studied?

   **Answer** The three paralogues are expressed in overlapping domains with the same anterior limits and encode very similar homeodomain proteins, so each can largely substitute for the others (redundancy); a phenotype appears only when the combined dose falls below a threshold. The code is therefore organized by paralogous groups, and the function of a group is revealed only by removing all its members (compound mutants), or by gain-of-function experiments; single knockouts underestimate the roles of vertebrate *Hox* genes.

6. Using Eq. (17.2), compare the leftward pumping of a cilium with cone half-angle 30° at tilts of 20° and 40°. Why can the tilt not simply be increased to 90° to maximize the flow?

   **Answer** $\sin^2 30° = 0.25$; $\sin 20° = 0.342$, $\sin 40° = 0.643$. Pumping is proportional to $0.25 \times 0.342 = 0.086$ and $0.25 \times 0.643 = 0.161$, nearly twice as much at 40°. The tilt is limited by the geometry: the cilium must not strike the cell surface during its rotation, which requires $\theta + \psi \le 90°$, here $\theta \le 60°$. The formula also assumes the force acts at the tip and the wall effect is proportional to height, approximations that fail when the cilium sweeps close to the surface.

7. Predict the situs of (a) mice lacking Kif3b, (b) *iv* mice placed in an imposed leftward flow, (c) wild-type mice in an imposed rightward flow, (d) mice lacking Lefty1. Explain each in terms of the four steps.

   **Answer** (a) Random situs, about half normal and half reversed or heterotactic: without cilia there is no flow (step 1 missing), and the later steps amplify random fluctuations. (b) Normal situs in most embryos: the imposed flow substitutes for the missing ciliary flow; the cilia are needed only to make it. (c) Reversed situs: the external flow overrides the ciliary one and the signal goes to the right lateral plate. (d) Left isomerism: Nodal, transferred normally to the left lateral plate, spreads to the right because the midline barrier (step 3) is missing, so both sides develop left character.

8. Patients with primary ciliary dyskinesia have situs inversus in about half of cases. Explain why the fraction is one half rather than all or none, and why patients with situs inversus totalis and normal cilia are usually healthy, whereas those with heterotaxy are not.

   **Answer** Without functional node cilia there is no directed flow, so the choice of the side on which Nodal is amplified is made by chance, with equal probabilities for left and right; a patient therefore has a 50 % chance of normal and nearly 50 % of reversed situs (with a small fraction of discordant, heterotactic arrangements). In situs inversus totalis all organs are mirrored together, so their connections, notably of the heart to the great vessels, are correct. In heterotaxy, organs are lateralized discordantly or isomerically, which disturbs the connections between heart chambers and vessels and causes congenital heart disease, as well as asplenia or polysplenia.

9. In the SALI model, what happens if Lefty diffuses as slowly as Nodal? Use Eq. (17.3) to explain why a long-range inhibitor is needed to convert a 10 % bias into all-or-none expression.

   **Answer** If $D_L = D_N$, Lefty made on the left stays on the left, where it limits Nodal locally, but does not reach the right side, which then develops its own Nodal self-enhancement from its own, slightly smaller input: both sides express Nodal, perhaps to different levels, and the bias is not converted into an all-or-none difference. Lateralization requires that the side that wins send inhibition to the other side faster than that side can amplify itself: a long-range inhibitor, together with self-enhancement of the activator, is the condition for winner-take-all between the two sides.

10. (Open question) Chick embryos establish left–right asymmetry without motile cilia at the node. Propose how one could test, in the chick, whether the leftward cell movement around Hensen's node is a cause or a consequence of the asymmetric expression of *Shh* and *Fgf8*.

    **Answer** Approaches: (i) block the cell movements (for example by inhibiting myosin II or the relevant guidance cues locally, or by physically restraining the tissue) before asymmetric expression appears and ask whether *Shh* and *Fgf8* still become asymmetric; (ii) track cells by live imaging and lineage labels to determine whether the asymmetric domains arise by displacement of cells already expressing the genes (consequence of movement) or by asymmetric activation in place; (iii) impose a reversed movement, or graft the node reversed, and see whether the expression follows; (iv) make *Shh* or *Fgf8* expression symmetric and ask whether the movement still occurs. If asymmetric expression requires the movements and the movements do not require the expression, the movements are upstream; the H⁺/K⁺-ATPase and membrane-potential asymmetry would then be tested as their cause.
