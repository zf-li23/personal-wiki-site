The **morphogen** gradients of the previous chapter ([7.5.11](/7.5.11)) are imposed by an asymmetric source. Many patterns arise instead from a field of cells that is initially uniform, or nearly so: the spacing of hair follicles and feathers, the periodic condensations that become digits, the head of a regenerating *Hydra*, the stripes of a zebrafish. Such a field organizes itself. Turing (1952) showed that two substances reacting and diffusing at different rates can destabilize a uniform state, and Gierer and Meinhardt (1972) identified the biological principle behind it: local self-enhancement coupled with long-range inhibition. This chapter develops the theory in the order in which its questions arise. When can chemistry create a pattern at all? When does diffusion destabilize a uniform state? What stationary patterns then form, and how do they depend on the size of the tissue? How can a weak, imprecise bias select an orientation? The linear theory of the two-species instability is derived in [2.5.7](/2.5.7#sec-7.5.2) and the **Brusselator** is treated in [6.3.18](/6.3.18); here we take those results as known and concentrate on the nonlinear patterns and their developmental meaning. The chapter ends with lateral inhibition by direct cell contact, the mechanism introduced in [7.3.24](/7.3.24), and with the applications that the following chapters on hair follicles ([7.5.22](/7.5.22)), digits ([7.5.32](/7.5.32)), left–right asymmetry ([7.5.17](/7.5.17)) and regeneration ([7.5.35](/7.5.35)) will use.

## 12.1. Self-Organization in Hydra and Hydromedusae

**Definition 12.1.1 (Self-Organization)** A developing system shows **self-organization** when a spatial pattern arises from interactions among initially equivalent components, so that the information specifying the pattern (its spacing, number or orientation) is generated by the dynamics rather than inherited from a preexisting template.

The freshwater polyp *Hydra* is the classical test case. A small piece of the body column regenerates a complete, small animal with the original polarity: the end that was nearer the head forms the head. The same piece of column can thus become a head or a foot, depending on what it is attached to. Grafts of pieces onto a host column, threaded on a hair, answer more specific questions. Polarity reversal takes a long time, but head determination is fast. A small amount of head tissue induces a head nearby in the column if the original head is far away, and buds appear only outside a certain range from the head. The inhibition appears to be diffusible, needing time to spread from a newly made head. Its static range can be measured with grafts like those in the figure: grafting column piece 1 onto a host column 1-2-3-4 gives no head at the junction, 1-2 does; a graft H-1-2 (with its head) gives no second head, H-1-2-3 does.

![Axial grafts in Hydra. A host column 1-2-3-4 receives pieces of column from the upper body. A second head forms at the junction when the graft is large enough (1-2 but not 1 alone) and far enough from an existing head (three column levels but not two): head formation is self-enhancing and inhibited at a distance](./plots/hydra-grafts.py)

The results summarize what a mechanism must do: short-range activation, long-range diffusible inhibition, and perfect regulation of the pattern to the size of the organism. Today the head activator is identified with Wnt3 signaling through **β-catenin** in the hypostome, which activates its own expression, and part of the inhibition with the transcription factor Sp5, which Wnt signaling induces and which represses *Wnt3* (Vogg and colleagues, 2019); the biology of *Hydra* regeneration is described in [7.5.35](/7.5.35). In the activator–inhibitor models of Section 12.5, with a weak activator source decreasing from head to foot, the inhibitor in non-activated tissue obeys $\dot h=-\lambda h+\nu h''$ and decays as $\mathrm{e}^{-(\lambda/\nu)^{1/2}x}$: the range of inhibition is $(\nu/\lambda)^{1/2}$. Small animals behave as the idealized switch point predicts (one head, proportioned to size); in axial grafts the final number of heads is set largely by the initial pattern of activator source, a large enough internal peak giving a pattern of three half-cycles (two heads); and after removal of a head from the middle of a graft, the time to form a new one follows the diffusional delay derived for the slime mould in [7.5.13](/7.5.13#sec-13.2).

Egg size provides a second, older observation. Across the hydromedusae and related cnidarians, large eggs (*Liriope*, about $0.15\,\mathrm{mm}$) develop directly into medusae, medium eggs (*Gonionemus*, about $0.07\,\mathrm{mm}$) form a planula that settles as a polyp and buds off medusae, and very small eggs develop only after taking up material from nurse cells. The idea that a single parameter, the size of the system, selects among developmental routes is natural for self-organizing mechanisms, in which the number of activated regions depends on the size of the field (Section 12.11); the comparative data are, however, qualitative, and they mix species whose life cycles differ for many reasons.

## 12.2. Chemical Kinetics: Why Closed Systems Make No Pattern

### 12.2.1. Autocatalysis and a Two-Box Model

Let an activator $X$ catalyze its own production from a precursor $Y$ and decay, $2X+Y\to3X$ and $X\to B$, with unit rate constants. At fixed $Y$, $\dot X=X^2Y-X$ has the stable state $X=0$ and the unstable state $X=1/Y$, and the solution $X=1/(Y+C\mathrm{e}^t)$ either decays or explodes in finite time. Supplying the precursor from an inexhaustible source, $A\to Y$, gives $\dot Y=A-X^2Y$ and the unique stationary point $X_0=A$, $Y_0=1/A$. Its Jacobian

$$
J=\begin{bmatrix}1&A^2\\-2&-A^2\end{bmatrix},\qquad 2\Omega=1-A^2\pm\sqrt{(1-A^2)^2-4A^2},
$$
shows that the state is stable for $A>1$, with damped oscillations for $1<A<1+\sqrt2$ and monotone decay above.

Now couple two such boxes, $(X_1,Y_1)$ and $(X_2,Y_2)$, by exchange of the precursor at rate $D$, $\dot Y_1=A-X_1^2Y_1+D(Y_2-Y_1)$ and symmetrically. In terms of the sums and differences, $x=\frac12(X_1-X_2)$ and $y=\frac12(Y_1-Y_2)$, the difference mode at the uniform state has the Jacobian $\begin{bmatrix}1&A^2\\-2&-A^2-D\end{bmatrix}$, whose determinant is $A^2-D$. As soon as $D>A^2$ the determinant is negative, one eigenvalue is positive, and the boxes become different: a box with a little more activator draws precursor from its neighbour faster than diffusion can return it. Exchange, which one expects to equalize, has destabilized the uniform state.

### 12.2.2. The Linearized Reaction–Diffusion System

For $N$ species with concentrations $\xi_\alpha$, reactions $F_\alpha(\xi)$ and diffusion coefficients $D_\alpha$,

$$
\dot\xi_\alpha=F_\alpha(\xi)+D_\alpha\Delta\xi_\alpha .
$$
Linearizing about a uniform steady state and writing perturbations as $\mathrm{e}^{\Omega t}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{r}}$, $\Omega$ must be an eigenvalue of

$$
M(k^2)=J-k^2\operatorname{diag}(D_1,\dots,D_N),\qquad J_{\alpha\beta}=\frac{\partial F_\alpha}{\partial\xi_\beta}.
\tag{12.1}
$$
On an interval of length $L$ with no-flux boundaries the spatial factor is $\cos kx$ with $k=p\pi/L$, $p=0,1,2,\dots$

**Proposition 12.2.1** If $J$ is stable (all eigenvalues have negative real parts), $M(k^2)$ is also stable for every $k$ in each of the following cases: (a) all $D_\alpha$ are equal; (b) $J$ is symmetric; (c) $J$ is sign-stable, that is, stable for every matrix with the same sign pattern.

**Proof** (a) $M=J-k^2DI$ shifts every eigenvalue by $-k^2D$. (b) For symmetric matrices the largest eigenvalue is the maximum of the Rayleigh quotient, and subtracting a positive semidefinite diagonal matrix cannot raise it. (c) Sign-stability is characterized by sign conditions on the diagonal entries, on products $J_{\alpha\beta}J_{\beta\alpha}$ and on longer cycles (Quirk and Ruppert); subtracting a positive diagonal matrix keeps every sign, so $M$ has the same sign pattern as $J$. $\square$

Diffusion-driven instability therefore needs unequal diffusion and a Jacobian that is neither symmetric nor sign-stable: some species must enhance itself.

### 12.2.3. Thermodynamics: the Gibbs Function Is a Lyapunov Function

Why are patterns not found in a beaker at equilibrium? In an **ideal dilute solution** at fixed temperature and pressure the **chemical potential** of a solute is $\mu_X=k_{\mathrm{B}}T\ln(X/X_0)$, and the **Gibbs energy** per unit volume is

$$
\phi=\mu_0+k_{\mathrm{B}}T\sum_X X\Big(\ln\frac X{X_0}-1\Big).
$$
At equilibrium $\mathrm{d}\phi=\sum\mu_X\,\mathrm{d}X=0$ for every change allowed by a reaction; for $X+2A\rightleftharpoons B$ this gives $\mu_X+2\mu_A=\mu_B$, the **law of mass action** $XA^2/B=X_0A_0^2/B_0$. With the kinetics $\dot X=-kA^2X+k'B$ (and $\dot A=2\dot X$, $\dot B=-\dot X$), agreement of kinetic and thermodynamic equilibrium requires $k'/k=X_0A_0^2/B_0$.

**Theorem 12.2.1** For a **closed system** of reactions with mass-action kinetics whose rate constants satisfy these relations for every reaction, $\phi$ never increases: $\dot\phi\le0$, with equality only at equilibrium.

**Proof** For the reaction above, $\dot\phi=\dot X(\mu_X+2\mu_A-\mu_B)=-k'B_0k_{\mathrm{B}}T\,(y-z)(\ln y-\ln z)$ with $y=\frac{XA^2}{X_0A_0^2}$ and $z=\frac B{B_0}$, and $(y-z)(\ln y-\ln z)\ge0$, with equality only if $y=z$. Each reaction contributes a term of this form. $\square$

$\phi$ is a **Lyapunov function** ([2.4.11](/2.4.11)): a closed system with a unique equilibrium approaches it. Diffusion changes nothing, since exchange between neighbouring volumes $A_1\rightleftharpoons A_2$ is just another monomolecular reaction with equal forward and backward rates. Patterns need a system in which the separate reactions cannot all be in equilibrium simultaneously: **detailed balance** (equality of each forward and backward rate, [6.3.18](/6.3.18)) must fail. Three idealizations break it.

1. A reaction loop $A\rightleftharpoons B\rightleftharpoons X\rightleftharpoons A$ with rate constants $k,k'$, $l,l'$, $m,m'$: detailed balance requires $k'l'm'=klm$, the same product of rate constants around the loop in both directions. If this fails, a steady state with a circulating current exists, but it is not the minimum of any Gibbs function.
2. A source and a sink: $A\to X\rightleftharpoons Y\to B$ with $A$ and $B$ held fixed and the return reactions absent (for example $A\to\infty$, $k\to0$ at fixed $kA$). The intermediates reach a steady state $\bar Y=kA/m$, $\bar X=(l'+m)kA/(lm)$ with a net flux through the system. Living tissue, with synthesis and degradation of every molecule, is of this kind.
3. An infinite rate constant: in $A\rightleftharpoons B+C$, $C\rightleftharpoons X+Y$ with $l\to\infty$, the intermediate $C$ is consumed as soon as it is made, and the net reaction $A\to B+X+Y$ has no reverse.

### 12.2.4. Eliminating Fast Intermediates

The last case also shows how effective **rate laws** arise. If $X+E\rightleftharpoons E^*$ is fast with **dissociation constant** $K=k'/k$ and the total enzyme $E+E^*=E_0$ is conserved, then $E^*=E_0X/(K+X)$ at every moment. For $X+E\rightleftharpoons E^*\rightleftharpoons Y+E$ this gives

$$
\dot Y=\frac{lE_0X-Kl'E_0Y}{K+X},
$$
the reversible form of the **Michaelis–Menten equation** ([7.1.9](/7.1.9)); since $\dot X+\dot Y=-\dot E^*$, the total substrate obeys $\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\big(X+\frac{E_0X}{K+X}\big)=-\dot Y$. A competing inhibitor $I$ with $I+E\rightleftharpoons E^{**}$ (dissociation constant $K^*$) adds a term to the denominator, $E^*=E_0(X/K)/(1+I/K^*+X/K)$, which is **competitive inhibition**. Saturating and inhibited terms of this kind appear in every model below; they are the shadows of fast binding steps.

## 12.3. Linear Instability: Turing's Condition

For two species $a$ (activator) and $h$ (inhibitor) in one dimension,

$$
a_t=f(a,h)+\varepsilon a_{xx},\qquad h_t=g(a,h)+\nu h_{xx},
\tag{12.2}
$$
and a uniform steady state stable to uniform perturbations ($f_a+g_h<0$, $f_ag_h-f_hg_a>0$), the growth rate $\Omega(k^2)$ of a mode $\cos kx$ satisfies $(\Omega+\varepsilon k^2-f_a)(\Omega+\nu k^2-g_h)=f_hg_a$.

**Theorem 12.3.1 (Turing Condition)** The uniform state of (12.2) is unstable to some mode $k>0$ if and only if

$$
\nu f_a+\varepsilon g_h>2\sqrt{\varepsilon\nu\,(f_ag_h-f_hg_a)} .
$$
This requires $f_a>0$ (self-enhancement of the activator) together with $g_h<0$ and $\nu/\varepsilon$ large enough: the inhibitor must diffuse faster.

The proof is in [2.5.7](/2.5.7#sec-7.5.2): the trace of $M(k^2)$ stays negative, and instability occurs when the determinant $\varepsilon\nu k^4-(\varepsilon g_h+\nu f_a)k^2+\det J$ becomes negative. Beyond threshold a band of wave numbers grows; which one grows fastest determines the initial spacing.

**Formula 12.3.1 (Fastest-Growing Mode)** With $P=-f_hg_a>0$ and $\nu>\varepsilon$, the real growth rate $\Omega(k^2)$ is maximal at

$$
k^2_{\max}=\frac{(\nu+\varepsilon)\sqrt{P/(\varepsilon\nu)}+g_h-f_a}{\nu-\varepsilon},\qquad \Omega_{\max}=\frac{\nu f_a-\varepsilon g_h-2\sqrt{\varepsilon\nu P}}{\nu-\varepsilon}.
$$

**Proof** Let $u=\Omega-f_a+\varepsilon k^2$ and $w=\Omega-g_h+\nu k^2$, so that $uw=-P$. Differentiating with respect to $k^2$ at a maximum ($\mathrm{d}\Omega/\mathrm{d}k^2=0$) gives $\varepsilon w+\nu u=0$. Hence $w=-\nu u/\varepsilon$ and $u^2=\varepsilon P/\nu$; the branch with the larger $\Omega$ has $u=-\sqrt{\varepsilon P/\nu}$, $w=\sqrt{\nu P/\varepsilon}$. Equating the two expressions for $\Omega$, $f_a-\varepsilon k^2-\sqrt{\varepsilon P/\nu}=g_h-\nu k^2+\sqrt{\nu P/\varepsilon}$, gives $k^2_{\max}$; substituting back gives $\Omega_{\max}$. $\square$

**Example 12.3.1 (Turing's Example)** Turing's model, in suitable units, is

$$
f=\frac{a(h-1)}{16}-\frac34,\qquad g=1-\frac{ah}{16},
$$
with the uniform state $a_0=h_0=4$. Then $f_a=\frac3{16}$, $f_h=\frac14$, $g_a=g_h=-\frac14$, so $f_a+g_h=-\frac1{16}$ and $\det J=\frac1{64}$: the well-mixed system is stable. The Turing condition reads $\frac3{16}r-\frac1{4r}>\frac14$ with $r=\sqrt{\nu/\varepsilon}$, that is $3r^2-4r-4>0$, $r>2$: the inhibitor must diffuse more than four times faster than the activator. Chemically the model corresponds to $X+Y\to B$ (degradation of the activator by the inhibitor), $C+X+Y\to2X+Y$ (activation catalyzed by both), $A\to Y$ (supply of inhibitor), $X\to M$ (decay), and a removal of $X$ at a constant rate through an enzyme $D$ saturated in the limit $D\to0$, $k\to\infty$ ($X+D\rightleftharpoons F\to G+D$). Here the inhibitor is also a cofactor, an "activator–substrate" ambiguity common in real systems.

## 12.4. Inhomogeneous Steady States

Linear theory says that a pattern starts; the nonlinear terms decide which stationary pattern it becomes. Since the growth may end in a stationary state, an oscillation or an irregular state, we concentrate on stationary states, which give the cells a constant message to interpret.

### 12.4.1. The Energy Integral

Instability needs $\nu/\varepsilon$ large. Take the extreme $\varepsilon=0$, and suppose that $f(a,h)=0$ has a unique solution $a=A(h)$. The stationary problem becomes

$$
\nu h''+g(A(h),h)=0,\qquad h'(0)=h'(L)=0,
\tag{12.3}
$$
and multiplying by $h'$ gives the energy integral

$$
\frac\nu2h'^2+V(h)=E,\qquad V(h)=\int^hg(A(s),s)\,\mathrm{d}s,
$$
the motion of a particle of mass $\nu$ in the potential $V$, with $x$ as time ([7.5.11](/7.5.11#sec-11.3.4) treats the one-component case). Starting with zero velocity at $x=0$, the particle must stop again at $x=L$; one traversal between the turning points $h_0(E)$ and $h_1(E)$ takes

$$
\chi(E)=\sqrt{\frac\nu2}\int_{h_0(E)}^{h_1(E)}\frac{\mathrm{d}h}{\sqrt{E-V(h)}} .
$$

**Proposition 12.4.1** Let $\chi_{\min}=\min_E\chi(E)$. If $L<\chi_{\min}$, only the uniform state exists. If $\chi_{\min}<L<2\chi_{\min}$, a pattern with one half-cycle (one high and one low region) exists. In general a pattern with $p$ half-cycles exists when $L=p\chi(E)$ has a solution, and several $(p,E)$ are possible in a long tissue; which one is realized depends on the initial perturbation.

**Example 12.4.1 (The Second Turing System)** For Turing's example $f=0$ gives $A(h)=12/(h-1)$, and $g(A(h),h)=1-\frac{3h}{4(h-1)}$, so

$$
V(h)=\int_4^h\Big(1-\frac{3s}{4(s-1)}\Big)\mathrm{d}s=\frac{h-4}4-\frac34\ln\frac{h-1}3 .
$$
Near the minimum, $V(4)=V'(4)=0$ and $V''(4)=\frac3{4\cdot9}=\frac1{12}$, so $V\approx(h-4)^2/24$ and

$$
\chi(E)\approx\sqrt{\frac\nu2}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}h}{\sqrt{E-(h-4)^2/24}}=\sqrt{\frac\nu2}\sqrt{24}\int_{-1}^1\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\sqrt{1-y^2}}=\pi\sqrt{12\nu},
$$
independent of $E$; this is $\chi_{\min}$, since $\chi$ increases with $E$. With $\nu=\frac14$ and $L=60$, about eleven half-cycles fit. Because $\chi(E)$ is nearly flat at small $E$, a narrow band of linearly unstable modes cannot select a definite number of half-cycles.

### 12.4.2. Absolute and Relative Size Regulation

Two extremes are biologically important. In absolute regulation the activated region has a fixed size, set by diffusion and degradation, whatever the size of the tissue; in relative regulation it is a fixed fraction of the tissue. Which one a model gives is read off the shape of $V$ near its turning points. Measure $x_0$ to the left and $x_1$ to the right of the potential minimum $V_M$ at $h_M$. Changing variable from $h$ to $V$ in the first half-cycle,

$$
x_1(V)=\sqrt{\frac\nu2}\int_{V_M}^V\frac{h_1'(V')\,\mathrm{d}V'}{\sqrt{E-V'}}=\sqrt{2\nu}\,h_1'(E)\Big[(E-V_M)^{1/2}-(E-V)^{1/2}\Big]+\cdots,
$$
so that the extent of the high-$h$ region is $x_1(E)\approx\sqrt{2\nu}\,h_1'(E)(E-V_M)^{1/2}$, and similarly $x_0(E)\approx\sqrt{2\nu}\,(-h_0'(E))(E-V_M)^{1/2}$, with $\chi=x_0+x_1$.

**Proposition 12.4.2** (a) The high region has a size independent of $L$ (absolute size regulation) if $h_1'(E)(E-V_M)^{1/2}$ is constant, that is, if $V$ is parabolic, $V=V_M+(h-h_M)^2/c^2$. The profile itself, however, then depends on $E$ through $(h-h_M)/(E-V_M)^{1/2}$; nearly absolute regulation of the whole profile requires $V$ to reach a maximum $\bar h$, near which a small change of $E$ allots an unlimited length of tissue. (b) The two regions keep a fixed ratio (relative size regulation) if $x_1/x_0=h_1'(E)/(-h_0'(E))$ is constant. With power laws $h_0=b_0-c_0(V-V_M)^{q_0}$ and $h_1=b_1+c_1(V-V_M)^{q_1}$ this requires $q_0=q_1$, and if the power laws hold down to $V_M$ (so that $b_0=b_1=h_M$) a derivative of $V$ is discontinuous at $V_M$; the pattern is then invariant in the rescaled variables $x^*=x/(E-V_M)^{q_1-1/2}$ and $h^*=(h-h_M)/(E-V_M)^{q_1}$.

The limiting case $q_0=q_1=1$, a potential with a kink, gives perfect relative regulation, as in [7.5.11](/7.5.11) (Proposition 11.3.1). The kink arises naturally from a switch between two activator branches (Section 12.5).

### 12.4.3. Stability of the Nonuniform State

Is the pattern (12.3) stable for the dynamics $\dot a=f(a,h)$, $\dot h=g+\nu h''$? Since (12.3) is the Euler–Lagrange equation of $\int(\frac\nu2h'^2-V)\,\mathrm{d}x$, try a functional $\mathcal{L}=\int\big(\frac\nu2h'^2-W(a,h)\big)\mathrm{d}x$.

**Theorem 12.4.1** If $W$ can be chosen so that $4fW_a\ge(W_h-g)^2$, with equality only where $f=0=W_h-g$, then $\mathcal{L}$ decreases along solutions until the steady state (12.3) is reached, which is therefore stable. In particular, with $W=w(a)+\int^hg(a,s)\,\mathrm{d}s$, so that $W_h=g$ and $W_a=\int_{h(a)}^hg_a(a,s)\,\mathrm{d}s$ (where $h(a)$ solves $f(a,h)=0$), the condition is $fW_a>0$ whenever $h\ne h(a)$.

**Proof** Using the equations and integrating by parts with no-flux ends,

$$
\dot{\mathcal{L}}=\int\big[\nu h'\dot h'-\dot aW_a-\dot hW_h\big]=-\int\big[(g+\nu h'')^2+(W_h-g)(g+\nu h'')+fW_a\big],
$$
and completing the square,

$$
\dot{\mathcal{L}}=-\int\Big[g+\nu h''+\tfrac12(W_h-g)\Big]^2\mathrm{d}x-\int\Big[fW_a-\tfrac14(W_h-g)^2\Big]\mathrm{d}x\le0 .\qquad\square
$$

**Definition 12.4.1 (Edelstein Switch)** The **Edelstein switch** is the activator–inhibitor model

$$
f(a,h)=\frac{1+\alpha h^2}{1+h^2}-a,\qquad g(a,h)=\lambda a-\mu h,\qquad\alpha>1,
$$
whose uniform steady states number one for small or large $\mu/\lambda$ and three (two stable) for an intermediate range, so that a small change of $\lambda$ switches the operating point between low and high $a$.

**Example 12.4.2** For the Edelstein switch, $A(h)=\frac{1+\alpha h^2}{1+h^2}$ and $g(A(h),h)=\lambda\alpha-\frac{\lambda(\alpha-1)}{1+h^2}-\mu h$, so $V(h)=\lambda\alpha h-\frac\mu2h^2-\lambda(\alpha-1)\tan^{-1}h$. Its two maxima mean that, by adjusting parameters, an unlimited amount of tissue can be allotted to either extreme of $h$; periodic patterns are generic unless carefully avoided. The nonuniform state is stable: $h(a)=\big(\frac{a-1}{\alpha-a}\big)^{1/2}$ and $\lambda^{-1}W_a=h-\big(\frac{a-1}{\alpha-a}\big)^{1/2}$, so

$$
fW_a=\lambda\Big(\frac{1+\alpha h^2}{1+h^2}-a\Big)\Big(h-\Big(\frac{a-1}{\alpha-a}\Big)^{1/2}\Big),
$$
and since $\frac{1+\alpha h^2}{1+h^2}-a=\alpha-a-\frac{\alpha-1}{1+h^2}$ is increasing in $h$ and vanishes at $h=h(a)$, both factors have the same sign: $fW_a\ge0$ (for $1\le a\le\alpha$, near $f=0$).

## 12.5. Multistability and the Gierer–Meinhardt Model

**Definition 12.5.1 (Gierer–Meinhardt Model)** The **Gierer–Meinhardt model** is the activator–inhibitor system

$$
a_t=\frac{p_aa^2}{(1+k_aa^2)h}-\gamma_aa+\mu_a+D_a\Delta a,\qquad h_t=p_ha^2-\gamma_hh+\mu_h+D_h\Delta h,
$$
in which the activator $a$ enhances its own production cooperatively (with saturation $k_a$), the inhibitor $h$, produced in proportion to $a^2$, suppresses it, both decay, $\mu_a$, $\mu_h$ are basal sources and $D_h\gg D_a$. A scaled form with $\mu_h=0$ is

$$
f(a,h)=\frac{a^2}{h(1+a^2)}-\frac a2+\mu,\qquad g(a,h)=a^2-\lambda h .
\tag{12.4}
$$

The saturation is the Michaelis–Menten shadow of a fast binding step (Section 12.2.4). Unlike the systems of Section 12.4, $f(a,h)=0$ now has one or three solutions: for $\mu=0$, $A_0(h)=0$ and $A_{1,2}(h)=\frac1h\mp\sqrt{\frac1{h^2}-1}$ for $h\le1$. Suppose the system jumps from the lowest to the highest branch at a switch point $h_s$. Along such a path $V=\int g\,\mathrm{d}h$ is piecewise linear near $h_s$, $V=V_s-\lvert g_0\rvert(h-h_s)$ below and $V_s+g_1(h-h_s)$ above, with $g_0=-\lambda h_s$ and $g_1=A_2(h_s)^2-\lambda h_s$.

**Proposition 12.5.1 (Relative Regulation at a Switch Point)** With $V_s=0$ and the switch at $x=0$, the steady pattern is

$$
h=h_s+\frac E{g_1}-\frac{g_1}{2\nu}\Big(x-\frac{\sqrt{2\nu E}}{g_1}\Big)^2\ (x>0),\qquad h=h_s-\frac E{\lvert g_0\rvert}+\frac{\lvert g_0\rvert}{2\nu}\Big(x+\frac{\sqrt{2\nu E}}{\lvert g_0\rvert}\Big)^2\ (x<0),
$$
on a tissue of length $L=\sqrt{2\nu E}\big(\frac1{g_1}+\frac1{\lvert g_0\rvert}\big)$. The activated region ($h>h_s$, $a=A_2$) occupies the fraction $\lvert g_0\rvert/(g_1+\lvert g_0\rvert)$ of the tissue, whatever $L$.

**Proof** On each side $\nu h''=-V'$ is a constant, $-g_1$ or $+\lvert g_0\rvert$; the solutions pass through $h_s$ at $x=0$ with $\frac\nu2h'^2=E$ and have turning points at $x=\sqrt{2\nu E}/g_1$ and $-\sqrt{2\nu E}/\lvert g_0\rvert$. $\square$

As $E$ grows, $V$ departs from linearity, first acquiring a horizontal tangent, so that the pattern shifts towards absolute regulation in large tissues; if a vertical tangent came first, there would be a maximum period, and larger tissues would have to repeat the pattern.

Where is the switch point? A jump of $a$ needs the activator diffusion that we neglected. With $\varepsilon a''+f(a,h)=0$ and small $\varepsilon$, $h$ (and even $h'$, since $\nu h''=-g$ is bounded) is continuous across the jump, and in the stretched variable $x=x_s+\varepsilon^{1/2}X$ the inner problem is

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}^2a}{\mathrm{d}X^2}+f(a,h_s)=0,\qquad \frac{\mathrm{d}a}{\mathrm{d}X}=0\ \text{at}\ a=A_0(h_s),\ A_2(h_s) .
$$

**Theorem 12.5.1 (Equal-Area Rule)** The inner solution exists only if

$$
\int_{A_0(h_s)}^{A_2(h_s)}f(a,h_s)\,\mathrm{d}a=0,
$$
that is, if the potential $U(a)=\int^af(s,h_s)\,\mathrm{d}s$ has equal maxima at the two outer branches (an "anti-Maxwell" rule). The jump must occur, because the middle branch is unstable on the inner scale: perturbations there grow at $\Omega=f_a-\varepsilon k^2>0$ for $k\to0$ on that scale.

**Proof** The inner equation has the integral $\frac12(\mathrm{d}a/\mathrm{d}X)^2+U(a)=E'$; the boundary conditions require $U(A_0)=E'=U(A_2)$. $\square$

**Example 12.5.1** For (12.4) with $\mu=0$, $\int_0^{a_s}\Big(\frac{a^2}{h_s(1+a^2)}-\frac a2\Big)\mathrm{d}a=\frac{a_s-\tan^{-1}a_s}{h_s}-\frac{a_s^2}4=0$, and on the upper branch $h_s=2a_s/(1+a_s^2)$. Hence

$$
\tan^{-1}a_s=a_s\,\frac{1+a_s^2/2}{1+a_s^2},\qquad a_s=1.515,\quad h_s=0.920 .
$$
The switch point is close to $h=1$, where the three branches merge, which suggests that the system can be steered by small external signals. A quick estimate replaces $f$ by its generic cubic, for which the equal-area condition places the middle root at the mean of the outer ones, $A_1\approx\frac12(A_0+A_2)$: $\frac1h-\sqrt{h^{-2}-1}=\frac12\big(\frac1h+\sqrt{h^{-2}-1}\big)$ gives $h=(8/9)^{1/2}=0.943$.

![The switch point of the Gierer-Meinhardt model (12.4) with mu = 0 and lambda = 1. A: the three branches of the activator null-cline; the jump from A0 to A2 occurs at h_s. B: at h_s the areas of f(a, h_s) above and below the axis between A0 and A2 cancel (a_s = 1.515, h_s = 0.920). C: the resulting potential V(h) has a kink at h_s, the condition for relative size regulation](./plots/gm-switch.py)

Near the switch point all three-state systems behave alike when $\varepsilon\to0$: an activated plateau on $A_2(h_s)$ to one side of the switch, $A_0$ on the other, going over to the corresponding branches $A(h)$ for larger excursions.

### 12.5.1. Amplification of a Weak Source and Polarity

So far the orientation of the pattern (which end is activated) was arbitrary. Gierer and Meinhardt's point was that a reaction-diffusion system is a clever amplifier, converting a weak, erratic, graded signal into a strong, ordered pattern. Let the activator source $\mu(x)$ of (12.4) be weak and graded, and linearize about the source-free uniform state, with $\delta a=\delta h=0$ at $t=0$. Take the **Laplace transform** in time ([2.5.6](/2.5.6)) and the cosine transform in space, $\hat a(k,\Omega)=\int_0^L\cos(kx)\,\tilde a(x,\Omega)\,\mathrm{d}x$ with $k=p\pi/L$. Then

$$
\begin{bmatrix}\hat a\\\hat h\end{bmatrix}=\frac{\hat\mu(k)}{\Omega\det(\Omega I-M(k^2))}\begin{bmatrix}\Omega-g_h+\nu k^2\\g_a\end{bmatrix},
$$
with $M(k^2)$ as in (12.1). The poles are at $\Omega=0$ and at the eigenvalues of $M(k^2)$, and the long-time behaviour is dominated by the eigenvalue of largest real part, real and positive for $k^2$ between the roots of $\det M(k^2)=0$,

$$
\frac12\Big(\frac{g_h}\nu+\frac{f_a}\varepsilon\Big)\pm\Big[\frac14\Big(\frac{g_h}\nu-\frac{f_a}\varepsilon\Big)^2+\frac{f_hg_a}{\varepsilon\nu}\Big]^{1/2},
$$
with its maximum given by Formula 12.3.1. Expanding about this pole, each mode grows as $\hat\mu(k)\,\mathrm{e}^{\Omega_{\max}(k^2)t}$ times a fixed factor.

**Proposition 12.5.2** If only one mode $k=p\pi/L$ lies in the unstable band, only the component $\hat\mu(k)$ of the source is amplified, and its sign fixes the polarity of the pattern. If the source is later removed or altered, the new source competes with the exponentially amplified old one, which dominates while the linear approximation holds. If several modes lie in the band with nearly equal growth rates, the early pattern depends on the details of $\mu(x)$; as $\varepsilon\to0$ the band widens without limit and many modes, and boundary layers at both ends, are excited at once.

For a tissue a little longer than one half-period, the slightest gradient of the source thus decides which end becomes the head. Beyond the linear stage, with $\varepsilon\to0$, $f(a,h)+\mu(x)=0$ and $\nu h''+g=0$; if $a=A(h,\mu)$ is single-valued, the energy becomes position dependent, $\frac\nu2h'^2+\int_{h_0(x)}^hg(s,x)\,\mathrm{d}s=E(x)$, with

$$
E'(x)=\int_{h_0(x)}^hg_x(s,x)\,\mathrm{d}s\approx-\mu'(x)\int_{h_0}^h\frac{g_a(A(s),s)}{f_a(A(s),s)}\,\mathrm{d}s,
$$
using $A(h,\mu)\approx A(h)-\mu/f_a$ for small $\mu$. In auto- and cross-catalytic systems $g_a/f_a<0$ ($-\lambda$ for the Edelstein model, $-h/(h-1)$ for Turing's), so for $\mu'>0$ the energy increases where $h$ rises with $x$ and decreases where it falls. Since $\chi_{\min}=\pi\big(\nu/V_M''\big)^{1/2}$ then rises rapidly for a trajectory with $h'>0$, patterns with many half-cycles are suppressed and high-$h$ regions are placed where the source is large.

**Proposition 12.5.3 (Switch Points in a Graded Source)** In the three-state case with a weak source $\mu$, the switch point moves according to

$$
\frac{\partial h_s}{\partial\mu}=-\frac{A_1-A_0}{\int_{A_0}^{A_1}f_h(a,h_s)\,\mathrm{d}a},\qquad \frac{\partial A_i}{\partial\mu}=-\frac{1+f_h^{(i)}\,\partial h_s/\partial\mu}{f_a^{(i)}},
$$
(here $A_1$ denotes the upper outer branch), and on each side of a switch point $\nu h''=\gamma_{0,1}(x)$ with $\gamma(x)=-g+\mu(x)\,(g_a-J\,\partial h_s/\partial\mu)/f_a$, $J=f_ag_h-f_hg_a$. For a pattern starting low at $x=0$ with switch points $x_1<\dots<x_p$, the conditions $h'(L)=0$ and $h(x_{i+1})-h(x_i)=\frac{\partial h_s}{\partial\mu}(\mu(x_{i+1})-\mu(x_i))$ become, for $p=1$, $\int_0^{x_1}\gamma_0+\int_{x_1}^L\gamma_1=0$, and for $p>1$

$$
\int_0^{x_1}\gamma_0+\int_{x_1}^{x_2}\frac{x_2-y}{x_2-x_1}\gamma_1\,\mathrm{d}y=\nu\frac{\partial h_s}{\partial\mu}\frac{\mu(x_2)-\mu(x_1)}{x_2-x_1},
$$
with analogous conditions for the later switch points (right sides $\approx0$ in the interior and $\approx-\nu\,\partial_\mu h_s\,\mu'$ at the last switch for linear $\mu$).

**Proof** Differentiate $f(A_0,h_s)+\mu=f(A_1,h_s)+\mu=\int_{A_0}^{A_1}(f(a,h_s)+\mu)\,\mathrm{d}a=0$ with respect to $\mu$ at $\mu=0$: the end-point terms of the integral vanish because the integrand is zero there, leaving $\int_{A_0}^{A_1}(f_h\,\partial_\mu h_s+1)\,\mathrm{d}a=0$. The equation for $h$ on each branch follows from $g(A_i(h_s+\partial_\mu h_s\,\mu),\dots)$ to first order in $\mu$. Integrating $\nu h''=\gamma$ from $0$ and using $\int_a^b\int_a^xf\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_a^b(b-y)f(y)\,\mathrm{d}y$ gives the conditions; successive subtraction separates them. $\square$

Take the typical signs $f_h<0$ (so $\partial h_s/\partial\mu>0$), $f_a<0$, $g_a>0$, $g_h<0$ on the outer branches, $-g_0>0$, $-g_1<0$ and $g_1/(-g_0)\gg1$. Then as $\mu$ increases, $\gamma_1$ becomes more negative while $\gamma_0$ starts positive and decreases. Three regimes follow: for a steep source ($\mu'$ large) neither the $p=1$ nor the $p>1$ conditions can be met, and there is no switch point; for a moderate slope ($\nu\mu'>\max_x\int_0^x\gamma_0>0$) there is exactly one switch, a single activated region at the high end of the source; for a shallow source several switch points become possible, and in the limit $\varepsilon\to0$ arbitrarily many. A finite $\varepsilon$ limits the resolution.

**Formula 12.5.1 (Minimal Half-Period)** With the cubic approximation $f=-\alpha(a-A_2)^3+\beta(a-A_2)$ near the switch, $A_1-A_2=\sqrt{\beta/\alpha}$, $f_a(A_1)=-2\beta$, and $\frac\varepsilon2(\mathrm{d}a/\mathrm{d}x)^2\big|_{A_2}=\int_{A_2}^{A_1}f\,\mathrm{d}a=\frac{\beta^2}{4\alpha}$, the transition between the outer branches needs at least

$$
\Delta x=\frac{A_1-A_0}{\mathrm{d}a/\mathrm{d}x}=\frac{2\sqrt{\beta/\alpha}}{\beta/\sqrt{2\varepsilon\alpha}}=4\Big(\frac{\varepsilon}{-f_a'}\Big)^{1/2},
$$
where $f_a'=f_a(A_1,h_s)$: no half-cycle can be shorter than this.

## 12.6. Weakly Nonlinear Dynamics

Developing tissues rarely wait for a steady state, so the dynamics after the linear stage matters. Write the deviation from the unstable uniform state as $q=(a-\bar a,h-\bar h)$, so that

$$
\dot q=Mq+\Delta(q)+\boldsymbol{D}\Delta q,\qquad \boldsymbol{D}=\operatorname{diag}(D,D'),
$$
where $M$ is the Jacobian and $\Delta(q)$ collects the terms of second and higher order. Let $\psi_0$ be the dominant unstable mode, $(M+\boldsymbol{D}\Delta)\psi_0=\lambda_0\psi_0$ with no-flux boundaries, and let $\varphi_k$ be the dual eigenfunctions of the adjoint operator, normalized by $\int\varphi_k^*\psi_{k'}=\delta_{kk'}$. Writing $q=q_0(t)\psi_0+Q$ with $Q$ orthogonal to $\psi_0$ and projecting,

$$
\dot q_0=\lambda_0q_0+\int\varphi_0^*\,\Delta\big(q_0\psi_0+Q\big)\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{r}\approx\lambda_0q_0+\int\varphi_0^*\,\Delta(q_0\psi_0)\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{r},
$$
an "equivalent linearization" applied to space.

**Example 12.6.1** In the Gierer–Meinhardt depletion (activator–substrate) model, $\dot a=c+a^2h-\gamma a+Da''$, $\dot h=c'-a^2h+D'h''$, the substrate $h$ is consumed by the production of $a$. The uniform state is $\bar a=(c+c')/\gamma$, $\bar h=c'\gamma^2/(c+c')^2$, and

$$
M=\begin{bmatrix}\gamma\frac{c'-c}{c'+c}&\frac{(c'+c)^2}{\gamma^2}\\-2\gamma\frac{c'}{c'+c}&-\frac{(c'+c)^2}{\gamma^2}\end{bmatrix},\qquad \Delta=\big(\bar h\,\delta a^2+2\bar a\,\delta a\,\delta h+\delta a^2\delta h\big)\begin{bmatrix}1\\-1\end{bmatrix}.
$$
The dominant mode is $\psi_0\propto\cos kx$, $k=\pi K/L$. The quadratic terms contain only $\cos^2kx$, which has no $\cos kx$ component, and $\cos^3kx=\frac34\cos kx+\frac14\cos3kx$, so only the cubic term survives the projection and

$$
\dot q_0=\lambda_0q_0-Bq_0^3,
$$
a pitchfork normal form: the amplitude saturates at $\sqrt{\lambda_0/B}$ when $B>0$. The projection neglects the second-order modes ($\cos0$ and $\cos2kx$) that the quadratic terms generate; in a consistent expansion (centre-manifold reduction) these modify $B$ at the same order and can even change its sign, making the bifurcation subcritical, as in the **chemotaxis** model of [7.5.13](/7.5.13).

## 12.7. Static Size Regulation: Summary

The two generic potentials of stationary, one-component, no-flux reaction-diffusion are a smooth minimum, which gives absolute size regulation, and a slope discontinuity, which gives relative size regulation as long as the curvature stays small ([7.5.11](/7.5.11#sec-11.3.4)). Biochemical examples of the first are common; the second needs a switch. Limiting **two-component systems** supply it: with $D=0$, $f(a,h)=0$ may have several branches $A_i(h)$, of which those with $f_a<0$ are stable without diffusion; on the intermediate branch, $\mathrm{d}a/\mathrm{d}h=-f_h/f_a$. Whatever branch is followed, the inhibitor obeys $g(A(h),h)+D'h''=0$, the no-flux conditions on $h$ imply those on $a$, and if the intermediate branch (with diffusion) is unstable, $h$ jumps between the outer branches at a switch point determined by the equal-area rule (Theorem 12.5.1). The resulting energy integral $\frac{D'}2h'^2+\varphi(h)=K$, with $\varphi'=g(A(h),h)$, has exactly the kinked form needed for relative regulation. The saturating production $a^2/(1+k_aa^2)$ of the Gierer–Meinhardt model, which creates the third branch, is the Michaelis–Menten form of a fast enzymatic step $S+E\rightleftharpoons C\to E+P$ with $K_M=k'/k$, $C=E_0S/(K_M+S)$ and $\dot P=lE_0S/(K_M+S)$. Size regulation is therefore not a fine-tuned property: any self-enhancing reaction that saturates, coupled to a fast inhibitor, regulates the proportions of its pattern.

## 12.8. Prepatterns and Mode Selection

**Definition 12.8.1 (Prepattern)** A **prepattern** is a weak, graded nonuniformity, such as a spatially varying source density, that does not itself specify the final pattern but biases which mode of a self-organizing system grows, and so fixes the orientation or position of the pattern.

It is too easy, in one dimension, to build a model that holds any desired monotone profile $\tilde a(x)$. Define $F$ by $F(\tilde a(x))=\tilde a''(x)$ and take

$$
\dot a=-F(a)+\sigma(a-h)+a'',\qquad \dot h=-\nu F(h)+\nu\sigma(a-h)+\nu h'' .
$$
Then $a=h=\tilde a$ is a steady state. Expanding perturbations in the eigenfunctions $\psi_l$ of $-F'(\tilde a(x))+\mathrm{d}^2/\mathrm{d}x^2$ (eigenvalues $l$, bounded above), each mode obeys the matrix $\begin{bmatrix}l+\sigma&-\sigma\\\nu\sigma&\nu l-\nu\sigma\end{bmatrix}$, with characteristic equation $\Omega^2+[(\nu-1)\sigma-(\nu+1)l]\Omega+\nu l^2=0$. For $\nu>1$ and $\sigma>\frac{\nu+1}{\nu-1}l_{\max}$ all roots have negative real part: the profile is stable. A Bicoid-like gradient can thus be made without any source, but it does not regulate: its shape is fixed in absolute coordinates.

Why, then, is the Turing mechanism so widespread in models? Because it does not arise from turning on diffusion ($D=0\to D>0$, with reactions independent at every point). The idealized initial state is fully correlated in space, effectively $D=\infty$; as interactions become local, $D$ becomes finite and the uniform state destabilizes. The instability is invariant under $(\delta a,\delta h)\to(-\delta a,-\delta h)$ and allows several modes, whose amplitudes are unknown; the prepattern and the initial fluctuations decide among them.

**Proposition 12.8.1 (Mode Selection by a Prepattern)** Let $\dot a=f+\mu(\boldsymbol{r})+D\Delta a$, $\dot h=g+D'\Delta h$ in $\Omega$ with no-flux boundaries, and expand deviations and the source in the Neumann eigenfunctions $\psi_k$ ($\Delta\psi_k=-k^2\psi_k$). Each component $\delta c_k=(\delta a_k,\delta h_k)$ obeys $\dot{\delta c_k}=M_k\delta c_k+\mu_k(1,0)^{\mathsf T}$, so

$$
\delta c_k(t)=\mathrm{e}^{tM_k}\delta c_k(0)+\big(\mathrm{e}^{tM_k}-I\big)M_k^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}\mu_k\\0\end{bmatrix}.
$$
If $\lambda_k^\pm$ are the eigenvalues of $M_k$ with $\operatorname{Re}\lambda_k^+\ge\operatorname{Re}\lambda_k^-$, then for large $t$

$$
\delta c_k(t)\approx\frac{M_k-\lambda_k^-}{\lambda_k^+-\lambda_k^-}\Big[\delta c_k(0)+\frac{\mu_k}{\lambda_k^+}\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\end{bmatrix}\Big]\mathrm{e}^{t\lambda_k^+} .
$$
The effective amplitude is $\delta a_k(0)+\mu_k/\lambda_k^+$: when the growth rates of several modes are nearly equal, the mode most strongly represented in the source wins, even if its growth rate is slightly smaller.

**Proof** For a $2\times2$ matrix with distinct eigenvalues, any function satisfies $p(M)=p(\lambda^-)\frac{M-\lambda^+}{\lambda^--\lambda^+}+p(\lambda^+)\frac{M-\lambda^-}{\lambda^+-\lambda^-}$ (Sylvester's formula). Applying it to $p=\mathrm{e}^{tz}$ and $p=(\mathrm{e}^{tz}-1)/z$ and keeping the growing exponential gives the result. $\square$

## 12.9. Thermodynamic Stability Criteria

Nonequilibrium thermodynamics offers a stability criterion of its own. For a unit reaction $k$ with stoichiometric coefficients $\nu_{k\alpha}$ (negative for consumed species), the **Gibbs energy** changes by $\Delta\phi_k=k_{\mathrm{B}}T\sum_\alpha\nu_{k\alpha}\ln(\xi_\alpha/A_\alpha)$, and with reaction velocities $u_k$, $\dot\phi=k_{\mathrm{B}}T\sum_ku_k\sum_\alpha\nu_{k\alpha}\ln(\xi_\alpha/A_\alpha)$, where diffusion counts as a monomolecular reaction. Glansdorff and Prigogine called a state stable if every nearby state gives up free energy, $\sum\delta u_k\nu_{k\alpha}\delta\ln\xi_\alpha<0$; since $F_\alpha=\sum_ku_k\nu_{k\alpha}$, this is $\sum\delta F_\alpha\,\delta\ln\xi_\alpha<0$, the condition of negative **excess entropy production** ([6.3.18](/6.3.18)). Written as a quadratic form,

$$
\frac1{\xi_\alpha}\frac{\partial F_\alpha}{\partial\xi_\beta}+\frac1{\xi_\beta}\frac{\partial F_\beta}{\partial\xi_\alpha}\quad\text{is negative definite.}
\tag{12.5}
$$

**Theorem 12.9.1 (Lyapunov's Matrix Criterion)** A matrix $M$ is stable (all eigenvalues have negative real parts) if and only if there is a positive definite Hermitian $Q$ such that $QM+M^{\mathsf H}Q$ is negative definite.

**Proof** (of "if") If $M\psi=\lambda\psi$ with $\operatorname{Re}\lambda\ge0$, then $\psi^{\mathsf H}(QM+M^{\mathsf H}Q)\psi=(\lambda+\bar\lambda)\psi^{\mathsf H}Q\psi\ge0$, so $QM+M^{\mathsf H}Q$ cannot be negative definite. $\square$

Criterion (12.5) is the special choice $Q=\operatorname{diag}(1/\xi_\alpha)$ with $M=J$. It is therefore sufficient for stability, but it cannot prove instability: a state violating (12.5) may still be stable, with stability shown by another $Q$, and counterexamples are easily constructed. Turing instabilities are found by the dispersion relation, not by such criteria.

## 12.10. Juxtacrine Lateral Inhibition

In epithelia, inhibition often travels not by diffusion but through direct contact: **lateral inhibition** by Delta–Notch signaling ([7.3.24](/7.3.24#def-24.3.3)). A cell that by chance makes slightly more Delta activates Notch in its neighbours more strongly; Notch activity represses Delta and the proneural genes (*neurogenin*, *NeuroD* in vertebrates; *achaete–scute* in flies) in the receiving cell, which therefore inhibits its neighbours less. The double-negative loop between neighbours amplifies small differences. The model of Collier and colleagues (1996), equation [(24.1) of 7.3.24](/7.3.24#eq-24.1), and its instability condition on a hexagonal sheet are derived there. On a row of cells, the alternating mode has neighbour average $\kappa=-1$ times a cell's own deviation, and the uniform state loses stability when the loop gain exceeds one, $f'(D^*)\,\lvert g'(N^*)\rvert>1$. The bifurcation is supercritical: the amplitude of the pattern grows continuously from zero beyond the threshold.

**Contact inhibition** differs from Turing patterns in its length scale. The spacing is set by the range of contact, not by a ratio of diffusion coefficients: with contact only between immediate neighbours the finest pattern is one high cell in two (on a row) or one in three (on hexagons). Cells can extend their reach with long basal protrusions, as the epidermal cells that space the sensory bristles of the fly notum do, and the bristles are then spaced several cells apart (Cohen and colleagues, 2010). Cis-inhibition, in which Delta in a cell inactivates Notch in the same cell, makes each cell either a sender or a receiver and sharpens the switch (Sprinzak and colleagues, 2010).

## 12.11. Applications

Two-dimensional reaction-diffusion produces spots or stripes depending on its parameters. In the Gierer–Meinhardt model, saturation of the activator decides: without strong saturation the activator concentrates in isolated peaks; with saturation the peaks cannot grow tall, the activated regions spread sideways and form stripes. Spacing in both cases is intrinsic, of the order of the wavelength of the fastest mode.

![Two-dimensional Gierer-Meinhardt patterns grown from noise on a periodic square. Weak saturation (kappa = 0.1) gives isolated peaks on a roughly hexagonal lattice; stronger saturation (kappa = 0.45) gives stripes and labyrinths with the same intrinsic spacing](./plots/turing-2d.py)

Hydromedusae. If unactivated tissue corresponds to the polyp and activated tissue to the medusa, the idealized switching mechanism in two or three dimensions reads $\nu\Delta h=-\lvert g_0\rvert$ where $h<h_s$ and $g_1$ where $h>h_s$, a problem analogous to electrostatics with charge densities of opposite sign in the two regions and an insulating boundary ($\partial h/\partial n=0$ playing the role of zero external dielectric constant). A large egg is easily activated as a whole; whether the "hot spot" activation of a small egg reflects a change of shape, of boundary conditions or of local inhibitor degradation is not decided by the model.

Vascular patterns. An activated region, once formed, can switch cells irreversibly into a new state, which changes the sources and so the next stage of patterning. In Meinhardt's model of leaf venation, an activator $A$ depletes a substrate $S$ and switches cells into a differentiated state $Y$ through an Edelstein-type switch:

$$
\dot A=cA^2S-\mu A+D_a\Delta A,\qquad \dot S=c_0-cA^2S-\gamma S-\varepsilon YS+D_s\Delta S,\qquad \dot Y=dA-eY+\frac{Y^2}{1+fY^2}.
$$
At $A=0$ the switch has the stable states $Y_0=0$ and $Y_0=\big(1+\sqrt{1-4\mathrm{e}^2f}\big)/(2ef)$, and a positive $A$ shifts them in proportion to $A$, so that a high level of $A$ triggers a lasting high $Y$. A differentiated cell consumes $S$, which lowers $A$ locally; the activated spot moves on, inhibited from behind and by any boundary, and can split into one or two channels, leaving a branching trail of differentiated cells. Current work explains venation through the polarized transport of auxin by PIN proteins (canalization), a related self-reinforcing mechanism ([7.12](/7.12)).

Proving that a given pattern is generated by a Turing mechanism requires more than resemblance: the mechanism predicts that the spacing changes with diffusivities and kinetic parameters but not with tissue size, that removing the inhibitor makes the pattern denser or uniform rather than absent, and that perturbing the field (cutting, grafting) leads to regulation rather than loss of pattern elements.

## Questions

1. Why can a single diffusing substance with no source not form a stable pattern, while two substances can? Relate your answer to Theorem 11.3.2 of [7.5.11](/7.5.11) and to Proposition 12.2.1.

   **Answer** With one substance and no-flux boundaries the dynamics is a gradient flow of a Lyapunov functional, and every nonuniform steady state has an unstable mode (the derivative of the profile itself provides a test function with nonnegative Rayleigh quotient). With two substances the dynamics need not be a gradient flow: when the Jacobian is not symmetric and the diffusion coefficients differ, diffusion can destabilize the uniform state (Turing), and the resulting nonuniform states can be stable (Theorem 12.4.1). Proposition 12.2.1 lists the cases in which even several substances cannot do this: equal diffusion, a symmetric Jacobian, or sign-stable kinetics.

2. In the two-box model of Section 12.2.1 with $A=2$, how large must the exchange rate $D$ be for the boxes to become different? Which box wins?

   **Answer** The difference mode has determinant $A^2-D$, so $D>4$. The box that starts with slightly more activator wins: it consumes precursor faster, the precursor flows into it from the neighbour, and the neighbour, deprived of precursor, loses its activator. Note that it is the precursor, not the activator, that diffuses: an "activator–depletion" mechanism.

3. Verify the Turing condition for Turing's example with $\nu/\varepsilon=8$, and compute the fastest wave number and its growth rate from Formula 12.3.1 for $\varepsilon=1$. What wavelength does this give?

   **Answer** $f_a=0.1875$, $f_h=0.25$, $g_a=g_h=-0.25$, $\det J=1/64$. Left side $8(0.1875)-0.25=1.25$, right side $2\sqrt{8/64}=0.707$: unstable. With $P=1/16$: $k^2_{\max}=[9\sqrt{1/128}-0.25-0.1875]/7=(0.7955-0.4375)/7=0.0511$, $k_{\max}=0.226$; $\Omega_{\max}=[1.5+0.25-2\sqrt{0.5}]/7=(1.75-1.414)/7=0.048$. Wavelength $2\pi/k_{\max}\approx27.8$. (These agree with the dot in the figure.)

4. Explain why a closed chemical system obeying mass-action kinetics with detailed balance cannot form a pattern, and identify which of the three ways of breaking detailed balance operates in a living embryo.

   **Answer** The Gibbs function decreases monotonically (Theorem 12.2.1) and reaches its minimum at the unique equilibrium, which is uniform because diffusion is a reaction that equalizes concentrations. Patterns require that not all reactions can be at equilibrium simultaneously. In embryos every molecule is synthesized from precursors supplied by metabolism and degraded to products that are removed: these are the source–sink idealizations, with the return reactions negligible on developmental time scales, maintained by the free energy of ATP and other metabolites.

5. Using Proposition 12.5.1, compute the fraction of the tissue occupied by the activated region in the Gierer–Meinhardt model (12.4) with $\mu=0$ and $\lambda=1$.

   **Answer** $h_s=0.920$, $A_2(h_s)=a_s=1.515$. $g_0=-\lambda h_s=-0.920$, $g_1=a_s^2-\lambda h_s=2.295-0.920=1.375$. Fraction $=\lvert g_0\rvert/(g_1+\lvert g_0\rvert)=0.920/2.295=0.40$. The activated region is 40 % of the tissue, independent of its length (as long as one half-cycle fits and $\varepsilon$ is small).

6. Show that the cubic approximation of the equal-area rule places the middle branch at the mean of the outer branches, and use it to estimate $h_s$ for (12.4).

   **Answer** A cubic $f=-\alpha(a-A_0)(a-A_1)(a-A_2)$ with roots $A_0<A_1<A_2$ has $\int_{A_0}^{A_2}f\,\mathrm{d}a=0$ exactly when it is antisymmetric about the middle root, i.e. $A_1=\frac12(A_0+A_2)$. For (12.4), $A_0=0$ and $A_{1,2}=\frac1h\mp s$ with $s=\sqrt{h^{-2}-1}$; setting $\frac1h-s=\frac12(\frac1h+s)$ gives $s=\frac1{3h}$, $h^{-2}-1=\frac1{9h^2}$, $h^2=\frac89$, $h=0.943$, compared with the exact $0.920$.

7. (Experimental design) A periodic array of feather buds is proposed to arise by a Turing mechanism. Design three experiments that could refute the proposal.

   **Answer** (i) Change the size of the skin explant: a Turing mechanism keeps the spacing and changes the number of buds; a prepattern mechanism (buds placed by a map) would rescale or lose buds. (ii) Reduce the inhibitor (for example by a DKK or BMP antagonist, or a mutant): Turing predicts denser buds or fused, enlarged domains; if buds simply disappear or remain unchanged, the candidate is not the inhibitor of a Turing pair. (iii) Dissociate and reaggregate the dermal and epidermal cells: a self-organizing mechanism re-forms buds with the same spacing in the reaggregated, uniform skin. Quantitative tests add that spacing should change with the measured diffusivities and kinetic rates in the predicted direction.

8. In Example 12.4.1, show that $\chi(E)$ increases with $E$. Why does this mean that a narrow band of unstable modes does not fix the number of half-cycles?

   **Answer** $V$ is steeper than its parabolic approximation on the left (it diverges at $h=1$) but flatter on the right (it grows only linearly), and the slow right side dominates: as $E$ increases, the particle spends longer near the right turning point, so $\chi$ grows (the figure shows the numerical $\chi(E)$). Since a pattern with $p$ half-cycles requires $L=p\chi(E)$, any $p$ with $p\chi_{\min}<L$ has a solution with some $E$: the steady states exist for a whole range of $p$, and which one appears depends on the initial perturbation, not on the narrow linear band.

9. For the minimal half-period (Formula 12.5.1) estimate $\Delta x$ for $\varepsilon=10^{-4}$ and $f_a'=-1$. Compare with the peak widths in the one-dimensional simulations of Section 12.5.

   **Answer** $\Delta x=4\sqrt{10^{-4}}=0.04$. The peaks in the simulation (with $\varepsilon^2$-type activator diffusion $10^{-4}$ and activator decay rate 1) have widths of a few hundredths, consistent with this estimate: the activator diffusion length $\sqrt{\varepsilon/\lvert f_a\rvert}$ sets the sharpness of every activated region.

10. A cell on a row of cells using Delta–Notch lateral inhibition with immediate neighbours only develops as the inhibiting fate. Show that, at best, half the cells of the row adopt this fate, and that on a hexagonal sheet the best is one in three. How could an organism space its bristles four or five cells apart with the same signaling pathway?

    **Answer** Two inhibiting cells cannot be neighbours, since each would repress the other; on a row the densest arrangement alternates (one in two). On a hexagonal lattice, a set of cells in which no two are adjacent can contain at most one third of the cells (the three-colouring of the triangular lattice of cell centres), and the fastest-growing mode with $\kappa=-1/2$ is exactly this arrangement. To space structures farther apart, the inhibition must reach farther: through long basal protrusions carrying Delta, through a diffusible inhibitor, or by combining lateral inhibition with a prepattern of proneural clusters that restricts where precursors can arise.
