Development does not stop at birth. Blood, skin and gut lining are renewed throughout life, and many animals can do far more: a *Hydra* cut into pieces gives as many small polyps, a planarian rebuilds its whole body from a fragment, a salamander regrows a limb, a zebrafish its heart. Regeneration is the reactivation of developmental programs in an adult, and it poses the same questions as the embryo in a sharper form. Where do the new cells come from? How does the remnant know what is missing, and how much to make? Which signals start and stop the process? This chapter answers them for the classical models in turn, and its organizing idea is the one of [7.5.11](/7.5.11) and [7.5.12](/7.5.12): regenerating tissue reads and restores **positional information**. We apply the activator–inhibitor model of Chapter 12 to the grafts and cuts of *Hydra*, formalize the intercalation rules of the polar coordinate model, and model the regrowth of the liver. The **stem cells** of adult tissues and their stochastic dynamics follow in [7.5.36](/7.5.36), the decline of regeneration with age in [7.5.37](/7.5.37); **stem cells**, niches, crypts and iPSCs are introduced in [7.3.24](/7.3.24), **apoptosis** and compensatory proliferation in [7.3.25](/7.3.25), and the developing limb, which the regenerating limb recapitulates, in [7.5.31](/7.5.31) and [7.5.32](/7.5.32).

## 35.1. Renewal, Repair and Regeneration

Tissues replace cells on three scales. In homeostatic maintenance, cells lost by aging or **apoptosis** are replaced continuously, as in the gut epithelium or the blood. In repair after injury (a toxic drug, an infection), a tissue compensates for lost cells by making new ones. In regeneration proper, a structure lost by amputation or ablation, in part or entirely, is rebuilt with its original pattern.

**Definition 35.1.1 (Regeneration)** **Regeneration** is the restoration, after embryonic development, of a lost or damaged body part to its original structure, pattern and function by the remaining tissue.

Four modes are distinguished by where the new cells come from and whether the remnant grows.

**Definition 35.1.2 (Modes of Regeneration)** In **morphallaxis**, the remaining tissue is repatterned into a smaller complete structure with little or no cell proliferation. In **epimorphosis**, cells at the wound proliferate to form a **blastema**, a mass of undifferentiated or dedifferentiated **progenitor cells** under the wound epidermis, which grows and redifferentiates into the missing parts. In **compensatory regeneration**, differentiated cells of the remnant divide while keeping their differentiated functions, restoring mass rather than form. Stem-cell-mediated regeneration replaces cells from resident stem cells.

| Mode | Source of new cells | Growth | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Morphallaxis | existing cells, repatterned | little | *Hydra* cut below the head |
| Epimorphosis | blastema of dedifferentiated or stem cells | yes, distal outgrowth | salamander limb, zebrafish fin, planarian |
| Compensatory regeneration | division of differentiated cells | yes, form not restored | mammalian liver, zebrafish heart (in part) |
| Stem-cell-mediated | resident stem cells | turnover | blood, gut, hair, planarian neoblasts |

The modes are not exclusive: *Hydra* uses morphallaxis or epimorphosis depending on where it is cut (35.2.3), and the zebrafish heart uses three modes at once (35.5). The central questions, which recur through the chapter, are the cell source (**dedifferentiation**, resident stem cells, or cells arriving from elsewhere), the measurement of what is missing and the control of size, the signals, the differences between organs, and the evolution of regenerative ability, which is distributed very unevenly among animals.

## 35.2. *Hydra*

*Hydra* is a freshwater cnidarian about 0.5 cm long: a tubular body column with a head (a conical hypostome around the mouth and a ring of tentacles) at its apical end and a basal disc, the foot, at the other. It is diploblastic, with an ectodermal and an endodermal epithelium (both are epitheliomuscular) separated by the mesoglea, and reproduces mostly asexually by budding, about two thirds of the way down the column; sexual reproduction occurs under crowding, starvation or cold. Experimental biology began with it: in 1744 Abraham Trembley reported that a polyp cut into pieces gives as many complete animals.

### 35.2.1. Three Lineages in Constant Flux

The body of *Hydra* is permanently renewed. Its roughly ten cell types (epithelial cells, interstitial cells, nematoblasts and nematocytes, **neurons**, gland and mucous cells, gametes) come from three self-renewing lineages. The ectodermal and endodermal epithelial cells are unipotent stem cells that divide every 3–4 days throughout the column; the multipotent interstitial stem cells, located between the ectodermal epithelial cells, divide every 24–30 h and give rise to **neurons**, nematocytes, gland cells and gametes. Labelled tissue shows that cells produced in the column are displaced towards the two ends and shed from the tentacle tips and the basal disc, so that a cell changes its position, and with it its fate, as it ages: a *Hydra* is always regenerating. Epithelial *Hydra*, from which the interstitial lineage has been eliminated by drugs or irradiation, still feed (when force-fed), bud and regenerate, so the epithelial cells carry the patterning system. Dissociated cells reaggregate and form new polyps, a striking case of **self-organization**.

### 35.2.2. Grafts Reveal an Organizer and Two Gradients

Any piece of the column can form a head and a foot, and regenerates them with its original polarity: the end that faced the head forms the head. Grafting experiments, begun by Ethel Browne in 1909, show how this is controlled.

**Example 35.2.1 (Grafts and Their Logic)** A ring of hypostome grafted into the side of the column of an intact host induces a secondary axis with a head at its tip (panel A of the figure). Labelling the host with carbon particles shows that the new head is made mostly of host cells (Broun and Bode 2002): the hypostome does not just differentiate itself, it instructs its neighbors, and transient contact suffices. It is an organizer, the first described (Browne's experiment predates that of Spemann and Mangold, [7.5.16](/7.5.16)). Subhypostomal tissue, which forms a head when isolated, does not do so when grafted just below an intact head (B), but does if the host head is removed (C) or if the graft is placed far down the column (D). The head therefore makes an inhibitor of head formation, which falls off with distance; the inhibitor is labile (half-life 2–3 h, estimated from the time a decapitated host takes to lose its inhibition). Grafts of tissue from successive levels show that the capacity to form a head (head activation) declines from the hypostome to the foot; a matching pair of gradients of foot activation and foot inhibition runs from the basal disc. Buds form where the inhibitions from both ends are weakest, about two thirds of the way down; the *H. magnipapillata* mutant L4, with excess head inhibitor, buds only at about twice the normal length.

### 35.2.3. Molecules: Wnt3, β-Catenin and Sp5

The head activator is canonical Wnt signaling. *Wnt3* is expressed in the hypostome and at the apex of early buds (Hobmayer and colleagues 2000); inhibiting GSK3 throughout the animal (with alsterpaullone) makes every level of the column behave like hypostome, with ectopic tentacles and buds, and transgenic polyps overexpressing **β-catenin** form ectopic buds all along the column, even on buds (Gee and colleagues 2010). The hypostome induces *Brachyury* in the column in a Wnt-dependent way, as vertebrate organizers do, although *Hydra* has no **mesoderm**. One inhibitory component is the zinc-finger transcription factor Sp5: Wnt/**β-catenin** signaling induces *Sp5*, and Sp5 represses *Wnt3*; knocking down *Sp5* produces multiheaded animals (Vogg and colleagues 2019). Wnt3 activating itself (through β-catenin/TCF) and an inhibitor that represses it is exactly the architecture of an activator–inhibitor system. The inhibitor of the grafting experiments is probably more than Sp5, since a transcription factor does not diffuse between cells; the long-range component remains to be identified. Foot formation involves its own genes (the tyrosine kinase *shinguard*, graded from the basal disc, and the paired-like **homeobox** gene *manacle*), and buds appear where *shinguard* expression fades.

The level of the cut decides the mode of head regeneration (Chera and colleagues 2009). After a cut just below the head, *Wnt3* is upregulated in the epithelial cells at the wound and existing cells are remodeled into a head without proliferation: morphallaxis. After a cut at mid-column, interstitial-lineage cells below the cut undergo apoptosis, release a burst of Wnt3 that activates β-catenin in neighboring interstitial cells, and a zone of proliferation forms: epimorphosis. Apoptosis-induced proliferation of this kind occurs in many regenerating systems ([7.3.25](/7.3.25)).

### 35.2.4. The Activator–Inhibitor Model of the *Hydra* Axis

The phenomenology of Example 35.2.1 (short-range self-enhancement, long-range diffusible inhibition, regulation to size) led Gierer and Meinhardt (1972) to their model, and *Hydra* is its most studied application. Take the **Gierer–Meinhardt model** (Definition 12.5.1) with a graded source density, a property of the tissue that declines from head to foot and is carried with a piece of tissue when it is cut or grafted.

**Formula 35.2.1 (A Graded Activator–Inhibitor Model of the *Hydra* Axis)** With $x$ the position along the column (head at $x = 0$), activator $a$ and inhibitor $h$,

$$
\frac{\partial a}{\partial t} = \varepsilon^2\frac{\partial^2a}{\partial x^2} + \rho(x)\Big(\frac{a^2}{h} + \mu\Big) - a,\qquad
\tau\frac{\partial h}{\partial t} = D\frac{\partial^2h}{\partial x^2} + a^2 - h,
$$
with no-flux ends. Time is measured in activator lifetimes; $\varepsilon \ll \sqrt D$ (the inhibitor spreads much farther than the activator); $\mu$ is a small basal activator production; $\rho(x)$ is the source density, high at the head and low at the foot, and lowered near the foot to represent foot inhibition. In the simulations below $\varepsilon^2 = 10^{-4}$, $D = 0.2$, $\tau = 0.5$, $\mu = 0.05$ and $\rho = \bigl(1 + 0.1(1 - x/L)\bigr)\bigl(1 - 0.3\,\mathrm{e}^{-(L-x)/0.1}\bigr)$ on a polyp of length $L$.

In tissue that is not activated, $a$ is small and the inhibitor obeys $\tau\dot h = D h'' - h$, whose steady state decays as $\mathrm{e}^{-x/\sqrt D}$.

**Proposition 35.2.1 (Range of Inhibition)** In the linear inhibitor equation $\dot h = -\lambda h + \nu h''$ of the scaled model (12.4) ($\nu$ the inhibitor diffusivity, $\lambda$ its decay rate), the inhibition produced by an activated region falls off as $\mathrm{e}^{-(\lambda/\nu)^{1/2}\lvert x - x_0\rvert}$, with range $\ell_h = (\nu/\lambda)^{1/2}$. A second activated region can form spontaneously only where the inhibition from existing ones has fallen below the switch level (Example 12.5.1), at a distance of the order of a few $\ell_h$.

**Proof** The steady state of $\nu h'' = \lambda h$ away from sources is a combination of $\mathrm{e}^{\pm(\lambda/\nu)^{1/2}x}$; the solution that stays bounded away from the source is the decaying one. $\square$

**Example 35.2.2** The head inhibitor has a half-life of 2–3 h, so $\lambda \approx \ln 2/2.5\ \mathrm{h} \approx 0.28\ \mathrm{h^{-1}}$. For a small molecule moving through tissue with an effective diffusivity of $50\ \mathrm{\mu m^2\,s^{-1}}$ ($1.8 \times 10^{5}\ \mathrm{\mu m^2\,h^{-1}}$), $\ell_h \approx (1.8\times10^5/0.28)^{1/2}\ \mathrm{\mu m} \approx 0.8\ \mathrm{mm}$, a fraction of the 5 mm of an adult polyp, consistent with grafts that are inhibited over two to three of the five levels of the column. If the polyp is much shorter than a few $\ell_h$, as in most regeneration experiments, the whole tissue lies within the range of one head and the idealized switch-point behavior of Proposition 12.5.1 applies: one head, proportioned to the size of the piece.

The model reproduces the experiments qualitatively (figure below). A polyp of length 1 (in units of $\sqrt D/0.45$) has one head; as it grows to length 1.5, the inhibition at the far end of the column falls below the threshold and a second peak, a bud, forms about three quarters of the way down. After decapitation the inhibitor, no longer produced, decays first; once it has fallen, the activator rises at the cut, where the source density is now highest, and a head forms within a few activator lifetimes. A polyp cut in two regenerates a head on each piece at the end that faced the old head, because each piece retains its slice of the source gradient: polarity is stored in $\rho$, which changes slowly (polarity reversal by grafting takes days, while head determination takes hours). Grafted activated tissue is absorbed near an existing head and forms a secondary axis far from it; in a decapitated host it becomes the new head. In axial grafts the final number of heads is set largely by the initial pattern of source density: a sufficiently large internal peak of $\rho$ gives a pattern of three half-cycles, two heads. The recovery of inhibition after the removal of a head from the middle of a graft follows the diffusional delay derived for the slime mould in [7.5.13](/7.5.13#sec-13.2): a perturbation of the inhibitor reaches a point at distance $\ell - x$ after a time of order $(\ell - x)^2/\nu$.

B: after removal of the tissue in front of x = 0.2 (dotted: before the cut) the activator in the column first rises as the inhibitor decays, then concentrates into a new head at the cut (snapshots at t = 0, 2, 4, 6, 10 and 40 activator lifetimes). **Remark 35.2.1** The model is phenomenological. It accounts for polarity, the graft results, budding and size regulation with one mechanism, and its molecular counterpart (Wnt3 self-activation, Sp5 repression) was found decades later; but the source density $\rho$ remains a description, not a molecule, and in *Hydra* mechanical cues also orient the axis: aggregates and tissue pieces inherit the alignment of the supracellular actin fibers of the epithelium, and regenerating pieces form heads where the fibers converge (Livshits and colleagues 2017). The cnidarian relatives of *Hydra* show the other side of the same problem: in hydromedusae the size of the egg decides whether development goes directly to a medusa or through a polyp, discussed with the model in [7.5.12](/7.5.12#sec-12.1).

## 35.3. Planarians

Freshwater planarians (*Schmidtea mediterranea*, *Dugesia japonica*) are flatworms with a brain, eyes, a gut with a pharynx, a nervous system with two ventral nerve cords, muscles, protonephridia and, in sexual strains, gonads. Many reproduce by fission. Every part of the body can regenerate, and a fragment of 1/279 of the body suffices to make a complete worm (Morgan 1898).

### 35.3.1. Polarity and Its Molecular Basis

A worm cut transversely regenerates a head at the cut surface that faces anterior and a tail at the one that faces posterior, so the same tissue makes a head or a tail depending on where it lies relative to the cut. Morgan and Child (1905) inferred a gradient of an "anterior-producing" substance; very thin pieces regenerate abnormally, often with heads at both ends.

The anterior–posterior axis is set by Wnt/β-catenin signaling, which in planarians promotes tail identity. **RNA interference** against *β-catenin-1* before amputation makes the posterior blastema form a head (two-headed worms), and complete knockdown in an intact worm turns its periphery into head, with eyes all around; knockdown of *APC*, which raises β-catenin, makes anterior blastemas form tails (Gurley, Petersen and Reddien, Iglesias and colleagues 2008). After any wound, *wnt1* is induced in muscle cells at the wound as part of a generic wound response; it then persists only at posterior-facing wounds. At anterior-facing wounds the Wnt inhibitor *notum*, a deacylase that inactivates Wnt proteins, is expressed in muscle cells at the midline; *notum* RNAi makes anterior-facing wounds regenerate tails. Among 4401 genes compared, *notum* was the only one expressed differently at anterior- and posterior-facing wounds at the onset of regeneration (Wurtzel and colleagues 2015), which places the polarity decision on a single antagonism. ERK signaling promotes head formation where Wnt is low. Some planarian species that cannot regenerate heads (*Dendrocoelum lacteum*, *Procotyla fluviatilis*) have excess Wnt activity in anterior blastemas, and inhibiting β-catenin restores head regeneration (Liu, Sikes, Umesono and colleagues 2013): the incapacity is a defect of patterning, not of cells.

The other axes have their own signals: BMP4 on the dorsal midline specifies dorsal tissue (its knockdown ventralizes the worm), the reverse of vertebrates, in which BMP is ventral ([7.5.17](/7.5.17)), in line with the dorso-ventral inversion between protostomes and chordates; Slit at the midline and Wnt5 laterally pattern the mediolateral axis.

### 35.3.2. Neoblasts

The new cells come from neoblasts. They make up about a quarter to a third of the cells of the parenchyma, are the only dividing somatic cells, express the PIWI homolog *smedwi-1*, and are killed by irradiation, after which the worm cannot regenerate and dies within weeks.

**Definition 35.3.1 (Neoblast)** A **neoblast** is a proliferating adult stem cell of planarians (and of acoel flatworms), distributed through the parenchyma; neoblasts include pluripotent cells that can regenerate every cell type, and lineage-specified ones.

**Example 35.3.1 (Single-Cell Rescue)** To test whether individual neoblasts are pluripotent, Wagner, Wang and Reddien (2011) irradiated worms with a dose (17.5 Gy) that kills almost all neoblasts. In some worms a single surviving neoblast formed a colony that produced differentiated cells of all **germ layers**: such clonogenic neoblasts (cNeoblasts) are pluripotent. In a second experiment worms received a dose (60 Gy) that kills every dividing cell, and a single cNeoblast from a genetically distinct strain was transplanted. Untreated controls all died within about six weeks, degenerating from the head; in 7 of 130 transplanted worms the donor cell expanded, the worms survived, regenerated and later reproduced, and all their cells had the donor genotype. One adult cell had rebuilt a whole animal, which shows directly that adult pluripotent stem cells exist. The low frequency shows the limits of the assay (transplant survival, engraftment) rather than the rarity of **pluripotency**.

Neoblasts are heterogeneous. Most express transcription factors of particular lineages (eye, gut, epidermis, muscle, nephridia) and are specified, though still dividing; a subpopulation marked by the surface protein Tspan-1 is enriched for pluripotent cells (Zeng and colleagues 2018). Neoblasts do not respond to tissue loss by migrating far in an intact animal; after amputation they move towards the wound and form the blastema, which is fed by their proliferation in the old tissue nearby. The **positional information** that tells them what to make is carried by another cell type: the body-wall muscle expresses position control genes (PCGs, more than 30 genes including the *wnt*, *notum*, *bmp* and *slit* genes above and **Hox genes**) in regional patterns, a map of the body (Witchley and colleagues 2013). After amputation the pattern is reset: *wnt1* first appears at all wounds, *notum* then appears at anterior-facing ones, and over days the expression domains of posterior genes such as *wntP-2* are re-scaled to the size of the fragment; new tissue is then made according to the new map, and the worm grows as it feeds.

## 35.4. The Salamander Limb

Adult urodeles (newts, the axolotl *Ambystoma mexicanum*) regenerate limbs, tail, jaws, parts of the heart and the eye, and the spinal cord. Lazzaro Spallanzani described limb regeneration in 1768. A limb amputated at any level regenerates exactly the parts distal to the cut, in about 6–10 weeks depending on species, size and temperature.

### 35.4.1. Wound Epidermis, AEC and Blastema

Within 6–12 h of amputation, epidermal cells migrate from the stump over the wound and cover it with a thin wound epidermis; unlike a mammalian wound, the dermis does not follow, and no scar forms. Over the next days the wound epidermis thickens into the apical epidermal cap (AEC), the functional equivalent of the **apical ectodermal ridge** of the limb bud ([7.5.31](/7.5.31)): it secretes FGF8 and is required for outgrowth. Beneath it, proteases degrade the **extracellular matrix** and release single cells from the dermis, cartilage, muscle and connective tissue; they lose their differentiated characteristics (muscle genes such as *mrf4* and *myf5* are downregulated), express genes of the limb bud **progress zone** (*msx1*, *prrx1*), and enter the **cell cycle**. These cells, together with immune cells and regenerating nerves, form the blastema (Definition 35.1.2), which grows into a cone, elongates and redifferentiates in a proximal-to-distal sequence, re-expressing the Hox and signaling genes of the developing limb.

**Example 35.4.1 (Is the Blastema Pluripotent? Lineage Tracing)** Kragl and colleagues (2009) grafted a single tissue (cartilage, muscle, dermis, Schwann cells or epidermis) from a GFP-transgenic axolotl embryo into the **limb field** of an unlabeled one, let the limb develop, amputated through the labeled region and asked which tissues of the regenerate were green. Each tissue gave back mainly itself: muscle only muscle, epidermis only epidermis, Schwann cells only Schwann cells, cartilage cartilage; only the dermal fibroblasts contributed more widely, to cartilage, tendons and dermis, which are all connective tissues. The blastema is therefore not a mass of pluripotent cells but a mixture of lineage-restricted progenitors, unlike the planarian blastema. The same grafts tested position: cartilage and dermis cells from the upper arm contributed to all levels of the regenerate, but those from the hand only to the hand, so connective tissue cells carry a memory of their proximo-distal position, whereas Schwann cells did not. Later work found that the cellular route differs between species: in the newt, myofibers fragment and dedifferentiate, while in the axolotl the new muscle comes from Pax7-positive **satellite cells** (Sandoval-Guzmán and colleagues 2014), so "**dedifferentiation**" names different processes in different animals.

### 35.4.2. Nerve Dependence

Regeneration requires nerves. A limb denervated before amputation forms a wound epidermis but no blastema, and the stump heals; Singer (1940s–1950s) found that a minimum density of nerve fibers, sensory or motor, is needed, but that nerve conduction and acetylcholine are not: nerves supply **growth factors**. The paradox of aneurogenic limbs, which develop without nerves (after the **neural tube** is removed from the embryo) and yet regenerate, showed that the dependence is acquired: their epidermis produces the factor itself. The best candidate is newt anterior gradient protein (nAG), a secreted protein induced in the Schwann cells of regenerating nerves and in glands of the wound epidermis; electroporating *nAG* into the blastema of a denervated limb restores regeneration (Kumar and colleagues 2007), and its receptor is the GPI-anchored protein Prod1. In the axolotl, nerve-derived FGF and BMP are sufficient: when a nerve is deviated to a skin wound on the side of the limb, a bump forms, and if skin from the opposite side of the limb is also grafted beside it (or beads with BMP2/7 and FGF2/8 are implanted instead of the nerve), a complete supernumerary limb grows (the accessory limb model, Endo and colleagues 2004; Makanae and colleagues 2014). Three ingredients thus make a limb: a wound epidermis, a nerve and a confrontation of different **positional values**.

### 35.4.3. Positional Memory

Regeneration proceeds only distally: a hand cut at the wrist makes carpals and digits, an arm cut through the humerus makes everything from the distal humerus on. The stump knows its **positional value** ([7.5.11](/7.5.11)), and the blastema retains it: a blastema transplanted to a neutral site (the dorsal fin crest, or the anterior chamber of the eye) forms the structures appropriate to its origin. Its growth depends on the level of the cut, not on what lies distally: in a classic experiment the hand of a newt limb was inserted into the flank, and after it was vascularized the limb was cut through the humerus; both cut surfaces regenerated distal structures, although one of them already had a forearm and hand beyond it.

**Definition 35.4.1 (Positional Memory)** **Positional memory** is the stable retention by adult cells of the **positional values** they acquired in development, so that cells taken from a structure, on regenerating, make the parts appropriate to their original position and no others.

Several properties correlate with proximo-distal position. Mesenchyme from a proximal blastema engulfs that from a distal one in culture, as more cohesive tissue does in sorting ([7.5.7](/7.5.7)), and a distal blastema grafted onto the dorsal surface of a proximal one moves, during regeneration, to the level it came from. Prod1 is expressed in a gradient, higher proximally, and an antibody against it prevents the **engulfment**. Retinoic acid (RA) proximalizes: a limb amputated at the wrist and treated with RA regenerates a whole limb from the humerus on, in a dose-dependent way, with an extra radius, ulna and humerus in series (Maden 1982). Its action requires the receptor RARδ2 and can be cell-autonomous: distal blastema cells transfected with a chimeric receptor activated by thyroxine, then grafted to a proximal stump and given thyroxine, move proximally in the regenerate. RA raises *Prod1* and *Meis* expression, and *Meis* specifies proximal values in the developing limb too. Treating tail-regenerating frog tadpoles with RA turns the tail blastema into hindlimbs, a **homeotic transformation** that remains unexplained. Anteroposterior memory has a molecular basis as well: posterior connective tissue cells keep a low level of the transcription factor *Hand2* that, after amputation, reactivates *Shh*, and Shh in turn maintains *Hand2*, a positive feedback that both stores and reads the memory (Otsuki and colleagues 2025).

### 35.4.4. Intercalation and the Polar Coordinate Model

When cells with non-adjacent positional values are put side by side, growth restores the missing values between them. The clearest examples are insect legs, which regenerate through a blastema at molts. In the cockroach tibia, a distal piece (value 5) grafted on a proximal stump (value 1) makes a normal tibia, intercalating values 2–4, regardless of the orientation of the graft; a proximal piece grafted on a distal stump (values 1–4 followed by 1–5) makes an abnormally long tibia, because the regenerate intercalates the values 3 and 2 between 4 and 1, with reversed polarity (its bristles point the wrong way). Removing a longitudinal strip of epidermis brings non-neighboring circumferential positions together, and the next molt restores the missing circumferential values. Cells are indifferent to the overall pattern; they act locally to remove discontinuities.

**Definition 35.4.2 (Intercalary Regeneration)** **Intercalary regeneration** is growth at a junction between tissues with non-adjacent positional values that produces cells with the intermediate values, restoring a continuous sequence.

French, Bryant and Bryant (1976) summarized these results in a formal model for insect legs and amphibian limbs.

**Definition 35.4.3 (Polar Coordinate Model)** In the **polar coordinate model**, each cell of an appendage has a positional value made of a circumferential value, one of a continuous cycle of values around the limb (a clock face, $0 \equiv 12$), and a radial value, which increases from proximal to distal. Two rules govern regeneration: intercalation by the shortest route between confronted circumferential values (Algorithm 35.4.1), and the **complete circle rule** of distal transformation: a wound surface on which every circumferential value is represented produces distal outgrowth with increasing radial values, a complete limb.

**Algorithm 35.4.1 (Shortest Intercalation)** Given a host value $h$ and a confronted graft value $g$ on the clock $\mathbb{Z}/12$ (or $\mathbb{R}/12\mathbb{Z}$):

1. Compute the signed difference $d = g - h$ reduced to the interval $(-6, 6]$.
2. Fill the junction with the values $h, h + \operatorname{sgn}(d), \dots, h + d$ (mod 12), the shorter of the two arcs between $h$ and $g$.
3. If $\lvert d\rvert = 6$, both arcs are equally short; the point is singular and either series may form.

A stump that has lost one sector of values (a half limb, a strip of epidermis) thus heals by intercalating the shorter arc and does not form a complete circle; a complete circle formed at a junction triggers distal outgrowth. The model's most remarkable prediction concerns grafts that reverse one axis.

**Theorem 35.4.1 (Supernumerary Limbs as Winding Number)** Let the junction between a stump and a graft be the annulus between two circles, carrying positional values $v(\theta, s)$ that interpolate by shortest intercalation from the stump values $h(\theta)$ (at $s = 0$) to the graft values $g(\theta)$ (at $s = 1$), continuous except at isolated singular points. Let $\deg h$ and $\deg g$ be the winding numbers of the two circle maps, $+1$ for a limb of the stump's handedness and $-1$ for its mirror image. Then the indices (local winding numbers of $v$) of the singular points add up to $\deg h - \deg g$. In particular a contralateral graft that reverses one axis ($\deg g = -1$) leaves at least two singular points, each surrounded by a complete circle of values; by the complete circle rule each produces a supernumerary limb.

**Proof** The winding number of $v$ along a closed curve in the annulus is an integer that does not change when the curve is deformed without crossing a singular point. The curve made of the outer boundary, traversed in one direction, and the inner boundary, traversed in the opposite one, joined by a cut, encloses all singular points; its winding number is $\deg g - \deg h$ with the orientation of the outer curve, and it equals the sum of the windings around each singular point taken with that orientation. Reversing the orientation gives the stated sign. With $\deg h = 1$ and $\deg g = -1$ the sum is 2, so the singular points cannot be absent; a small loop around a point of index $\pm1$ takes every value of the clock once, which is a complete circle. $\square$

**Example 35.4.2** A right limb blastema grafted onto a left stump with the dorso-ventral axis kept reverses the antero-posterior axis: opposite a host value $h$ lies $g = -h$ (mod 12) when dorsal is 12 and anterior 3. The difference $g - h = -2h$ reaches $\pm6$ at $h = 3$ and $h = 9$, the anterior and posterior points, which are the only singularities; the computation in the figure gives an index of $+1$ at each. Two supernumerary limbs are predicted at the anterior and posterior positions, with the handedness of the stump, and this is what Bryant and Iten (1976) observed in newts and French and colleagues in cockroaches. An ipsilateral graft rotated by $90^\circ$ has $g = h + 3$ everywhere, no singular point and no supernumerary limb. The model also explains why a double-anterior or double-posterior limb (made by grafting two anterior halves) often fails to regenerate, or regenerates a spike: its wound surface lacks half the circumferential values, so no complete circle forms.

![The polar coordinate model. A: circumferential positional values of a left stump and of a right blastema grafted on it with dorsal (D) kept: anterior (A, 3) and posterior (P, 9) swap. B: shortest intercalation at 12 points of the junction, from host values (inner ring) to graft values (outer ring); along the anterior and posterior spokes (red) host and graft values are opposite and both routes are equally short. The 1976 model required complete circles to be formed at the wound by intercalation; a revision (Bryant, French and Bryant 1981) dropped the assumption that distal values are produced by intercalation, replacing it with the complete circle rule as stated, since a proximo-distal sequence is now known to be specified progressively in the blastema itself, as in the limb bud (Wolpert's progress-zone and two-signal debates, [7.5.31](/7.5.31)). The accessory limb model of 35.4.2 is the modern test: the skin graft from the opposite side supplies the missing circumferential values, the nerve supplies the **growth factors**, and FGF8 from anterior cells and Shh from posterior cells, which must meet, are the molecular counterparts of "anterior" and "posterior" positional values (Nacu and colleagues 2016). Zebrafish fins and mammalian digit tips obey the same distal rule (35.5, 35.6).

### 35.4.5. Regeneration Throughout Life

Salamanders regenerate repeatedly into old age: a newt lens was removed 18 times over 16 years and regenerated every time (Eguchi and colleagues 2011). Senescent cells are induced in the blastema and then cleared by macrophages, so they do not accumulate (Yun and colleagues 2015); depleting macrophages before amputation blocks axolotl limb regeneration, which resumes once they return (Godwin and colleagues 2013). **Cellular senescence** ([7.3.25](/7.3.25#sec-25.7)) is here a transient, useful state, and its clearance a requirement for repeated regeneration ([7.5.37](/7.5.37)).

## 35.5. Zebrafish

The zebrafish regenerates its fins, heart, retina (from Müller glia), spinal cord, liver, kidney and sensory hair cells throughout life, and its genetics make it the main vertebrate model of regeneration.

### 35.5.1. The Fin

The caudal fin has 16–18 segmented bony rays, with fibroblasts, blood vessels, nerves and pigment cells between them. After amputation the epidermis covers the wound and forms an apical cap within a day; **osteoblasts** at the cut dedifferentiate, migrate distally and, with fibroblasts, form a blastema at the tip of each ray, whose distal part proliferates little and directs growth while proximal cells proliferate and redifferentiate. **Osteoblasts**, like salamander cells, remain lineage-restricted, although osteoblasts can also arise de novo when all existing ones are ablated (Singh and colleagues 2012). Wnt/β-catenin signaling is active in the distal blastema and is required, acting indirectly through other signals (FGF, Hedgehog, RA, IGF); blocking it with a ubiquitous or osteoblast-specific inducible Axin1 reduces outgrowth and ossification (Wehner and colleagues 2014). Regeneration is proportioned: a proximal cut regrows faster than a distal one, so both restore the original length in about the same time. Calcineurin signaling restrains outgrowth: its inhibition by FK506 causes overgrowth (Kujawski and colleagues 2014), and lets a cut surface that faces proximally, which normally does not regenerate, form a blastema (Cao and colleagues 2021).

### 35.5.2. The Heart

When about 20% of the ventricle is resected, a fibrin clot closes the wound, and within about 60 days the missing muscle is replaced without a scar (Poss, Wilson and Keating 2002). Genetic **lineage tracing** with cardiomyocyte-specific Cre lines showed that the new muscle comes from pre-existing cardiomyocytes, which partly disassemble their **sarcomeres**, re-enter the **cell cycle** and divide (Jopling and colleagues, Kikuchi and colleagues 2010); multicolor clonal labeling showed that a few cardiomyocyte clones regenerate most of the wound (Gupta and Poss 2012). The epicardium is activated throughout the heart within days (expressing *raldh2*, *tbx18*), covers the wound and supplies RA, FGF and PDGF signals and perivascular cells; endocardium and macrophages are also required. Cardiomyocytes far from the wound also increase their proliferation, a compensatory response. In larvae, after genetic ablation of almost all ventricular cardiomyocytes (with nitroreductase, which converts the prodrug metronidazole into a toxin), atrial cardiomyocytes migrate into the ventricle, switch on ventricular genes and rebuild it, a Notch-dependent **transdifferentiation** (Zhang and colleagues 2013). One organ thus uses epimorphosis, compensatory proliferation and **transdifferentiation**.

### 35.5.3. Liver and Kidney

The same nitroreductase–metronidazole system can kill all hepatocytes of a larva. Biliary epithelial cells then dedifferentiate into bipotent progenitors that make new hepatocytes, a lineage conversion that is normally negligible (He and colleagues 2014; Choi and colleagues 2014). The zebrafish kidney, unlike the mammalian one, forms new nephrons after injury throughout life, from clusters of **progenitor cells** (neonephrogenesis; Diep and colleagues 2011).

## 35.6. Mammals

Mammals regenerate few complex structures (deer antlers, which regrow every year from a blastema-like tissue derived from periosteum, and the tips of digits are the exceptions) but many tissues regrow or repair well. Their strategy is less "rebuild from scratch" than "make what remains bigger".

### 35.6.1. The Liver

The standard assay is the partial hepatectomy of Higgins and Anderson (1931): two of the five lobes, about two thirds of the liver, are removed from a rat or mouse. The removed lobes do not grow back; the remaining ones enlarge until the original mass is restored, in about a week in rodents and in weeks to months in humans, which is why living donors can give part of their liver for transplantation. Up to about 75% can be removed. This is compensatory regeneration: hepatocytes, normally quiescent, re-enter the cycle once or twice, followed by the biliary, endothelial, stellate and Kupffer cells; each type keeps its identity and the liver keeps working while it grows. The loss is sensed through the blood: **lipopolysaccharide** and **bile acids** reaching the remnant activate Kupffer cells to secrete IL-6 and TNF-α, which prime hepatocytes; stellate and endothelial cells secrete HGF (whose receptor Met is activated within an hour; its **deletion** blocks regeneration), Wnt2 and other **mitogens**; **bile acids** act through the **nuclear receptor** FXR. Growth stops at the right size: the liver-to-body mass ratio is restored, a liver transplanted into a larger or smaller recipient grows or shrinks to fit, and in parabiosis, hepatectomy of one rat makes the liver of the other grow (Moolten and Bucher 1967). The Hippo–**YAP** pathway is part of this set point ([7.5.34](/7.5.34)).

**Formula 35.6.1 (Logistic Recovery of Liver Mass)** Let $M(t)$ be the liver mass as a fraction of its original value, $m_0 = M(0)$ after resection. Model a priming phase of length $t_{\mathrm{lag}}$ without growth, followed by growth proportional to the mass and to the remaining deficit,

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}M}{\mathrm{d}t} = rM\Big(1 - \frac{M}{M^*}\Big),\qquad
M(t) = \frac{M^*}{1 + \dfrac{M^* - m_0}{m_0}\,\mathrm{e}^{-r(t - t_{\mathrm{lag}})}}\quad(t \ge t_{\mathrm{lag}}),
$$
where $M^* = 1$ is the set point fixed by body size and $r$ the maximal growth rate. The time to recover 90% of the original mass is

$$
t_{90} = t_{\mathrm{lag}} + \frac1r\ln\frac{9(1 - m_0)}{m_0}.
$$

**Example 35.6.1** With $t_{\mathrm{lag}} = 1$ d and $r = 0.45\ \mathrm{d^{-1}}$, a two-thirds hepatectomy ($m_0 = 1/3$) gives $t_{90} = 1 + \ln 18/0.45 \approx 7.4$ d, and a one-third resection ($m_0 = 2/3$) gives $1 + \ln 4.5/0.45 \approx 4.3$ d, of the order of the times observed in mice. The model treats regeneration as a feedback towards a set point and nothing more. It also has a measurable limit: after a one-third resection, mouse hepatocytes restore the mass mainly by enlarging, with few divisions, whereas after two thirds they both enlarge and divide about once (Miyaoka and colleagues 2012), so the response is not simply proportional to the deficit.

A: liver mass after removal of one third or two thirds, in the logistic model with a one-day priming phase (Formula 35.6.1, r = 0.45 per day); points are simulated measurements with 4 % noise; ticks mark the time to recover 90 % of the original mass (Example 35.6.1). B: the order of events after a two-thirds hepatectomy in the mouse (schematic): priming by cytokines and growth factors in the first day, a synchronous wave of hepatocyte DNA synthesis peaking at about 36-40 h, non-parenchymal cells about a day later, and growth that slows as the mass approaches its set point](./plots/liver-regeneration.py)

Which cells make new hepatocytes after injury depends on the injury. After hepatectomy and most toxic injuries, hepatocytes themselves do; genetic tracing in mice shows little contribution from biliary cells or putative liver **stem cells** ("oval cells"). When hepatocyte proliferation is blocked (by senescence, chronic damage or experimentally, for example by deleting **Mdm2** or overexpressing **p21**), biliary epithelial cells proliferate in a "ductular reaction" and can generate hepatocytes (Raven and colleagues 2017). The choice of fate of bipotent progenitors follows the injury: after biliary injury, myofibroblasts present Jagged1 and activate Notch in the progenitors, which become biliary cells; after hepatocyte injury, macrophages that have engulfed hepatocyte debris secrete Wnt3a, which induces the Notch inhibitor Numb in the progenitors, which become hepatocytes (Boulter and colleagues 2012). The same Notch–Jagged choice separates hepatocytes from cholangiocytes in development ([7.5.30](/7.5.30)).

### 35.6.2. The Heart

Mammalian hearts heal infarcts with a fibrotic scar, but the newborn mouse heart regenerates: after removal of about 15% of the ventricular apex at postnatal day 1, the muscle is restored within 21 days, by proliferation of pre-existing cardiomyocytes that partly disassemble their **sarcomeres**; by day 7 the capacity is lost and a scar forms (Porrello and colleagues 2011). The loss coincides with cardiomyocytes leaving the cycle and becoming polyploid.

| Species | Adult cardiomyocytes | Regeneration |
|---|---|---|
| Zebrafish, newt | mononuclear, **diploid** (over 95%) | lifelong |
| Mouse | over 75% binucleate (two diploid nuclei) | first postnatal week only |
| Human | mostly mononuclear, mostly polyploid (tetraploid or more) | turnover below 1% per year in adults; infarcts scar |

Several causes are under study, and they are not exclusive. **Polyploidy**: across inbred mouse strains, the fraction of mononuclear **diploid** cardiomyocytes, which varies from about 2% to 10% and depends partly on the kinase gene *Tnni3k*, predicts how well the heart recovers after injury (Patterson and colleagues 2017). Endothermy and thyroid hormone: across vertebrates the fraction of diploid cardiomyocytes falls as thyroid hormone levels and body temperature rise, and inactivating thyroid hormone signaling in mouse cardiomyocytes keeps them proliferative (Hirose and colleagues 2019). Oxygen: the switch from the hypoxic fetal environment to air breathing raises **reactive oxygen species** and DNA damage, which arrest the cycle (Puente and colleagues 2014). The **extracellular matrix**: the matrix protein agrin, abundant in the newborn heart, promotes cardiomyocyte division (Bassat and colleagues 2017). In adult humans, radiocarbon from atmospheric nuclear tests incorporated into DNA showed that cardiomyocytes are renewed at about 1% per year at age 25, falling to about 0.45% at 75 (Bergmann and colleagues 2009): fewer than half of the cardiomyocytes are replaced over a lifetime.

### 35.6.3. Other Tissues

| Tissue | Response in mammals | Source and signals |
|---|---|---|
| Digit tip | regenerates (mouse, children) if amputated distal to the nail bed | lineage-restricted blastema; *Msx1*-expressing cells under the nail organ; BMP, Wnt |
| Kidney | tubules repair, nephrons are not replaced (remaining ones hypertrophy) | surviving tubular epithelial cells dedifferentiate and divide, repeating developmental programs |
| Pancreas | β cells renew slowly | self-duplication of β cells (Dor and colleagues 2004); after near-total β-cell loss, α cells (adults) or δ cells (juveniles) transdifferentiate into β cells (Thorel 2010; Chera 2014) |

| Spinal cord | no regeneration; glial scar | fish and salamanders: ependymo-radial glia make **neurons** and a glial bridge (connective tissue growth factor) |
| Peripheral nerve | axons regrow about 1 mm per day | distal axon degenerates; Schwann cells dedifferentiate into repair cells (c-Jun) that guide regrowth with macrophages |
| Blood vessels | endothelium repaired | neighboring endothelial cells; in zebrafish brain, lymphatic vessels serve as tracks for new blood vessels |
| Thymus, adrenal cortex, thyroid, lung | limited, decline with age | resident progenitors (lung basal cells and alveolar type II cells) or division of differentiated cells |

**Example 35.6.2 (Digit Tips)** Mice, and young children, regrow the tip of a digit if the amputation leaves part of the nail organ; amputation more proximally ends in a scar. The regenerating region corresponds to the domain where the **homeobox** gene *Msx1* stays expressed after birth, as at the tip of the embryonic limb bud. **Lineage tracing** shows that the blastema is a mixture of restricted progenitors, as in the salamander: *Sox9*-lineage cells make only bone, epidermis comes from epidermis (Lehoczky and colleagues 2011; Rinkevich and colleagues 2011). BMP is required, and applied to a proximal amputation it induces a partial regenerative response. Since the same distal rule and the same lineage restriction hold in mammals and salamanders, the incapacity of mammals is not a lack of the basic machinery.

### 35.6.4. Why Can't We Regenerate Limbs?

The phylogenetic distribution of regeneration is patchy: closely related annelids, planarians or salamanders differ, and regeneration has been lost many times. But it has also been elaborated locally: Prod1 is a salamander-specific protein, and the newt genome contains other genes without mammalian homologs whose expression changes during regeneration, so salamander limb regeneration is in part a salamander invention rather than an ancestral vertebrate ability that mammals lost. The other differences are probably quantitative: fibrosis and inflammation that close wounds quickly (an advantage for a large, endothermic animal that cannot afford infection or blood loss), polyploid and postmitotic differentiated cells, the absence of nerve-dependent growth factors, and the size and time involved, since a human limb would take years to regrow at the rates of a blastema. Regenerative medicine therefore pursues several routes in parallel: modulating the wound response (reducing fibrosis), reactivating cell division in cardiomyocytes or **neurons**, and supplying cells or engineered tissues, the subject of [7.5.36](/7.5.36).

## Questions

1. Distinguish morphallaxis, epimorphosis and compensatory regeneration, and give for each an experiment that would show which one is at work in a regenerating organ.

   **Answer** Morphallaxis repatterns existing cells without proliferation; epimorphosis forms a proliferating blastema of dedifferentiated or stem cells; compensatory regeneration is division of differentiated cells that keep their function. Tests: block proliferation (hydroxyurea, irradiation) and see whether a smaller complete structure still forms (morphallaxis) or regeneration fails (epimorphosis, compensatory growth); pulse with EdU and stain for differentiation markers in the dividing cells (dividing cells that express hepatocyte markers indicate compensatory regeneration; dividing cells that have lost differentiation markers and form a mass at the wound indicate a blastema); lineage-trace differentiated cells with an inducible Cre before injury to see whether they give the new tissue.

2. A piece of subhypostomal tissue grafted halfway down an intact *Hydra* forms a head only rarely, but always if the host was decapitated three hours earlier, and rarely again if the host was decapitated 48 hours earlier. Explain with the activator–inhibitor model.

   **Answer** The intact head produces an inhibitor that reaches the graft site, keeping the graft below the switch threshold. After decapitation inhibitor production stops and the labile inhibitor (half-life 2–3 h) decays, so at 3 h the graft is released and forms a head. By 48 h the host has regenerated a new head (within about a day or two), which again produces inhibitor, so the graft is inhibited once more. The time course of graft success after decapitation is a measure of the inhibitor's decay and of the time to re-establish a head.

3. In Formula 35.2.1 with $D = 0.2$ (in squared units of polyp length), estimate the length beyond which a polyp should bud, and compare with the simulation, in which a bud appears between lengths 1.2 and 1.5. Why does the bud not form at the foot?

   **Answer** The inhibitor range is $\sqrt D \approx 0.45$. A second peak needs the inhibition from the head, $\propto \mathrm{e}^{-x/0.45}$, to fall well below its value near the head; a factor of about 10 is reached at $x \approx 0.45 \ln 10 \approx 1.0$. Allowing for the zone near the foot where activation is suppressed (**decay length** 0.1, factor 0.7), the earliest site lies at about 1.0–1.1, so a polyp of about 1.2–1.5 can bud, as in the simulation (bud at $x \approx 1.1$ in a polyp of length 1.5, about three quarters of the way down). The bud does not form at the foot because the source density is reduced there (foot inhibition), as in the real animal, where buds form above the basal disc.

4. In the planarian, *notum* is the only gene among thousands expressed differently in anterior- and posterior-facing blastemas at the start of regeneration. Design experiments to show that it is necessary, and whether it is sufficient, for head identity.

   **Answer** Necessity: knock down *notum* by RNAi, amputate head and tail, and score the anterior-facing blastema (it forms a tail, with posterior markers such as *wntP-2* and without eyes and brain). Also combine *notum* RNAi with partial *β-catenin-1* knockdown, which should rescue heads if Notum acts by inhibiting Wnt. Sufficiency is harder because the gene is induced by wounding: one can ask whether ectopically raising Notum activity (or applying a Wnt inhibitor) at a posterior-facing wound gives a head; the equivalent experiment, *β-catenin-1* RNAi, does convert tails into heads, which shows that lowering Wnt is sufficient. Lineage and timing: check by in situ **hybridization** that *notum* is expressed in muscle cells at the anterior wound before neoblast-derived head tissue appears.

5. In the experiment of Wagner and colleagues, 7 of 130 lethally irradiated planarians were rescued by one transplanted neoblast. What conclusions can and cannot be drawn about the fraction of neoblasts that are pluripotent?

   **Answer** It proves that at least some single neoblasts are pluripotent and can rebuild every tissue. It does not estimate their frequency: the 5% success includes failures of transplantation (the cell may die, fail to engraft or not be transferred), and the donor cells were selected as clonogenic (they were cells that had formed colonies after sublethal irradiation), not random neoblasts. A frequency requires single-cell transplants of defined subpopulations with transplantation efficiency measured separately; such work later enriched pluripotent cells by the marker Tspan-1.

6. A right forelimb blastema of a newt is grafted onto a left forelimb stump, (a) keeping the dorso-ventral axis, (b) keeping the antero-posterior axis. Predict with the polar coordinate model where supernumerary limbs form and what handedness they have.

   **Answer** (a) Antero-posterior is reversed: $g = -h$ with dorsal 12 and anterior 3; singular points where $g - h = \pm6$, at $h = 3$ and $9$, so two supernumeraries at the anterior and posterior positions (Example 35.4.2). (b) Dorso-ventral is reversed: $g = 6 - h$, so $g - h = 6 - 2h = \pm6$ at $h = 0$ and $h = 6$: two supernumeraries at the dorsal and ventral positions. In both cases the indices are $+1$ each (Theorem 35.4.1: total $\deg h - \deg g = 2$), so both supernumeraries have the handedness of the stump (left), with the graft itself forming a right limb between them: three limbs in all, as observed.

7. Retinoic acid given to a limb amputated at the wrist makes it regenerate a complete arm. Why is this called proximalization rather than an extra limb, and how does the chimeric-receptor experiment show that the effect acts on individual cells?

   **Answer** The regenerate starts with structures proximal to the plane of amputation (humerus, radius, ulna) and continues to the hand: the blastema behaves as if it had the **positional value** of the shoulder, so its value was shifted proximally; no new axis or extra limb is formed. In the experiment, only some cells of a distal blastema carry the chimeric receptor, which activates RA signaling when thyroxine is given; after grafting to a proximal stump and adding thyroxine, these cells (and not their untransfected neighbors) end up in proximal parts of the regenerate. The shift in **positional value** is therefore a property of the cells that receive the signal (cell-autonomous), read out by their sorting during intercalation.

8. Use Formula 35.6.1 to estimate $r$ for a human living-donor liver that regrows from 40% to 90% of its original volume in 8 weeks, assuming no lag. Is the logistic model a good description of growth in humans?

   **Answer** $t_{90} = \ln\bigl(9(1 - m_0)/m_0\bigr)/r$ with $m_0 = 0.4$: $\ln(9 \times 1.5) = \ln 13.5 \approx 2.60$, so $r \approx 2.60/56\ \mathrm{d} \approx 0.046\ \mathrm{d^{-1}}$, about ten times slower than in mice. Clinical volumetry shows that most growth occurs in the first one or two weeks and then slows, often stopping short of the original volume, so the logistic curve captures the deceleration but not the precise shape; the set point is the volume required by the body (liver-to-body weight ratio), which may be lower than the original volume.

9. Mouse cardiomyocytes lose the ability to proliferate in the first postnatal week. List the hypotheses that have been proposed and an experiment that distinguishes each.

   **Answer** Polyploidization and binucleation (test: increase the fraction of mononuclear diploid cells genetically, for example via *Tnni3k*, and measure regeneration after injury in adults); thyroid hormone and endothermy (test: cardiomyocyte-specific inactivation of thyroid hormone receptor signaling, which preserved proliferation and regeneration); oxygen and DNA damage after birth (test: keep neonates in hypoxia or scavenge mitochondrial ROS and see whether the window extends); **extracellular matrix** changes (test: supply agrin to adult hearts, or remove it from neonatal hearts). Each manipulation is followed by EdU labeling of cardiomyocytes, **cytokinesis** markers (not just DNA synthesis, which can give polyploidy), lineage tracing and scar size.

10. (Open question) Why might **natural selection** favor scarring rather than regeneration in mammals, and what kind of evidence could test the idea?

    **Answer** Points: fast closure by fibrosis limits blood loss and infection in large, active, endothermic animals; regeneration of large structures would take long and consume energy; postmitotic, polyploid cells may protect against **cancer** in long-lived animals; immune systems geared to fight infection promote fibrosis. Tests: comparative studies across species with different body size, endothermy and immune profiles (the spiny mouse regenerates skin and ear tissue, and its macrophage responses differ from those of laboratory mice); phylogenetic analyses of gains and losses of regenerative ability; experiments that reduce inflammation or fibrosis in mammals and measure regeneration and infection risk.
