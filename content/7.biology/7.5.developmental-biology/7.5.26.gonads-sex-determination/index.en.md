**Sex determination** decides whether an embryo develops as a male or a female; its chromosomal and genetic basis, the X and Y chromosomes, the sex-determining genes of mammals, flies and nematodes and the inheritance of sex-linked traits, are the subject of [7.4.6](/7.4.6). This chapter is about the developmental execution of the decision in vertebrates, above all in mammals. The decision is made in a single organ, the gonad, whose rudiment is unique among organ primordia in that it can become either of two very different organs, a testis or an ovary. Once formed, the gonad secretes hormones that make the rest of the body male or female: the genital ducts, the external genitalia, and later the secondary sexual characteristics. The two steps, a nearly digital choice in the gonad and a more graded hormonal control of the body, explain the variety of human differences of sex development, and the comparison with birds, reptiles and fishes shows how flexibly the same molecular switch can be triggered. The gonad arises from the intermediate mesoderm of [7.5.25](/7.5.25); the germ cells that colonize it, and the decision between egg and sperm, follow in [7.5.27](/7.5.27); the reproductive endocrinology of the adult is treated in [7.14](/7.14).

## 26.1. The Bipotential Gonad

### 26.1.1. The Genital Ridge

**Definition 26.1.1 (Genital Ridge and Bipotential Gonad)** The **genital ridge** is the thickening of the coelomic epithelium and the underlying mesenchyme on the ventromedial surface of the mesonephros from which the gonad develops. Until sex determination its somatic cells are **bipotential**: the same rudiment can form a testis or an ovary, and the gonad at this stage is called the indifferent or **bipotential gonad**.

In the human embryo the genital ridges appear in week 4–5; primordial germ cells, which arise far away at the base of the allantois, migrate into them during weeks 5–6 ([7.5.27](/7.5.27)); and the gonad remains morphologically indifferent until about week 7. In the mouse the ridge forms at about embryonic day 10 (E10) and sex determination occurs around E11.5. The ridge grows by proliferation of the coelomic epithelium, whose cells ingress into the mesenchyme and provide the supporting cells of the future gonad. A set of transcription factors is needed to form the ridge at all; mice lacking any of them have no gonads, and their loss in humans causes gonadal agenesis or dysgenesis.

| Factor | Role in the bipotential gonad | Loss in mouse or human |
|---|---|---|
| WT1 (Wilms tumour 1) | survival and proliferation of the coelomic epithelium; the isoform with the tripeptide KTS (+KTS) is needed for *Sry* expression | no gonads or kidneys; Frasier syndrome (+KTS loss): XY gonadal dysgenesis; Denys–Drash syndrome |
| SF1 (NR5A1, steroidogenic factor 1) | gonad and adrenal formation; later steroidogenesis and *Amh* | no gonads or adrenals; human XY DSD, primary ovarian insufficiency |
| LHX9, EMX2 | proliferation of the coelomic epithelium | no gonads |
| GATA4 with its cofactor FOG2 | *Sry* expression | XY sex reversal |
| CBX2 (a Polycomb protein) | *Sry* expression, repression of ovarian genes | XY sex reversal |

### 26.1.2. Bipotential Cell Lineages

The somatic cells of the gonad belong to two bipotential lineages, each with a male and a female fate:

| Lineage | Testis | Ovary | Function |
|---|---|---|---|
| Supporting cells (from the coelomic epithelium) | Sertoli cells | granulosa cells | enclose and nourish the germ cells; make AMH (testis) or, with theca cells, estrogens (ovary) |
| Steroidogenic (interstitial) cells | Leydig cells | theca cells | make androgens |
| Others | peritubular myoid cells, endothelium of the coelomic vessel | stroma | structure and vascularization |

The decision is made in the supporting cells and spreads from them. Lineage tracing with a reporter of *Sry* expression in XY mouse gonads showed that the cells that express *Sry* become Sertoli cells, and that in XX gonads the corresponding cells become granulosa cells (Albrecht and Eicher 2001); in chimeric XX↔XY gonads that develop as testes, the Sertoli cells are mostly XY, while the other cell types are of either genotype. The Sertoli cells, once specified, recruit and instruct the rest.

## 26.2. Primary Sex Determination in Mammals

**Definition 26.2.1 (Primary and Secondary Sex Determination)** **Primary sex determination** is the determination of the gonad as a testis or an ovary. **Secondary sex determination** is the development of the sexual phenotype outside the gonads (the genital ducts, the external genitalia and the secondary sexual characteristics), which in mammals is controlled by hormones from the gonads.

### 26.2.1. SRY

In mammals primary sex determination is chromosomal. XXY individuals (Klinefelter syndrome, about 1 in 600 male births) are male and XO individuals (Turner syndrome, about 1 in 2500 female births) are female, so a testis-determining factor lies on the Y ([7.4.6](/7.4.6)). It was located by mapping the Y fragments carried by XX males and missing from XY females, which narrowed it to a 35 kb region near the tip of the short arm containing a gene, *SRY* (sex-determining region of the Y), that encodes a transcription factor of the HMG-box family (Sinclair et al. 1990; the human protein has 204 amino acids). Three kinds of evidence identify *SRY* as the testis-determining gene.

1. Correlation: *SRY* is present in XX males (translocated to an X by an aberrant crossover in the father's meiosis) and absent or mutated in many XY females; about 10–15% of XY women with complete gonadal dysgenesis (Swyer syndrome) carry point mutations in the HMG box that abolish DNA binding.
2. Expression: in the mouse, *Sry* is expressed only in the supporting cells of the XY genital ridge, from about E10.5 to E12.5, peaking at E11.5, just before Sertoli cells differentiate; it is then switched off.
3. Sufficiency: XX mouse embryos transgenic for a 14 kb genomic fragment containing *Sry* develop testes and male internal and external genitalia (Koopman et al. 1991). They are sterile, because two X chromosomes and the absence of the other Y genes prevent spermatogenesis.

### 26.2.2. SOX9 and Its Enhancers

SRY acts for only a few hours, and its main target is the autosomal gene *Sox9*, which encodes another HMG-box transcription factor and is the ancient, conserved testis gene of vertebrates (SRY exists only in therian mammals; it evolved from SOX3). In XY mouse gonads, *Sox9* is up-regulated just after *Sry*; SRY and SF1 bind its enhancers. A 1.3 kb testis-specific enhancer core (TESCO), 13 kb upstream, was identified first (Sekido and Lovell-Badge 2008), but deleting it reduces *Sox9* only partly. The essential element lies much further away: a 557 bp enhancer about 565 kb upstream (Enh13), bound by SRY and SOX9, whose deletion alone causes complete XY sex reversal in mice (Gonen et al. 2018). In humans, duplications or deletions of the corresponding far-upstream region cause XX male or XY female development without any change in the *SOX9* coding sequence.

SOX9 is necessary and sufficient for the testis.

- Gain: XX mice transgenic for *Sox9* develop testes (the *Odsex* insertion that activates *Sox9* in XX gonads; Bishop et al. 2000), and XX humans with a duplication of *SOX9* are male.
- Loss: XY mice with *Sox9* deleted in the gonad develop ovaries, although *Sry* is expressed; heterozygous loss of *SOX9* in humans causes campomelic dysplasia, a skeletal disorder ([7.5.24](/7.5.24#sec-24.7)), and about three quarters of XY patients develop as females.

SOX9 then makes the decision permanent and collective through positive feedback loops: it activates its own gene; it induces *Fgf9*, whose product, signalling through FGFR2, maintains *Sox9* and represses *Wnt4*; and it induces prostaglandin D synthase, whose product PGD2 acts on neighbouring cells to recruit SOX9 to the nucleus. These paracrine loops act as a community effect that recruits supporting cells that expressed little *Sry* into the Sertoli fate. *Fgf9*$^{-/-}$ XY mice develop as females. SOX9 also activates *Amh* together with SF1, WT1 and GATA4.

### 26.2.3. The Ovarian Pathway

The ovary is not formed by default. XX gonads actively express ovary-promoting genes that repress the testis program.

- WNT4 and R-spondin 1 (RSPO1), a secreted potentiator of Wnt signalling, activate β-catenin. XX mice lacking *Wnt4* or *Rspo1* develop partially masculinized gonads that express *Sox9*, *Amh* and steroidogenic enzymes (and lose their germ cells); humans homozygous for *RSPO1* mutations are XX males (with a skin disease, palmoplantar hyperkeratosis; Parma et al. 2006); XY individuals with a duplication of the region containing *WNT4* and *RSPO1* develop as females; stabilized β-catenin in XY gonads blocks testis formation.
- FOXL2, a forkhead transcription factor of granulosa cells, acts in parallel: XX mice lacking both *Wnt4* and *Foxl2* (or *Rspo1* and *Foxl2*) develop testis-like gonads with Sertoli cells (Ottolenghi et al. 2007); a mutation that silences *FOXL2* causes XX sex reversal in goats (polled intersex syndrome).
- DAX1 (NR0B1), on the X chromosome, antagonizes testis development in a dose-dependent way: duplication of the region containing it (DSS) causes XY sex reversal, by interfering with SF1 and SRY. Yet DAX1 is also needed for normal testis cords; it modulates both pathways rather than being an ovary-determining gene.

The two pathways inhibit each other: SOX9 and FGF9 repress *Wnt4* and β-catenin; β-catenin and FOXL2 repress *Sox9*. A pathway for cell specification thus has two components, "make a testis" and "do not make an ovary", and conversely.

### 26.2.4. A Bistable Switch with a Time Window

Timing is essential. On some genetic backgrounds the *Sry* allele of one mouse strain fails to make testes because it is expressed too late or too weakly (the *Mus domesticus* Y on the C57BL/6 background gives XY ovaries or ovotestes; Eicher and Washburn 1986). With *Sry* placed under a heat-inducible promoter, testis formation succeeded when *Sry* was induced at the normal time and failed, with ovaries or ovotestes forming, when induction was delayed by only about 6 hours (Hiramatsu et al. 2009). Why should a few hours matter? Mutual inhibition with positive feedback makes a **bistable** switch ([7.3.18](/7.3.18)), and the ovarian side gains momentum with time.

**Formula 26.2.1 (A Toggle Switch for Gonadal Fate)** Let $S$ be the activity of the testis module (SOX9 with its FGF9 and PGD2 loops) and $W$ that of the ovary module (WNT4/RSPO1/β-catenin with FOXL2) in a supporting cell, in units of their half-maximal levels. Each activates itself (Hill coefficient 2) and represses the other:

$$
\dot S = \frac{\alpha_S + \frac{S^2}{1 + S^2}}{1 + (W/K)^2}\,\beta + \sigma(t) - S,\qquad \dot W = \frac{\alpha_W + \frac{W^2}{1 + W^2}}{1 + (S/K)^2}\,\beta - W,
\tag{26.1}
$$
with basal rates $\alpha_S < \alpha_W$ (the ovarian genes are expressed in both sexes at first), maximal synthesis $\beta$, repression constant $K$, and an input $\sigma(t)$ from SRY: a pulse of amplitude $A$ from time $t_0$ to $t_0 + \Delta$ in XY cells, zero in XX cells. Without input, the system has two stable states, testis ($S$ high, $W$ low) and ovary ($W$ high), separated by a saddle; started from the bipotential state (both low), it drifts towards the ovarian state.

The model shows three properties of the real decision. A strong and early SRY pulse pushes the cell across the separatrix into the testis basin; after the pulse ends, the positive feedback keeps SOX9 on: SRY is needed only transiently. A pulse of the same amplitude given later finds $W$ already high, and repression by $W$ prevents $S$ from crossing the separatrix: the required amplitude rises steeply with delay, a time window. And because the outcome is all-or-none in each cell, cell-to-cell variation in SRY level gives a mixture of Sertoli and granulosa cells when SRY is marginal or late, an ovotestis, unless paracrine FGF9 and PGD2 recruit the hesitant cells.

### 26.2.5. Building the Testis and the Ovary

Once Sertoli cells are specified, testis morphogenesis is fast. Sertoli cells proliferate, polarize and aggregate around the germ cells into testis cords, the future seminiferous tubules, connected to the rete testis near the mesonephros. FGF9 and other Sertoli signals induce endothelial cells to migrate from the mesonephros into the XY gonad and form the coelomic vessel, the major artery of the testis: when mesonephroi from mice that express *lacZ* in every cell are recombined with unlabelled gonads, labelled cells migrate into XY gonads (or XX gonads carrying an *Sry* transgene, or incubated with FGF9) but not into XX gonads, and without this migration testis cords do not form (Capel and colleagues). Peritubular myoid cells surround the cords. Sertoli cells induce the differentiation of fetal Leydig cells in the interstitium by Desert hedgehog (Dhh) and PDGF; Leydig cells make testosterone. Germ cells in the testis stop dividing and do not enter meiosis until puberty, because Sertoli cells degrade retinoic acid with CYP26B1 and produce FGF9, which keep them as prospermatogonia (Bowles et al. 2006; [7.5.27](/7.5.27)).

In the XX gonad nothing happens morphologically at first. The medullary cords degenerate and cortical cords form from the coelomic epithelium; each germ cell becomes enclosed by granulosa cells, forming a primordial follicle, and the surrounding stroma provides theca cells. Germ cells in the ovary enter meiosis in the fetus (in humans from about week 10) and arrest in prophase I. Germ cells are needed to maintain the ovary: without oocytes the follicles degenerate into cord-like structures that express male markers, whereas testis cords form without germ cells.

### 26.2.6. Maintenance of Gonadal Sex

Primary sex determination is not irreversible. When *Foxl2* is deleted in the ovaries of adult mice, granulosa cells reprogram into Sertoli-like cells that express *Sox9*, the follicles become tubule-like and theca cells become Leydig-like cells that make testosterone (Uhlenhaut et al. 2009). Conversely, deletion of the transcription factor gene *Dmrt1* in the testes of adult mice turns Sertoli cells into granulosa-like cells expressing *Foxl2* (Matson et al. 2011), and forced expression of *Dmrt1* in the ovary masculinizes it. FOXL2 and DMRT1 maintain the two fates throughout life by mutual antagonism, the adult form of the switch. DMRT1, a relative of the fly gene *doublesex*, is the male determinant of many animals (Section 26.5); in mammals SRY has taken over the initial decision, but DMRT1 keeps a role in maintenance.

## 26.3. Secondary Sex Determination

### 26.3.1. The Genital Ducts

The body outside the gonads is sexually neutral at first and is instructed by gonadal hormones. Alfred Jost (1947, 1953) removed the gonads of rabbit fetuses before they had differentiated: all developed as females, whatever their chromosomes, so the female phenotype does not require ovaries in the fetus, while the male phenotype requires testes. Grafting a testis beside the ovary of a female fetus, or castrating one side of a male fetus, showed that the testis acts locally on the ducts of its own side and through two separate factors: crystals of testosterone placed beside the ducts maintained the Wolffian duct but did not cause regression of the Müllerian duct, which required a second testicular substance.

**Definition 26.3.1 (Müllerian Duct)** The **Müllerian duct** (paramesonephric duct) is an epithelial duct that forms by invagination of the coelomic epithelium alongside the Wolffian duct, in both sexes, and grows caudally guided by it, to open into the urogenital sinus. In females it forms the oviducts, the uterus, the cervix and the upper part of the vagina; in males it regresses. The Wolffian duct (Definition 25.2.1) forms the epididymis, the vas deferens and the seminal vesicles in males and regresses in females.

**Definition 26.3.2 (Anti-Müllerian Hormone)** **Anti-Müllerian hormone** (AMH; Müllerian-inhibiting substance) is a glycoprotein of the TGF-β family, secreted by Sertoli cells from early testis differentiation, that binds the type II receptor AMHR2 on the mesenchyme surrounding the Müllerian duct and causes the duct epithelium to regress by apoptosis and epithelial–mesenchymal transition, during a critical window (in humans about weeks 8–10).

The male internal tract therefore needs both hormones: AMH removes the Müllerian duct and testosterone, from fetal Leydig cells, maintains and differentiates the Wolffian duct. Men with mutations in *AMH* or *AMHR2* have persistent Müllerian duct syndrome: they are normal males with a uterus and oviducts, often discovered at surgery for undescended testes. The regression of the Wolffian duct in females is not merely the absence of testosterone; it is actively promoted by the transcription factor COUP-TFII (NR2F2) in the duct mesenchyme, which represses the response to androgens (Zhao et al. 2017), so neither sex is simply a default. Estrogen, made by the fetal ovary and the placenta, is needed for the full postnatal development of the Müllerian derivatives; female mice lacking estrogen receptors still form a uterus and oviducts, so their fetal differentiation is largely estrogen-independent, but the adult tract is hypoplastic and infertile. Estrogen is needed in males too: efferent duct cells, under estrogen, resorb most of the fluid from the rete testis and so concentrate the sperm; male mice lacking estrogen receptor α are infertile (Hess et al. 1997).

### 26.3.2. Testosterone, Dihydrotestosterone and the External Genitalia

Testosterone does not act directly on all male structures. In the urogenital sinus and the genital tubercle, the enzyme 5α-reductase type 2 (*SRD5A2*) converts testosterone into dihydrotestosterone (DHT), which binds the androgen receptor more tightly and is needed for the prostate, the penis and the scrotum; the Wolffian duct, which lacks the enzyme at this stage, responds to testosterone itself. The external genitalia begin as a genital tubercle, urethral folds and labioscrotal swellings, identical in both sexes until about week 9. Under DHT, the tubercle elongates into the penis, the urethral folds fuse to enclose the penile urethra and the labioscrotal swellings fuse into the scrotum (weeks 9–14); without androgens they become the clitoris, the labia minora and the labia majora. Androgen receptor in the mesenchyme acts in part by suppressing the Wnt antagonist Dickkopf, so that Wnt/β-catenin signalling drives growth of the male structures. Incomplete fusion of the urethral folds causes hypospadias, one of the commonest birth defects (about 1 in 200–300 male births).

The dependence on DHT is shown by 5α-reductase 2 deficiency, an autosomal recessive condition first studied in families in the Dominican Republic (Imperato-McGinley et al. 1974): XY children are born with female-appearing or ambiguous external genitalia, internal male ducts and testes, and at puberty, when testosterone rises, the phallus enlarges and they virilize. Complete androgen insensitivity (loss of the X-linked androgen receptor) gives women with testes and no uterus, because AMH acts normally ([7.4.6](/7.4.6)). Excess androgen in a female fetus, most often from congenital adrenal hyperplasia (21-hydroxylase deficiency, in which cortisol precursors are diverted into adrenal androgens), virilizes the external genitalia of XX infants with normal ovaries and Müllerian structures.

### 26.3.3. Differences of Sex Development

**Definition 26.3.3 (Differences of Sex Development)** **Differences (or disorders) of sex development** (DSD) are congenital conditions in which the development of chromosomal, gonadal or anatomical sex is atypical. The current nomenclature (Chicago consensus 2006) classifies them as sex chromosome DSD, 46,XY DSD and 46,XX DSD, replacing the older terms "hermaphrodite" and "pseudohermaphrodite".

| Class | Examples | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| Sex chromosome DSD | 45,X (Turner) and 47,XXY (Klinefelter) syndromes; 45,X/46,XY mosaicism (mixed gonadal dysgenesis) | aneuploidy, mosaicism ([7.4.6](/7.4.6)) |
| 46,XY DSD: gonadal development | *SRY* mutation (Swyer syndrome), *SF1*, *WT1* (Denys–Drash, Frasier), *SOX9* (campomelic dysplasia), *DHH*, *MAP3K1*, duplication of *DAX1* or *WNT4*/*RSPO1* | failure of testis determination; streak gonads, female phenotype with Müllerian structures |
| 46,XY DSD: androgen synthesis or action | Leydig cell hypoplasia (LH receptor), 17β-HSD3 deficiency, 5α-reductase 2 deficiency, androgen insensitivity | testes present; undervirilization |
| 46,XY DSD: other | persistent Müllerian duct syndrome (*AMH*, *AMHR2*), hypospadias | |
| 46,XX DSD: gonadal development | *SRY* translocation (XX male), *SOX9* or *SOX3* duplication, *RSPO1* or *WNT4* loss; ovotesticular DSD | testis or ovotestis in an XX individual |
| 46,XX DSD: androgen excess | congenital adrenal hyperplasia, placental aromatase deficiency, maternal androgens | virilization with ovaries |

Ambiguous genitalia at birth occur in about 1 in 4500 births; the frequency of DSD as a whole depends on the definition (hypospadias and cryptorchidism alone are much commoner). Ovotesticular DSD (formerly true hermaphroditism) means both ovarian and testicular tissue, in one ovotestis or in separate gonads; it can result from *SRY* expressed in only part of the gonad (for instance when a Y fragment is translocated onto an X that is randomly inactivated in some cells, [7.4.18](/7.4.18)) or from *SRY* acting too late, as in the mouse experiments of Section 26.2.4. The developmental mechanisms explain why chromosomal, gonadal and phenotypic sex can come apart and why clinical decisions are taken by specialist teams, with the wishes of the person concerned at their centre.

## 26.4. Birds: Cell-Autonomous Sex

In birds the female is the heterogametic sex (ZW) and the male homogametic (ZZ). The gonads of birds also form from bipotential rudiments, but the decision works differently. The leading candidate for the sex-determining gene is the Z-linked *DMRT1*: birds have no complete dosage compensation of Z-linked genes, so ZZ embryos have about twice the DMRT1 of ZW embryos. Knocking down DMRT1 in ZZ chick embryos by RNA interference feminizes their gonads, which express FOXL2 and aromatase (Smith et al. 2009), and ZZ chickens with one functional copy of *DMRT1* develop ovaries and become female-like, while ZW with one copy are normal females (Ioannidis et al. 2021): a two-dose signal, a Z-dosage mechanism. Estrogen plays a larger role than in mammals: aromatase inhibitors given to ZW eggs produce testes, and estrogen given to ZZ eggs produces ovotestes.

Most strikingly, the somatic cells of birds have a sex identity of their own, independent of hormones. Bilateral gynandromorph chickens, male on one side and female on the other, have ZZ cells on the male side and ZW cells on the female side, with mixtures in many tissues; their gonads were a testis on the ZZ side and an ovary (or ovotestis) on the ZW side, and in mixed tissues each cell kept the gene expression of its own chromosomal sex although all cells shared the same circulating hormones (Zhao et al. 2010). When ZZ and ZW cells were mixed in chimeric embryos, donor cells of the "wrong" sex in a gonad did not adopt the fate of the host: ZW cells in a testis did not become Sertoli cells. Avian sex is therefore cell-autonomous sex identity (CASI), set by the sex chromosomes in every cell, on which hormones act secondarily. In mammals, by contrast, somatic cells outside the gonad are thought to be largely neutral, although XX and XY cells differ subtly even before gonads form (in the expression of X- and Y-linked genes), which is one reason sex differences in disease cannot all be attributed to hormones.

## 26.5. Flies, Nematodes and Dosage Compensation

In *Drosophila*, sex is decided by counting X chromosomes: the double dose of X-linked signal elements in XX embryos switches on *Sex-lethal* (*Sxl*), which starts a cascade of alternative splicing through *transformer* to the transcription factor Doublesex, whose female and male forms direct somatic sexual differentiation; the decision is made in every cell independently, as gynandromorphs show ([7.4.6](/7.4.6)). In *Caenorhabditis elegans*, XX animals are hermaphrodites and XO animals males; the X : autosome ratio is read by *xol-1*, which, through a cascade of signalling and the secreted protein HER-1, controls the transcription factor TRA-1. Like Doublesex in flies, the nematode MAB-3 belongs to the DM-domain family of DMRT1: although the signals that start sex determination differ completely, from chromosomes to temperature, the DM-domain transcription factors that execute male development are shared from nematodes to mammals, a deep conservation at the bottom of very divergent cascades.

The difference in X-chromosome number between the sexes requires **dosage compensation** ([7.4.6](/7.4.6)), achieved differently in the three groups: in mammals, one X in each female cell is silenced by the non-coding RNA Xist (**X-chromosome inactivation**, [7.4.18](/7.4.18)), randomly in the embryo proper, the paternal X in extraembryonic tissues of mice and in all cells of marsupials, and the inactive X is reactivated in the germ line; in *Drosophila*, the male-specific lethal complex with the RNAs roX1 and roX2 doubles transcription from the single male X; in *C. elegans*, a condensin-like complex halves transcription from each of the two X chromosomes of the hermaphrodite. In birds compensation is only partial, which makes the dose of *DMRT1* a sex signal.

## 26.6. Environmental Sex Determination

**Definition 26.6.1 (Environmental Sex Determination)** In **environmental sex determination**, the sex of an individual is decided after fertilization by conditions of the environment experienced during a sensitive period, such as incubation temperature, social interactions, density or the site where a larva settles, rather than by its genotype.

### 26.6.1. Temperature

In many turtles, all crocodilians, the tuatara and some lizards and fishes, sex is decided by the incubation temperature of the egg, **temperature-dependent sex determination** ([7.4.6](/7.4.6), with the logistic model of its sex ratio, [Formula 6.1.2](/7.4.6#fml-6.1.2)). In pattern Ia (most turtles) low temperatures produce males and high temperatures females, in pattern Ib (some lizards and the tuatara) the reverse, and in pattern II (crocodilians, snapping turtles) females form at both extremes and males at intermediate temperatures. The temperature acts during a sensitive period in the middle third of incubation: shifting eggs from a male-producing to a female-producing temperature, or the reverse, at different times locates it; before it the shift has no effect, after it the sex has been decided.

The temperature signal converges on the same gonadal genes as in mammals (*Sox9*, *Dmrt1* versus *Foxl2*, *Rspo1*) and, prominently, on aromatase, the enzyme that converts androgens to estrogens. Treating eggs at a male-producing temperature with estrogen makes females, and treating eggs at a female-producing temperature with an aromatase inhibitor makes males; aromatase expression in the gonad rises at female-producing temperatures. Upstream, in the red-eared slider turtle (*Trachemys scripta*) warm temperature increases Ca$^{2+}$ influx and phosphorylation of STAT3, which represses the histone demethylase gene *Kdm6b*; at cool temperatures KDM6B removes repressive H3K27me3 marks from the *Dmrt1* promoter and activates it, and knocking down *Kdm6b* at a male-producing temperature produces females (Ge et al. 2018; Weber et al. 2020). The cold-induced RNA-binding protein CIRBP, whose alleles bias the sex ratio in snapping turtles, and the temperature-sensitive channel TRPV4 have also been implicated.

How is the population sex ratio kept balanced? Fisher's principle applies whatever the mechanism: if one sex becomes rare, individuals of that sex have, on average, more offspring, so any heritable tendency to produce the rare sex is favoured and the ratio returns towards 1 : 1. Under TSD the heritable traits are the pivotal temperature and the mother's choice of nest site and laying date; populations at different latitudes have evolved different pivotal temperatures. Why TSD evolved at all is explained by the Charnov–Bull model: it is favoured when the incubation temperature affects the fitness of males and females differently, so that each develops as the sex that benefits more from its conditions. In the jacky dragon (*Amphibolurus muricatus*), a lizard with TSD, eggs incubated at all temperatures with hormonal manipulation produced both sexes, and males from male-producing temperatures and females from female-producing temperatures had the highest lifetime reproductive success, as the model predicts (Warner and Shine 2008). Under climate warming the balancing mechanisms may be too slow: in the green turtles (*Chelonia mydas*) of the northern Great Barrier Reef, which nest on warm beaches, about 99% of juveniles and subadults were female (Jensen et al. 2018).

### 26.6.2. Fishes and Other Animals

Fishes show every kind of sex determination. Domesticated zebrafish have no strong genetic sex determinant (wild strains carry a ZW-like locus on chromosome 4, lost in laboratory strains). All zebrafish first form a juvenile ovary, with oocytes, between about 10 and 21 days after fertilization; in individuals that will become males, the oocytes undergo apoptosis during the following weeks and the gonad transforms into a testis, while in females the ovary persists. The decision depends on the germ cells: oocytes are needed to maintain the ovary, zebrafish depleted of germ cells all become males, and conditions that reduce growth or germ cell number (hypoxia, high density, poor nutrition, high temperature) bias development towards males (Slanchev et al. 2005; Dranow et al. 2013). Many fishes change sex as adults, sequential hermaphrodites: the rice field eel (*Monopterus albus*) is first female and later male, clownfish change from male to female and many wrasses from female to male when the social hierarchy changes ([7.4.6](/7.4.6)).

In the marine echiuran worm *Bonellia viridis*, sex is decided by where the larva settles. A larva that lands on the sea floor develops into a large female, about 10 cm long with a long proboscis; a larva that lands on the proboscis of a female is taken up, becomes a tiny male a few millimetres long, and lives in the female's reproductive tract, fertilizing her eggs. Extracts of the proboscis masculinize larvae in culture. The settlement site, and hence the presence of females, thus sets the sex, a social form of environmental sex determination that tends to produce males where they can mate.

- Primary sex determination (Definition 26.2.1) is a bistable decision in the supporting cells of a bipotential gonad, triggered in mammals by a short pulse of SRY that must arrive within a window; SOX9 with its feedback loops makes it permanent and spreads it, while WNT4/RSPO1/β-catenin and FOXL2 drive and maintain the ovary.
- Secondary sex determination is hormonal: AMH and testosterone (through DHT for the external genitalia) make the male phenotype; neither sex is a mere default.
- Differences of sex development (Definition 26.3.3) arise at every step and show that chromosomal, gonadal and phenotypic sex can differ.
- Birds have Z-dosage and cell-autonomous sex identity; flies and nematodes use other signals, but DM-domain factors execute male development across animals; dosage compensation differs among groups.
- Environmental sex determination (Definition 26.6.1) uses temperature, germ cells, social context or settlement site to drive the same gonadal genes; sex ratios are balanced by Fisherian selection, and TSD is favoured when environments affect the fitness of the sexes differently.

## Questions

1. How would you show that *Sry* is sufficient for testis formation, and why are the resulting XX males sterile? What would you expect in XY mice with a deletion of *Sox9* in the gonad, and what does the comparison show?

   **Answer** Inject a genomic fragment containing *Sry* and its regulatory region (14 kb) into the pronuclei of fertilized mouse eggs, genotype the offspring for sex chromosomes (XX) and the transgene, and examine the gonads and genitalia: XX transgenic mice form testes and male ducts and external genitalia (Koopman et al. 1991). They are sterile because spermatogenesis requires other Y-linked genes and is disrupted by two X chromosomes (as in XXY men). XY mice lacking *Sox9* in the gonad form ovaries although *Sry* is expressed. Thus *Sry* is sufficient but acts through *Sox9*, which is necessary downstream; SRY is a trigger, SOX9 the effector.

2. In Capel's recombination experiment, mesonephroi from *lacZ*-expressing mice are combined with unlabelled gonads of various genotypes. Predict which combinations show migration of labelled cells into the gonad, and explain what this reveals about FGF9 and the origin of the coelomic vessel.

   **Answer** Labelled mesonephric cells migrate into XY gonads, into XX gonads carrying an *Sry* transgene and into XX gonads incubated with FGF9, but not into normal XX gonads or XY gonads lacking *Sry*; the genotype of the mesonephros does not matter. The migration is therefore induced by the gonad, downstream of SRY, and FGF9 is sufficient to induce it. The migrating cells are endothelial and form the coelomic vessel and the vasculature of the testis, which is needed for the formation of the testis cords: vascularization is part of testis morphogenesis.

3. In the toggle switch of Formula 26.2.1, explain why the required SRY amplitude increases with the onset time, and why a gonad might become an ovotestis rather than switching sharply from testis to ovary.

   **Answer** Without SRY the system drifts from the bipotential state towards the ovary state, so $W$ rises with time. $W$ represses the synthesis of $S$, so a later pulse faces stronger repression and must be larger to push $S$ across the separatrix before the pulse ends; once $W$ is near its stable high level, no pulse of reasonable size suffices. A gonad contains many supporting cells with varying SRY levels and onset times; near the threshold some cells cross and become Sertoli cells, others become granulosa cells, so the organ contains both, an ovotestis; paracrine FGF9 and PGD2 normally recruit marginal cells and reduce this mixing, and when they fail (or when SRY is late, as with the heat-inducible transgene or the *Mus domesticus* Y on the C57BL/6 background), ovotestes form.

4. Jost castrated rabbit fetuses before gonadal differentiation. What were the results, and what experiment distinguishes between one testicular factor and two?

   **Answer** All castrated fetuses developed female internal ducts and external genitalia, regardless of genotype: male development requires the testis, female development in the fetus does not require the ovary. Grafting a testis next to one ovary of a female fetus caused regression of the Müllerian duct and maintenance of the Wolffian duct on that side only, showing local action. Replacing the testis by a crystal of testosterone maintained the Wolffian duct but did not cause Müllerian regression, so a second testicular factor was needed: AMH, later identified as a TGF-β family glycoprotein of Sertoli cells.

5. A 46,XY newborn has female-appearing external genitalia; ultrasound shows testes in the inguinal canals and no uterus. List the possible causes and the tests that would distinguish them.

   **Answer** Absence of a uterus means AMH was made and acted, so testes formed and functioned at least partly: the defect is in androgen synthesis or action. Causes: complete androgen insensitivity (androgen receptor), 5α-reductase 2 deficiency, 17β-HSD3 deficiency or other steroidogenic defects, Leydig cell hypoplasia (LH/hCG receptor). Tests: serum testosterone and DHT (high testosterone/DHT ratio, especially after hCG stimulation, in 5α-reductase deficiency; low testosterone with high precursors such as androstenedione in 17β-HSD3 deficiency; normal or high testosterone and LH in androgen insensitivity), and sequencing of *AR*, *SRD5A2*, *HSD17B3* and *LHCGR*. Swyer syndrome (SRY mutation) is excluded because it would leave a uterus and streak gonads.

6. Adult female mice in which *Foxl2* is deleted develop testis-like gonads. What does this show about the stability of cell fate, and how does it compare with the role of DMRT1?

   **Answer** Granulosa cells require continuous FOXL2 to repress *Sox9*; without it they transdifferentiate into Sertoli-like cells and theca cells into Leydig-like cells, so gonadal sex is actively maintained, not fixed once and for all. DMRT1 has the reciprocal role in the testis: its deletion in adult Sertoli cells induces *Foxl2* and granulosa-like cells. The two factors maintain a bistable state by mutual repression throughout life, a continuation of the embryonic switch, and explain why some fishes can change sex as adults by tipping the same balance.

7. A bilateral gynandromorph chicken has a testis on its ZZ side and an ovary on its ZW side, although both sides are exposed to the same hormones. What does this imply, and how could you test cell-autonomous sex identity experimentally?

   **Answer** Somatic cells interpret their own sex chromosome constitution, not only circulating hormones: cell-autonomous sex identity. Tests: make chimeric embryos by transplanting ZW mesoderm (labelled, for instance with GFP) into ZZ hosts at the stage before gonad formation and examine donor cells in the host testis: if they remain FOXL2- or aromatase-positive and do not become Sertoli cells, identity is autonomous; analyse sex-specific gene expression cell by cell in mixed tissues of gynandromorphs (single-cell RNA sequencing, with Z- and W-linked transcripts identifying genotype); and alter DMRT1 dose in single cells (mosaic knockdown) to see whether the effect is cell-autonomous.

8. In a turtle with pattern Ia TSD, pivotal temperature 29 °C and a logistic transition with $\beta = 1.5\,^\circ\mathrm{C}^{-1}$ ([Formula 6.1.2](/7.4.6#fml-6.1.2)), what fraction of eggs from a nest at 30.5 °C become males? If mean nest temperature rises by 1.5 °C, how does the population sex ratio change, and what limits compensation?

   **Answer** $\mathbb{P}(\text{male}) = 1/(1 + \mathrm{e}^{1.5 \times 1.5}) = 1/(1 + 9.49) \approx 0.095$, about 10% males. The population ratio averages the logistic over the distribution of nest temperatures. When the mean equals the pivotal temperature, cool and warm nests balance at 50%; after warming by 1.5 °C most nests lie above the pivotal temperature and cool nests become rare, and since the logistic saturates, the remaining warm nests produce almost only females, so the population fraction of males falls to about 20% (Figure B), close to the single-nest value because the spread of nest temperatures still includes some cool nests; with further warming, as on the beaches of the northern Great Barrier Reef, it approaches zero. Compensation requires evolution of the pivotal temperature or of nest-site choice, which may be slower than the change in climate.

9. Zebrafish depleted of germ cells all develop as males. Explain this result in terms of the juvenile ovary, and predict the effect of a treatment that increases oocyte apoptosis during the juvenile period.

   **Answer** All zebrafish first form a juvenile ovary whose maintenance requires oocytes: oocytes signal to the somatic cells (supporting granulosa fate and repressing the testis program). Without germ cells there are no oocytes, the ovarian program cannot be maintained, and the somatic gonad differentiates as a testis (without sperm). A treatment that increases oocyte apoptosis in the juvenile period (hypoxia, crowding, high temperature, or drugs) reduces the number of oocytes below the level needed to maintain the ovary in more individuals, biasing the population towards males; this is how environmental factors act on sex in domesticated zebrafish.

10. (Open) Fisher's principle predicts a 1 : 1 sex ratio for a wide range of mechanisms. Under which conditions do you expect environmental rather than genetic sex determination to evolve, and how would you test the prediction in a lizard with TSD?

    **Answer** Environmental sex determination is favoured when the environment predicts which sex will have higher fitness (Charnov–Bull): when incubation temperature, size at hatching or local mating opportunities affect male and female fitness differently, and when offspring cannot otherwise "choose" their environment. It is disfavoured when environments fluctuate so much that the population sex ratio would swing widely, or when environmental cues are unreliable. Test (as Warner and Shine 2008 did with the jacky dragon): incubate eggs at several temperatures and use an aromatase inhibitor or estrogen to produce both sexes at every temperature, release the hatchlings into semi-natural enclosures, and measure lifetime reproductive success by genetic paternity analysis: TSD is adaptive if males from male-producing temperatures and females from female-producing temperatures outperform their counterparts from the other temperatures.
