Every animal that reproduces sexually keeps, beside the body that will die, a lineage of cells that can make the next generation: the germ line. Its founders, the **primordial germ cells**, are specified early, often before the **germ layers** have formed, and in most animals far from the gonad, to which they must migrate. Once there they multiply, decide whether to make sperm or eggs, enter **meiosis** at a time that differs between the sexes, and differentiate into gametes whose structure was described in the chapter on **fertilization** ([7.5.4](/7.5.4)). This chapter follows that path. It draws on the gonad of [7.5.26](/7.5.26), which provides the somatic environment; on the mechanics of **meiosis** ([7.3.21](/7.3.21)) and its genetics ([7.4.5](/7.4.5)), which are not repeated; on the **pole plasm** of the *Drosophila* oocyte ([7.5.14](/7.5.14)); and on the genetics of **genomic imprinting** ([7.4.18](/7.4.18)), whose developmental side, the erasure and resetting of imprints in the germ line and the effects of imprinted genes on growth, is treated here. Hormonal control of the reproductive cycle belongs to physiology ([7.14](/7.14)).

## 27.1. Germ Cells and the Germ Line

**Definition 27.1.1 (Germ Cell, Primordial Germ Cell)** A **germ cell** is a cell of the lineage that gives rise to the gametes: the **primordial germ cells**, the gonial stem and **progenitor cells**, the meiotic cells and the gametes themselves. **Primordial germ cells** (PGCs) are the founding germ cells of the embryo, specified before or during **gastrulation**, which migrate to the developing gonads and there become oogonia or prospermatogonia (gonocytes).

The somatic cells of the gonad (Sertoli, granulosa, Leydig and theca cells) are not germ cells, although they come from the same gonadal primordium, the **genital ridge** ([7.5.26](/7.5.26)). The separation of germ line and soma was Weismann's central idea ([7.5.1](/7.5.1)); it holds in most animals with a fixed early germ line, but not in sponges, cnidarians, flatworms and plants, where germ cells can arise from somatic **stem cells** throughout life.

Germ cells of very different animals share three features.

- A conserved set of RNA-binding proteins: Vasa (a DEAD-box helicase), Nanos (with its partner Pumilio, a translational **repressor**), Tudor and the Piwi proteins with their piRNAs, which silence transposons in the germ line ([7.4.17](/7.4.17)). They are found from cnidarians to mammals and are concentrated in perinuclear germ granules (nuage).
- Transcriptional and translational quiescence during specification and migration. PGCs of flies and worms block RNA polymerase II, those of mice repress the somatic program by Blimp1. The germ cells seem to be shielded from the inductive signals that pattern the body around them.
- A latent capacity for **pluripotency**. Mouse PGCs express Oct4, Nanog and Sox2, and in culture with LIF, Kit ligand and FGF2 they can be converted into pluripotent embryonic germ (EG) cells; PGCs that stray from their route can give rise to teratomas and germ-cell tumours. In the embryo, however, a PGC makes only gametes: it is unipotent, not totipotent. **Totipotency** is regained only when the gametes fuse at **fertilization**.

There are two ways of setting the germ line apart (Extavour and Akam 2003). In **preformation** (inheritance), a specialized cytoplasm of the egg, the **germ plasm**, segregates into a few **blastomeres**, which become PGCs whatever their surroundings. In **epigenesis** (**induction**), PGCs are induced among pluripotent cells by signals from neighbouring tissues after the **blastula** stage. The phylogenetic distribution suggests that induction is ancestral and germ plasm evolved several times independently: it is found in flies, nematodes, teleosts, frogs and birds, while mammals, urodele amphibians (axolotl), crickets and many other insects induce their germ cells.

B: in *C. elegans* the P granules and the transcriptional repressor PIE-1 segregate at each division to the P cell, and P4 founds the germ line. ## 27.2. Preformation: Germ Plasm

**Definition 27.2.1 (Germ Plasm)** The **germ plasm** is a specialized region of egg cytoplasm, containing germ granules made of maternal RNAs (*nanos*, *vasa*, *oskar*, *dead end*) and proteins, that is localized during **oogenesis** or cleavage and specifies the cells that inherit it as **primordial germ cells**. The **pole plasm** of *Drosophila* (Definition 14.2.1) and the P granules of *C. elegans* are examples.

### 27.2.1. *Drosophila*: Pole Plasm and Oskar

In *Drosophila* the first cells to form are the pole cells, which bud from the posterior pole of the syncytial embryo about 90 minutes after **fertilization**, an hour before the rest of the embryo cellularizes ([7.5.14](/7.5.14)). The logic that pole plasm specifies them was established by three experiments.

1. Necessity. Ultraviolet irradiation of the posterior pole destroys the pole plasm; no pole cells form, the somatic cells of the region develop normally, and the flies are sterile.
2. Sufficiency. Posterior cytoplasm injected into the anterior pole of another embryo causes the nuclei there to form pole cells (Illmensee and Mahowald 1974).
3. Function in the correct place. These ectopic anterior pole cells, which carry a genetic marker of the host, were transplanted into the posterior pole of a third embryo; they migrated into its gonads and gave rise to functional gametes of the marker genotype. Anterior pole cells left where they are do not, because they cannot reach the gonad from the head, which answers whether "anterior pole cells can become germ cells": they are germ cells but must be moved to the route.

The molecular basis came from maternal-effect mutants of the "grandchildless" class (*oskar*, *vasa*, *tudor*, *valois*, *staufen*): mutant mothers produce offspring that develop normally but lack germ cells. Of these, *oskar* is the organizer. Its mRNA is localized at the posterior pole of the oocyte by its 3′ UTR and **microtubule** transport, and Oskar protein recruits Vasa, Tudor and the other components of the polar granules, together with *nanos* and *germ cell-less* mRNA. When the *oskar* 3′ UTR was replaced by the *bicoid* localization signal, *oskar* mRNA went to the anterior pole, and the embryos formed pole cells at both ends (and anterior abdominal structures, because the pole plasm also localizes *nanos*): Oskar is sufficient to assemble **germ plasm**. Germ cell-less (Gcl), translated from a nurse-cell mRNA after fertilization, is needed for the pole buds to form and to shut down zygotic transcription in them; *gcl* mutants make few pole cells.

The amount of Oskar sets the amount of germ plasm. Mothers with one, two and four copies of *oskar* produce embryos with about 12–15, 35 and 50 pole cells (Ephrussi and Lehmann 1992).

**Example 27.2.1 (Reading the Oskar Dose)** A Hill curve $N(c)=N_{\max}c^n/(K^n+c^n)$ through the three data points, with $c$ the number of gene copies, gives $N_{\max}\approx55$, $K\approx1.6$ copies and $n\approx2.6$. Three points determine three parameters, so the fit only describes the data, but its shape is informative. It is sigmoidal: the first copy yields fewer cells than the second, a sign that pole plasm must exceed a threshold before it makes germ cells, as expected from the self-reinforcing assembly of polar granules. It saturates: doubling from two to four copies adds only 15 cells, because the number of nuclei that reach the posterior pole during the 9th and 10th nuclear cycles is limited.

**Formula 27.2.1 (Threshold on an Exponential Profile)** Suppose that germ plasm decays from the posterior pole as $g(x)=c\,g_0\,\mathrm{e}^{-x/\lambda}$, proportional to the dose $c$, and that a nucleus at distance $x$ becomes a pole cell if $g(x)>\theta$. The fated region extends to $x^*=\lambda\ln(c\,g_0/\theta)$, and if nuclei are spread along the axis at density $\rho$,

$$
N(c)=\rho\lambda\,\ln\frac{c}{c_0},\qquad c_0=\frac{\theta}{g_0}.
\tag{27.1}
$$

A gradient read at a threshold converts dose into a logarithmic response, the argument used for **morphogens** in [7.5.11](/7.5.11). With the one- and two-copy points, $\rho\lambda=32.5$ and $c_0=0.68$ copies, which predicts 58 cells for four copies against 50 observed: the logarithmic law explains why doubling the dose adds a constant number of cells, but not the saturation.

The Hill curve through the points (N_max 55, K 1.6 copies, n 2.6) shows a threshold at low dose and saturation at high dose; the dashed curve is the logarithmic law of Formula 27.2.1, fitted to the first two points, which overestimates the four-copy embryos because the number of nuclei at the pole is limited](./plots/oskar-dose.py)

### 27.2.2. *C. elegans*: P Granules and PIE-1

In the nematode the germ line descends from a single **blastomere**, P4, born at the fourth cleavage after three **asymmetric divisions** of the P lineage ([7.5.6](/7.5.6)). The P granules, phase-separated condensates of RNA and proteins (PGL-1, the Vasa homologues GLH-1 to GLH-4), are spread through the oocyte; after fertilization the PAR polarity system makes them condense at the posterior and dissolve at the anterior, so at each division they go to the P daughter. P4 divides into Z2 and Z3, the two PGCs of the larva. P granules are not themselves the determinant: embryos in which granules are mislocalized can still make germ cells. The decisive protein is PIE-1, a maternal CCCH zinc-finger protein, also segregated to the P cells, which blocks transcription by inhibiting phosphorylation of the C-terminal domain of RNA polymerase II. In *pie-1* mutants the P2 blastomere transcribes somatic genes and adopts the fate of its sister EMS, making muscle and gut instead of germ cells.

### 27.2.3. Zebrafish and *Xenopus*

In zebrafish, germ plasm RNAs (*vasa*, *nanos3*, *dead end*), assembled in the oocyte by Bucky ball, are carried after fertilization to the distal ends of the first and second cleavage furrows. By the 32-cell stage they lie in four cells; the germ plasm then segregates asymmetrically to one daughter at each division, so that at the 512-cell stage there are still four germ-plasm cells. At the sphere stage the plasm spreads through the cytoplasm, divisions become symmetric, and four clusters of about 30 PGCs in all form. Dead end protects germ-plasm mRNAs from miR-430, the microRNA that clears maternal transcripts at the **maternal-to-zygotic transition** ([7.5.5](/7.5.5)); knocking down *dead end* removes the PGCs and makes sterile fish, a method used in aquaculture.

In *Xenopus* the germ plasm forms patches at the vegetal pole. Ultraviolet irradiation of the vegetal pole of the fertilized egg yields embryos without germ cells, and injection of vegetal cytoplasm rescues them. The plasm-containing cells end up in the floor of the **blastocoel** among the presumptive **endoderm**; until **gastrulation** they are not yet committed and, transplanted elsewhere, can form somatic cells of all **germ layers**. In urodeles no germ plasm is found and the PGCs are induced in the lateral plate **mesoderm**, as in mammals.

### 27.2.4. Birds

Bird PGCs were long thought to be induced, but the chicken Vasa homologue (CVH) and Dazl proteins are localized in the cleavage furrows of the earliest embryos and mark a few cells at the centre of the area pellucida, the precursors of the PGCs (Tsunekawa et al. 2000); birds use germ plasm. During gastrulation these cells are carried to the anterior extraembryonic region, the germinal crescent, from which they enter the newly formed blood vessels. They circulate in the blood and leave it in the region of the **genital ridges**, an unusual route that lets avian PGCs be collected from blood and cultured, the basis of germ-line transgenesis and gene banking in birds.

## 27.3. Induction: Mammalian PGCs

There is no **germ plasm** in the mouse. A cultured **embryonic stem cell** injected into a **blastocyst** can contribute to the germ line as to any somatic tissue, and descendants of a single epiblast cell labelled at E6.0 can form both germ cells and extraembryonic **mesoderm** (Lawson and Hage 1994): germ-cell fate is not fixed before **gastrulation**.

Specification proceeds in three steps.

1. **Competence**. Wnt3, expressed in the posterior epiblast, makes epiblast cells competent to respond to BMP, by inducing the mesodermal transcription factor Brachyury (T).
2. **Induction**. BMP4 and BMP8b from the extraembryonic **ectoderm**, and BMP2 from the visceral **endoderm**, induce *Prdm1* (Blimp1) and *Prdm14* in a few cells of the proximal posterior epiblast at E6.25; mice lacking *Bmp4* or its signal transducers have no **PGCs**. By E7.25 about 40 PGCs lie at the base of the **allantois**, recognizable by alkaline phosphatase and by Stella (Dppa3), a protein that protects maternal imprints and **chromatin**.
3. A tripartite network. Blimp1, Prdm14 and Tfap2c (AP2γ) together repress the mesodermal and somatic program (*Hoxb1*, *T*, *Snai1*), re-express **pluripotency** genes (*Sox2*, *Nanog*) and initiate the global epigenetic **reprogramming** of 27.8. Expressed together in epiblast-like cells in culture, the three factors are sufficient to make PGC-like cells (Nakaki et al. 2013).

Knowledge of these signals made it possible to reconstitute the germ line in culture. Mouse epiblast-like cells derived from ES or **iPS cells**, exposed to BMP4, give PGC-like cells (PGCLCs) that, transplanted into the testes of sterile mice, make functional sperm (Hayashi et al. 2011); with reaggregated fetal ovarian somatic cells they make eggs that give fertile offspring (Hayashi et al. 2012), and the whole of **oogenesis** has since been reproduced in vitro (Hikabe et al. 2016).

Primates differ in the factors and perhaps in the tissue. Human PGCs and PGCLCs use SOX17 as a key specifier instead of SOX2, and PRDM14 is less important (Irie et al. 2015). In cynomolgus monkeys the first PGCs appear in the dorsal **amnion** before gastrulation, induced by BMP4 from the amnion itself and Wnt3a from the extraembryonic mesenchyme (Sasaki et al. 2016); in human embryos and stem-cell models, both the posterior epiblast and the amnion have been proposed as sources, and the question is open.

## 27.4. Migration to the Gonad

From their birthplace **PGCs** travel to the **genital ridges**. The route is fixed by the environment, not by the cells: *Xenopus* PGCs transplanted to the wrong place in the **blastula** do not reach the gonad. The cells move actively, often by bleb-based amoeboid motility, guided by attractive and repulsive cues and supported by survival signals along the way. Lost PGCs usually die; the few that survive in the wrong place can form extragonadal germ-cell tumours.

| Species | Route | Cues |
|---|---|---|
| *Drosophila* | carried into the posterior midgut pocket during **gastrulation**; pass through the midgut epithelium; migrate dorsally and pair with the somatic gonadal precursors of the **mesoderm** | repulsion by lipid phosphate phosphatases Wunen and Wunen-2 in the gut; attraction by HMG-CoA reductase activity in the gonadal mesoderm (a geranylgeranylated signal); the GPCR Tre1 |
| Zebrafish | the four clusters migrate to the level of the first **somite** and then to the gonad region, in a few hours | Sdf1a (Cxcl12a) attracting through Cxcr4b; Cxcr7 shapes the gradient |
| *Xenopus* | with the **endoderm** into the gut, then dorsally along the dorsal mesentery into the ridges; about 30 cells per side | **fibronectin** substrate; Sdf1–Cxcr4 |
| Chick | germinal crescent → blood vessels → leave the circulation near the gonad | Sdf1–Cxcr4 |
| Mouse | from the base of the **allantois** into the hindgut endoderm (E8.5); out of the gut dorsally through the dorsal mesentery (E9.5); laterally into the genital ridges (E10.5–E11.5) | Sdf1 (Cxcl12)–Cxcr4 attraction; Kit ligand (SCF) from the cells around them, a "travelling niche" for survival and motility; E-cadherin on arrival |

The mouse PGCs multiply as they migrate. Mice with mutations in *Kit* (the receptor, *Dominant white spotting*) or *Kitl* (*Steel*) have few PGCs, as well as defects in melanocytes and blood, the other migrating **stem cells** that need the same signal ([7.5.20](/7.5.20), [7.5.29](/7.5.29)). The chemotactic part of the guidance uses the same receptor and ligand as the **neural crest** and lateral line ([7.5.20](/7.5.20)); the physics of **chemotaxis** is in [7.5.13](/7.5.13).

**Example 27.4.1 (How Fast Do PGCs Divide?)** About 40 mouse PGCs at E7.25 give about 25 000 at E13.5, when proliferation stops (approximate counts after Tam and Snow 1981). If growth is exponential, $N(t)=N_0\,2^{t/T_{\mathrm{d}}}$, the number of doublings is $\log_2(25\,000/40)=9.3$ in 6.25 days, and the doubling time is $T_{\mathrm{d}}=6.25\times24/9.3\approx16$ h. The counts lie close to a straight line in log scale throughout, so the **cell cycle** does not slow while the cells migrate. Human PGCs grow from perhaps a hundred at the end of the third week to about 1000–2000 entering each ovary and, in the female, to about 7 million germ cells at 20 weeks.

## 27.5. Mitosis or Meiosis, Sperm or Egg

### 27.5.1. Mammals: the Soma Decides

**PGCs** that reach the gonad do not decide their own sex. In the mouse, XX germ cells in a testis (of an XX↔XY **chimera**, or after transplantation) follow the male pathway as far as the prospermatogonium; XY germ cells in an ovary enter **meiosis** and begin **oogenesis**. Germ cells that stray into the adrenal gland or the **mesonephros** enter meiosis and form oocytes whatever their chromosomes. Meiosis is thus the default program of germ cells at this stage, and the testis actively prevents it.

| | Oogenesis | **Spermatogenesis** |
|---|---|---|
| Entry into meiosis | once, in the fetus (mouse E13.5; human from week 11–12), in a finite population | continuously from puberty, from a self-renewing stem-cell population |
| Products of one meiosis | one egg and two or three polar bodies | four sperm |
| Arrests | **prophase** I (dictyate) for months to decades; **metaphase** II until **fertilization** | none prolonged |
| Differentiation | while **diploid**, in the growing oocyte in prophase I | after meiosis (spermiogenesis), in **haploid** cells |
| **Sex chromosomes** | recombine and are transcribed like **autosomes** | X and Y largely unpaired, silenced in meiotic prophase (meiotic sex chromosome inactivation) |

The timing has been explained by retinoic acid (RA). The mesonephros makes RA; in the fetal ovary it was thought to induce *Stra8* (stimulated by retinoic acid 8) in the germ cells, and Stra8 commits them to meiosis. In the fetal testis Sertoli cells express CYP26B1, which degrades RA, and FGF9 and the RNA-binding protein Nanos2 (expressed in male germ cells after E13.5) repress *Stra8*; male germ cells enter mitotic arrest in G0/G1 as prospermatogonia. In *Cyp26b1* mutants male fetal germ cells enter meiosis and die (Bowles et al. 2006; Koubova et al. 2006). STRA8 and its partner MEIOSIN form a transcription factor that activates the meiotic program (Ishiguro et al. 2020), and *Stra8* is required in both sexes. But fetal ovaries in which all RA synthesis by the aldehyde dehydrogenases is genetically removed still express *Stra8* and enter meiosis on time (Kumar et al. 2011; Vernet et al. 2020; Chassot et al. 2020), so RA is sufficient in culture but apparently not required in vivo, and the signal that triggers meiosis in the fetal ovary is not settled. In the adult testis, by contrast, pulses of RA do trigger the differentiation of spermatogonia and their entry into meiosis (27.6).

### 27.5.2. Flies and Worms

In *Drosophila* the sex of the germ cells depends both on their own chromosomes and on the soma: XY germ cells form sperm whatever gonad they are in (autonomous), whereas XX germ cells become oocytes in an ovary and attempt spermatogenesis in a testis (non-autonomous) ([7.4.6](/7.4.6)).

The hermaphrodite gonad of *C. elegans* makes two decisions. The first, between **mitosis** and meiosis, is controlled by a niche of a single cell. The distal tip cell caps each gonad arm and extends processes that present the Delta-like ligand LAG-2 to the receptor GLP-1, a Notch homologue, on nearby germ cells ([7.3.24](/7.3.24)). Germ cells in contact keep dividing; those displaced proximally, out of reach, enter meiosis. Killing the distal tip cell with a laser makes all germ cells enter meiosis, and moving it to another position in the gonad makes the germ cells next to it keep dividing (Kimble and White 1981); *glp-1* **loss-of-function** mutants have a handful of germ cells, all meiotic, and **gain-of-function** mutants have germ-cell tumours. The second decision, between sperm and oocyte, is made in time: germ cells that enter meiosis during the last larval stage make sperm, which are stored in the spermatheca; in the adult the switch flips and all later germ cells make oocytes, which are fertilized as they pass through the spermatheca. The switch acts through translational control of *fem-3* and *tra-2* mRNAs by the RNA-binding proteins FBF and GLD-1.

The *Drosophila* gonads have two well-studied germline **stem-cell niches**. In the testis, about 12 somatic hub cells secrete Unpaired, which activates JAK–STAT signalling in the 5–9 germline **stem cells** attached to them; each stem cell divides with its **spindle** oriented perpendicular to the hub, so that one daughter stays attached and remains a stem cell while the other, the gonialblast, leaves and differentiates. In the ovary, cap cells at the tip of the germarium secrete Dpp (BMP), which represses the differentiation gene *bag of marbles* in the two or three attached stem cells; the daughter displaced from the cap cells expresses *bam* and becomes a cystoblast. Both are textbook examples of a **stem-cell niche** ([7.3.24](/7.3.24)), in which position, not lineage, decides fate.

## 27.6. Spermatogenesis

### 27.6.1. Stem Cells and Syncytial Clones

**Spermatogenesis** ([7.4.5](/7.4.5)) takes place in the seminiferous tubules, between the Sertoli cells, which span the epithelium from the **basement membrane** to the lumen, nourish the germ cells and form, by **tight junctions** between them, the blood–testis barrier that encloses the meiotic and haploid cells. It has three phases.

1. A proliferative phase. Spermatogonial **stem cells** lie on the basement membrane among the undifferentiated spermatogonia (A_single, A_paired and A_aligned chains in rodents, A_dark and A_pale in humans). They depend on GDNF, made by Sertoli and peritubular myoid cells and received through the receptor RET and its co-receptor GFRα1, and on FGF and CSF1 from the interstitium; GDNF itself is controlled by FSH. Transplanted into the tubules of a mouse whose own **spermatogenesis** has been destroyed, a few stem cells can restore it, the defining assay of these cells (Brinster and Zimmermann 1994). Their daughters, primed by Wnt (Ngn3- and Sox3-positive **progenitors**), respond to RA with *Stra8* and *Kit*, divide several more times as differentiating type A spermatogonia and then type B spermatogonia, and enter **meiosis**.
2. A meiotic phase. Each type B spermatogonium divides into two primary spermatocytes, each of which passes through the long prophase I and gives two secondary spermatocytes and then four spermatids.
3. Spermiogenesis, the differentiation of the round spermatid into a sperm. The acrosome forms from Golgi vesicles as a cap over the nucleus; the flagellum grows from the distal centriole; mitochondria wrap the base of the flagellum as the midpiece; the histones are replaced first by transition proteins and then by protamines, which compact the nucleus and stop transcription; most of the cytoplasm is shed as the residual body, phagocytosed by the Sertoli cell. Sperm then mature further in the epididymis, from which they acquire small RNAs carried by vesicles, and complete their maturation, **capacitation**, in the female tract ([7.5.4](/7.5.4)).

**Cytokinesis** is incomplete in all divisions after the stem cell: the germ cells of one clone stay joined by cytoplasmic bridges (ring canals), up to hundreds of cells, which enter meiosis and spermiogenesis in synchrony. The bridges also let haploid spermatids share gene products, so that a spermatid that carries an X and one that carries a Y are phenotypically equivalent although their genomes differ; transmission-ratio distorters that act after meiosis must therefore escape sharing, as the *t*-haplotype does with its poorly shared *Smok* product ([7.4.17](/7.4.17)). Chains can break up, and fragments can return to the stem-cell state, so that the stem-cell pool is maintained by a balance of **self-renewal** and fragmentation rather than by strictly **asymmetric divisions** (Hara et al. 2014).

### 27.6.2. The Timetable

Each section of tubule passes through a fixed sequence of cell associations, the cycle of the seminiferous epithelium, as successive generations of spermatogonia enter the process at a constant interval. In the mouse the cycle lasts 8.6 days and a germ cell needs about four cycles, 34.5 days, from type A spermatogonium to sperm (about 8 days as spermatogonia, 13 days in meiosis and 13.5 days in spermiogenesis); in humans the cycle lasts about 16 days, and the whole process about 64 days by classical estimates (Heller and Clermont 1963) and about 70–74 days by recent isotope labelling (Misell et al. 2006). In rodents the stages progress regularly along the tubule, a wave of spermatogenesis; in humans several stages are found in one cross-section. The interval between entries is controlled by RA, made in pulses by the Sertoli cells at one stage of the cycle, which induces *Stra8* in the next generation of spermatogonia and the release of the mature spermatids; blocking RA synthesis and then adding RA back synchronizes the whole testis (Hogarth et al. 2015).

The output is enormous: about 1000 sperm per second, $10^{12}$–$10^{13}$ in a lifetime. Sperm production is also sensitive. In the WHO reference values (2021), the lower fifth percentile of fertile men is 16 million sperm per millilitre and 39 million per ejaculate; below these values (oligozoospermia), and especially below a few million per millilitre, fertility falls. Meta-analyses report a decline of sperm concentration in Western countries since the 1970s by about half (Levine et al. 2017, 2023), although differences in methods and sampling make the size of the trend debated. Microdeletions of the AZF regions of the Y chromosome, which remove genes such as *DAZ*, are the most common known genetic cause of severe oligozoospermia and azoospermia.

## 27.7. Oogenesis

### 27.7.1. Meiotic Arrest and the Asymmetric Divisions

**Oogenesis** ([7.4.5](/7.4.5)) differs from **spermatogenesis** in timing, in output and in the growth of the gamete. In the human fetus the roughly 1000–2000 **PGCs** that colonize each ovary divide as oogonia, in clusters joined by bridges, until about the fifth month; from about week 11 they enter **meiosis** and become primary oocytes, which pass through leptotene to diplotene and arrest there, in the dictyate stage. At about 20 weeks the two ovaries contain about 7 million germ cells; most die by **apoptosis** before birth, leaving 1–2 million at birth and about 300 000–400 000 at puberty. The arrest lasts until ovulation, from about 12 to about 50 years. Meiosis I is resumed in response to the surge of luteinizing hormone (LH). Before the surge, the granulosa cells keep the oocyte arrested by supplying cGMP through **gap junctions**: natriuretic peptide C (NPPC) made by mural granulosa cells acts on its receptor NPR2 in the cumulus cells, the cGMP diffuses into the oocyte and inhibits the phosphodiesterase PDE3A, so **cAMP** stays high and protein kinase A keeps the **cyclin** B–Cdk1 complex inactive. LH lowers cGMP in the granulosa cells and closes the gap junctions; cAMP in the oocyte falls, Cdk1 is activated and the germinal vesicle breaks down.

The two divisions are highly asymmetric (**polar body**, [7.3.21](/7.3.21)). The spindle migrates to the cortex and the first **polar body** takes half of the chromosomes and almost no cytoplasm; the secondary oocyte arrests again in metaphase II, held by cytostatic factor, and is ovulated in this state. Meiosis II is completed only at **fertilization** ([7.5.4](/7.5.4)), giving the second polar body. In humans the first polar body usually degenerates without dividing. If the second polar body is not extruded, the embryo is triploid; errors in either division give **aneuploidy**, whose frequency rises steeply with maternal age because cohesion decays during the long arrest ([7.3.21](/7.3.21)).

Animals with large yolky eggs add other strategies for making a big cell. A *Xenopus* oocyte grows over about three years: in the first two its diameter rises from about 10 μm to about 80 μm and its volume some 500-fold (in prophase I, with four copies of each gene, and with lampbrush chromosomes transcribing intensely), and in the third it fills with yolk made in the liver as vitellogenin, carried by the blood and taken up by receptor-mediated endocytosis, to a diameter of about 1.2 mm. Its ribosomal RNA genes are amplified from hundreds to about 2 million copies in extrachromosomal nucleoli (**gene amplification**, [7.4.17](/7.4.17)). In *Drosophila*, a germline **stem cell**'s daughter, the cystoblast, divides four times with incomplete cytokinesis into a cyst of 16 cells connected by ring canals; an organelle called the fusome passes through the bridges and orients the divisions, so that the two cells with four bridges are the oldest, and one of them becomes the oocyte and the other 15 become nurse cells, which make mRNAs and proteins and transfer them through the ring canals into the oocyte ([7.5.14](/7.5.14)). The follicle cells around it amplify the **chorion** genes for the eggshell.

### 27.7.2. Follicles and the Ovarian Reserve

**Definition 27.7.1 (Ovarian Follicle, Ovarian Reserve)** An **ovarian follicle** is the functional unit of the ovary: an oocyte surrounded by somatic granulosa cells (and, from the secondary stage, theca cells), which supply it with metabolites and signals and produce the steroid hormones of the cycle. The **ovarian reserve** is the population of primordial follicles in the ovaries, which is formed before or shortly after birth, is not replenished, and declines through activation and atresia until menopause.

Primordial follicles leave the resting pool continuously, from birth on, independently of hormones. Activation is held in check by the PI3K–PTEN–FOXO3 pathway in the oocyte: deleting *Pten* or *Foxo3* in mouse oocytes activates the whole pool at once, causing premature ovarian failure, and **AMH** from growing follicles slows recruitment. Two oocyte-derived members of the TGF-β family, GDF9 and BMP15, drive the proliferation of the granulosa cells; *Gdf9*-null follicles stop at the primary stage. Growth to the preantral stage takes months; the antral stages depend on follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) and take a few weeks. In each human cycle a cohort of antral follicles is recruited, and the one most sensitive to FSH, which keeps growing as FSH falls (the FSH threshold and window), becomes **dominant**; the others undergo atresia. The LH surge causes the oocyte to resume meiosis, the cumulus to expand and the follicle wall to rupture through local proteases and prostaglandins; the remaining follicle becomes the corpus luteum. The **endocrine** cycle itself is treated in [7.14](/7.14). Only about 400–500 oocytes are ovulated in a lifetime, fewer than one in two thousand of those present at birth.

**Formula 27.7.1 (A Model of the Ovarian Reserve)** Let $N(t)$ be the number of non-growing follicles at age $t$. If each leaves the pool (by activation or atresia) at a per-capita rate $\lambda(N)$,

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}N}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\lambda(N)\,N,\qquad
\lambda(N)=\lambda_1+\frac{\lambda_2-\lambda_1}{1+(N/N^*)^4},
\tag{27.2}
$$

with $\lambda_1=0.097$ per year, $\lambda_2=0.237$ per year and $N^*=25\,000$, a smooth version of the observation that the decline is close to exponential until about 25 000 follicles remain, in the late thirties, and then accelerates (Faddy et al. 1992). Menopause occurs when about $N_{\mathrm{m}}=1000$ follicles remain. Separating variables,

$$
t_{\mathrm{m}}=\int_{N_{\mathrm{m}}}^{N_0}\frac{\mathrm{d}N}{\lambda(N)\,N}
\approx\frac{\ln(N_0/N^*)}{\lambda_1}+\frac{\ln(N^*/N_{\mathrm{m}})}{\lambda_2},
\tag{27.3}
$$

where the approximation treats the switch as sharp. For $N_0=10^6$, $t_{\mathrm{m}}\approx38.0+13.6=51.6$ years. Because the age at menopause depends on the logarithm of the reserve at birth, a twofold difference in $N_0$ shifts menopause by only $\ln2/\lambda_1\approx7$ years, and a log-normal spread of $N_0$ with $\sigma=0.4$ gives a nearly normal distribution of ages with a standard deviation of about 4 years.

The model is phenomenological: it describes the counts of follicles in ovaries of women of different ages, and the acceleration may reflect the decline of AMH and inhibin, which normally restrain recruitment, rather than a change in the oocytes. Its predictions match the epidemiology: about 1 % of women reach menopause before 40 and about 5–10 % before 45. The same logic explains why chemotherapy or ovarian surgery that removes a fraction of follicles advances menopause by a few years per halving of the reserve, and why measures of the remaining pool (serum AMH, the count of antral follicles by ultrasound) predict the response to ovarian stimulation in assisted reproduction better than they predict natural fertility.

### 27.7.3. Is the Reserve Ever Renewed?

The dogma that the mammalian ovary has no germline stem cells after birth was challenged when Johnson and colleagues (2004) argued, from the rate of follicle loss, that new follicles must be formed in adult mice, and cells expressing the germ-cell helicase DDX4 (Vasa) were isolated from adult mouse and human ovaries and reported to form oocytes after transplantation (Zou et al. 2009; White et al. 2012). **Lineage tracing** has not supported a physiological role: in mice in which all germ cells present at birth were labelled, no unlabelled follicles appeared later (Lei and Spradling 2013), and single-cell transcriptomes of human ovarian cortex found no germline stem cells, the DDX4-positive cells being perivascular cells (Wagner et al. 2020). The current view is that the reserve is fixed at birth in mammals; whether rare cells with oogenic potential exist in culture is a separate question from whether they replenish the ovary.

### 27.7.4. Disorders of the Oocyte

- Primary ovarian insufficiency (POI; premature ovarian failure): loss of ovarian function before 40, in about 1 % of women, from a small reserve at birth (Turner syndrome, *FMR1* premutation carriers, mutations in genes of meiosis such as *STAG3* and *MCM8*), accelerated loss (autoimmunity, chemotherapy, radiation) or failure of recruitment; it accounts for a large share of primary amenorrhea.
- Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), with hyperandrogenism, irregular or absent ovulation and many small antral follicles, affects about 6–13 % of women of reproductive age depending on the criteria; follicles are recruited but none becomes dominant.
- Defective eggs: mutations in the **zona pellucida** genes *ZP1*, *ZP2* and *ZP3* give eggs without a zona and infertility; mutations in *TUBB8*, an oocyte-specific β-tubulin, arrest maturation in meiosis I (Feng et al. 2016); mutations in *PATL2* and the subcortical maternal complex genes arrest maturation or early cleavage. Many oocytes with abnormal appearance (dark granular cytoplasm, **vacuoles**, clusters of smooth ER, a large perivitelline space) are seen in assisted reproduction; their predictive value is modest, and automated image analysis is being evaluated to select embryos.

## 27.8. Reprogramming the Germ-Line Epigenome and Parental Imprinting

### 27.8.1. Erasure and Resetting

The germ line must undo the epigenetic marks acquired by the soma. During and after migration, mouse **PGCs** lose the repressive histone mark H3K9me2, gain H3K27me3 and lose **DNA methylation** almost completely: global CpG methylation falls from about 70 % in the epiblast to a few per cent by E13.5, the lowest level of any cell in the life cycle (Seisenberger et al. 2012). The loss is mostly passive, by replication without maintenance, since UHRF1 is repressed in PGCs, helped by TET1 and TET2, which oxidize 5-methylcytosine at **imprinting control regions** and germline genes. The imprints of both parental chromosomes are erased between E10.5 and E12.5, together with the methylation of germline-specific genes (*Dazl*, *Ddx4*), which must be demethylated to be expressed; on the X chromosome of female PGCs, the inactive X is reactivated ([7.4.18](/7.4.18)). Some young transposons, especially IAP elements, escape demethylation, which leaves a route for epigenetic inheritance. Human PGCs go through the same process in weeks 5–11 (Guo et al. 2015; Tang et al. 2015).

New methylation is laid down by DNMT3A and DNMT3L according to the sex of the gonad: in prospermatogonia between E15 and birth, while they are arrested in G0/G1, and in oocytes after birth, during their growth, so that the maternal imprints are established long after the paternal ones and only at the ICRs that lie in transcribed regions of the oocyte genome. The cycle of imprints (erasure, establishment, maintenance) and its genetic consequences are in [7.4.18](/7.4.18); its developmental side is that the window of **reprogramming** coincides with the migration and the sex decision of the germ cells, and that the two germ lines produce gametes with differently marked genomes.

### 27.8.2. Parental Genomes Are Not Equivalent

That the two parents contribute differently is visible in hybrids: a mule (from a male donkey and a female horse) and a hinny (from a male horse and a female donkey) carry the same two genomes but differ, the hinny being smaller, with a shorter head and ears and a fuller mane and tail. In the mouse, embryos made by pronuclear transfer with two maternal genomes (gynogenetic or parthenogenetic) develop a small embryo with poor extraembryonic tissues, and embryos with two paternal genomes (androgenetic) develop a **placenta** and **yolk sac** but a stunted embryo ([7.4.18](/7.4.18)). **Chimeras** reveal the effect on growth: chimeras containing androgenetic cells are up to about 50 % heavier than normal, those with parthenogenetic cells about 50 % lighter, and the two kinds of cell distribute differently, androgenetic cells to mesodermal tissues such as muscle and parthenogenetic cells to the brain (Barton, Surani and colleagues). The paternal genome promotes growth.

The genes responsible include the paternally expressed **growth factor** *Igf2* and the maternally expressed *Igf2r*, which encodes a receptor that removes IGF2 from circulation; *Igf2* mutations transmitted by the father reduce birth weight to about 60 %, transmitted by the mother they have no effect. At the *Igf2*–*H19* locus the **imprinting control region** acts as a CTCF-dependent **insulator** that is methylated only on the paternal chromosome ([Definition 18.2.2](/7.4.18#def-18.2.2)). In humans, loss of methylation at the paternal *H19*/*IGF2* ICR, which halves *IGF2* expression, causes about half of the cases of Silver–Russell syndrome, with intrauterine and postnatal growth restriction, a relatively large head, a triangular face and body asymmetry (about 1 in 30 000–100 000 births); gain of methylation on the maternal ICR, which doubles *IGF2*, causes about 5–10 % of the cases of Beckwith–Wiedemann syndrome, an overgrowth disorder with macroglossia, abdominal wall defects and embryonal tumours (about 1 in 10 000), whose most common cause is instead loss of methylation at the second ICR of the region (KvDMR1), which silences the growth inhibitor *CDKN1C*. Most cases of both syndromes are sporadic.

Editing imprinted loci shows that **imprinting** is the barrier to uniparental mammals. Bimaternal mice were obtained by combining a non-growing oocyte genome, in which the *H19* ICR and the *Dlk1*–*Dio3* ICR had been deleted so that it behaved as a paternal one, with a mature oocyte genome (Kono et al. 2004); bipaternal mice, which need many more corrections, have been reported after editing about 20 imprinted regions in haploid **ES cells**, and some survived to adulthood, although with abnormalities and low efficiency (Li et al. 2025).

### 27.8.3. Why the Parents Disagree

The direction of the effects, paternal genes promoting and maternal genes restraining fetal growth, is predicted by the kinship theory of David Haig ([7.4.18](/7.4.18)). A simple optimization makes the argument quantitative.

**Formula 27.8.1 (A Kinship Model of Fetal Demand)** Let a gene expressed in a fetus set the demand $x$ the fetus places on its mother. The fetus gains $B(x)=1-\mathrm{e}^{-x}$ (diminishing returns), and the mother's other offspring lose $C(x)=x$ (resources not available to them). The gene favours the demand that maximizes its inclusive fitness $W(x)=B(x)-r\,C(x)$, where $r$ is its probability of being present in the other offspring through the parent from which it was inherited. Setting $W'(x)=\mathrm{e}^{-x}-r=0$,

$$
x^*=\ln\frac1r.
\tag{27.4}
$$

A maternally inherited **allele** is present in each of the mother's other offspring with probability $r_{\mathrm{m}}=1/2$, so $x^*_{\mathrm{m}}=\ln2\approx0.69$. A paternally inherited allele is present in a half-sibling with another father with probability $0$, so if a fraction $p$ of the mother's other offspring share the father, $r_{\mathrm{p}}=p/2$ and $x^*_{\mathrm{p}}=\ln(2/p)$, which is larger than $x^*_{\mathrm{m}}$ whenever $p<1$: $1.39$ for $p=1/2$, $2.08$ for $p=1/4$.

The model predicts conflict only where offspring draw on maternal resources after **fertilization** and mothers can have offspring by several fathers, which is why imprinting is found in placental mammals and flowering plants (endosperm) but not in birds; it predicts that the maternal allele of a growth gene is silenced (or that a maternal inhibitor such as *Igf2r* is expressed), which explains the *Igf2*–*Igf2r* pair; and it predicts no conflict in strictly monogamous species ($p=1$). Not all imprinted genes fit: some, such as those at the Prader–Willi locus, act in the brain on postnatal behaviour, where the same logic predicts effects on suckling, and others may be imprinted for reasons unrelated to conflict, such as dosage control of the X chromosome in marsupials.

## Summary

- Germ cells and **PGCs** (Definition 27.1.1): a lineage set apart early, with conserved RNA-binding proteins (Vasa, Nanos, Tudor, Piwi), transcriptional quiescence and latent **pluripotency**; unipotent in the embryo.
- Preformation by **germ plasm** (Definition 27.2.1) in flies (**pole plasm**, Oskar sufficient and dose-dependent: 12–15, 35, 50 pole cells for 1, 2, 4 copies; Example 27.2.1, Formula 27.2.1 gives $N\propto\ln(c/c_0)$ for a threshold on an exponential profile), worms (P granules, PIE-1 blocks Pol II), fish (furrow **germ plasm**, Dead end), frogs (vegetal germ plasm) and birds (CVH; blood route). **Induction** in mammals and urodeles, probably ancestral.
- Mouse PGC induction: Wnt3 **competence**, BMP4/BMP8b/BMP2, Blimp1–Prdm14–Tfap2c; about 40 cells at E7.25; PGC-like cells and gametes in vitro. Primates: SOX17, **amnion** or posterior epiblast.
- Migration: through the gut and dorsal mesentery (mouse, frog), through the midgut (fly), through the blood (bird); Sdf1–Cxcr4 attraction, Wunen repulsion, Kit ligand as a travelling niche; doubling time about 16 h in the mouse (Example 27.4.1).
- Sex of germ cells: in mammals set by the gonad; **meiosis** is the default, prevented in the fetal testis (CYP26B1, FGF9, Nanos2); STRA8–MEIOSIN drives meiotic entry, and whether RA triggers it in the fetal ovary is disputed. *C. elegans*: the distal tip cell niche (LAG-2–GLP-1) separates mitosis from meiosis; sperm then oocytes in time. Fly niches: hub (Unpaired, JAK–STAT) and cap cells (Dpp represses *bam*).
- **Spermatogenesis**: GDNF-dependent **stem cells**, syncytial clones joined by bridges, four sperm per meiosis, spermiogenesis (acrosome, flagellum, protamines); mouse 34.5 days, human 64–74 days, cycles of 8.6 and 16 days set by RA pulses; about 1000 sperm per second.
- **Oogenesis**: 7 million germ cells at 20 weeks, 1–2 million at birth; dictyate arrest held by cGMP from granulosa cells; **asymmetric divisions** and polar bodies; growth strategies (lampbrush chromosomes, rDNA amplification, vitellogenin, nurse cells). Follicles and reserve (Definition 27.7.1): PTEN–FOXO3, GDF9/BMP15, FSH selection; Formula 27.7.1: menopause age $\approx\ln(N_0/N^*)/\lambda_1+\ln(N^*/N_{\mathrm{m}})/\lambda_2$, logarithmic in the reserve. No physiological germline stem cells in the adult mammalian ovary.
- **Reprogramming**: PGCs erase DNA methylation and imprints (E10.5–12.5 in the mouse); new imprints in prospermatogonia before birth and growing oocytes after birth. Parental genomes differ (mule and hinny, androgenetic and parthenogenetic **chimeras**; *Igf2*, *Igf2r*); SRS and BWS at 11p15.5; uniparental mice need edited **ICRs**. Kinship model (Formula 27.8.1): $x^*=\ln(1/r)$, paternal demand exceeds maternal.

## Questions

1. Design an experiment to show that *Drosophila* **pole plasm** is sufficient to make functional germ cells, and say why anterior pole cells left in place do not contribute to the germ line.

   **Answer** Transfer posterior cytoplasm from a donor egg into the anterior pole of a host embryo carrying a genetic marker (for example a pigmentation or enzyme variant); the nuclei that become enclosed in it bud as pole cells at the anterior end. Transplant these anterior pole cells into the posterior pole of a third embryo of a different, distinguishable genotype, and test the gametes of the adult (by crossing) for the marker of the second embryo. Gametes of that genotype show that the induced cells were functional germ cells (Illmensee and Mahowald 1974). Left at the anterior pole the cells are specified but cannot reach the gonad, because migration requires passage through the posterior midgut to the gonadal **mesoderm**; fate and position are separate requirements.

2. With the Hill fit of Example 27.2.1 ($N_{\max}=54.7$, $K=1.60$, $n=2.585$), predict the pole-cell number for three copies of *oskar*, and compare with the logarithmic law of Formula 27.2.1 ($\rho\lambda=32.5$, $c_0=0.68$).

   **Answer** Hill: $N(3)=54.7\cdot3^{2.585}/(1.60^{2.585}+3^{2.585})$; $3^{2.585}=17.1$ and $1.60^{2.585}=3.37$, so $N\approx54.7\cdot17.1/20.5\approx46$. Logarithmic law: $N=32.5\ln(3/0.68)=32.5\times1.48\approx48$. The two agree at three copies and diverge at higher doses, where the Hill curve saturates near 55 and the logarithm keeps rising; measuring six or eight copies would discriminate them.

3. A *pie-1* mutant embryo forms extra muscle and gut and no germ cells. Explain the phenotype, and predict what happens if PIE-1 is made to persist in the EMS **blastomere**.

   **Answer** PIE-1 blocks transcription in the P lineage by inhibiting phosphorylation of the Pol II C-terminal domain, so the P blastomeres cannot respond to the maternal transcription factors (such as SKN-1) that specify somatic fates. Without it P2 transcribes the genes that EMS uses and adopts an EMS-like fate (mesoderm and **endoderm**), and the germ line is lost. If PIE-1 persisted in EMS, transcription there would be repressed and EMS would fail to make its somatic descendants (MS and E fates, pharynx and gut would be reduced); it would not necessarily become a germ cell, since germ-cell fate also needs **germ plasm** components and later germline genes.

4. About 40 mouse **PGCs** at E7.25 become about 25 000 at E13.5. Estimate the doubling time, and the number expected at E10.5 if growth is exponential.

   **Answer** Doublings: $\log_2(625)=9.3$ in 6.25 days, so $T_{\mathrm{d}}=150/9.3\approx16$ h. At E10.5, 3.25 days later than E7.25, there have been $78/16\approx4.8$ doublings, $40\times2^{4.8}\approx1100$ cells, close to the counted value of about 1000 (Example 27.4.1).

5. XX germ cells that reach the adrenal gland of a male or female mouse embryo enter **meiosis** and form oocytes, and so do XY germ cells in an ovary. What do these observations imply about the sex of germ cells, and how would you test whether the testis acts by degrading retinoic acid?

   **Answer** They imply that germ-cell sex is set by the somatic environment and that meiotic entry (the female program) is the default for germ cells at this stage; the testis must actively prevent it. To test the role of RA degradation, delete *Cyp26b1* in Sertoli cells (or inhibit CYP26 in testis culture): male germ cells should express *Stra8* and enter meiosis. Conversely, culture fetal ovaries with an inhibitor of RA receptors or remove the RA-synthesizing enzymes genetically: if RA is required, meiosis should fail. The second test has in fact shown that meiotic entry in the ovary can occur without RA synthesis, so the testis prevents meiosis by additional means (FGF9, Nanos2).

6. **Spermatogenesis** produces about 1000 sperm per second. How many sperm is that per day and in 50 years of reproductive life? How many divisions separate a spermatogonial **stem cell** from a sperm if one differentiating clone gives about 1000 sperm?

   **Answer** Per day $1000\times86\,400\approx8.6\times10^7$; in 50 years $8.6\times10^7\times365\times50\approx1.6\times10^{12}$, within the range $10^{12}$–$10^{13}$. A clone of $2^k$ spermatids from one cell after $k$ divisions gives $2^{10}=1024$ for $k=10$: about 8 mitotic divisions of spermatogonia followed by the 2 meiotic divisions. Because the stem cells themselves keep dividing for decades, the paternal germ line accumulates many more replications with age (about 23 divisions per year after puberty), which is why new **point mutations** rise with paternal age.

7. Using Formula 27.7.1 with the sharp-switch approximation, calculate the age at menopause for a woman with 1 million follicles at birth; then for a woman with half that reserve; then for a woman who at age 20 loses 90 % of her follicles through chemotherapy.

   **Answer** (a) $\ln(10^6/25\,000)/0.097+\ln(25)/0.237=3.69/0.097+3.22/0.237=38.0+13.6=51.6$ years. (b) Half the reserve shifts the first term by $\ln2/0.097=7.1$ years: about 44.5 years. (c) At 20 years $N=10^6\mathrm{e}^{-0.097\times20}=1.44\times10^5$; after losing 90 % she has $1.44\times10^4$, already below $N^*$, so the remaining time is $\ln(14\,400/1000)/0.237=2.67/0.237=11.3$ years: menopause at about 31 years. The logarithmic dependence means that small losses matter little but a large loss in youth advances menopause by decades, which is why ovarian tissue or oocytes are cryopreserved before chemotherapy.

8. Explain the cGMP mechanism of meiotic arrest in mammalian oocytes, and predict the effect of a drug that inhibits PDE3A in a cultured cumulus–oocyte complex.

   **Answer** Granulosa cells produce cGMP (through NPPC acting on NPR2), which passes through gap junctions into the oocyte and inhibits PDE3A, the phosphodiesterase that degrades cAMP. High cAMP keeps PKA active, which keeps the Cdk1–cyclin B complex (MPF) inactive and the oocyte in prophase I. The LH surge lowers cGMP and closes the gap junctions, so PDE3A becomes active, cAMP falls and MPF is activated. A PDE3A inhibitor keeps cAMP high even after LH or after removal of the cumulus cells, so the oocyte stays arrested; such inhibitors have been tested as contraceptives and are used to synchronize maturation in vitro.

9. In the kinship model (Formula 27.8.1), compute the optimal demand of a maternally and a paternally inherited allele when a mother's other offspring have the same father in a fraction $p=1/3$ of cases. When does the model predict no **imprinting**, and why does it predict growth-promoting genes to be paternally expressed?

   **Answer** Maternal: $x^*_{\mathrm{m}}=\ln2\approx0.69$. Paternal: $r_{\mathrm{p}}=p/2=1/6$, $x^*_{\mathrm{p}}=\ln6\approx1.79$. With $p=1$ (strict monogamy) $r_{\mathrm{p}}=r_{\mathrm{m}}$ and both alleles agree, so there is no selection for parent-specific expression; nor is there conflict when the mother provides nothing after **fertilization** (no **placenta**), since then the fetus cannot change her investment. Since the paternal allele values the mother's other offspring less, it favours greater demand; the expression level that maximizes its fitness for a growth-promoting gene is higher than the maternal optimum, and the stable outcome of such a tug-of-war is often the silencing of the maternal copy (the "loudest voice prevails"), with maternally expressed restrainers such as *Igf2r* on the other side.

10. A child has Beckwith–Wiedemann syndrome caused by gain of methylation at the maternal *H19*/*IGF2* **imprinting control region**. Explain the change in gene expression, and contrast it with Silver–Russell syndrome caused by loss of methylation at the paternal ICR.

    **Answer** Normally the maternal ICR is unmethylated and binds CTCF, which insulates *IGF2* from the **enhancers** downstream of *H19*: the maternal allele expresses *H19* only. Gain of methylation on the maternal ICR prevents CTCF binding, the enhancers reach the maternal *IGF2* **promoter** and *H19* is silenced, so *IGF2* is expressed from both alleles, about twice the normal dose, and growth is excessive. In Silver–Russell syndrome the paternal ICR loses methylation, CTCF binds on the paternal allele as well, *IGF2* is silenced on both chromosomes (about half dose or less, depending on mosaicism) and *H19* is biallelic: growth is restricted. The two syndromes are opposite dosage disturbances of the same insulator.
