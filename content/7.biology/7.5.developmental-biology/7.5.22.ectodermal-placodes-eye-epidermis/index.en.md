The **ectoderm** that does not become **neural plate** or **neural crest** forms the surface of the body and much of the apparatus with which an animal senses its surroundings. It produces the epidermis and its appendages (hairs, feathers, scales, teeth, glands), and, at the border of the anterior neural plate, a set of thickened patches, the placodes, that give rise to the lens of the eye, the inner ear, the olfactory epithelium, the anterior pituitary and many sensory **neurons**. The eye combines three tissues, **neural tube** (retina), surface ectoderm (lens, cornea) and neural crest (corneal stroma), and is the classical example of a sequence of **reciprocal inductions**. The appendages illustrate a second theme: periodic patterns that arise from interactions between an epithelium and the mesenchyme beneath it, for which both reaction-diffusion models ([7.5.12](/7.5.12)) and a mechanochemical model, in which cells pull on their matrix, have been proposed. This chapter continues the ectodermal derivatives of [7.5.18](/7.5.18)-[7.5.21](/7.5.21); the function of the eye and ear belongs to [7.14](/7.14), the **stem cells** of the skin are introduced in [7.3.24](/7.3.24).

## 22.1. Cranial Placodes

**Definition 22.1.1 (Placode)** A **placode** is a localized thickening of an embryonic epithelium, usually the surface **ectoderm**, whose cells become columnar and then form an organ or migrate to form **neurons**. The cranial sensory placodes form the lens, the inner ear, the olfactory epithelium, the anterior pituitary and sensory neurons of cranial ganglia; the placodes of the skin initiate hair follicles, feathers, scales, teeth and glands (22.4).

The cranial placodes arise from a common territory, the preplacodal region (PPR), a horseshoe of ectoderm surrounding the anterior **neural plate**, outside the **neural crest**. It is marked by the transcription factors Six1 and Eya1 and by members of the Dlx and Foxi families. Its **induction** requires FGF from the head **mesoderm** and **endoderm** and low BMP and Wnt signalling; the neural crest arises where Wnt and intermediate BMP are present, so inhibition of Wnt and BMP distinguishes the placodal fate from the crest fate at the same neural plate border (Litsiou, Hanson and Streit 2005). The PPR then resolves into individual placodes, in a fixed sequence from anterior to posterior, under local signals: Otx2 and Gbx2 divide anterior from posterior placodes, FGF and Wnt from the hindbrain induce the otic placode, Pax6 marks the lens and olfactory placodes, Pax3 the ophthalmic trigeminal, Pax2/8 the otic and epibranchial placodes.

Many sensory ganglia of the head are built from both placodes and crest: in the trigeminal ganglion the large, early-born proximal neurons come mostly from placodes and the glia from the crest, and the interaction between the two is needed for the ganglion to condense (the chase-and-run of [7.5.20](/7.5.20) is one example of their mutual dependence). Mutations of *EYA1* or *SIX1* cause branchio-oto-renal syndrome (hearing loss, branchial cysts and kidney malformations), reflecting the roles of these genes in placodes, pharyngeal arches and the kidney ([7.5.25](/7.5.25)); defects of the olfactory placode and of GnRH neuron migration cause Kallmann syndrome (anosmia with hypogonadotropic hypogonadism).

## 22.2. The Eye

### 22.2.1. The Eye Field and Cyclopia

The eye begins in the anterior **neural plate**, where a single, median eye field is specified during **gastrulation** by a set of eye-field transcription factors (Pax6, Rx, Six3, Lhx2, Six6), repressed posteriorly by Otx2 and Wnt signalling. The single field is divided into two by Shh secreted from the prechordal **mesoderm** beneath the midline, which represses *Pax6* in the centre and induces *Pax2* there (the future optic stalks and ventral diencephalon). The experiments are clear-cut. In zebrafish *cyclops* (a Nodal mutant lacking prechordal mesoderm) and in *Shh* **knockout mice**, a single median eye forms; in humans, mutations in *SHH*, *SIX3*, *ZIC2* or *TGIF* cause holoprosencephaly, failure of the forebrain to divide into two hemispheres, with cyclopia in its most severe form and milder facial midline defects (a single central incisor) in others. Cyclopamine, a steroidal alkaloid of the corn lily *Veratrum californicum*, causes cyclopia in lambs whose mothers grazed on it; it blocks **Smoothened**, which led to the discovery of the role of Shh signalling and later to Smoothened inhibitors used against basal cell **carcinoma**.

### 22.2.2. Optic Vesicle, Optic Cup and Lens Induction

The eye-field cells evaginate from the diencephalon as the optic vesicles, which grow towards the surface **ectoderm** and touch it. Where they touch, the ectoderm thickens into the lens placode, which then invaginates as the lens pit and pinches off as the lens vesicle. At the same time the optic vesicle folds in on itself to form the two-layered optic cup: the inner layer, facing the lens, becomes the neural retina, the outer layer the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE). The ventral side of the cup and stalk is closed only gradually, by fusion along the choroid fissure, through which blood vessels enter and ganglion cell axons leave; failure of this fusion leaves a coloboma, a gap in the iris, retina or optic nerve.

**Definition 22.2.1 (Lens Induction)** **Lens induction** is the process by which the head surface ectoderm, made competent by earlier signals, is induced to form the lens placode and lens; its final step requires contact with the optic vesicle.

Lens induction was the first **induction** studied experimentally, and its history shows how the concept of induction had to be refined. Spemann (1901) destroyed the eye rudiment of the frog *Rana fusca* with a hot needle and no lens formed, suggesting that the optic vesicle induces the lens. But in *Rana esculenta* a lens formed without an optic vesicle, and transplantation showed that the response of the ectoderm differs between species and stages. The resolution came from the analysis of **competence** (Grainger and colleagues): lens induction is a multistep process. The head ectoderm acquires a lens-forming bias during gastrulation and **neurulation**, from the anterior neural plate and the **endoderm** and mesoderm beneath; it becomes specified to form lens before the optic vesicle arrives (explants of it then form lentoids); and contact with the optic vesicle completes the determination and localizes the lens to the right place. Species differ in how much is done before the last step, which is why ablation of the vesicle prevented lens formation in one frog and not in the other.

The molecules fit this sequence. The ectoderm must express Pax6, which makes it competent: in *Small eye* mice, **heterozygous** for *Pax6*, the eyes are small, and in **chimeras** made of *Pax6* mutant and **wild-type** cells, mutant ectoderm cells are excluded from the lens even next to a wild-type optic vesicle, so Pax6 acts **cell-autonomously** in the responding tissue (Quinn et al. 1996). The optic vesicle provides BMP4 and FGFs; with BMP signalling, Pax6 and Sox2 activate lens genes (Pax6 and Sox2 together bind the **enhancer** of δ-crystallin in the chick, Kamachi et al. 2001). The induction is reciprocal: the lens in turn signals to the optic cup, and FGF from the lens and surface ectoderm specifies the inner layer of the cup as neural retina (expressing Vsx2/Chx10), while the outer layer, exposed to BMP and Wnt from the surrounding mesenchyme and Activin, becomes RPE (expressing Mitf and Otx2). Mitf and Vsx2 repress each other; in the absence of Vsx2, the retina transdifferentiates into pigmented epithelium, and an FGF bead can convert prospective RPE into neural retina.

### 22.2.3. Lens and Cornea

The posterior cells of the lens vesicle elongate into primary lens fibres that fill its cavity; the anterior cells remain as the lens epithelium, which divides at the equator throughout life and adds secondary fibres around the core. Lens fibres make large amounts of crystallins, soluble proteins packed at concentrations above 300 mg mL⁻¹ (α-crystallins, small heat shock proteins that prevent aggregation, and β- and γ-crystallins); they degrade their nuclei, mitochondria and other organelles, a programmed removal that involves DNase IIβ and phospholipase-dependent organelle breakdown, so that the fibres are transparent. Mutations in crystallin genes cause congenital cataracts. The surface ectoderm that closes over the lens becomes the corneal epithelium; **neural crest** mesenchyme migrates in to form the corneal stroma and endothelium (and the iris stroma), and the stroma becomes transparent as it lays down regularly spaced **collagen** fibrils and dehydrates under the action of thyroid hormones. The iris and ciliary body form from the rim of the optic cup.

### 22.2.4. The Retina

The neural retina is a layered piece of the central nervous system. Its seven main cell classes (ganglion, amacrine, horizontal and bipolar cells, rod and cone photoreceptors, and Müller glia) are born from a common pool of **multipotent** **progenitors** in a conserved, overlapping order: ganglion cells, horizontal cells, cones and amacrine cells first, rods, bipolar cells and Müller glia last. **Lineage tracing** with retroviruses showed that a single progenitor late in development can produce clones containing rods, bipolar cells and Müller glia together, so the progenitors are multipotent (Turner and Cepko 1987). The competence model explains the order: progenitors pass through a series of competence states, in each of which they can produce only certain cell types, regulated by temporal transcription factors (Ikzf1, the vertebrate homologue of the *Drosophila* Hunchback, for early fates; Casz1, homologue of Castor, for late fates), a direct parallel with the temporal series of insect **neuroblasts** ([7.5.19](/7.5.19)). Extrinsic factors shape the proportions: ganglion cells secrete Shh and GDF11, which limit the further production of ganglion cells.

Bottom: the birth periods of the seven main cell classes of the retina, which are born in a conserved, overlapping order from the same multipotent progenitors: ganglion cells, horizontal cells, cones and amacrine cells first, rods, bipolar cells and Mueller glia last](./plots/retina-timeline.py)

The ganglion cells, the first **neurons** born, send axons along the inner surface of the retina to the optic disc and out through the optic stalk, the beginning of the retinotectal projection of [7.5.21](/7.5.21). The light-sensitive photoreceptors lie in the outer layer, against the RPE: the vertebrate retina is inverted, a consequence of its origin from the folded optic vesicle, and the RPE is needed to recycle the photopigment chromophore and to phagocytose the shed outer segments.

### 22.2.5. Pax6 and Deep Homology

*Pax6* is required for eye development throughout the animals. Loss of one copy causes aniridia in humans (absent iris, with cataract and corneal defects) and *Small eye* in mice; loss of both copies, no eyes and no nose. The *Drosophila* gene *eyeless* is its homologue, and targeted expression of either *eyeless* or mouse *Pax6* in the imaginal discs of legs, wings or antennae induces ectopic compound eyes on those appendages (Halder, Callaerts and Gehring 1995). The mouse gene thus triggers a fly eye: Pax6 is a **master regulator** whose downstream network differs completely between the two eyes, although some structural genes (opsins, phototransduction) are shared. This is the classical example of **deep homology** ([7.5.39](/7.5.39)): structures that are not homologous in their anatomy (the camera eye and the compound eye evolved separately) are built with homologous regulatory genes inherited from a common ancestor that had photoreceptor cells controlled by a Pax6-like gene.

## 22.3. The Epidermis

The surface **ectoderm** that remains after the **neural plate**, crest and placodes have formed becomes the epidermis. At first a single layer covered by a flattened periderm, it becomes stratified in mid-gestation: a basal layer of dividing cells on a **basement membrane** gives rise to suprabasal cells that stop dividing, move outwards and differentiate through the spinous and granular layers into the dead, flattened cells of the cornified layer, filled with keratin and surrounded by a cornified envelope that forms the barrier of the skin. The transcription factor p63 is required for stratification (mice and humans lacking it have no epidermis or severe ectodermal dysplasias), Notch signalling drives the switch from basal to suprabasal differentiation, and the **keratins** change from K5/K14 in basal cells to K1/K10 in suprabasal cells. Mutations in *KRT5* or *KRT14* weaken the basal cells and cause epidermolysis bullosa simplex, in which the skin blisters at the slightest friction.

The basal layer contains the **stem cells** of the interfollicular epidermis ([7.3.24](/7.3.24)). **Lineage tracing** in the mouse showed that most basal cells behave as an equipotent population of **progenitors** whose divisions are balanced only on average: each division gives two dividing cells, two differentiating cells or one of each with fixed probabilities, so clones drift neutrally in size and some are lost while others grow (Clayton et al. 2007), the stochastic dynamics of [7.5.36](/7.5.36). The human epidermis is renewed in about four to eight weeks.

## 22.4. Ectodermal Appendages

### 22.4.1. Placode, Condensate and Recombination

**Definition 22.4.1 (Ectodermal Appendage)** An **ectodermal appendage** is an organ that develops from a placode of the surface epithelium together with a condensation of the underlying mesenchyme: hairs, feathers, scales, teeth, nails, and exocrine glands (sweat, sebaceous, salivary, mammary and lacrimal glands).

All appendages begin the same way: a placode of thickened epithelium appears above a dermal condensate, the two signal to each other, and the epithelium grows into the mesenchyme (bud) or out of the surface, before the organ-specific morphogenesis. The signals are shared: WNT/**β-catenin** (with the transcription factor Lef1) initiates the placode, EDA (ectodysplasin, a tumour necrosis factor family ligand) acting through its receptor EDAR and **NF-κB** promotes it, FGF20 induces the dermal condensate, BMPs inhibit placode formation between the appendages, and SHH from the placode drives the downgrowth.

Who determines what kind of appendage forms, and where? Recombination of epidermis and dermis from different regions and species, an experimental design that goes back to Saunders, Sengel and Dhouailly, gives a consistent answer.

**Example 22.4.1 (Dermis Decides Type and Pattern, Epidermis Decides Form)** In the chick, epidermis from the featherless region of the leg recombined with back dermis forms feathers; back epidermis on leg (scale-forming) dermis forms scales: the dermis determines the type of appendage and the pattern of the field. In recombinations between species (Dhouailly 1973), chick epidermis on mouse dermis forms feather buds arranged in the pattern of mouse hair, and mouse epidermis on chick dermis forms hairs: the epidermis determines the class of appendage it can make (a feather or a hair), according to its own genome. In teeth, mouse dental mesenchyme recombined with chick oral epithelium induced the epithelium to form tooth-like structures, although birds have lacked teeth for some 70 million years (Kollar and Fisher 1980), and the chick *talpid2* mutant forms tooth primordia (Harris et al. 2006): the epithelial capacity was retained, and its loss in birds is due to the loss of signals from the mesenchyme.

The EDA pathway illustrates the clinical side. **X-linked** hypohidrotic ectodermal dysplasia (XLHED), caused by mutations in *EDA*, combines sparse hair, missing and conical teeth and absent sweat glands, so that affected boys are at risk of hyperthermia; mutations of *EDAR* and of its adaptor *EDARADD* cause similar **autosomal** forms. Recombinant EDA given to affected fetuses in utero (intra-amniotically) restored sweat gland formation in three boys (Schneider et al. 2018). A common human variant, *EDAR* V370A, which increases receptor signalling, became frequent in East Asian and Native American populations; mice carrying it have thicker hair and more eccrine glands (Kamberov et al. 2013), a case in which a developmental signalling variant has been under positive selection in human evolution.

### 22.4.2. Teeth

Teeth form from the oral epithelium (enamel, from ameloblasts) and **neural crest** mesenchyme (dentin and pulp, from odontoblasts). The epithelium thickens into the dental lamina and then proceeds through bud, cap and bell stages. At the cap stage a cluster of non-dividing epithelial cells, the enamel knot, forms at the tip of the epithelial bud; it is a signalling centre that expresses Shh, FGF4, BMPs and Wnts, stimulates growth of the surrounding epithelium and, through secondary enamel knots at the sites of future cusps, determines the shape of the crown; the knot is then removed by **apoptosis** (Jernvall et al. 1998). Tooth type along the jaw (incisor or molar) is set by homeobox genes in the crest mesenchyme (*Dlx1/2* and *Barx1* in the molar field, *Msx1* and *Msx2* in the incisor field), so that misexpressing *Barx1* in the incisor region converts incisors into molars.

The successive molars develop one after another from the back of the dental lamina, and each inhibits the next. Kavanagh, Evans and Jernvall (2007) cultured mouse molar germs and showed that separating the second molar germ from the first increases its size; they summarized the observations by a simple rule.

**Formula 22.4.1 (Inhibitory Cascade of Molar Size)** If each molar germ is activated by the mesenchyme at a rate $a$ and inhibited by the preceding molar at a rate $i$, so that the size of each successive molar relative to the first changes by a constant step, the sizes $m_1, m_2, m_3$ of the first, second and third molars satisfy

$$
\frac{m_2}{m_1} = 1 + \frac{a - i}{i},
\qquad
\frac{m_3}{m_1} = 1 + 2\,\frac{a - i}{i}
\;\;\Longrightarrow\;\;
\frac{m_3}{m_1} = 2\,\frac{m_2}{m_1} - 1,
\tag{22.1}
$$

so the second molar is always the average of the first and the third, one third of the total molar row.

The rule predicts that a change in the balance $a/i$ moves the relative sizes along a single line, and the molar proportions of murine rodents (and, with modifications, of many mammals, including the reduction of the third molar in humans) fall close to it. A single developmental parameter thus constrains the variation available to evolution, an example of the developmental constraint discussed in [7.5.39](/7.5.39).

### 22.4.3. Glands

Sweat glands, sebaceous glands and mammary glands arise from placodes in the same way. The mammary glands of mammals form from the paired mammary lines along the trunk, in which five pairs of placodes appear in the mouse (one pair in humans; supernumerary nipples along the lines are common) under the control of Wnt, FGF10 from the somites and Tbx3 (whose mutation in ulnar-mammary syndrome causes hypoplasia of the breasts and of the forelimb). The mammary epithelium then branches into the fat pad, a **branching morphogenesis** that resumes at puberty under hormonal control ([7.5.25](/7.5.25)).

## 22.5. Spacing of Appendages

Hairs and feathers are not scattered at random: in a field of skin they form a regular array, with a minimum distance between neighbours, and feathers appear in rows that propagate from the dorsal midline. Three mechanisms have been proposed, and they now seem to act together.

### 22.5.1. Reaction-Diffusion and Lateral Inhibition

In the mouse, the spacing of hair follicles is consistent with a reaction-diffusion system in the sense of [7.5.12](/7.5.12) (a **Turing pattern**), with WNT as the activator and DKK (Dickkopf), a secreted WNT inhibitor induced by WNT, as the inhibitor (Sick et al. 2006). The evidence comes from testing the predictions of the model rather than from measuring the diffusion coefficients: WNT/β-catenin activity appears first in a diffuse, low-level pattern that then sharpens into spots; overexpression of DKK in the epidermis lowers the follicle density in a dose-dependent way, as predicted when inhibitor production is increased; and local changes in WNT activity shift the positions of follicles relative to one another in the way the model predicts. In the chick, BMPs from each feather primordium inhibit primordium formation around it while FGF promotes it (Jung et al. 1998), a form of **lateral inhibition** with the same mathematical structure.

**Formula 22.5.1 (Activator-Inhibitor Model of Appendage Spacing)** In a field of skin, let $a(\boldsymbol{x}, t)$ be the activator (WNT activity) and $h(\boldsymbol{x}, t)$ the inhibitor (DKK, or BMP), with **competence** $m(\boldsymbol{x}, t) \in \{0, 1\}$ of the tissue to respond. In the Gierer–Meinhardt form of [7.5.12](/7.5.12) (**Gierer–Meinhardt model**),

$$
\frac{\partial a}{\partial t} = D_a\nabla^{2}a + m\Bigl(\rho\,\frac{a^{2}}{h} + \sigma\Bigr) - \mu a,
\qquad
\frac{\partial h}{\partial t} = D_h\nabla^{2}h + m\,\rho a^{2} - \nu h,
\tag{22.2}
$$

with $D_h \gg D_a$. The uniform state is unstable to perturbations in a band of wavelengths of order $2\pi\bigl(D_aD_h/\mu\nu\bigr)^{1/4}$ (the Turing condition of [2.5.7](/2.5.7)), and the stable patterns are arrays of activator peaks, the placodes, at a characteristic spacing independent of the size of the field.

How the competence arrives matters. In the chick, the ability to form feather primordia spreads as a wave from the dorsal midline, and the primordia appear row by row; each new row forms at the edge of the previous one, in register with it, and the result is a nearly hexagonal lattice. In mouse skin, much of a hair field becomes competent at once, and the follicles appear simultaneously with less regular packing. Because such patterns are multistable, the final spacing depends on how the pattern was laid down as well as on the parameters: sequential formation gives each new peak room to mature and suppress its surroundings, which tends to produce wider spacing than simultaneous formation, whose spacing is close to the fastest-growing wavelength of the linear theory.

(A) Competence spreads as a front from the left (the dorsal midline), as in chick feather tracts, where rows of primordia are added one after another; each new row forms in register with the last, giving a fairly regular array. ### 22.5.2. A Mechanochemical Model

Mesenchymal cells pull on the **extracellular matrix** through their protrusions and deform it over long distances; if they also move up gradients of matrix density (haptotaxis), traction can concentrate both cells and matrix. Oster, Murray and Harris (1983) proposed that this mechanical feedback, without any diffusible **morphogen**, could create periodic condensations of dermal cells, the papillae of feathers, scales and teeth. Percus and Childress give a reduced version of their model and its stability analysis.

**Definition 22.5.1 (Mechanochemical Model)** A **mechanochemical model** of morphogenesis is a model in which mechanical forces generated by cells (contraction, traction on the extracellular matrix) and the deformation of the tissue are coupled to cell movement, proliferation or chemical signalling, so that mechanical and chemical variables together determine the pattern.

**Formula 22.5.2 (The Oster–Murray Model of Dermal Condensation)** Let $n(\boldsymbol{r}, t)$ be the density of mesenchymal cells, $\rho(\boldsymbol{r}, t)$ the density of extracellular matrix and $\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{r}, t)$ the displacement of the matrix from its unstressed position, with strain $\varepsilon = \tfrac12\bigl(\nabla\boldsymbol{u} + (\nabla\boldsymbol{u})^{\mathsf T}\bigr)$ and dilation $\theta = \nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{u}$. Cell conservation is $\dot n = -\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{j} + M$, with a current made of three parts,

$$
\boldsymbol{j} = \underbrace{-D\nabla n + D'\nabla\nabla^{2}n}_{\text{random motion, long range}} \;+\; \underbrace{\alpha\,n\nabla\rho}_{\text{haptotaxis}} \;+\; \underbrace{n\,\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}}_{\text{carried by the matrix}},
\qquad
M = r\,n(N - n),
$$

where the fourth-order term comes from averaging the cell-cell interaction $\bar n(\boldsymbol{r}) = \int w(\boldsymbol{r}')\,n(\boldsymbol{r} + \boldsymbol{r}')\,\mathrm{d}^{3}r'$ over a kernel with $\int w = 1$, $\int \boldsymbol{r}'w = \boldsymbol{0}$, $\int \boldsymbol{r}'\boldsymbol{r}'w = -\gamma I$, expanded to second order. The matrix is conserved, $\dot\rho + \nabla\cdot(\rho\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}) = 0$, and in mechanical equilibrium, without inertia, $\nabla\cdot\sigma - s\rho\,\boldsymbol{u} = \boldsymbol{0}$, where $-s\boldsymbol{u}$ is the elastic tethering of the matrix to the substratum. The stress is viscoelastic plus cell traction,

$$
\sigma = \mu_1\dot\varepsilon + \mu_2\dot\theta I + E'\bigl(\varepsilon + \nu'\theta I\bigr) + \frac{\tau\,n\,(\rho + \beta\nabla^{2}\rho)}{1 + \lambda n^{2}}\,I,
\tag{22.3}
$$

with shear and bulk viscosities $\mu_1, \mu_2$, $E' = E/(1 + \nu)$ for Young's modulus $E$ and Poisson ratio $\nu$, $\nu' = \nu/(1 - 2\nu)$, traction coefficient $\tau$, long-range traction $\beta$ and contact inhibition of traction $\lambda$.

To find whether a uniform state $n = \rho = 1$ (after scaling), $\boldsymbol{u} = \boldsymbol{0}$, is stable, linearize and simplify: set $\lambda = 0$, drop the long-range and haptotactic terms ($D' = \beta = \alpha = 0$) and **mitosis** ($r = 0$), take $\mu_1 = \mu_2 = \mu$ and absorb the factors $1 + \nu'$ into the parameters. The reduced, linearized system is

$$
\dot n = D_1\nabla^{2}n - \dot\theta,
\qquad
\nabla\cdot\bigl[\mu(\dot\varepsilon + \dot\theta I) + \varepsilon + \nu'\theta I + \tau(n + \rho)I\bigr] = s\boldsymbol{u},
\qquad
\dot\rho + \dot\theta = 0 .
\tag{22.4}
$$

**Theorem 22.5.1 (Dispersion Relation and Critical Traction)** For perturbations proportional to $\mathrm{e}^{\lambda t + \mathrm{i}\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{r}}$ with $K = \lvert\boldsymbol{k}\rvert^{2}$, the longitudinal modes of Eq. (22.4) satisfy $\lambda\bigl[\mu K\lambda^{2} + b(K)\lambda + c(K)\bigr] = 0$ with

$$
b(K) = \mu D_1K^{2} + (1 - 2\tau)K + s,
\qquad
c(K) = D_1K\bigl[(1 - \tau)K + s\bigr].
\tag{22.5}
$$

Instability first occurs when $\min_K b(K) < 0$, that is for traction

$$
\tau > \tau_{\mathrm{c}} = \tfrac12 + (\mu s D_1)^{1/2},
\qquad
\text{at wavenumber } k_{\mathrm{c}} = \Bigl(\frac{s}{\mu D_1}\Bigr)^{1/4}.
$$

**Proof** Consider one Fourier mode along $\boldsymbol{k}$ (a longitudinal mode, $\boldsymbol{u} \parallel \boldsymbol{k}$, which in one dimension is the only kind), with amplitudes $n, \rho, u$. Matrix conservation gives $\lambda\rho = -\lambda\,\mathrm{i}ku$, so $\rho = -\mathrm{i}ku$ for $\lambda \ne 0$. Cell conservation gives $\lambda n = -D_1Kn - \lambda\,\mathrm{i}ku$, so $n = -\mathrm{i}k\lambda u/(\lambda + D_1K)$. In the force balance, with $\varepsilon = \theta = \mathrm{i}ku$ for a longitudinal mode and the viscous and elastic factors absorbed, the divergence of the stress is $\mathrm{i}k\bigl[\mu\lambda\,\mathrm{i}ku + \mathrm{i}ku + \tau(n + \rho)\bigr] = -K(\mu\lambda + 1)u + \mathrm{i}k\tau(n + \rho)$. Substituting $n$ and $\rho$, $\mathrm{i}k\tau(n + \rho) = \tau K u\bigl(\lambda/(\lambda + D_1K) + 1\bigr)$, and the balance $\nabla\cdot\sigma = su$ becomes

$$
-K(\mu\lambda + 1 - \tau) + \frac{\tau K\lambda}{\lambda + D_1K} - s = 0 .
$$

Multiplying by $-(\lambda + D_1K)$ and collecting powers of $\lambda$ gives $\mu K\lambda^{2} + \bigl[\mu D_1K^{2} + (1 - 2\tau)K + s\bigr]\lambda + D_1K\bigl[(1-\tau)K + s\bigr] = 0$, together with the root $\lambda = 0$ excluded above (a neutral mode from conservation of matrix). For $\tau < 1$ the coefficient $c(K)$ is positive, so the quadratic has a root with positive real part exactly when $b(K) < 0$. As a function of $K$, $b$ is a parabola with minimum at $K^{*} = (2\tau - 1)/(2\mu D_1)$ and minimum value $s - (2\tau - 1)^{2}/(4\mu D_1)$, which is negative iff $2\tau - 1 > 2(\mu sD_1)^{1/2}$. At the threshold $K^{*} = (s/\mu D_1)^{1/2}$, which gives $k_{\mathrm{c}}$. $\square$

Two remarks qualify the result. At the threshold $b = 0$ and $c > 0$ (when $\tau_{\mathrm{c}} < 1$), so the two critical roots are purely imaginary: in this reduced model the instability first appears as an oscillation of growing amplitude at wavenumber $k_{\mathrm{c}}$, whereas a real (stationary) root becomes positive only when $c < 0$, which requires $\tau > 1 + s/K$, at short wavelengths. Stationary patterns of fixed wavelength, as observed, are recovered in the fuller model, where the long-range terms $D'$ and $\beta$ penalize short wavelengths and haptotaxis adds a destabilizing coupling. Second, the threshold behaves as expected physically: strong tethering to the substratum (large $s$), large viscosity or fast cell diffusion stabilize the tissue, and the selected wavelength, $2\pi/k_{\mathrm{c}} = 2\pi(\mu D_1/s)^{1/4}$, grows only as the fourth root of these parameters, so the spacing of papillae is robust to moderate changes in them.

**Example 22.5.1** With $\mu = D_1 = 1$ and $s = 0.1$ in scaled units, $\tau_{\mathrm{c}} = 0.5 + \sqrt{0.1} = 0.82$ and $k_{\mathrm{c}} = 0.1^{1/4} = 0.56$, a wavelength of $2\pi/0.56 \approx 11$ length units. Doubling the substrate stiffness to $s = 0.2$ raises $\tau_{\mathrm{c}}$ to 0.95 and shortens the wavelength by only a factor $2^{1/4} \approx 1.19$.

### 22.5.3. Current View: Mechanics and Signalling Together

For two decades the mechanochemical model was regarded as an elegant alternative without experimental support. Since 2017 it has been revived. In chick skin, dermal cells self-organize into aggregates by their own contractility, without prior molecular patterning: blocking actomyosin contraction prevents the aggregates, and the compression of the overlying epidermis at an aggregate activates β-catenin and starts the follicle programme, a mechanical **induction** of the placode (Shyer et al. 2017). In mouse skin, dermal condensation involves cell movement driven by **chemotaxis** towards FGF20 and other signals from the placode (Glover et al. 2017), and the dermal cells and their matrix become transiently fluid-like, allowing aggregation (Palmquist et al. 2022). In feather tracts, the wave of patterning and the spacing depend on both FGF/BMP signalling and on mesenchymal mechanics (Ho et al. 2019). The current view is therefore a combination: a mechanical instability of the dermis, as in Oster and Murray's model, coupled to activator-inhibitor signalling of the WNT-DKK and FGF-BMP kind, each reinforcing the other.

## 22.6. The Hair Cycle and Its Stem Cells

A hair follicle is an epithelial tube continuous with the epidermis, with a sebaceous gland, an arrector pili muscle and, at its base, the bulb, where the matrix cells surround the dermal papilla, a condensate of specialized fibroblasts. The matrix cells divide rapidly and produce the hair shaft and its sheaths. Throughout life the follicle cycles between growth (anagen, years for human scalp hair), regression (catagen, weeks, in which the lower follicle dies by **apoptosis** and the papilla is drawn upwards) and rest (telogen, months).

The cycle depends on **stem cells** in the bulge, the region of the outer root sheath at the attachment of the arrector pili. Label-retaining cells were found there (Cotsarelis, Sun and Lavker 1990), and **lineage tracing** with bulge markers (K15, CD34, Lgr5, Sox9) showed that bulge cells regenerate the whole follicle in each cycle, and also the sebaceous gland and, after wounding, the interfollicular epidermis (Ito et al. 2005). At the end of telogen, signals from the dermal papilla, activation of WNT and relief of BMP inhibition (the papilla secretes BMP antagonists, while the surrounding dermis and adipocytes release BMPs in a cycle), activate first the secondary hair germ and then the bulge to start a new anagen. In male pattern baldness, the bulge stem cells of the scalp are retained but the hair germ **progenitors** are not activated, in part because of androgen-dependent prostaglandin D2 in the scalp (Garza et al. 2012). Large wounds in adult mice can regenerate new follicles from epidermal cells in a WNT-dependent process (wound-induced hair neogenesis, Ito et al. 2007), a case of regeneration of an embryonic programme in the adult ([7.5.35](/7.5.35)).

## Questions

1. Spemann's ablation of the optic vesicle prevented lens formation in one frog species but not in another. Explain how the multistep model of lens induction reconciles the results, and design an experiment to determine when the head **ectoderm** becomes specified to form lens.

   **Answer** In the multistep model the ectoderm receives lens-promoting signals before the optic vesicle arrives (from anterior **neural plate**, **endoderm** and **mesoderm**); in species where these early steps suffice for specification, removing the vesicle late still allows a lens, whereas in others the final step is needed. To find the time of specification, explant presumptive lens ectoderm at successive stages into a neutral environment (culture medium, or transplanted to the flank) and score crystallin expression and lentoid formation; the earliest stage at which explants form lens marks specification, and transplantation into a different region of the head (to test determination) shows when the fate becomes fixed.

2. In **chimeras** of *Pax6* mutant and wild-type mouse cells, mutant cells are excluded from the lens even when the optic vesicle is wild-type. What does this show, and what result would have indicated that Pax6 acts in the **inducer**?

   **Answer** Pax6 is required **cell-autonomously** in the responding ectoderm, for its **competence**: wild-type neighbours and a wild-type inducer cannot rescue mutant ectoderm cells. If Pax6 acted in the optic vesicle (inducer), lens formation would depend on the genotype of the vesicle, and mutant ectoderm cells next to a wild-type vesicle would contribute to the lens normally, while a mutant vesicle would fail to induce wild-type ectoderm.

3. Why do mutations in *SHH* cause both holoprosencephaly and cyclopia, and why are the facial features often milder than the brain defect in heterozygous carriers?

   **Answer** Shh from the prechordal mesoderm and ventral forebrain is required to divide the median eye field and to separate the forebrain into two hemispheres; with reduced Shh the field is not split (cyclopia, or close-set eyes) and the forebrain stays undivided. Shh from the forebrain and facial ectoderm also drives the outgrowth of the midline face. Heterozygous loss halves the dose; whether a threshold is crossed depends on other genetic and environmental factors (modifier genes, maternal cholesterol, alcohol), so phenotypes vary from severe alobar holoprosencephaly to only a single central incisor, a dose-sensitive, threshold phenotype.

4. Chick epidermis combined with mouse dermis forms feather buds arranged in a mouse hair pattern. What does each part of this result tell us, and what would you expect from mouse epidermis on chick scale-forming dermis?

   **Answer** The kind of structure (a feather) follows the genotype of the epidermis, so the epidermis determines the class of appendage it can build; the arrangement and the instruction to form an appendage come from the dermis. Mouse epidermis on chick leg (scale) dermis should form hair follicles, in the arrangement imposed by the chick dermis; the dermis provides "make an appendage here" and regional type signals, the epidermis interprets them in its species-specific way.

5. Using Formula 22.4.1, predict the relative size of the third molar if the second molar is 80% the size of the first. What happens to the third molar if $a/i$ falls below 1/2?

   **Answer** $m_3/m_1 = 2(0.8) - 1 = 0.6$. With $m_2/m_1 = 1 + (a - i)/i = a/i$, and $m_3/m_1 = 2a/i - 1$; for $a/i < 1/2$, $m_3/m_1 < 0$, meaning the third molar does not form, as in mammals (and many humans) that lack third molars. The model predicts that loss of m3 should be associated with a small m2 (below half the size of m1), a pattern seen in murine rodents.

6. Show from Theorem 22.5.1 that in the reduced mechanochemical model the instability at $\tau = \tau_{\mathrm{c}}$ is oscillatory, and compute its frequency for $\mu = D_1 = 1$, $s = 0.1$.

   **Answer** At $\tau_{\mathrm{c}}$ and $K = K_{\mathrm{c}} = \sqrt{s/\mu D_1} = 0.316$, $b = 0$, so $\mu K\lambda^{2} + c = 0$, $\lambda = \pm\mathrm{i}\sqrt{c/(\mu K)}$. With $\tau_{\mathrm{c}} = 0.816$, $c = D_1K[(1 - \tau_{\mathrm{c}})K + s] = 0.316(0.184\times0.316 + 0.1) = 0.316\times0.158 = 0.050$, so $\omega = \sqrt{0.050/0.316} = 0.40$ in scaled time units, a period of about 16 units; this matches the frequency near $k_{\mathrm{c}}$ in panel C of the figure. Since $c > 0$, both roots are complex: the instability is oscillatory.

7. The WNT-DKK model predicts that overexpressing DKK in the epidermis decreases hair follicle density. Explain why, and how one could distinguish a reaction-diffusion mechanism from a **prepattern** in which follicle positions are specified by an independent grid.

   **Answer** In an activator-inhibitor system, more inhibitor (or a lower activator-to-inhibitor ratio) increases the range of inhibition around each activator peak, so peaks are spaced further apart and density falls, in a graded way with DKK dose. A prepattern would predict that the positions of follicles are fixed and that DKK would only remove some follicles from their positions, leaving the remaining ones on the original grid with gaps. Reaction-diffusion predicts instead a rescaled, regular pattern with larger spacing; also, local perturbations (removing a primordium, or boosting WNT at one point) cause neighbouring primordia to shift and rearrange, which a fixed prepattern does not. Sick et al. found the graded change in spacing.

8. Why are feather primordia in the chick laid down row by row, and what does the simulation of Formula 22.5.1 suggest about the effect of this sequential mode on the regularity of the pattern?

   **Answer** Competence to form primordia spreads as a front from the dorsal midline (driven by a wave of signalling, such as EDA and FGF, and by maturation of the dermis), so each row forms next to an existing one. Each new primordium is positioned by inhibition from the previous row, which locks it in register, and the pattern grows as an ordered lattice from the first row, like crystal growth from a seed; simultaneous formation over a large field, as in the simulation and in mouse hair fields, nucleates many domains with different orientations and gives irregular packing.

9. Bald scalp retains bulge **stem cells**. Explain why this matters for treatment and what signalling manipulation might restore hair growth.

   **Answer** Because the stem cells remain, baldness is a failure of activation of the hair germ and **progenitors**, not a loss of the stem cell pool, so it is in principle reversible. Treatments that activate the hair germ could restore anagen: blocking the PGD2 receptor GPR44 (DP2), inhibiting androgen action (finasteride reduces DHT), activating WNT signalling or relieving BMP inhibition, and pharmacological activation of anagen (minoxidil acts on follicle blood supply and potassium channels); strategies that depend on stem cells being absent (such as transplants) are not the only option.

10. (Open) The *EDAR* V370A **allele** became common in East Asia. Outline how one would test whether it was positively selected and which developmental trait might have been the target.

    **Answer** Population genetics: show that the allele lies in a long haplotype with high frequency and low diversity (high extended haplotype homozygosity, iHS), that its frequency differs strongly between populations (high FST), and date the sweep (about 30 000 years ago, estimated from haplotype length). Development: knock the variant into mice and measure appendage traits (hair thickness, number of eccrine glands, mammary branching), then associate the variant with the same traits in humans (hair thickness, sweat gland density, tooth shape such as shovel incisors). Kamberov et al. (2013) found that mice with V370A have thicker hair, more eccrine glands and altered mammary glands; the target of selection might have been sweating in a hot, humid climate or mammary gland features, which remain hypotheses since a single allele affects several traits.
