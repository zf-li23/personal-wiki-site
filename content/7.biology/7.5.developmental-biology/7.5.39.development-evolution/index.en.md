Evolution changes adult forms, but adults are built by development, so every heritable change of form is a change of development. Evolutionary developmental biology ("evo-devo") asks which changes of development produce the variation on which **natural selection** acts, and why some kinds of variation are common and others never appear: in a phrase of the field, it studies the arrival of the fittest as well as their survival. This chapter collects the evolutionary threads that ran through the course, the conserved toolkit of signaling pathways ([7.5.3](/7.5.3)), the **Hox genes** and their colinearity ([7.5.15](/7.5.15), [7.5.17](/7.5.17)), the regulatory evolution of the limb ([7.5.32](/7.5.32#sec-32.7)), and organizes them by mechanism. It begins with the old observation that embryos of different animals resemble each other more than adults do, then describes the properties of developmental systems that make evolutionary change possible (modularity, **duplication**, a shared toolkit), the four ways in which gene expression can change (in place, time, amount and kind), case studies in which the mutations are known, the constraints that make some forms unreachable, and the role of environmentally induced variation and its genetic assimilation. The population genetics of these changes, phylogenetics and macroevolution belong to [7.10](/7.10); the environmental induction of phenotypes was described in [7.5.38](/7.5.38#sec-38.8).

## 39.1. Embryos and Evolution

### 39.1.1. Von Baer's Laws and Recapitulation

In 1828 Karl Ernst von Baer described a regularity of vertebrate embryos that he could not explain, since he rejected evolution: he had two unlabelled embryos in alcohol and could not tell whether they were lizards, birds or mammals.

**Definition 39.1.1 (Von Baer's Laws)** **Von Baer's laws** of embryology state that (1) the general characters of a large group of animals appear earlier in development than the special characters of its subgroups; (2) less general characters develop from more general ones; (3) the embryo of a species departs progressively from the embryos of other species instead of passing through their adult stages; (4) the embryo of a higher animal therefore resembles the embryo, not the adult, of a lower animal.

Darwin read von Baer's laws as evidence of common descent: shared early stages reflect a common ancestor, later divergence reflects descent with modification. He was delighted that the larvae of barnacles showed them to be crustaceans, and Kowalevsky (1866) showed that the tadpole-like larvae of tunicates have a notochord and a dorsal nerve cord, uniting them with the vertebrates as chordates. Ernst Haeckel turned the laws into the "biogenetic law" that ontogeny recapitulates phylogeny, the embryo passing through the adult stages of its ancestors, with evolution proceeding by adding stages to the end of development (terminal addition). Recapitulation was abandoned because of too many exceptions; the **pharyngeal arches** and clefts of a human embryo are not the gills of an adult fish but the embryonic structures from which the gills of a fish-like ancestor developed. Walter Garstang (1922) summarized the reversal: "ontogeny does not recapitulate phylogeny, it creates it."

### 39.1.2. The Phylotypic Stage and the Hourglass

**Definition 39.1.2 (Phylotypic Stage)** The **phylotypic stage** of a phylum is the stage of development at which its members most resemble each other and show the characteristic features of the body plan; for vertebrates, the pharyngula or tailbud stage, with notochord, dorsal **neural tube**, **somites** and **pharyngeal arches**.

Development is less conserved both before and after this stage. Early stages differ greatly because of different modes of reproduction: a yolky bird egg, the cell layer of a fish on its yolk ([7.5.5](/7.5.5)), a mammalian **blastocyst** with trophoblast and **inner cell mass** that serves a **placenta**. Later stages diverge in the specializations of each class. This pattern, wide at both ends and narrow in the middle, is called the developmental hourglass (Duboule 1994; Raff 1996). The phylotypic stage is not identical across species, as Richardson and colleagues (1997) emphasized: **somite** numbers at the tailbud stage range from about 11 to more than 60, and limb buds appear at different times. Comparisons of **transcriptomes** nevertheless support the hourglass: genes expressed at the phylotypic period are, on average, older and more conserved in sequence and expression level than those expressed earlier or later, in flies, fish and plants (Kalinka and colleagues 2010; Domazet-Lošo and Tautz 2010). A favoured explanation is that the phylotypic stage is the time of greatest interaction between modules: the axes, **germ layers** and **Hox colinearity** are being integrated, and a change at this stage would have many consequences (Raff 1996; Galis and Metz 2001).

## 39.2. Preconditions for Evolution: The Structure of Developmental Systems

Development is finely tuned. How can it change without destroying the organism? If a protein is altered, it is altered everywhere it is made. Two properties of developmental systems resolve the difficulty: modularity, and the reuse of a small set of genes, enlarged by **duplication**.

### 39.2.1. Modularity

**Definition 39.2.1 (Modularity)** **Modularity** is the organization of a developing organism into units (modules) that are internally integrated and develop relatively independently of each other, so that one module can change without changing the others. Modules exist at several levels: morphogenetic fields (limb, eye, heart), organ primordia and **imaginal discs**, **cell lineages**, signaling pathways, and the **enhancers** that control expression of a gene in one tissue.

Experimental embryologists knew the anatomical side: a limb bud from a large salamander species grafted onto a small one grows to the size typical of the donor (Twitty and Schwind 1931), so the **limb field** controls its own growth. The genetic side is more recent. A gene expressed in several tissues usually has a separate **enhancer** for each, and each **enhancer** can be gained, lost or modified without effect on the others ([7.2](/7.2)). A mutation in an enhancer is therefore much less pleiotropic than a mutation in the coding sequence, and it is now thought that changes in cis-regulatory sequences are the most common cause of morphological differences between closely related animals (Carroll 2008; Stern and Orgogozo 2008). The **ZRS**, the limb enhancer of *Shh*, whose loss removed the limbs of snakes and whose **point mutations** cause polydactyly ([7.5.32](/7.5.32#sec-32.1.3)), is the standard example; the stickleback *Pitx1* enhancer (39.5.1) is another.

### 39.2.2. Co-option

**Definition 39.2.2 (Co-option)** **Co-option** (recruitment) is the use in a new place or at a new time of an existing gene, pathway or gene regulatory network, which there acquires a new developmental role.

The larval skeleton of sea urchins is made by the micromere-derived **skeletogenic mesenchyme** ([7.5.6](/7.5.6)). In other echinoderms the skeletogenic genes are active only in the adult; in sea urchins a change in the enhancer of one gene placed the whole skeletogenic subcircuit under the control of the micromere **double-negative gate**, and a larval skeleton appeared. In beetles, the forewings are hardened elytra. In *Drosophila* and beetles alike, the **selector gene** *apterous* specifies the dorsal compartment of the wing disc; in the beetle *Tribolium*, Apterous also activates the genes of the exoskeleton in the forewing, while the **Hox gene** *Ultrabithorax* keeps the hindwing membranous (Tomoyasu and colleagues 2009). The eyespots of butterflies (39.5.3) are a co-option of wing and leg patterning genes.

### 39.2.3. A Small Toolkit, Enlarged by Duplication

Although animals differ enormously, their development uses the same few families of transcription factors and signaling pathways ([7.5.3](/7.5.3)). Cnidarians such as the sea anemone *Nematostella vectensis* have Wnts, BMPs and most other families, and use BMP and Wnt asymmetries around the **blastopore**. Gene families arise by duplication and divergence: after duplication, one copy can retain the old function while the other accumulates mutations and takes on a new role (**neofunctionalization**), or the two copies divide the functions of the ancestral gene between them, each keeping some of its enhancers (**subfunctionalization**; the duplication–degeneration–complementation model of Force and colleagues 1999). Copies within a genome are **paralogs**; corresponding genes in different species are **orthologs**.

The **Hox genes** are the clearest example. Hox genes are found in bilaterians and cnidarians but not in sponges or ctenophores, so the ancestral cluster arose by tandem duplications of a **homeobox** gene in the common ancestor of cnidarians and bilaterians. The bilaterian ancestor probably had a cluster of about seven genes; insects have eight to ten, with *bicoid* and *zen* derived from a group 3 gene and no longer acting as Hox genes ([7.5.14](/7.5.14)); amphioxus has a single cluster of 15 genes; and the two rounds of **whole-genome duplication** at the origin of vertebrates produced four clusters, which lost different genes and now contain 39 genes in mammals ([7.4.16](/7.4.16#sec-16.6.3)). Teleost fishes, after a third duplication, have seven or eight clusters. The order of genes in the clusters and the colinearity of their expression have been conserved: human HOXB4 can substitute for its *Drosophila* **ortholog** Deformed (Malicki and colleagues 1992). Why colinearity is conserved is still discussed; in vertebrates, enhancers outside the clusters act on neighbouring genes in a polar way, which makes the gene order functionally important ([7.5.32](/7.5.32#sec-32.7.1)).

Colours: anterior, group 3, central and posterior groups. The 3′-to-5′ order corresponds to anterior and early expression to posterior and late expression](./plots/hox-clusters.py)

Duplication continues. The gene *SRGAP2*, which regulates the maturation of dendritic spines and the migration of cortical **neurons**, was partially duplicated two to three million years ago in the human lineage. The truncated copy *SRGAP2C* encodes a protein that inhibits the ancestral SRGAP2A; expressed in mice, it delays spine maturation and increases spine density (Charrier and colleagues 2012). A partial human-specific duplicate of a Notch receptor gene, *NOTCH2NL*, prolongs the proliferation of cortical progenitors (**radial glial cells**, [7.5.19](/7.5.19)) in **organoids**. Such duplications may have contributed to the prolonged development of the human cortex.

## 39.3. Deep Homology

**Definition 39.3.1 (Deep Homology)** **Deep homology** is the sharing, by structures that are not homologous as structures or evolved independently, of genetic regulatory mechanisms that are homologous, inherited from a common ancestor in which these mechanisms built a simpler precursor (Shubin, Tabin and Carroll 1997, 2009).

- Eyes. The compound eye of a fly and the camera eye of a vertebrate are not homologous as eyes; both depend on the transcription factor Pax6 (Eyeless in flies). Expressing mouse *Pax6* in the leg disc of *Drosophila* induces ectopic compound eyes on the legs (Halder, Callaerts and Gehring 1995): the mouse gene activates the fly's eye program, not a mouse eye. Pax6, photoreceptor opsins and some phototransduction proteins were present in the common ancestor, which had at most a simple light-sensitive organ ([7.5.22](/7.5.22)).
- Hearts. The insect dorsal vessel and the vertebrate heart both depend on Tinman/Nkx2.5 ([7.5.28](/7.5.28)).
- Appendages. The legs of insects, the parapodia of annelids, the tube feet of echinoderms and the limbs of vertebrates all express *Distal-less*/*Dlx* at their tips (Panganiban and colleagues 1997), although they are not homologous as appendages.
- The anterior–posterior axis. Hox genes pattern the axis in all bilaterians, and *Otx* genes the anterior head region.
- The dorso-ventral axis. In *Drosophila*, the BMP Dpp is dorsal and its antagonist Short **gastrulation** (Sog) ventral; the nerve cord forms ventrally where BMP signaling is lowest ([7.5.14](/7.5.14)). In vertebrates BMP4 is ventral and its antagonist Chordin, secreted by the **Spemann organizer**, dorsal; the **neural plate** forms dorsally ([7.5.16](/7.5.16)). The molecules are interchangeable: Dpp ventralizes *Xenopus* embryos and Chordin acts like Sog in flies (Holley, De Robertis and colleagues 1995). Within the neuroectoderm, the same three **homeodomain** genes subdivide the nervous system into columns from the midline outwards: *vnd*, *ind* and *msh* in flies, *Nkx2.2*, *Gsx* and *Msx* in vertebrates.

![Deep homology of dorso-ventral patterning (schematic cross sections; the *Xenopus* section is drawn dorsal side down). In both, a BMP (Dpp in *Drosophila*, BMP4 in *Xenopus*) is high on the side opposite the nervous system and its antagonist (Sog, Chordin) on the neural side; the neuroectoderm is subdivided from the midline outwards by homologous genes (vnd, ind, msh and Nkx2.2, Gsx, Msx). The bars show the BMP activity gradients. The whole system is inverted relative to the mouth](./plots/dv-inversion.py)

Étienne Geoffroy Saint-Hilaire had proposed in 1822, from anatomy, that the vertebrate is an inverted arthropod, an idea ridiculed for more than 150 years. The molecular data suggest that the common ancestor of protostomes and deuterostomes (the urbilaterian) already used a BMP–Chordin gradient to position its nervous system, and that the axis was inverted in the chordate lineage relative to the mouth, which in deuterostomes forms as a new opening. Studies of annelids and of hemichordates, which use the system differently, show that the history was more complex than a single inversion, and whether the urbilaterian had a centralized nervous system is debated. Deep homology does not mean that structures are homologous; it means that evolution builds new structures with old regulatory circuits.

## 39.4. Mechanisms of Evolutionary Change

Mary-Claire King and Allan Wilson (1975) found that human and chimpanzee proteins are about 99 % identical and concluded that the anatomical differences must lie mainly in "a few regulatory systems". François Jacob (1977) compared evolution with a tinkerer who reuses what is at hand. Wallace Arthur (2004) classified the ways in which gene expression can change during development.

**Definition 39.4.1 (Heterotopy, Heterochrony, Heterometry, Heterotypy)** An evolutionary change in development is a **heterotopy** if a gene is expressed or a process occurs in a new place; a **heterochrony** if the timing or rate of a developmental process changes relative to other processes or to the ancestor; a **heterometry** if the amount of a gene product or the size of a structure changes; and a **heterotypy** if the gene product itself changes in kind, by a mutation in the coding sequence.

The first three are usually caused by changes in regulatory sequences and are possible because of modularity; heterotypy changes the protein everywhere it is made and is therefore commoner in genes with few expression domains, although changes in transcription factors have also been important.

### 39.4.1. Heterotopy

- Webbed feet. In chick limbs, BMPs induce **interdigital cell death**; in ducks, the BMP antagonist Gremlin is expressed in the webbing of the hindlimb and the cells survive. Bats retain the webbing of the forelimb in a similar way, with Gremlin and interdigital FGF8, and inhibiting FGF signaling restores interdigital death in bat limbs (Weatherbee and colleagues 2006; [7.5.32](/7.5.32#sec-32.6)).
- The turtle shell. Turtles are the only animals whose shoulder girdle lies inside the rib cage. In the turtle embryo a ridge of dermis on the flank, the carapacial ridge, expresses FGF10 and attracts the growing ribs, which grow laterally into the dermis instead of curving ventrally; there they ossify by **endochondral ossification** and their BMPs induce the surrounding dermis to form bone, building the carapace (Burke 1989; Cebra-Thomas and colleagues 2005). The muscles attach in new places, and the scapula ends up inside the ribs. The fossil *Odontochelys* (220 million years ago), with a plastron but broadened ribs and no carapace, fits this developmental sequence.
- Heterotopy of a *Hox* code. In the corn snake, rib-bearing vertebrae extend along almost the whole trunk. Hox10 proteins, which suppress ribs in the lumbar region of mammals, are expressed in the posterior trunk of snakes, but a single nucleotide change in an enhancer of *Myf5*, a target required for rib formation, prevents Hox10 proteins from repressing it; elephants, which have long rib cages, have the same change (Guerreiro and colleagues 2013). The interpretation of a code changed rather than the code.

### 39.4.2. Heterochrony

Heterochrony was the first developmental mechanism of evolution to be studied systematically (Gould 1977). Its effects can be described with a simple model of growth trajectories.

**Formula 39.4.1 (Growth Trajectories)** Let a trait (a size or a shape variable) grow from onset time $\alpha$ to offset time $\beta$ at rate $k$:

$$
z(t)=k\,(t-\alpha)\quad(\alpha\le t\le\beta),\qquad z^*=k\,(\beta-\alpha),
\tag{39.1}
$$
where $z^*$ is the adult value (Alberch, Gould, Oster and Wake 1979). A descendant whose adult value is smaller than the ancestor's, resembling an ancestral juvenile, is paedomorphic; one whose adult value exceeds it is peramorphic. Each can be produced in three ways: a lower or higher rate $k$, an earlier or later offset $\beta$, or a later or earlier onset $\alpha$.

**Definition 39.4.2 (Paedomorphosis and Peramorphosis)** **Paedomorphosis** is the retention of juvenile characters of the ancestor in the adult descendant, by **neoteny** (slower somatic development, smaller $k$), **progenesis** (earlier maturation and an earlier end of growth, smaller $\beta$) or postdisplacement (later onset). **Peramorphosis** is the extension of development beyond the ancestral adult, by acceleration (larger $k$), **hypermorphosis** (later offset) or predisplacement (earlier onset).

If the trait is a shape that changes with size by **allometry**, $y=ax^b$ ([7.5.34](/7.5.34#sec-34.6)), the proportions of an adult depend on where its growth stopped: $y/x=ax^{b-1}$, so a descendant that stops growing at a smaller size keeps the proportions of the ancestor's juvenile without any change in the developmental program itself. This is called ontogenetic scaling.

![Heterochrony in the growth-trajectory model (Formula 39.4.1). A: an ancestral trajectory (onset 2, offset 10, rate 1; black) and six descendants whose adult value differs by 30 %: paedomorphic by a lower rate (neoteny), an earlier offset (progenesis) or a later onset (postdisplacement), and peramorphic by a higher rate (acceleration), a later offset (hypermorphosis) or an earlier onset (predisplacement). B: if a part grows allometrically (exponent 1.4), its proportion relative to the body rises with size; a descendant that stops growing earlier keeps juvenile proportions, one that grows longer exaggerates them](./plots/heterochrony.py)

**Example 39.4.1** The adult value in Formula 39.4.1 is the same for $(k,\alpha,\beta)=(0.7,2,10)$, $(1,2,7.6)$ and $(1,4.4,10)$: $z^*=5.6$ in each case. The adult alone cannot tell which process occurred; the trajectories, measured on growth series of the ancestor and descendant, can. Tropical salamanders of the genus *Bolitoglossa* that climb trees have smaller, more webbed feet with shorter digits than terrestrial species; the feet of the arboreal species resemble the juvenile feet of terrestrial ones, because foot growth stops at an earlier stage (Alberch and Alberch 1981): progenesis of a module.

Examples of heterochrony are found throughout the course:

| Example | Change | Process |
|---|---|---|
| Axolotl (*Ambystoma mexicanum*) | reproduces as an aquatic larva with gills; metamorphoses if given thyroid hormone ([7.5.33](/7.5.33)) | neoteny (paedomorphosis) |
| *C. elegans* **heterochronic genes** (*lin-4*, *let-7*) | mutants repeat or skip larval stages ([7.5.6](/7.5.6)) | changes in the timing of cell fates |
| Human brain | prolonged proliferation of cortical progenitors and late maturation of synapses; the cortical **transcriptome** of adult humans resembles that of juvenile chimpanzees (Somel and colleagues 2009) | hypermorphosis and neoteny of brain development |
| Snakes | more than 300 somites in some species: the **segmentation clock** runs about four times faster relative to the growth of the presomitic **mesoderm** than in other amniotes (Gomez and colleagues 2008; [7.5.23](/7.5.23)) | acceleration of a clock |
| Marsupials | forelimbs and jaws develop early, so the newborn can climb to the teat | predisplacement of limb development |
| Birds | the skull of adult birds resembles that of juvenile dinosaurs (Bhullar and colleagues 2012) | paedomorphosis |
| Lizards *Hemiergis* | species with three, four or five digits; fewer digits with shorter duration of *Shh* expression in the **zone of polarizing activity** (Shapiro and colleagues 2003) | progenesis of a signaling centre |
| Dolphin flipper | extra phalanges (hyperphalangy); prolonged FGF8 in the **apical ectodermal ridge** | hypermorphosis of the **progress zone** |
| Direct-developing frogs (*Eleutherodactylus*), sea urchins (*Heliocidaris erythrogramma*) | the larva is lost, adult structures develop early from a large egg | acceleration, loss of a stage |

For segmentation, the arithmetic of the **clock-and-wavefront model** ([7.5.23](/7.5.23)) shows why a change in relative rate is enough: the number of segments is approximately the duration of somitogenesis divided by the clock period, and the length of a segment is the speed of the wavefront multiplied by the period. A clock that runs faster relative to axis growth produces more and smaller segments in the same total length.

### 39.4.3. Heterometry

- Darwin's finches. The deep, broad beaks of the seed-cracking ground finches of the Galápagos (*Geospiza magnirostris*, *G. fortis*) and the long, pointed beaks of the cactus finches (*G. scandens*) form from the neural crest-derived mesenchyme of the frontonasal process ([7.5.20](/7.5.20)). Abzhanov and colleagues (2004) found that BMP4 is expressed earlier and at higher levels in the beak primordia of species with deeper beaks; overexpressing BMP4 in the chick frontonasal mesenchyme made the chick beak deeper and broader, and the BMP antagonist Noggin made it smaller. In a microarray screen, **calmodulin** was expressed about 15-fold more strongly in the long beaks of cactus finches, and raising calmodulin-dependent signaling in chicks lengthened the beak (Abzhanov and colleagues 2006). Beak shapes are related by scaling transformations of a common shape (Campàs and colleagues 2011). Genome sequencing later identified the loci with the largest effects in the wild: a **haplotype** of the transcription factor *ALX1* associated with beak shape (Lamichhaney and colleagues 2015) and one of the **chromatin** protein gene *HMGA2* associated with beak size, which was selected during the drought of 2004–2005, when small-beaked *G. fortis* competed less with *G. magnirostris* for large seeds (Lamichhaney and colleagues 2016; [7.10](/7.10)).
- Cavefish. Cave populations of the Mexican tetra *Astyanax mexicanus* have lost their eyes and pigment. The embryos form small eyes that degenerate because the lens dies by **apoptosis**; a lens transplanted from a surface fish into a cavefish restores much of the eye, and a cavefish lens in a surface fish causes degeneration, so the lens is the trigger (Yamamoto and Jeffery 2000). The midline expression of Sonic hedgehog is expanded in cavefish embryos, which reduces *Pax6* in the optic primordia and induces lens **apoptosis**, and also enlarges the jaws and increases the number of taste buds (Yamamoto and colleagues 2004): one heterometric change has several effects, some of them advantageous in darkness. Variation in eye size in surface fish is buffered by the chaperone Hsp90 and released under the low conductivity of cave water (Rohner and colleagues 2013), an example of the release of hidden variation (39.6).
- Body size in dogs. Small breeds share a haplotype of *IGF1*, and half of the variation in dog body size is explained by a handful of loci of the growth hormone–IGF axis and *HMGA2* ([7.5.34](/7.5.34#sec-34.5)).

Beak profiles of ground finches (left) and cactus finches (right) arranged by depth and length: earlier and stronger BMP4 expression in the beak mesenchyme correlates with deeper beaks, higher calmodulin expression with longer beaks; the shapes are related by scaling transformations of a common form](./plots/finch-beaks.py)

### 39.4.4. Heterotypy

- Six legs. Most arthropods have legs on many trunk segments; insects have legs only on the thorax. In insects, the Hox protein Ultrabithorax represses the leg gene *Distal-less* in the abdomen. Ubx proteins of crustaceans and onychophorans do not repress *Distal-less*; the insect protein has acquired a C-terminal region rich in alanine, and a shrimp Ubx engineered with this region represses *Distal-less* in flies (Galant and Carroll 2002; Ronshaugen, McGinnis and McGinnis 2002). Coding changes in Ubx and regulatory changes in its expression thus both contributed to the insect body plan. In crustaceans, shifts in the anterior boundaries of *Ubx* and *abd-A* expression correlate with the number of thoracic segments that carry feeding appendages (maxillipeds) instead of walking legs, and knocking down *Ubx* in the amphipod *Parhyale hawaiensis* transforms legs into maxillipeds (Averof and Patel 1997; Liubicich and colleagues 2009).
- Pregnancy. In placental mammals, the endometrium differentiates into decidual cells that support the embryo, and they express prolactin. Hoxa11 protein of eutherian mammals, together with the transcription factor FOXO1, activates the decidual prolactin enhancer, whereas Hoxa11 of opossum, platypus and chicken does not; the protein underwent rapid amino-acid change in the stem lineage of placental mammals (Lynch and colleagues 2008). Many enhancers active in decidual cells derive from transposable elements that spread in the same lineage.

## 39.5. Case Studies of Evolutionary Change

### 39.5.1. Pelvic Reduction in Sticklebacks

Threespine sticklebacks (*Gasterosteus aculeatus*) are marine fish with bony lateral plates, dorsal spines and a pair of pelvic spines that make them hard for predatory fish to swallow. After the last glaciation, marine sticklebacks colonized many newly formed lakes, and freshwater populations repeatedly lost armour plates and, in some lakes, the pelvic skeleton, probably because of fewer predatory fish, predation by insects that grasp spines, and low calcium. The genetics is unusually clear (Shapiro, Kingsley and colleagues 2004; Chan and colleagues 2010):

1. Crosses between a marine and a pelvic-reduced population, followed by genetic mapping in the F2, located a major factor for pelvic reduction near the gene for the **homeodomain** transcription factor *Pitx1*, required for hindlimb development ([7.5.31](/7.5.31)).
2. The Pitx1 protein is identical in both forms, but its expression is absent from the pelvic bud of pelvic-reduced fish, while it is normal in the thymus, nose and sensory **neurons**.
3. A 2.5 kb fragment upstream of the gene from marine fish drives GFP expression in the pelvis of transgenic fish; the corresponding region of pelvic-reduced fish does not, and in many freshwater populations it is deleted.
4. The marine enhancer linked to the *Pitx1* coding sequence restored pelvic spines in transgenic pelvic-reduced fish.

The **deletions** differ between populations, so the enhancer was lost independently many times; the region is fragile, with TG-repeat sequences that favour double-strand breaks. A **deletion** of a tissue-specific enhancer removes one module and leaves the essential functions of *Pitx1* untouched, which is why it can be selected. Pelvic reduction in sticklebacks is often stronger on the right side, as in *Pitx1* knockout mice, probably because the related gene *Pitx2*, expressed on the left ([7.5.17](/7.5.17#sec-17.4)), partly compensates. The loss of armour plates involves cis-regulatory alleles of *Eda* (ectodysplasin), which are present at low frequency in the sea, about two million years old, and are picked up from standing variation in each new lake (Colosimo and colleagues 2005).

### 39.5.2. Long-Germ and Short-Germ Insects

The segmentation cascade of *Drosophila*, in which maternal gradients, **gap genes** and **pair-rule genes** specify all segments simultaneously in a syncytium ([7.5.15](/7.5.15)), is not typical of insects. In long-germ insects such as *Drosophila* the blastoderm **fate map** contains the whole body; in short-germ insects such as the flour beetle *Tribolium castaneum*, only the head and part of the thorax are specified at the blastoderm stage, and the remaining segments are added one after the other from a posterior growth zone after **gastrulation**; in "extreme" short-germ insects such as the grasshopper *Schistocerca* the blastoderm forms only a small posterior disc.

![Long-germ and short-germ development. A: blastoderm fate maps (anterior to the left): in *Drosophila* the embryonic anlage contains head, thorax and abdomen; in *Tribolium* the head, part of the thorax and a posterior growth zone, the rest being extraembryonic; in *Schistocerca* a small posterior disc with head lobes and growth zone. B: segments are specified all at once in long-germ insects and one after another, from a clock in the growth zone, in short-germ insects](./plots/germ-band.py)

In *Tribolium* the **pair-rule genes** *even-skipped*, *runt* and *odd-skipped* oscillate in the growth zone, with one oscillation per two segments, like the **segmentation clock** of vertebrates ([7.5.23](/7.5.23)) (Sarrazin, Peel and Averof 2012; El-Sherif, Averof and Brown 2012). Short-germ development is ancestral; long-germ development evolved several times independently, in flies, wasps and some beetles. A plausible scenario is that the **gap genes**, at first regulators of the timing of the clock, took over the spatial positioning of stripes, so that the pattern could be laid down simultaneously before cellularization, and in higher flies the new gene *bicoid* took over anterior patterning from the older Orthodenticle and Hunchback gradients ([7.5.14](/7.5.14)). The selective advantage may have been speed: a *Drosophila* larva hatches and feeds within a day, important for an animal that lives on rotting fruit. The **parasegment** boundaries, the **segment-polarity genes** and the extended germ-band stage are shared by both modes: the phylotypic stage of insects (39.1.2).

### 39.5.3. Butterfly Eyespots

The eyespots on butterfly wings are an evolutionary novelty of the Nymphalidae. They develop late, in the pupal wing, around a central focus that acts as an organizer: killing the focus abolishes the eyespot, and a focus grafted to a new position induces one around it (Nijhout 1980). The focus expresses genes with older roles in wing and leg patterning, *Distal-less*, the Hox gene *Antennapedia*, *spalt* and *engrailed*, and the Notch and Hedgehog pathways; which of them are expressed differs between species, and the patterns of their expression in the larval disc predict the number and position of eyespots. CRISPR knockouts showed that *spalt* promotes eyespot colour patterns in several species (Zhang and Reed 2016) and that *Distal-less* is required for eyespot formation in *Bicyclus anynana* (Connahs and colleagues 2019), although its effects differ between species. A whole gene regulatory network, originally used to pattern the distal parts of appendages, was co-opted into small fields on the wing. Eyespot size in *B. anynana* is also plastic, controlled by the timing of the **ecdysone** rise in the pupa ([7.5.38](/7.5.38#sec-38.8.1)), and has been changed by artificial selection in a few generations (Brakefield and colleagues 1996).

### 39.5.4. Limbs

The evolution of fins into limbs, the regulatory landscape of *Hoxd* in the **autopod**, the loss of limbs in snakes through the **ZRS** and other limb modifications are described in [7.5.32](/7.5.32#sec-32.7). The loss of forelimbs in snakes is associated with the expansion of thoracic *Hox* expression along the trunk ([7.5.17](/7.5.17#sec-17.3.2)), and in chick and mouse the position of the forelimb correlates with the anterior boundary of *Hoxc6*, which lies at somite 12 in the mouse (seven cervical vertebrae), at somite 19 in the chick (14 cervical vertebrae) and at somite 22–23 in the goose, which has three more (Burke and colleagues 1995).

## 39.6. Developmental Constraints

There are about 35 animal phyla, and many conceivable body plans never evolved. Population biologists use "constraint" for the limits on the optimality of adaptations; developmental biologists also mean that some phenotypes cannot be produced, or are produced much more easily than others.

**Definition 39.6.1 (Developmental Constraint)** A **developmental constraint** is a bias on the production of variant phenotypes, or a limitation on phenotypic variability, caused by the structure, character, composition or dynamics of the developmental system (Maynard Smith and colleagues 1985).

Three kinds are distinguished:

- Physical constraints. Diffusion, mechanics and hydraulics exclude some forms: a vertebrate cannot have wheels, because blood cannot circulate through a rotating joint, and an insect the size of a dog could not supply its tissues with oxygen through tracheae.
- Morphogenetic constraints. When development is perturbed, the outcomes are limited. Over 350 million years of tetrapod evolution, some limb modifications have never been seen, such as a middle digit shorter than both its neighbours or a **zeugopod** proximal to the **stylopod**; digits are lost in a characteristic order, usually first digit 1, then 5. Reaction–diffusion mechanisms, which lay down the digit pattern as a **Turing pattern** in a growing bud ([7.5.32](/7.5.32#sec-32.5)), can produce only certain branching and segmentation patterns (Oster, Shubin, Murray and Alberch 1988), so that the variation available to selection is structured.
- Pleiotropic constraints. A gene that has acquired roles in several modules cannot change freely. Almost all mammals have seven cervical vertebrae, whereas birds have from 11 to 25. Galis (1999) proposed that in mammals the *Hox* genes that specify cervical identity are linked to other processes, so that changes are accompanied by other defects; in humans, cervical ribs (a **homeotic transformation** of the seventh cervical vertebra) are associated with stillbirth, multiple malformations and childhood **cancers** (Galis and colleagues 2006). The exceptions, sloths and manatees with six to ten cervical vertebrae, have low metabolic rates and also show other skeletal anomalies. The evidence is correlational, but the hypothesis explains why a feature so variable in other vertebrates is fixed in mammals.

Constraints also explain why development keeps steps that seem useless: cave animals still form eye primordia that then degenerate, presumably because the early eye field interacts with the development of the forebrain and face. Constraint and bias are two sides of the same fact: a developmental system makes some variants easy, and these are more likely to be the material of evolution.

## 39.7. Selectable Epigenetic Variation and Genetic Assimilation

### 39.7.1. Epialleles and Symbionts

Not all heritable variation is in DNA sequence. **Epialleles** ([7.4.18](/7.4.18#sec-18.6.3)), states of **chromatin** that are inherited through the germ line, are common in plants: the radially symmetric peloric form of toadflax, described by Linnaeus, is due to a methylated, silent allele of *Lcyc*, the homolog of *CYCLOIDEA* (Cubas, Vincent and Coen 1999). In mammals, stable **epialleles** are rare (38.6). Inherited symbionts are a second source of variation ([7.5.38](/7.5.38#sec-38.9)). In the pea aphid *Acyrthosiphon pisum*, a point mutation in a heat-shock gene of the obligate endosymbiont *Buchnera aphidicola* trades heat tolerance for fecundity (Dunbar and colleagues 2007); the facultative symbiont *Rickettsiella* turns red aphids green, and *Hamiltonella defensa*, carrying a phage, protects its host against parasitoid wasps. These traits are inherited through the egg and can be selected, although they are encoded in another genome.

### 39.7.2. Genetic Assimilation

Development can also be changed first by the environment, and only later by genes. **Canalization** ([7.5.1](/7.5.1#sec-1.8.3)) buffers development against perturbations; an environmental stress can push some individuals off the normal path, and these individuals differ genetically in how easily they are pushed.

**Definition 39.7.1 (Genetic Assimilation)** **Genetic assimilation** is the process by which a phenotype that is at first produced only in response to an environmental stimulus becomes, after selection for individuals that show it, produced without the stimulus (Waddington 1953; Schmalhausen 1949). The more general process in which selection modifies the environmental sensitivity of a phenotype, raising or lowering it, is called genetic accommodation (West-Eberhard 2003).

Conrad Waddington exposed *Drosophila* pupae to a heat shock, which caused a few percent of flies to lack the posterior crossvein of the wing, and bred from these flies for many generations. The frequency of the response rose to more than 90 %, and from about generation 14 some flies of the selected line were crossveinless without heat shock; by further selection among them he obtained lines in which the trait was fully inherited (Waddington 1953). In a second experiment, ether treatment of embryos produced a bithorax **phenocopy**, with the halteres transformed towards wings, which was assimilated in the same way (1956). Gibson and Hogness (1996) repeated the ether experiment and showed that the selected flies carried particular alleles of *Ultrabithorax*, the gene whose mutations cause the bithorax phenotype; Suzuki and Nijhout (2006) assimilated a heat-induced green colour of larvae of the moth *Manduca sexta*, and traced it to changes in **juvenile hormone** titres. Waddington's interpretation requires no inheritance of acquired characters: selection acts on genetic variation that was already present but hidden.

**Formula 39.7.1 (Threshold Model of Genetic Assimilation)** Let an individual's **liability** to the phenotype be

$$
Y=G+S+E,
\tag{39.2}
$$
with a genotypic value $G$ (the sum of many small allelic effects, approximately normal with mean $\mu_G$ and variance $\sigma_G^2$), a stimulus effect $S$ ($S>0$ if the stimulus is applied, $0$ otherwise) and developmental noise $E\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_E^2)$. The phenotype appears when $Y>T$, a **threshold trait** with the **liability** of [7.4.24](/7.4.24#def-24.6.1). The frequencies of the phenotype with and without the stimulus are

$$
q_S=1-\Phi\!\left(\frac{T-S-\mu_G}{\sigma_P}\right),\qquad q_0=1-\Phi\!\left(\frac{T-\mu_G}{\sigma_P}\right),\qquad \sigma_P^2=\sigma_G^2+\sigma_E^2.
$$
Selecting, among exposed individuals, those that show the phenotype is **truncation selection** on **liability**. By the **breeder's equation** ([Theorem 24.5.1](/7.4.24#thm-24.5.1), [Formula 24.5.1](/7.4.24#fml-24.5.1)) the mean genotypic value rises each generation by $\Delta\mu_G=h^2\,i(q_S)\,\sigma_P$, where $h^2=\sigma_G^2/\sigma_P^2$ and $i(q)=\varphi(z)/q$ is the **selection intensity** for the selected fraction $q$ with $z=\Phi^{-1}(1-q)$. Once $\mu_G$ approaches $T$, $q_0$ becomes appreciable: the phenotype has been assimilated.

**Example 39.7.1** In the simulation of the figure (100 loci of effect $0.1$, starting **allele frequency** $0.2$, so $\mu_G=4.0$ and $\sigma_G\approx0.57$; $\sigma_E=0.5$, $S=1.5$, $T=7$), about 2 % of exposed flies show the phenotype in generation 0 and none of the unexposed. Selection of the responders raises the response to 54 % in generation 2 and to 85 % in generation 5; unexposed siblings show the phenotype at 4 % in generation 2, 17 % in generation 5, 36 % in generation 10 and 60 % in generation 20, as $\mu_G$ rises from 4.0 to about 6.9. In a line selected against the trait, neither response appears. The first step can be checked with Formula 39.7.1: with $\sigma_P\approx0.76$ and $q_S\approx0.024$, $z\approx1.98$, $i\approx2.36$ and $h^2\approx0.57$, so $\Delta\mu_G\approx0.57\times2.36\times0.76\approx1.0$, which brings the exposed population close to its threshold and raises $q_S$ to about 30 %, as in the simulation (34 %).

B: distributions of the genotypic value in the line selected for the trait at generations 0, 8 and 16: selection moves the genotypes past the threshold for exposed flies and towards the threshold for unexposed ones](./plots/genetic-assimilation.py)

The model makes three points. First, assimilation needs genetic variation in liability, which **canalization** had hidden; molecular capacitors such as the chaperone Hsp90 can release such variation, in *Drosophila*, *Arabidopsis* and cavefish ([7.5.1](/7.5.1#sec-1.8.3), 39.4.3). Second, the rate of assimilation depends on the heritability of liability and on how close the population is to the threshold. Third, the stimulus does not cause the genetic change; it makes the variation visible to selection.

Assimilation in nature is harder to demonstrate. Tiger snakes (*Notechis scutatus*) of the Australian mainland are born with small heads that grow larger if they eat large prey, whereas snakes on islands where only large prey are available are born with large heads and show less plasticity (Aubret and Shine 2009). Spadefoot toad tadpoles (*Spea*) develop a carnivorous morph when they eat shrimp ([7.5.38](/7.5.38#sec-38.8.1)); in a species where carnivores are favoured, the morph develops more readily, and in a related species it is fixed. Heat-shock-induced wing patterns of butterflies resemble the genetically fixed patterns of races from colder or warmer regions. Two arguments make environmentally initiated change potentially important (West-Eberhard 2003): the new phenotype is produced in many individuals at once, not in a single mutant, and it has already been exposed to selection in the environment that induces it. How often evolution has proceeded in this "plasticity first" order is an open question of current research ([7.10](/7.10)).

## Summary

- Embryos and evolution (39.1): von Baer's laws (Definition 39.1.1), not recapitulation; the phylotypic stage (Definition 39.1.2) and the transcriptomic hourglass.
- Preconditions (39.2): modularity of fields and **enhancers** (Definition 39.2.1); co-option (Definition 39.2.2); a small toolkit enlarged by **duplication** (neo- and **subfunctionalization**; Hox clusters by tandem and **whole-genome duplication**; *SRGAP2C*, *NOTCH2NL*).
- Deep homology (Definition 39.3.1): Pax6 in eyes, Tinman in hearts, *Distal-less* in appendages, BMP–Chordin and the vnd/ind/msh columns in an inverted dorso-ventral axis.
- Four kinds of change (Definition 39.4.1):

| Kind | Changes | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Heterotopy | place | Gremlin in duck and bat webbing; FGF10 in the turtle carapacial ridge; *Myf5* **enhancer** in snakes |
| Heterochrony | time, rate | axolotl; human brain; snake **segmentation clock**; *Hemiergis* digits; *Bolitoglossa* feet (Formula 39.4.1, Definition 39.4.2) |
| Heterometry | amount | BMP4 and **calmodulin** in finch beaks (with *ALX1*, *HMGA2*); midline Shh in cavefish; *IGF1* in dogs |
| Heterotypy | protein | insect Ubx represses *Distal-less*; eutherian Hoxa11 with FOXO1 and prolactin |

- Case studies (39.5): repeated deletion of the *Pitx1* pelvic enhancer in sticklebacks; long-germ segmentation derived from a short-germ clock; eyespots by co-option of appendage genes.
- Constraints (Definition 39.6.1): physical, morphogenetic (Turing patterns of digits), pleiotropic (seven cervical vertebrae); constraint is also bias.
- Genetic assimilation (Definition 39.7.1): Waddington's crossveinless and bithorax experiments; in a threshold model (Formula 39.7.1), **truncation selection** on exposed responders raises mean liability by $h^2 i\sigma_P$ per generation until the phenotype appears without the stimulus.

## Questions

1. Explain the difference between Haeckel's recapitulation and von Baer's laws using the **pharyngeal arches** of a human embryo.

   **Answer** Recapitulation claims that the human embryo passes through the adult stages of its ancestors, so its **pharyngeal arches** and clefts would be the gills of an adult fish. Von Baer's laws say that the human embryo resembles the embryo of a fish, not the adult: pharyngeal arches are a general embryonic character of vertebrates, which in fish develop into gill arches and in mammals into the jaw, middle ear bones, hyoid and larynx and, from the pouches, the thymus and parathyroids. Development diverges from a shared embryonic stage rather than adding stages to an ancestral adult.

2. Why are mutations in enhancers more likely than mutations in coding sequences to be the basis of morphological evolution? Give an example in which the same gene shows both kinds of change.

   **Answer** A coding mutation changes the protein in every tissue where it is made, so for a broadly used developmental regulator it usually has many harmful effects (**pleiotropy**). A mutation in a tissue-specific enhancer changes expression in one module only and leaves the other functions intact (stickleback *Pitx1*, snake **ZRS** of *Shh*). *Ubx* shows both: changes in its expression domains along the crustacean trunk correlate with the transformation of legs into maxillipeds (regulatory), while an insect-specific C-terminal alanine-rich region of the protein lets it repress *Distal-less* in the abdomen (coding, heterotypy).

3. A colleague concludes from the ectopic eyes induced in *Drosophila* by mouse *Pax6* that fly and mouse eyes are homologous. Do you agree?

   **Answer** No. The experiment shows that the regulatory function of Pax6 is conserved: the mouse protein can activate the fly's eye program, which makes a fly compound eye, not a mouse eye. This is deep homology (Definition 39.3.1): the common ancestor had Pax6 and opsin-based photoreceptor cells, perhaps a simple eyespot, and the compound and camera eyes evolved separately using this inherited circuit. Homology of the eyes as structures would require continuity of the structure itself, which the comparative evidence does not support.

4. Using Formula 39.4.1, show that the same adult trait value can be produced by neoteny and by progenesis, and propose measurements to distinguish them in a salamander.

   **Answer** For the ancestor $(k,\alpha,\beta)=(1,2,10)$, $z^*=8$. Neoteny with $k=0.75$ gives $z^*=0.75\times8=6$; progenesis with $\beta=8$ gives $z^*=1\times6=6$. To distinguish them one needs growth series: measure the trait (and body size, and the age at sexual maturity) in individuals of known age in both species. In neoteny the trait grows more slowly throughout the same period and sexual maturity is not advanced; in progenesis it grows at the ancestral rate but growth stops, and maturity occurs, earlier, so the descendant matures at a smaller size and younger age. Postdisplacement would show a delayed start of growth.

5. A snake has about 300 vertebrae and a mouse about 65. If somitogenesis lasted equally long in both and the mouse clock period were 2 h, what clock period would the snake need? What did Gomez and colleagues actually find?

   **Answer** With the number of **somites** $N\approx T_{\mathrm{seg}}/\tau$ and equal durations, $\tau_{\mathrm{snake}}\approx\tau_{\mathrm{mouse}}\times65/300\approx2\,\mathrm{h}\times0.22\approx26\,\mathrm{min}$. Gomez and colleagues (2008) found that the relevant quantity is the clock period relative to the rate of growth of the **presomitic mesoderm**: in corn snakes the clock is about four times faster relative to development than in chick, mouse or zebrafish, so that many more, smaller **somites** form before the tail stops growing; both the clock speed and the duration of axis elongation (heterochronies) contribute.

6. Outline the experimental logic that established *Pitx1* as the cause of pelvic reduction in sticklebacks, and explain why the same deletion is found in many lakes.

   **Answer** Mapping in crosses between marine and freshwater fish placed the major locus at *Pitx1*; sequence comparison showed no coding change; in situ **hybridization** showed loss of expression only in the pelvic bud; reporter **transgenes** showed that a marine 2.5 kb upstream fragment drives pelvic expression and the freshwater one does not; and a **transgene** with the marine enhancer and the *Pitx1* coding sequence restored pelvic spines, showing sufficiency. The deletions are of different sizes in different lakes, so they arose independently; the region contains TG repeats that promote double-strand breaks (a **mutational hotspot**), and because the deletion removes only the pelvic module, it is viable and can be selected wherever reduced pelvic spines are favoured.

7. Why do almost all mammals have seven cervical vertebrae, whereas birds vary? What kind of evidence supports the explanation, and what are its limits?

   **Answer** Galis proposed a pleiotropic constraint: in mammals, changes in the *Hox* code that would alter cervical number also disturb other developmental processes and are associated with cell proliferation, so they are strongly selected against. Evidence: in human fetuses and children, cervical ribs (a partial transformation of C7 towards a thoracic identity) are much more frequent among stillbirths and children with multiple malformations or embryonal **cancers** than in the general population; the exceptions (sloths, manatees) have low metabolic rates and other skeletal abnormalities. The evidence is correlational, it does not identify the linked processes, and it does not explain why birds are not constrained in the same way; the hypothesis is plausible but not proven.

8. In the threshold model of genetic assimilation (Formula 39.7.1), take $\mu_G=4$, $\sigma_P=0.76$, $h^2=0.57$, $S=1.5$, $T=7$. Compute the fraction of exposed and unexposed individuals that show the phenotype, and the expected change in $\mu_G$ after one generation of selecting all exposed responders.

   **Answer** Exposed: $z=(7-1.5-4)/0.76\approx1.97$, $q_S=1-\Phi(1.97)\approx0.024$. Unexposed: $z=3/0.76\approx3.95$, $q_0\approx4\times10^{-5}$. **Selection intensity** for $q=0.024$: $i=\varphi(1.97)/0.024\approx0.057/0.024\approx2.37$. Response: $\Delta\mu_G=h^2 i\sigma_P\approx0.57\times2.37\times0.76\approx1.03$. Then $q_S=1-\Phi((5.5-5.03)/0.76)=1-\Phi(0.62)\approx0.27$ and $q_0=1-\Phi((7-5.03)/0.76)=1-\Phi(2.6)\approx0.005$: the induced phenotype spreads quickly, and the uninduced phenotype begins to appear after a few more generations.

9. Waddington's critics argued that genetic assimilation is just ordinary selection and needs no special name. Discuss.

   **Answer** Mechanistically they are right: the model of Formula 39.7.1 is truncation selection on a **threshold trait**, and no inheritance of acquired characters is involved. What is distinctive is the order of events and the source of variation: the environment first produces a phenotype in many individuals at once, making hidden genetic variation in liability visible (often released when **canalization** is weakened, as by reduced Hsp90), and selection then fixes that phenotype; the direction of evolution is set by the developmental response. Whether this "plasticity-first" route is common in nature is an empirical question, investigated in cases such as spadefoot toads, cavefish and tiger snakes.

10. How could you test whether a difference in a developmental trait between two species is caused by heterochrony or by heterotopy?

    **Answer** Compare the spatial and temporal patterns of the relevant gene expression and of the process (cell proliferation, death, differentiation) in staged embryos of both species, using equivalent stages defined independently of the trait (for example by somite number or other organs). If the same domain is present but starts, ends or proceeds at a different time or rate, the change is heterochronic (for example the duration of *Shh* in the ZPA of *Hemiergis*); if expression appears in a new place (Gremlin in duck interdigital mesenchyme), it is heterotopic. Then test causality: manipulate timing (prolonging or shortening the signal with beads or inhibitors) or location (ectopic expression) in one species to reproduce the other's phenotype, as done with BMP4 and calmodulin in chick beaks and FGF inhibition in bat wings.
