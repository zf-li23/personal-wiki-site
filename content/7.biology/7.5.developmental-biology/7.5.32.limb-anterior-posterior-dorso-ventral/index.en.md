The proximo-distal sequence of the limb (Chapter [7.5.31](/7.5.31)) is laid down at the tip by the **apical ectodermal ridge**. Two further signaling systems organize the other axes. A small group of posterior mesenchymal cells, the **zone of polarizing activity**, makes the anterior–posterior (thumb-to-little-finger) axis through Sonic hedgehog, the vertebrate homologue of the fly segment-polarity signal Hedgehog ([7.5.15](/7.5.15)), and the dorsal **ectoderm** makes the dorso-ventral axis through Wnt7a. The three centers maintain one another, so that the limb grows and is patterned in all three dimensions at once, and the whole system switches itself off when the limb has reached its size. Within this frame the digits themselves form as a periodic, self-organized pattern of the kind analyzed in [7.5.12](/7.5.12): their number is set by a Turing mechanism whose wavelength is tuned by **Hox genes**, their identities by the history of Shh exposure. The chapter ends with the separation of the digits by cell death, the joints, the evolution of the limb, which has been driven to a large extent by changes in its **enhancers**, and the common human limb malformations.

## 32.1. The Zone of Polarizing Activity and Sonic Hedgehog

### 32.1.1. Grafting Experiments

In 1968 Saunders and Gasseling grafted a small piece of posterior mesenchyme from one chick wing bud under the anterior AER of another. The wing developed with a mirror-image **duplication** of its digits: instead of the normal 2-3-4 (anterior to posterior), the sequence 4-3-2-2-3-4. The graft did not form the extra digits itself (marked grafts contribute little to them); it caused the host's anterior tissue to make posterior structures. This is the defining experiment for a polarizing region, the **zone of polarizing activity** of Definition 31.1.2.

The response is graded, as a **morphogen** model predicts ([7.5.11](/7.5.11)). Grafts of fewer ZPA cells, or grafts left in place for a shorter time, induce only an extra digit 2 (2-2-3-4); more cells or longer exposure induce 3-2-2-3-4 and then the full **duplication** (Tickle 1981). Polarizing activity is present along the posterior margin already before the bud forms, is strongest at the stages of outgrowth and then declines; tissue from other species (mouse, human, reptile) is active in the chick, and so are grafts from other signaling centers that express the same molecule (the **floor plate** of the **neural tube**, **Hensen's node**).

**Example 32.1.1 (A Morphogen or a Relay?)** If a diffusible signal from the ZPA specified digits by its local concentration, a barrier inserted between the graft and the anterior tissue should block the response, and the most posterior extra digit should form nearest the graft. Both are observed: an impermeable barrier prevents the duplication of digits beyond it, and the extra digits are ordered with the highest identity next to the graft. A relay mechanism (each cell inducing the next) would require a continuous chain of cells and would not be graded by the number of grafted cells; the dose dependence argues for a direct, graded signal. The identification of Shh (below) and the analysis of its time course modified this conclusion: identity depends on how long, as well as how much, Shh the cells have received.

### 32.1.2. Sonic Hedgehog

Riddle, Johnson, Laufer and Tabin (1993) found *Sonic hedgehog* (*Shh*) expressed precisely in the ZPA. Chick fibroblasts transfected with *Shh* and placed under the anterior AER, or beads soaked in Shh protein, mimic a ZPA graft, so Shh is sufficient for polarizing activity; in *Shh* knockout mice the limbs are truncated distally, the **zeugopod** has a single bone and the hindlimb a single digit, so Shh is necessary for posterior pattern and for the outgrowth of the **autopod**. Shh is received through the **Hedgehog signaling pathway** ([7.3.19](/7.3.19)), which requires **primary cilia**: chick and mouse mutants with defective cilia (*talpid3*) are polydactylous with digits of no clear identity, because they make neither the activator nor the **repressor** forms of the Gli transcription factors properly.

### 32.1.3. The ZRS, a Long-Range Enhancer

**Definition 32.1.1 (ZRS)** The *ZRS* (ZPA regulatory sequence, also called MFCS1) is an **enhancer** of about $800\,\mathrm{bp}$ that drives *Shh* expression in the posterior limb mesenchyme; it lies about $1\,\mathrm{Mb}$ upstream of *Shh*, within an intron of the unrelated gene *LMBR1*, and contacts the *Shh* **promoter** by **chromatin** looping within one **topologically associating domain**.

The ZRS explains a set of mutants that long seemed unrelated to Shh. Point mutations in it cause preaxial polydactyly in humans (extra digits on the thumb side, and triphalangeal thumbs), in the mouse *Hemimelic extra-toes* (*Hx*), in the polydactylous cats of Ernest Hemingway's house in Key West and in chickens; reporter genes driven by a mutant ZRS are expressed in the anterior as well as the posterior mesenchyme (Figure below, D). The enhancer therefore contains both activating and repressing elements. Its posterior activity depends on HAND2 and Hox proteins (Hoxd13), and the balance of ETS factors decides its range: the activators ETS1 and GABPα act posteriorly, the **repressors** ETV4 and ETV5 throughout, and mutations that create new activator sites or remove repressor sites extend expression anteriorly (Lettice et al. 2012). **Deletion** of the ZRS in the mouse abolishes limb *Shh* and phenocopies the *Shh* knockout in the limbs, while *Shh* expression elsewhere (**neural tube**, notochord) is untouched: a striking example of the modularity of **enhancers** ([7.2](/7.2)), which allows the limb expression of a pleiotropic gene to change without affecting its other functions.

## 32.2. Digit Identity

**Definition 32.2.1 (Digit Identity)** *Digit identity* is the set of characters that distinguish the digits of a limb from one another: the number and length of the phalanges, the shape of the metacarpal and the associated muscles and tendons; in the mouse, digit 1 (the thumb or hallux) has two phalanges and digits 2–5 three, with different lengths.

### 32.2.1. Time as Well as Concentration

Fate mapping of the Shh-expressing cells answered how Shh specifies identities. Harfe and colleagues (2004) marked every cell that had ever expressed *Shh* with a Cre reporter. The ZPA cells, unlike the AER cells, do not die: their descendants form digits 4 and 5 and part of digit 3 of the mouse hindlimb. Digits 4 and 5 are therefore specified by Shh produced by their own cells (**autocrine**), and the difference between them is the time of exposure: the cells of digit 5 express Shh longest. Digit 3 contains cells that expressed Shh briefly and also depends on Shh diffusing from the ZPA (it is lost when Shh is modified so that it cannot leave the producing cells), digit 2 depends entirely on diffused Shh, and digit 1 forms without Shh: in the chick mutant *oligozeugodactyly*, which lacks limb *Shh* because of a **deletion** in its ZRS, and in *Shh* knockout mice, the only digit is digit 1.

**Formula 32.2.1 (Temporal-Expansion Model)** Let cells at AP position $x$ ($x=0$ at the posterior margin) be carried anteriorly by growth, $x(t)=x_0\mathrm{e}^{gt}$, from a source occupying $x<x_s$ that makes Shh until a time $T$, and let **paracrine** Shh outside the source decay as $\mathrm{e}^{-(x-x_s)/\lambda}$. The exposure of a cell is

$$
E(x_0)=\int_0^T\Big[\boldsymbol{1}_{\{x(t)<x_s\}}+\boldsymbol{1}_{\{x(t)\ge x_s\}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-(x(t)-x_s)/\lambda}\Big]\mathrm{d}t ,
\tag{32.1}
$$
the time spent producing Shh plus the time integral of the **paracrine** level, and digits $2,\dots,5$ form where $E$ exceeds successive thresholds. Cells that start nearest the margin stay longest in the source, end most posterior and receive the highest exposure; the exposure decreases monotonically from posterior to anterior.

A: trajectories of cells over the paracrine Shh field, coloured by the digit they form: digits 5 and 4 come from cells that were in the source (autocrine), digit 3 partly, digit 2 from paracrine signal only, digit 1 without Shh. Conditional removal of *Shh* at different times showed that the specification of identities happens early and quickly, within the first hours of *Shh* expression, and that Shh then acts mainly as a **mitogen** that expands the posterior mesenchyme, so that enough cells are available to make the digits (Zhu et al. 2008; Towers et al. 2008): when *Shh* is removed early, posterior digits are lost, and when cell division is blocked, digits are lost although the posterior identities are specified. Specification and growth are coupled, and the number of digits is limited by the number of cells as well as by the signal.

### 32.2.2. Gli3: Shh Acts by Relieving Repression

In the absence of Hedgehog signaling, the transcription factor Gli3 is cleaved to a repressor form (Gli3R); Shh prevents this cleavage. Shh from the ZPA therefore creates an opposite gradient of Gli3R, high anteriorly, low posteriorly. Before *Shh* is expressed, Gli3 and the posterior bHLH factor Hand2 repress each other and divide the early bud into an anterior and a posterior part, and Hand2 activates *Shh* through the ZRS (te Welscher et al. 2002). The decisive genetic result is the double mutant: *Gli3* mutant mice (*extra-toes*) are polydactylous with digits of indistinct identity, and *Shh*; *Gli3* double mutants look like *Gli3* mutants, not like *Shh* mutants. Shh is not needed to make digits; its main function is to prevent Gli3R from repressing posterior identities and the proliferation of the posterior mesenchyme. Gli3R limits proliferation by repressing cell-cycle genes (*Cdk6*) and the BMP antagonist *Grem1*. In humans, **loss-of-function mutations** of *GLI3* cause Greig cephalopolysyndactyly (preaxial polydactyly and syndactyly), and truncating mutations that produce a constitutive repressor cause Pallister–Hall syndrome (central and postaxial polydactyly with a hypothalamic hamartoma).

### 32.2.3. The Interdigits Decide

The identity of a digit is not fixed when its condensation appears. Dahn and Fallon (2000) removed or manipulated the interdigital mesenchyme of the chick leg: the identity of each digit is determined by the interdigit posterior to it, and a bead of the BMP antagonist Noggin in that interdigit transforms the digit to a more anterior identity, with fewer phalanges. The interdigit carries its **positional value** (set earlier by Shh) as a level of BMP signaling to the phalanx-forming region at the tip of the growing digit (Suzuki et al. 2008), where the phalanges are added one by one. Shh thus acts at a distance in time: it sets **positional values** in the mesenchyme, which the interdigits later convert into the morphology of the digits.

## 32.3. The Shh–Gremlin1–FGF Loop and Its Termination

The ZPA and the AER depend on each other. Fgf4 and Fgf8 from the AER maintain *Shh* in the ZPA, and removing the posterior AER makes *Shh* disappear; Shh in turn maintains the AER, through the BMP antagonist Gremlin1. Shh induces *Grem1* in the mesenchyme, Gremlin1 binds BMPs, and BMP signaling, which would otherwise make the AER regress, is kept low. This is the explanation of a classical observation: the ZPA maintains the AER over the posterior part of the bud, and the AER is wider and the bud asymmetric because of it.

**Definition 32.3.1 (Shh–Gremlin1–FGF Loop)** The *Shh–Gremlin1–FGF loop* is the epithelial–mesenchymal feedback system of the limb bud in which AER-FGFs maintain *Shh* in the ZPA, Shh induces Gremlin1 in the mesenchyme, and Gremlin1 antagonizes the BMPs that would repress the AER-FGFs.

The loop raises an obvious problem: positive feedback, once on, should stay on, yet both centers disappear on schedule when the **autopod** has formed. Two features of the system terminate it (Scherz et al. 2004; Verheyden and Sun 2008; Bénazet et al. 2009). The descendants of Shh-expressing cells cannot express *Grem1*. As the limb grows, these cells accumulate between the source of Shh and the cells that can express *Grem1*, the Shh signal reaching the *Grem1* domain weakens, and Gremlin falls. In addition, high levels of FGF repress *Grem1*, a negative feedback within the loop. BMP4 starts the process: an early pulse of BMP induces *Grem1* rapidly, which allows the slower Shh-dependent loop to establish itself. Because the separation grows with the size of the limb, the loop measures growth, and its termination is a size-control mechanism of the kind discussed in [7.5.34](/7.5.34).

The loop also answers a question about the experiments of [7.5.31](/7.5.31#sec-31.4): why does a limb bud with an extra AER, as in *eudiplopodia*, make a second, mirror-image set of digits? An extra ridge is a second source of FGF; it can maintain *Shh* expression in the mesenchyme beside it and create a second polarizing focus with its own Gremlin domain, so that two sets of digits form, each graded away from its own source of Shh.

## 32.4. The Dorso-Ventral Axis and the Integration of the Axes

The DV axis of the limb is set in two steps. The early **mesoderm** of the **limb field** is specified along the DV axis before the bud forms (a bud rotated with its **ectoderm** develops according to its original orientation), but from the bud stage the ectoderm controls the DV pattern of the distal limb: if the ectoderm of a chick leg bud is removed, rotated through $180^\circ$ about the PD axis and replaced, the distal structures (the scales, the footpads and the arrangement of tendons and muscles of the foot) follow the orientation of the ectoderm, the proximal ones that of the **mesoderm** (MacCabe et al. 1974).

The molecules are known. The dorsal ectoderm secretes Wnt7a, which induces the LIM-homeodomain transcription factor Lmx1b in the dorsal mesenchyme; Lmx1b specifies dorsal fates. The ventral ectoderm expresses Engrailed-1, which represses *Wnt7a* there. The mutants behave as a three-step hierarchy predicts.

| Genotype | Expression | Phenotype |
|---|---|---|
| *Wnt7a* null (mouse) | no Lmx1b | double-ventral distal limb: footpads on the dorsum; loss of posterior digits |
| *Lmx1b* null | — | double-ventral limbs (Wnt7a present, but not read) |
| *En1* null | *Wnt7a* and *Lmx1b* also ventrally | double-dorsal distal limb; ventrally widened AER |
| *Lmx1b* misexpressed ventrally (chick) | — | double-dorsal limbs |
| Human *LMX1B* heterozygous | — | nail–patella syndrome: small or absent nails and patellae (dorsal structures) |

The DV boundary of the ectoderm also places the AER ([7.5.31](/7.5.31#sec-31.4)), and Wnt7a is needed to maintain *Shh* in the ZPA, which is why *Wnt7a* mutants lose posterior digits. The three axes are therefore linked: the AER (PD) maintains the ZPA (AP), the ZPA maintains the AER through Gremlin1, and the dorsal ectoderm (DV) maintains the ZPA through Wnt7a; removing any one of the three signals disturbs all three axes.

## 32.5. Digits as a Self-Organized Pattern

### 32.5.1. Evidence for a Turing Mechanism

Several observations show that the digits are not individually specified by **positional information** but form as a periodic pattern. *Gli3* mutants, with or without Shh, form many digits at regular spacing; dissociated limb mesenchyme reaggregated and grafted under an AER forms digits; and the number of digits varies with the width of the autopod, as in polydactylous breeds of dogs and cats and in experiments that widen the bud. Such behavior is expected of a **Turing pattern** ([2.5.7](/2.5.7), [7.5.12](/7.5.12)): the spacing is intrinsic, the number of elements is the width divided by the wavelength, and their identities must come from elsewhere.

Two experiments identified the mechanism. Sheth et al. (2012) reduced the dosage of the distal *Hox* genes in a *Gli3* mutant background: with each allele of *Hoxa13* and *Hoxd11–13* removed, the digits became thinner and more numerous (up to 12–14 per paw), as if the wavelength of a Turing system were shortened, and a reaction–diffusion simulation with the **Hox genes** modulating one parameter reproduced the series. Raspopovic et al. (2014) found that the precartilage marker Sox9 is expressed in stripes exactly out of phase with the BMP target pSmad and with Wnt targets (*Axin2*, *Lef1*), and built a three-component model in which BMP promotes Sox9, Wnt represses it, and Sox9 represses both, with BMP and Wnt diffusing; with the distal FGF and Hox levels modulating the parameters, the model reproduced the radial fan of digits and predicted the effects of BMP and Wnt pathway inhibitors on the spacing and fusion of digits in cultured limbs, which were then confirmed. Earlier, Newman and Frisch (1979) had proposed a reaction–diffusion model of chondrogenic condensation with TGF-β as activator and an inhibitor of condensation, and Zhu et al. (2010) divided the limb mesenchyme into an apical zone kept undifferentiated by AER-FGFs, an active zone in which the reaction–diffusion pattern forms, and a frozen zone in which the pattern is fixed as cartilage; as the bud grows, the active zone produces first one condensation (**stylopod**), then two (**zeugopod**), then several (autopod). A Turing system can thus generate the proximo-distal sequence of numbers as well, an alternative to the models of [7.5.31](/7.5.31#sec-31.5).

A-C: frozen activator patterns (cartilage where high) for decreasing distal Hox dose h, which enters as the kinetic rate g = 0.9/h² and so shortens the wavelength: four, five and then about eight narrower condensations, which at the lowest dose also break up into segments. **Example 32.5.1 (Digit Number and Wavelength)** For a Turing system in a strip of width $b$ with no-flux sides, the condensations are the maxima of $\cos(n\pi y/b)$, so $n$ half-wavelengths fit, $n\approx 2b/\Lambda$ for a wavelength $\Lambda$. A mouse autopod is about $b\approx1.2\,\mathrm{mm}$ wide when the digits form; five digits (with four interdigits, roughly nine half-wavelengths of the Sox9 pattern counted as alternating digit and interdigit) correspond to $\Lambda\approx2b/9\approx270\,\mathrm{\mu m}$, comparable with the measured period of the Sox9 stripes. Reducing $\Lambda$ by a factor of $0.6$, the shortening needed for the 8–9 digits of moderately Hox-deficient *Gli3* mutants, requires, if $\Lambda\propto\sqrt{D/k}$ in the kinetics, a 2.8-fold increase of the reaction rates or a corresponding decrease in diffusivity.

### 32.5.2. The Chamber Model of Condensation Number

Percus and Childress give a simple model of how growth changes the number of condensations, in the spirit of the earlier fibronectin-based model of Newman and Frisch. Treat the paddle-shaped chick bud as a rectangle $0<x<a$ (proximo-distal) by $0<y<b$ (antero-posterior), with a single **morphogen** $A$, at the time taken to be **fibronectin**, which promotes the adhesion and the clustering of the cells that form a condensation. $A$ does not cross the anterior and posterior margins, is produced at the AER and absorbed homeostatically (at a fixed flux) at the base of the bud:

$$
\frac{\partial A}{\partial y}\Big|_{y=0,b}=0,\qquad \frac{\partial A}{\partial x}\Big|_{x=0}=M_0,\qquad \frac{\partial A}{\partial x}\Big|_{x=a}=M_1 .
$$
Expanding the potential of the kinetics about its extremum ([7.5.12](/7.5.12#sec-12.4)) gives the linear dynamics $A_t=r(A-A_0)+D\Delta A$, in which $r>0$ represents the self-enhancement that makes the uniform state unstable.

**Proposition 32.5.1 (Condensations in a Growing Chamber)** The steady state of $A_t=r(A-A_0)+D\Delta A$ with these boundary conditions has the separated solution

$$
A(x,y)=A_0+\frac{aM_0}{\lambda}\cos\frac{n\pi y}{b}\,\mathrm{e}^{\lambda x/a},\qquad \lambda=\ln\frac{M_1}{M_0},\qquad\Big(\frac{n\pi}b\Big)^2=\frac rD+\frac{\lambda^2}{a^2} .
\tag{32.2}
$$
The number $n$ of transverse half-cycles, the condensations across the bud, increases as the length $a$ of the chamber decreases or as the diffusivity $D$ decreases. If instead the chamber is closed ($\partial A/\partial x=0$ at $x=0$ and $x=a$), the solution is $A=A_0+C\cos(n\pi y/b)\cos(\pi x/a)$ with $(n\pi/b)^2=r/D-\pi^2/a^2$, and $n$ increases with $a$ toward the maximum $b\sqrt{r/D}/\pi$.

**Proof** By **separation of variables**, $A-A_0=Y(y)X(x)$ with $Y=\cos(n\pi y/b)$ satisfying the no-flux conditions in $y$. At steady state $0=rXY+D(X''Y+XY'')$, so $X''=\big((n\pi/b)^2-r/D\big)X\equiv\kappa^2X$. With $X=c\,\mathrm{e}^{\kappa x}$ the boundary conditions require $c\kappa=M_0$ and $c\kappa\mathrm{e}^{\kappa a}=M_1$ (for the cosine-modulated part of the flux), hence $\kappa a=\ln(M_1/M_0)=\lambda$, $c=aM_0/\lambda$, and $(n\pi/b)^2=r/D+\lambda^2/a^2$. For the closed chamber $X=\cos(\pi x/a)$ gives $X''=-(\pi/a)^2X$. $\square$

In the model the relevant chamber is not the whole bud but the zone in which condensation can occur, and experimentally this chamber shortens with time; $n$ then increases from one (**stylopod**) to two (zeugopod) to several (autopod). A closed chamber, for which there is no evidence, would instead produce a maximum number of digits as it grows, provided the precondensations were "sealed in". The model also explains a role for an inhibitor. **Hyaluronan** is abundant in the distal mesenchyme and is cleared from the condensations, which it inhibits. Suppose that transfer of $A$ from one cell to the next uses a carrier or channel $C$, $A_-+C\rightleftharpoons B\to C+A_+$, and that the inhibitor $I$ blocks the carrier, $I+C\rightleftharpoons B_I$.

**Formula 32.5.1 (Inhibitor Reduces the Effective Diffusivity)** With rate constants $k$, $k'$ (binding and release of $A$), $l$ (transfer) and $k_I$, $k_I'$ (inhibitor), fast binding equilibria and conservation of the carrier, $C_0=C+B+B_I$, the rate of transfer is

$$
\dot A_+=\gamma A_-,\qquad \gamma=\frac{C_0\,l}{K(1+K_II)+A_-},\qquad K=\frac{k'+l}k,\ K_I=\frac{k_I}{k_I'},
\tag{32.3}
$$
a Michaelis–Menten form ([7.1](/7.1)). The effective diffusivity $D=\gamma(\Delta x)^2$ falls as the inhibitor concentration $I$ rises, and by (32.2), whatever the details of the kinetics, $n=\frac b\pi(r/D+\lambda^2/a^2)^{1/2}$ increases.

**Proof** At quasi-steady state $\dot B=kA_-C-(k'+l)B=0$ and $\dot B_I=k_IIC-k_I'B_I=0$, so $B=A_-C/K$ and $B_I=K_IIC$. Then $C_0=C(1+A_-/K+K_II)$, and $\dot A_+=lB=lA_-C/K=C_0lA_-/(K(1+K_II)+A_-)$. $\square$

A: the number of transverse half-cycles n against the chamber length a (with r b²/D = π² and λ = 4 for flux boundaries, and r b²/D = 25π² for a closed chamber), with the whole numbers that fit (dotted steps): as the chamber shortens, flux boundaries give one, then two, then several condensations, whereas a closed chamber loses condensations. B: an inhibitor that blocks the carrier of transfer lowers the effective diffusivity (Formula 32.5.1) and raises n at fixed a](./plots/pc-chamber.py)

The specific identification of **fibronectin** and **hyaluronan** as **morphogen** and inhibitor is no longer held: fibronectin is a marker and an effector of condensation rather than a diffusing signal, and the current candidates for the activator and inhibitor are the BMP, TGF-β and Wnt pathways and galectins (Section 32.5.1). The mathematics, that a growing or shortening active zone changes the number of modes that fit and that anything that reduces diffusivity or speeds the kinetics increases the number of condensations, carries over unchanged.

## 32.6. Interdigital Cell Death and Joints

In birds and mammals the digits are first connected by a web of mesenchyme that is then removed by *interdigital cell death*, a form of **apoptosis** ([7.3.25](/7.3.25)). The death is controlled by the mesenchyme: interdigital mesenchyme of a chick, grafted under duck ectoderm, still dies, whereas duck interdigital mesenchyme under chick ectoderm survives. BMP2, BMP4 and BMP7 are expressed in the interdigits and trigger the death; a dominant-negative BMP receptor expressed in the chick leg, or a Noggin bead, prevents it and gives a webbed foot (Zou and Niswander 1996). Ducks keep their webs because the interdigital mesenchyme also expresses the BMP antagonist Gremlin; a Gremlin bead in a chick foot produces webbing, and BMP beads in a duck foot remove it (Merino et al. 1999). Retinoic acid (acting through RARγ) and, in amniotes, **reactive oxygen species** also promote the death. Bats retain the webs of their forelimbs (the patagium) by interdigital expression of Gremlin and Fgf8 (Weatherbee et al. 2006). In frogs, whose toes separate without much death, differential growth of the digits and interdigits does the job.

Joints form where a condensation is interrupted by an interzone of flattened cells expressing *Gdf5*, *Wnt9a* and other Wnts that act through **β-catenin** to suppress cartilage; the interzone cells then form the articular cartilage and the joint capsule. Noggin is needed (loss of *Noggin* fuses the joints), and so is movement: in paralyzed chick embryos, or in mice without limb muscles, the joints fuse. Joint position along the digit, the phalangeal formula, is set by the BMP-dependent phalanx-forming region (Section 32.2.3).

## 32.7. Evolution of the Limb

### 32.7.1. From Fins to Limbs

Limbs evolved from the paired pectoral and pelvic fins of lobe-finned (sarcopterygian) fish in the Late Devonian. In *Eusthenopteron* the fin skeleton already has a single proximal element (a humerus), two middle ones (radius and ulna) and a branching series of distal elements; *Tiktaalik roseae* (about 375 million years old) had mobile wrist-like joints and could prop itself on its fins; the earliest tetrapods with digits, *Acanthostega* and *Ichthyostega*, had eight and seven digits, so the number five was fixed later. The stylopod and zeugopod are homologous to proximal fin elements; the origin of the autopod has been traced to the late phase of *Hoxd* and *Hoxa13* regulation. In the mouse the late, autopod-specific expression of *Hoxd13* is driven by enhancers in the centromeric regulatory landscape, and comparable enhancers in fish drive expression in the distal fin, where **lineage tracing** of *hoxa13*-expressing cells in zebrafish shows that they form the dermal fin rays (Nakamura et al. 2016): digits and fin rays share a distal Hox13 territory, but the endochondral digits are a tetrapod novelty. In fish, the **apical ectodermal ridge** of the fin bud soon converts into an apical fold that forms the fin rays; extending the time before this conversion lengthens the endochondral skeleton, which suggests a simple way in which distal endoskeleton could have increased.

### 32.7.2. Losing Limbs: Snakes

The elongated, limbless body of snakes evolved from four-limbed lizards. The forelimbs were lost first; pythons and boas retain small hindlimb rudiments and pelvic spurs. Two levels of change are involved. Axially, the *Hox* expression that defines the thoracic (rib-bearing) region extends along almost the whole trunk of the python embryo, so that no cervical–thoracic boundary is available for forelimb initiation (Cohn and Tickle 1999), although later analyses of the axial skeleton suggest that some regionalization is retained. In the hindlimb bud, *Shh* is expressed only weakly and briefly, and the AER is not maintained, so outgrowth stops early (Leal and Cohn 2016). The cause lies in the ZRS. Kvon et al. (2016) replaced the mouse ZRS with its orthologues: the human and even the coelacanth ZRS gave normal mouse limbs, the python and cobra ZRS severely truncated ones. The snake ZRS has lost a 17-bp sequence containing ETS **binding sites**, and restoring it in the python ZRS rescued normal limb development: a change in one enhancer, leaving all other functions of *Shh* intact, removed the limbs.

### 32.7.3. Other Modifications

| Change | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| Bat wing: elongated forelimb digits | increased **chondrocyte** proliferation and BMP signaling; a bat *Prx1* **enhancer** lengthens mouse forelimbs; interdigital Gremlin and Fgf8 keep the webs |
| Reduced digits of even-toed ungulates (pig, cow) | loss of Shh-dependent *Ptch1* regulation in the limb, making the digits symmetric about the axis between digits 3 and 4 |
| Horse, jerboa: one or three digits | reduced growth and increased death of lateral digit rudiments |
| Stickleback pelvic reduction | recurrent deletions of a pelvic enhancer of *Pitx1* in freshwater populations |
| Flightless birds (kiwi, emu): tiny wings | the size of the limb is set in the embryo, by the size of the **limb field** and the duration of outgrowth |

## 32.8. Limb Malformations

Limb malformations are among the most common birth defects, about 1 in 500 births. Most can be read in terms of the mechanisms of the last two chapters.

| Malformation | Example and mechanism |
|---|---|
| Preaxial polydactyly | ZRS **point mutations** (ectopic anterior *SHH*); *GLI3* loss (Greig syndrome) |
| Postaxial and central polydactyly | *GLI3* truncation (Pallister–Hall syndrome); ciliopathies |
| Syndactyly | failure of interdigital death; *HOXD13* polyalanine expansions (synpolydactyly) |
| Brachydactyly (short digits) | mutations in *IHH*, *GDF5*, *BMPR1B*, *ROR2*: growth of the phalanges and joints |
| Split hand–foot malformation | failure to maintain the central AER (*TP63*, *DLX5/6*) |
| Amelia, tetra-amelia | complete absence of limbs: *WNT3* loss in the **ectoderm** (no AER) |
| Upper-limb and heart defects | Holt–Oram syndrome (*TBX5*, [7.5.31](/7.5.31)) |
| Phocomelia (proximal deficiency) | thalidomide (a **teratogen**, [7.5.38](/7.5.38)); Roberts syndrome |
| Nail–patella syndrome | *LMX1B* (dorsal identity) |

## Summary

- ZPA grafts (Saunders and Gasseling) give mirror-image **duplications** whose extent depends on the number of cells and the time; Shh is sufficient (beads, transfected cells) and necessary (*Shh* knockout: one digit, one **zeugopod** bone) for posterior pattern.
- The ZRS (Definition 32.1.1), a 1-Mb-distant **enhancer** in *LMBR1*: point mutations give ectopic anterior Shh and preaxial polydactyly; **deletion** removes limb Shh only.
- Digit identity (Definition 32.2.1) depends on time and concentration (Formula 32.2.1): digits 4–5 descend from Shh-expressing cells, digit 3 partly, digit 2 by diffusion, digit 1 without Shh; specification is early and coupled to growth. Shh acts by preventing the Gli3 **repressor** (*Shh*; *Gli3* = *Gli3*); the interdigits later convert positional values into phalanx number.
- The Shh–Gremlin1–FGF loop (Definition 32.3.1) couples ZPA and AER and terminates itself as the Shh-descendant cells separate the source from the Grem1 domain and high FGF represses Grem1.
- DV axis: dorsal Wnt7a → Lmx1b, ventral En1 represses Wnt7a; *Wnt7a* and *Lmx1b* mutants double-ventral, *En1* double-dorsal; AER, ZPA and dorsal ectoderm maintain one another.
- Digits form as a Turing pattern: BMP–Sox9–Wnt, wavelength tuned by distal Hox dose (Example 32.5.1); the chamber model (Proposition 32.5.1) gives $n$ condensations with $(n\pi/b)^2=r/D+\lambda^2/a^2$, increasing as the active zone shortens or as an inhibitor lowers $D$ (Formula 32.5.1).
- Interdigital death by BMP (blocked by Gremlin in ducks and bats); joints by Gdf5 and Wnt interzones and by movement.
- Evolution: fins to limbs via the late Hox13 territory; snakes lost limbs partly through the ZRS (Kvon et al. 2016); enhancer changes (*Ptch1*, *Pitx1*, *Prx1*) underlie many modifications.

## Questions

1. A ZPA graft is placed under the anterior AER, and after 15 h it is removed. The resulting wing has the pattern 2-2-3-4. What does this show, and what would you expect after 24 h?

   **Answer** The extra digit has the lowest identity: a short exposure to polarizing signal suffices only for digit 2. Identity requires the cumulative exposure (dose × time) to pass successive thresholds; a longer exposure, about a day, is expected to give 3-2-2-3-4 or the full 4-3-2-2-3-4. This is the time dependence that the Shh lineage data (Formula 32.2.1) later explained.

2. How would you show that the extra digits after a ZPA graft are formed by the host and not by the graft? What result would support a relay mechanism instead of a direct morphogen?

   **Answer** Graft quail ZPA into a chick (or GFP-labeled tissue) and identify the cells of the extra digits: they are host cells. For relay versus direct action, make clones of cells unable to respond (lacking **Smoothened**, the obligatory transducer of Hedgehog signaling) in the anterior mesenchyme: under direct action only the mutant cells fail to respond, wherever they are; under relay, wild-type cells beyond the clone also fail. The same logic, applied to Dpp and its receptor Thickveins in the wing disc, established direct long-range action ([7.5.11](/7.5.11)).

3. Explain why *Shh*; *Gli3* double mutant limbs resemble *Gli3* rather than *Shh* mutants, and what this means for the logic of Shh signaling.

   **Answer** In *Shh* mutants Gli3 is fully processed to the **repressor** throughout the bud, which represses posterior genes and proliferation, so digits fail to form. Without Gli3 there is no repressor, and Shh has nothing left to counteract, so its absence makes no difference: the double mutant is epistatically like *Gli3* (**epistasis**, [7.4.4](/7.4.4)). Shh's main role is the derepression of a pathway that is repressive by default; Gli activators add only a small contribution in the limb. Digit formation itself is independent of Shh (it is a Turing-type pattern), while digit identity and the number of cells depend on the Shh–Gli3R balance.

4. In the temporal-expansion model (Formula 32.2.1), what happens to the identities if the growth rate $g$ is halved? If $T$ is halved?

   **Answer** With slower growth, cells stay longer in the source and in the high **paracrine** region, so exposures rise: more cells exceed the digit-5 and digit-4 thresholds, the posterior digits widen and anterior identities shift posteriorly, but there are fewer cells in total to make digits (fewer digits, as with cell-cycle inhibition). Halving $T$ lowers all exposures: posterior identities are lost first (digits 5 and 4 become 4 and 3), as when *Shh* is removed conditionally.

5. A Gremlin bead is placed in the anterior mesenchyme of a chick wing bud. Predict the effects on the AER, Shh and digit number, and explain.

   **Answer** Gremlin locally antagonizes BMP, so the anterior AER is maintained longer and FGF signaling is prolonged anteriorly; FGF can sustain *Shh* posteriorly and widen the bud anteriorly, and the anterior mesenchyme grows more. Expected: extra anterior outgrowth and possibly an extra anterior digit (digit 2-like) and webbing (no interdigital death near the bead). The experiment tests the BMP–AER link of the loop independently of Shh.

6. Using Example 32.5.1, estimate the wavelength of the digit pattern for a human hand plate about $3\,\mathrm{mm}$ wide at seven weeks. Why does the number of digits stay five in different-sized species?

   **Answer** Nine half-wavelengths across $3\,\mathrm{mm}$ give $\Lambda\approx2\times3/9=0.67\,\mathrm{mm}$. Across mammals the **autopod** at the time of condensation is not proportional to adult size, and the wavelength is set by kinetics that are tuned (by Hox dose, FGF, Shh-dependent growth) so that about five condensations fit; the Shh–Gli3 system and the coupling of growth to Shh keep the plate width in the range for five. Variation of the width beyond this range (large paws in some dog breeds, *Gli3* or Hox mutants) does change the number.

7. Derive from Proposition 32.5.1 the chamber length at which the second condensation appears, for $rb^2/D=\pi^2$ and $\lambda=4$. Why is the number of condensations not simply the integer part of $n(a)$ in a real limb?

   **Answer** $n^2=1+\lambda^2b^2/(\pi^2a^2)$; $n=2$ requires $a/b=\lambda/(\pi\sqrt3)=0.735$. In a real limb the pattern forms in a chamber that changes continuously, nonlinear kinetics select and stabilize particular modes, and condensations once formed are frozen (they become cartilage), so the pattern depends on history; the integer part of $n(a)$ is only the mode count of the linear problem at each instant.

8. Design an experiment to test whether the webbed foot of the duck is due to the mesenchyme or to the **ectoderm**, and one to test whether Gremlin is responsible.

   **Answer** Recombine interdigital mesenchyme and ectoderm between duck and chick leg buds and graft the recombinants to a host: the web persists when the mesenchyme is from the duck, whatever the ectoderm. For Gremlin: implant BMP4 beads in duck interdigits (expect death and separation) and Gremlin beads, or a virus expressing *Grem1*, in chick interdigits (expect webbing); knock down *Grem1* in duck interdigits (**morpholino** or RNAi by electroporation) and expect increased **apoptosis** (TUNEL) and reduced webbing.

9. Kvon et al. found that a coelacanth ZRS works in the mouse limb. What does this say about the conservation of the limb regulatory machinery since the divergence of lobe-finned fish and tetrapods, and why does the python ZRS fail?

   **Answer** The transcription factors that read the ZRS (HAND2, HOX, ETS) and the essential **binding site** arrangement have been conserved for more than 400 million years, since fins; the fin and limb use the same enhancer to make Shh posteriorly. The python ZRS has lost a sequence carrying ETS binding sites, so it cannot be activated sufficiently in the posterior mesenchyme; restoring it rescues activity. In a limbless lineage, mutations in this enhancer are not removed by selection, so it degenerates, and in snakes its partial loss of function may have been one of the steps of limb reduction.

10. (Open) Draw the regulatory network that integrates the three axes, and predict the phenotype of removing the AER from the posterior half of the bud only.

    **Answer** Network: AER-FGF ↔ ZPA-Shh (through Gremlin1 and BMP), dorsal Wnt7a → Lmx1b (dorsal identity) and Wnt7a → Shh, ventral En1 ⊣ Wnt7a and positioning of the AER, Shh → Grem1 ⊣ BMP ⊣ AER. Removing the posterior AER removes the FGF that maintains *Shh*: *Shh* falls, posterior digits are lost and the anterior AER, deprived of Gremlin, regresses as well, so the limb is truncated distally and posteriorly, more severely than the local damage alone. This is the result of classical partial AER removals, which led to the idea of a ZPA–AER interaction before the molecules were known.
