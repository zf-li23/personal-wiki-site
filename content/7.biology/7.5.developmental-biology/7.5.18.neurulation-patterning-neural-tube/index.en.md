At the end of **gastrulation** the dorsal **ectoderm** of a vertebrate embryo has been told by the organizer and its mesoderm to become nervous tissue rather than epidermis: this is **neural induction**, treated in [7.5.17](/7.5.17) together with the first, coarse division of the neural plate into anterior and posterior parts. This chapter follows what happens next. The neural plate folds into a tube, the rudiment of the brain and spinal cord; the tube is subdivided along its length into brain vesicles and segments, and across its dorso-ventral axis into a ladder of progenitor domains that each make one kind of neuron. Three themes recur: morphogenesis driven by cell shape and tissue mechanics (continuing [7.5.8](/7.5.8) and [7.5.9](/7.5.9)), **positional information** supplied by opposing **morphogen** gradients and read by gene regulatory networks (the theory of [7.5.11](/7.5.11)), and the clinical importance of the process, since failure of the tube to close is among the commonest serious birth defects. The cells that leave the borders of the neural plate, the **neural crest**, are the subject of [7.5.20](/7.5.20); the birth of neurons inside the tube and the growth of the brain follow in [7.5.19](/7.5.19).

## 18.1. The Nervous System and Its Embryonic Origin

The nervous system consists of the central nervous system (CNS), the brain and spinal cord, and the peripheral nervous system (PNS): sensory and autonomic ganglia, nerves and the enteric nervous system of the gut. Its cells are neurons, which conduct and transmit signals, and glia (astrocytes, oligodendrocytes and Schwann cells, and the microglia, which come from the blood lineage). Three ectodermal populations build it:

| Source | Gives rise to |
|---|---|
| Neural plate → neural tube | brain, spinal cord, retina; their neurons, astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, ependyma |
| Neural plate border → **neural crest** | sensory, sympathetic, parasympathetic and enteric neurons and glia; Schwann cells; also melanocytes, craniofacial skeleton ([7.5.20](/7.5.20)) |
| Cranial **placodes** | sensory neurons of the nose, ear and some cranial ganglia; lens ([7.5.22](/7.5.22)) |

**Definition 18.1.1 (Neurulation)** The **neural plate** is the region of dorsal ectoderm specified to a neural fate, a thickened sheet of columnar neuroepithelial cells. **Neurulation** is the process by which the neural plate is transformed into the **neural tube**, the hollow epithelial tube that is the rudiment of the whole central nervous system; an embryo undergoing it is a neurula.

Two modes of neurulation exist. In primary neurulation the plate itself folds and seals; in secondary neurulation a solid cord of mesenchyme condenses and then hollows out (18.4). In amniotes the anterior tube, down to the lumbar or sacral level, forms by primary neurulation and the tail tube by secondary neurulation. Defects of the nervous system are a large part of clinical embryology: neural tube defects (18.3), malformations of the brain and of cortical development ([7.5.19](/7.5.19)), defects of neural crest derivatives such as Hirschsprung disease and some congenital heart defects ([7.5.20](/7.5.20)), and congenital myasthenic syndromes that arise at the neuromuscular junction ([7.5.21](/7.5.21)).

## 18.2. Primary Neurulation

Primary neurulation is usually divided into four overlapping steps, which differ in their mechanisms and in the genes whose loss disturbs them.

### 18.2.1. Formation and Shaping of the Neural Plate

The plate appears as a thickening of the dorsal ectoderm while the underlying mesoderm is still gastrulating: the neural cells become taller and columnar, the epidermal cells flatten. It then elongates along the anterior-posterior axis and narrows mediolaterally by **convergent extension**, the cell behaviour already met in the mesoderm ([7.5.9](/7.5.9)): cells intercalate between their medial and lateral neighbours, which lengthens the tissue at constant area. The intercalation is oriented by the planar cell polarity (PCP) pathway (Frizzled, Dishevelled, Vangl2, Celsr1); in the mouse *loop-tail* mutant (*Vangl2*) the plate stays short and broad, the folds are too far apart to meet, and the tube remains open from the midbrain to the tail. Convergent extension therefore narrows the plate and brings its edges within reach of each other; it also accounts for most of its elongation. Cell division adds to the length, with daughters placed preferentially along the axis.

In amniotes these events progress from head to tail: in a chick embryo of one day the head is neurulating while the tail is still gastrulating, so a single embryo displays the whole sequence. The neural plate of the head is broad and that of the trunk narrow, which gives the plate seen from above a characteristic keyhole shape; a mechanical model of this shape is the subject of 18.5.

### 18.2.2. Bending: Hinge Points

**Definition 18.2.1 (Hinge Points)** The **median hinge point** (MHP) is the strip of neural plate cells in the midline, above the notochord, that become wedge-shaped (apically narrow, basally wide) and form a furrow, the neural groove. The **dorsolateral hinge points** (DLHPs) are paired strips near the edges of the plate, where a second bending folds the walls towards each other.

The MHP cells are anchored to the notochord beneath them, and their shape follows from three processes: apical constriction by an actomyosin network, the basal position of the nucleus (cells in this strip spend longer in the cell-cycle phases in which the nucleus lies basally), and a lengthened cell cycle. They are induced by the notochord: removing the notochord abolishes the MHP, and grafting a notochord beneath the lateral plate induces an ectopic one. The signal is not Shh alone, since Shh specifies the floor plate (18.7) but additional notochord signals are needed for the wedge shape.

The DLHPs are regulated by a balance of signals (Ybot-Gonzalez et al. 2007). BMPs from the epidermis inhibit bending; Noggin, a BMP antagonist made in the dorsal neural folds, relieves this inhibition; and Shh rising from the notochord and floor plate suppresses DLHP formation ventrally. Bending therefore occurs only where both BMP and Shh signalling are low, which places the DLHPs near the dorsal edge and makes them most prominent in the head and in the lower spine, where the plate is broad or where the notochord signal is weak. Mice lacking Noggin fail to close the spinal tube.

**Example 18.2.1 (Intrinsic and Extrinsic Forces)** Is bending driven by the plate itself or pushed by its surroundings? Two microsurgical experiments answer it. Isolated neural plate, cut free of the epidermis, forms an MHP but its folds do not rise; epidermis isolated with a strip of plate still pushes medially. Conversely, the folds still rise when the mesoderm beneath is removed, although less well. The conclusion is that wedging within the hinge points (intrinsic) creates the furrows, while the medially directed expansion of the epidermis (extrinsic) supplies the force that elevates the folds; neither alone completes the tube.

### 18.2.3. Elevation, Fusion and Separation

The two neural folds rise, meet in the dorsal midline and fuse. Fusion begins with the epidermis: cells of the non-neural ectoderm at the fold tips extend lamellipodia and filopodia across the gap and form bridges, followed by the neural cells themselves (Pyrgaki et al. 2011). The tube then separates from the epidermis, which closes over it as a continuous sheet. Separation correlates with a switch in adhesion molecules: the prospective epidermis keeps E-cadherin, while the neural plate switches from E-cadherin to N-cadherin (and N-CAM), so that the two populations sort apart like the tissues of the reaggregation experiments of [7.5.7](/7.5.7); in *Xenopus*, misexpression of N-cadherin in the epidermis prevents separation. Between the tube and the epidermis, the cells of the former fold tips undergo an **epithelial–mesenchymal transition** and become the migrating neural crest.

Closure does not proceed simultaneously along the axis. The openings that close last are the anterior neuropore and the posterior neuropore. In the chick, closure begins at the future midbrain and zips in both directions. In the mouse it is initiated at three separate sites, and further zipping from each closes the intervening gaps: closure 1 at the hindbrain-cervical boundary, closure 2 at the forebrain-midbrain boundary, closure 3 at the rostral end of the forebrain, leaving a hindbrain neuropore between sites 1 and 2 and the anterior neuropore between sites 2 and 3. In humans the tube begins to close near the hindbrain-cervical boundary at about 22 days after fertilization (the 8-10 somite stage), the anterior neuropore closes at about day 25 and the posterior neuropore at about days 26-28; how many initiation sites the human embryo uses has been debated (five were proposed from the distribution of defects, two or three from direct study of embryos). The practical point is the date: the tube has closed before many women know that they are pregnant.

## 18.3. Neural Tube Defects

**Definition 18.3.1 (Neural Tube Defect)** A **neural tube defect** (NTD) is a congenital malformation resulting from failure of the neural tube to close, or from a defect of the structures that cover it. The open defects follow failure of primary neurulation: anencephaly (the cranial tube stays open, the exposed forebrain degenerates; lethal at or shortly after birth), craniorachischisis (open from the midbrain to the low spine) and open spina bifida (myelomeningocele: the lumbosacral cord lies open on the back). Closed defects, covered by skin, such as encephalocele and spina bifida occulta, arise later.

The level of an open defect follows from the closure map: a failure of closure 1 gives craniorachischisis, a failure in the head gives anencephaly, a failure of the posterior neuropore gives spina bifida. Severity of spina bifida depends on the level: the lower the lesion, the less paralysis; hydrocephalus and the Chiari II hindbrain malformation are common companions. Worldwide prevalence is roughly 1-2 per 1000 births where folate intake is adequate and several times higher where it is not.

The causes are multifactorial in the sense of [7.4](/7.4): many genes of small effect, interacting with the environment. In the mouse more than 300 mutations cause NTDs. The best-understood classes are the PCP genes (convergent extension, 18.2.1; rare variants in *VANGL1/2*, *CELSR1* and *SCRIB* are found in human patients), genes of the Shh pathway and of the actin cytoskeleton, genes for transcription factors of the dorsal neural tube such as *Pax3* (the *Splotch* mouse, which also lacks neural crest derivatives), and genes of folate one-carbon metabolism. Environmental risk factors include low maternal folate, maternal diabetes and obesity, hyperthermia in early pregnancy, and drugs such as valproic acid and folate antagonists.

**Example 18.3.1 (Folic Acid Prevents Neural Tube Defects)** The Medical Research Council Vitamin Study (1991) randomized women who had already had an affected pregnancy, and were planning another, to 4 mg folic acid daily or no folic acid (with or without other vitamins) from before conception. Recurrences: 6 of 593 pregnancies with folic acid, 21 of 602 without. The relative risk is

$$
\mathrm{RR} = \frac{6/593}{21/602} = \frac{0.0101}{0.0349} = 0.29,
$$

with an approximate 95% confidence interval $\exp\bigl(\ln \mathrm{RR} \pm 1.96\sqrt{1/6 - 1/593 + 1/21 - 1/602}\bigr) = (0.12, 0.71)$, so about 70% of recurrences were prevented. The absolute risk reduction is $3.49\% - 1.01\% = 2.48\%$: about 40 women had to take folic acid to prevent one recurrence. A Hungarian trial (Czeizel and Dudás 1992) showed prevention of first occurrences, and a community programme in China (Berry et al. 1999), where prevalence was 4.8 per 1000 among women without folic acid in the northern provinces, found 0.4 mg daily periconceptionally reduced the risk by about 80% in the north and 40% in the south. Some districts of northern China, notably the Lüliang mountains of Shanxi, had among the highest prevalences ever recorded (on the order of 1% of births in surveys of the early 2000s), attributed to low intake of folate and other micronutrients together with environmental exposures such as indoor coal smoke. Recommendations followed: 0.4 mg daily for all women who could become pregnant, 4-5 mg after a previous NTD; mandatory fortification of flour in more than 80 countries reduced prevalence by 30-50%.

How folate acts is not fully known. Folate, as **tetrahydrofolate**, carries one-carbon units for the synthesis of thymidylate and purines and for the methionine cycle that supplies S-adenosylmethionine to methylation reactions ([7.1](/7.1)); a rapidly proliferating neural plate with a high demand for nucleotides, or a requirement for methylation of DNA and proteins during closure, are both plausible targets. Mutations of the folate receptor (*Folr1*) cause NTDs in mice that folate rescues, but many mouse NTDs, such as those of *curly tail* (a hypomorph of *Grhl3*), are folate-resistant; *curly tail* responds instead to inositol. About 30% of human NTDs are thought to be folate-resistant, which is why fortification lowers prevalence but does not abolish it. Prenatal detection uses maternal serum α-fetoprotein, which leaks from an open defect, and ultrasound; in utero surgical repair of myelomeningocele improves motor outcome (the MOMS trial, 2011).

## 18.4. Secondary Neurulation and Other Modes

**Definition 18.4.1 (Secondary Neurulation)** **Secondary neurulation** is the formation of the neural tube from a solid rod of mesenchymal cells (the medullary cord) that condenses beneath the surface ectoderm of the tail bud, undergoes a mesenchymal–epithelial transition and hollows out by cavitation; the lumen then joins that of the tube formed by primary neurulation.

In birds and mammals secondary neurulation builds the tube caudal to the sacral region (in humans the sacral and coccygeal segments); the junction between the two modes is a site of malformations, and closed, skin-covered defects of the lower spine (tethered cord, spina bifida occulta) are thought to arise from errors of secondary neurulation. The cells of the tail bud are remarkable: **lineage tracing** shows that single cells near the regressing node and in the caudal lateral epiblast give rise to both neural tube and somites.

**Definition 18.4.2 (Neuromesodermal Progenitors)** **Neuromesodermal progenitors** (NMPs) are bipotent cells of the caudal epiblast and tail bud that co-express Sox2 (neural) and Brachyury (mesodermal) and give rise to both the posterior neural tube and the paraxial mesoderm.

Wnt and FGF signalling from the tail bud keep them in this state and push them towards mesoderm, while retinoic acid from the somites promotes the neural fate and, opposing FGF and Wnt, sets the time at which posterior spinal cord cells begin to differentiate ([7.5.23](/7.5.23)). In the chick the posterior neural plate grows from a stem zone beside the node that moves back with the node, leaving cells behind that form the spinal cord.

Zebrafish form their tube differently. The neural plate converges into a solid neural keel, then a rod; the lumen opens later in the midline, as in secondary neurulation. During convergence, progenitors divide across the midline (the C-division), placing one daughter on each side, which builds the mirror-symmetric organization of the future tube.

## 18.5. The Mechanics of Neural Plate Shaping

The neural plate of amphibians changes shape on a time scale of hours, from a broad oval to a keyhole: wide anteriorly, where the brain will form, and narrow and long over the notochord. Jacobson and Gordon (1976) reproduced the change in a computer model that combined two processes: the plate cells shorten their apical surface (they become taller, so the area of the sheet falls) and the plate is carried by an elongating notochord beneath its posterior midline, which they represented as a source of flow at the notochord tip balanced by sinks along it, giving an irrotational flow confined to the plate. Percus and Childress asked whether the same flow follows from differential adhesion between the plate and the tissues beneath, using the thin-layer methods of [7.5.8](/7.5.8) and [7.5.9](/7.5.9).

**Formula 18.5.1 (Two-Layer Lubrication Model)** Let the neural plate (upper layer) ride on a layer of mesoderm and notochord (lower layer) of thickness $h(x, y, t)$ resting on a rigid substrate at $z = 0$, to which it adheres. Let $T(x, y, t)$ be the tension of the interface between the layers, and assume (i) the lower layer is a viscous fluid thin enough for lubrication theory, (ii) the shear stress at its upper surface is set by the gradient of interfacial tension (a Marangoni stress, $\mu\,\partial\boldsymbol{u}/\partial z = \nabla T$, with $\mu$ absorbed in the time unit), and (iii) the tension is a property of the cells and is carried with them. Then

$$
\frac{\partial T}{\partial t} + h\,\lvert\nabla T\rvert^{2} = 0,
\qquad
\frac{\partial h}{\partial t} + \nabla\cdot\Bigl(\frac{h^{2}}{2}\nabla T\Bigr) = 0,
\tag{18.1}
$$

and the upper surface of the lower layer, which carries the plate, moves with $\boldsymbol{u} = h\nabla T$.

**Proof** In lubrication theory the horizontal velocity varies across the layer while pressure gradients are negligible at leading order, since by assumption the driving stress $\nabla T$ is comparable to $\partial \boldsymbol{u}/\partial z$, and a pressure large enough to matter would have to be larger by the inverse aspect ratio. The momentum balance is then $\partial^{2}\boldsymbol{u}/\partial z^{2} = 0$, so $\boldsymbol{u} = \boldsymbol{F}(x, y, t)\,z + \boldsymbol{G}(x, y, t)$. The conditions $\boldsymbol{u} = \boldsymbol{0}$ at $z = 0$ and $\partial\boldsymbol{u}/\partial z = \nabla T$ at $z = h$ give $\boldsymbol{G} = \boldsymbol{0}$, $\boldsymbol{F} = \nabla T$, hence $\boldsymbol{u}(z) = z\nabla T$ and the surface velocity $h\nabla T$. Transport of $T$ by the surface velocity is $T_t + \boldsymbol{u}\cdot\nabla T = 0$, the first equation. The volume flux through the layer is $\int_0^h \boldsymbol{u}\,\mathrm{d}z = \tfrac12 h^{2}\nabla T$, and conservation of volume gives the second. $\square$

If the thickness stays close to a constant $h_0$, the system decouples: $T_t + h_0\lvert\nabla T\rvert^2 = 0$ determines $T$, and then

$$
\frac{\partial h}{\partial t} \approx -\frac{h_0^{2}}{2}\,\nabla^{2}T .
\tag{18.2}
$$

The surface velocity $\boldsymbol{u} = h_0\nabla T$ is then a gradient, which is exactly the irrotational flow that Jacobson and Gordon postulated.

**Formula 18.5.2 (Solution Along Characteristics)** Let $\boldsymbol{q} = 2h_0\nabla T$. Taking the gradient of $T_t + h_0\lvert\nabla T\rvert^{2} = 0$ and using $\nabla\lvert\boldsymbol{w}\rvert^{2} = 2(\boldsymbol{w}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{w}$ for an irrotational field $\boldsymbol{w}$ gives the equation of pressureless (free-streaming) flow,

$$
\frac{\partial \boldsymbol{q}}{\partial t} + (\boldsymbol{q}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{q} = \boldsymbol{0},
\tag{18.3}
$$

whose solution is constant along straight characteristics: if $\boldsymbol{q}(\boldsymbol{x}, 0) = \boldsymbol{q}_0(\boldsymbol{x})$, then $\boldsymbol{q}(\boldsymbol{x}, t) = \boldsymbol{q}_0(\boldsymbol{x}_0)$ where $\boldsymbol{x} = \boldsymbol{x}_0 + t\,\boldsymbol{q}_0(\boldsymbol{x}_0)$. Irrotationality is preserved, since $\boldsymbol{q}\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{q} = \tfrac12\nabla q^{2} - \boldsymbol{q}\times(\nabla\times\boldsymbol{q})$. The solution stays regular while the map $\boldsymbol{x}_0 \mapsto \boldsymbol{x}$ is invertible, that is while $\det\bigl(I + t\,\nabla\boldsymbol{q}_0\bigr) > 0$; where the initial field is converging, characteristics cross in finite time even for analytic data, as for the Burgers equation of [2.5.2](/2.5.2).

**Example 18.5.1** Take $\boldsymbol{q}_0 = c\,(y, x)$, a pure strain. The characteristics $x = x_0 + c t y_0$, $y = y_0 + c t x_0$ invert to $x_0 = (x - cty)/(1 - c^2t^2)$, $y_0 = (y - ctx)/(1 - c^2t^2)$, so

$$
\boldsymbol{q}(x, y, t) = \frac{c\,\bigl(y - ctx,\; x - cty\bigr)}{1 - c^{2}t^{2}},
$$

which becomes singular at $t = 1/c$: material converging along $x = -y$ collapses onto the line $x = y$. In the neural plate the analogous singularity is convergence of the tissue onto the midline.

Three conclusions follow. First, the keyhole shape is obtained easily: a tension that increases posteriorly behind the notochord tip produces extension along the notochord and medio-lateral convergence over it, while the anterior plate, beyond the reach of the notochord, stays broad. The general expansion of the lower layer means that the plate cells must still shrink their apical area, as in the original model. Second, the interfacial tension must be lower along the notochord than on either side (the notochord adheres strongly to the plate, the paraxial mesoderm less so, a reversal of tractions relative to the upper surface), so $\nabla^{2}T > 0$ near the midline and $\nabla^{2}T < 0$ beside it; by Eq. (18.2) the lower layer then thins in the midline and thickens on either side, predicting a groove flanked by two ridges, the configuration of the neural groove and folds. Third, the same two-layer geometry, with a reversal of stress at the interface and contractile stress directed towards the midline on the upper surface, suggests a push-pull mechanism for the formation of the avian primitive streak.

**Remark 18.5.1** The model is a demonstration that adhesion-driven interfacial stresses can produce the observed flow, not a description of the measured forces. Later work on amphibian neurulation (Keller and colleagues) showed that the plate elongates mainly by mediolateral intercalation of its own deep cells, an active, PCP-dependent convergent extension that proceeds in explants of neural plate without mesoderm, although it is stronger with it; apical constriction (Shroom3 recruiting Rho kinase and myosin II to apical junctions) drives the hinge points. In current terms the lubrication model captures the contribution of the tissues beneath the plate, one of several mechanical inputs that act together.

## 18.6. Anterior-Posterior Regionalization of the Neural Tube

### 18.6.1. Brain Vesicles

Even before the tube closes, its anterior part balloons into three primary vesicles: the prosencephalon (forebrain), mesencephalon (midbrain) and rhombencephalon (hindbrain); behind them is the spinal cord. By the fifth week in humans the prosencephalon has divided into the telencephalon, which forms the cerebral hemispheres, and the diencephalon, which forms the thalamus and hypothalamus and from which the optic vesicles grow ([7.5.22](/7.5.22)); the rhombencephalon has divided into the metencephalon (cerebellum and pons) and the myelencephalon (medulla). The cavities become the ventricles, filled with cerebrospinal fluid. The rapid expansion of the brain vesicles is partly hydrostatic: the spinal end of the tube is temporarily occluded and fluid pressure inflates the brain (Desmond and Jacobson 1977).

The coarse anterior-posterior pattern is set during neural induction by the opposing actions of anterior Wnt antagonists and of posteriorizing Wnt, FGF and retinoic acid signals ([7.5.17](/7.5.17)). It is then refined by local signalling centres inside the neural tube: the anterior neural ridge (FGF8) at the front of the telencephalon, the zona limitans intrathalamica (Shh) in the diencephalon, and the isthmic organizer at the midbrain-hindbrain boundary.

### 18.6.2. The Isthmic Organizer

**Definition 18.6.1 (Isthmic Organizer)** The **isthmic organizer** is a signalling centre at the boundary between the midbrain and the hindbrain (the isthmus). It forms where the territories of the transcription factors Otx2 (anterior) and Gbx2 (posterior) abut, secretes FGF8 (on the hindbrain side) and Wnt1 (on the midbrain side), and patterns the tectum of the midbrain and the cerebellum of the anterior hindbrain.

The experiments that define it follow the logic of organizer grafts ([7.5.16](/7.5.16)). Transplanted into the caudal diencephalon of a chick embryo, isthmic tissue induces the host tissue around it to express *Engrailed* and to form an ectopic midbrain tectum or cerebellum (Martinez et al. 1991); a bead soaked in FGF8 does the same (Crossley et al. 1996). The boundary itself is positioned by mutual repression: Otx2 and Gbx2 repress each other, and shifting the domain of either moves the isthmus and resizes the midbrain and cerebellum. *Fgf8*, *Wnt1*, *En1/2* and *Pax2/5* maintain one another in a positive feedback loop across the boundary; mice lacking *Wnt1* or *En1* lose the midbrain and cerebellum.

### 18.6.3. Rhombomeres

**Definition 18.6.2 (Rhombomere)** A **rhombomere** is one of seven or eight transient segments of the embryonic hindbrain (r1-r7 or r8). Each is a compartment of cell lineage: cells mix within a rhombomere but not across its boundaries, and each rhombomere has a distinct combination of gene expression, neurons and neural crest.

Lineage restriction was shown by marking single cells: a cell labelled with an intracellular dye before the boundaries form gives a clone that may span two rhombomeres, but a cell labelled after they form gives a clone confined to one (Fraser, Keynes and Lumsden 1990). The boundaries are maintained by Eph receptors and ephrins expressed in alternating rhombomeres (EphA4 in r3 and r5, ephrin-B ligands in r2, r4, r6): cells expressing a receptor are repelled from cells expressing its ligand and sort out, and the boundary cells themselves become specialized. The zinc-finger factor Krox20 (Egr2) is expressed in r3 and r5 and activates *EphA4* there; *Kreisler* (MafB) is expressed in r5 and r6. The rhombomeres prefigure the segmental organization of the cranial nerve nuclei (the motor neurons of cranial nerve V arise in r2-r3, VII in r4-r5) and of the neural crest streams that fill the pharyngeal arches ([7.5.20](/7.5.20)).

The identity of each rhombomere is specified by a nested set of **Hox gene** expression domains whose anterior limits coincide with rhombomere boundaries, with **Hox colinearity** between position in the cluster and anterior limit: paralogue group 1 up to the r3/r4 boundary (*Hoxb1* is strongly expressed in r4 alone), group 2 to r1/r2 (r1 is Hox-free), group 3 to r4/r5, group 4 to r6/r7. Altering the code transforms identity, which is read out in the neurons a rhombomere makes. In *Hoxb1* mutant mice r4 acquires an r2-like identity, and its facial motor neurons fail to undergo their characteristic caudal migration; when *Hoxb1* is expressed ectopically in r2, its motor neurons take on the r4 (facial) behaviour. Retinoic acid, which activates Hox genes in a dose-dependent way through retinoic acid response elements in their enhancers, posteriorizes the hindbrain when given in excess: r2-r3 adopt r4-r5 identities, one source of the hindbrain and craniofacial malformations caused by retinoid drugs in pregnancy ([7.5.38](/7.5.38)).

## 18.7. Dorso-Ventral Patterning of the Neural Tube

### 18.7.1. Signalling Centres and Experiments

The spinal cord is organized dorso-ventrally: sensory input arrives dorsally, where interneurons process it, and motor neurons sit ventrally and send their axons out of the cord. The pattern begins in the progenitors lining the lumen.

**Definition 18.7.1 (Floor Plate and Roof Plate)** The **floor plate** is the strip of non-neuronal epithelial cells at the ventral midline of the neural tube, induced by the notochord; it secretes Sonic hedgehog (Shh) and guides axons (Netrin-1, Slit; [7.5.21](/7.5.21)). The **roof plate** is the corresponding strip at the dorsal midline, derived from the closing neural folds, which secretes BMPs and Wnts.

The classical experiments are grafts and ablations in the chick (Placzek, Jessell, Yamada and colleagues):

| Manipulation | Result | Inference |
|---|---|---|
| Notochord grafted beside the lateral or dorsal neural tube | An ectopic floor plate forms next to the graft, with motor neurons beside it; dorsal markers (*Pax3*, *Pax7*) are repressed | The notochord is sufficient to induce ventral fates |
| Notochord removed before the floor plate forms | No floor plate, no motor neurons; dorsal markers expand ventrally | The notochord is necessary |
| Floor plate grafted ectopically | Induces a second floor plate and motor neurons | The floor plate relays the signal (homeogenetic induction) |
| Shh-expressing cells or Shh protein on neural plate explants | Ventral cell types in order of increasing concentration: V1, V2, MN, V3, floor plate | Shh acts as a morphogen with several thresholds |
| *Shh* knockout mouse, or function-blocking antibodies | No floor plate, motor neurons or V3 interneurons; dorsal fates expand (and cyclopia, [7.5.22](/7.5.22)) | Shh is the required ventral signal |
| Epidermis or BMP4/7 on intermediate neural plate | Dorsal cell types (roof plate, neural crest, dorsal interneurons) | Dorsal fates need BMP |

Shh is transduced through the **Hedgehog signaling pathway** in the **primary cilium**: Shh binding to Patched relieves repression of Smoothened, which converts the Gli transcription factors from repressor to activator forms (Gli2 and Gli3 activators; Gli3 repressor in the absence of signal). The output is the ratio of Gli activator to repressor, and it falls with distance from the floor plate. BMPs and Wnts from the roof plate act through the **TGF-β signaling pathway** (Smad1/5/8) and the **Wnt signaling pathway** to specify the dorsal interneuron progenitors, and they antagonize the ventral programme, so the two gradients oppose each other.

### 18.7.2. The Transcriptional Code of Progenitor Domains

The progenitors of the ventral half form five domains (p0, p1, p2, pMN, p3), defined by combinations of homeodomain and bHLH transcription factors (Briscoe et al. 2000). Class I factors are repressed by Shh at different thresholds (Pax7 at the lowest, then Dbx1, Dbx2, Irx3, Pax6); class II factors are induced at different thresholds (Nkx6.2, Nkx6.1, Olig2, Nkx2.2). Each boundary is the meeting point of a pair of factors that repress each other: Pax6 and Nkx2.2 at the pMN/p3 boundary, Irx3 and Olig2 at p2/pMN, Dbx2 and Nkx6.1 at p0/p1. The code is causal: in *Nkx2.2* mutants p3 cells become pMN-like and produce motor neurons; in *Pax6* mutants the pMN/p3 boundary moves dorsally; misexpression of Nkx2.2 in dorsal progenitors represses Pax6 and induces V3 interneurons. Each domain then gives one cardinal class of neuron (the V0-V3 interneurons of the locomotor circuits, the motor neurons), and after neurogenesis the pMN domain switches to producing oligodendrocyte precursors.

### 18.7.3. Reading a Gradient in Level and Time

The French flag view of [7.5.11](/7.5.11) (**French flag model**) is that each cell compares the local Shh concentration with fixed thresholds. Measurements in the mouse and chick show that this is only part of the story.

- Shh protein forms a gradient whose **decay length** is about 20 μm (Cohen et al. 2015), while the ventral half of the neural tube is about 100-150 μm high; the gradient grows in amplitude over the first day and its decay length stays nearly constant.
- Cells respond to duration as well as concentration. In explants, a given concentration of Shh first induces Olig2 and only after longer exposure Nkx2.2; increasing either concentration or duration ventralizes the fate (Dessaud et al. 2007). Cells adapt: Shh induces its own receptor Patched, which sequesters Shh and lowers signalling, so Gli activity peaks and then declines even at constant Shh.
- The gene regulatory network converts the graded, decaying input into stable, sharp domains, and it explains why Olig2 appears first, and Nkx2.2 later, in the most ventral cells (Balaskas et al. 2012).

**Formula 18.7.1 (A Model of the Pax6-Olig2-Nkx2.2 Network)** Let $P$, $O$, $N$ be the levels of Pax6, Olig2 and Nkx2.2 in a progenitor and $G(x, t)$ its Gli activity. With production terms that are activated by Gli and inhibited by the other factors (repression modelled as in the **Hill equation**, with **cooperativity** $h$) and first-order decay,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\dot P &= \frac{\alpha_P}{1 + (N/n_{PN})^{h} + (O/n_{PO})^{h}} - P,\\
\dot O &= \frac{\alpha_O\,G/(G + K_O)}{1 + (N/n_{ON})^{h} + (P/n_{OP})^{h}} - O,\\
\dot N &= \frac{\alpha_N\,G^{4}/(G^{4} + K_N^{4})}{1 + (O/n_{NO})^{h} + (P/n_{NP})^{h}} - N,
\end{aligned}
\tag{18.4}
$$

where $K_O < K_N$ (Olig2 needs less Gli than Nkx2.2), $n_{ON} < n_{NO}$ (Nkx2.2 represses Olig2 more strongly than the reverse) and $G$ follows the Shh gradient $\mathrm{e}^{-x/\lambda}$ through a saturating response that adapts in time.

The model reproduces the observations. Pax6 is made everywhere at first; where Gli is moderate, Olig2 rises, represses Pax6 and defines pMN; only where Gli is high and sustained does Nkx2.2 rise above the level at which it represses Olig2, and the ventral cells switch from Olig2 to Nkx2.2. Because the network is multistable, the domains do not shrink back when Gli activity declines by adaptation: the cells remember the signal they have received (**bistability** and **hysteresis**, [7.3.18](/7.3.18)). Two consequences matter. The pattern is more precise than the gradient: the boundaries are sharp although the input is smooth, and fluctuations of the input during the decision are integrated. And the same output can be reached by different combinations of level and duration, so that precise thresholds are not required.

**Remark 18.7.1** The precision of the boundaries can be estimated from the positional error of [7.5.11](/7.5.11): for an exponential gradient, a relative error $\delta c/c$ in the concentration estimate gives a position error $\delta x = \lambda\,\delta c/c$, about 4 μm for $\lambda = 20\ \mu\mathrm{m}$ and 20% noise, less than a cell diameter near the floor plate. Far from the source a single gradient is too shallow to be read precisely; combining the ventral Shh gradient with the opposing dorsal BMP gradient keeps the error small across the whole tube (Zagorski et al. 2017). The ventral gradient also scales: as the tube grows, the domains keep their proportions, partly because the gradient amplitude and the tissue grow together.

## 18.8. All Axes Come Together

The dorso-ventral code does not act alone. Along the spinal cord, Hox genes, activated in a colinear sequence as the axis elongates from the tail bud and regulated by the opposing gradients of retinoic acid (anterior) and FGF and Wnt (posterior), give each level its identity ([7.5.17](/7.5.17)). Motor neurons combine both codes: Olig2 history makes a cell a motor neuron; Hox proteins (Hoxc6 at brachial levels, Hoxc9 at thoracic levels) together with FoxP1 decide whether it joins the lateral motor column that innervates the limbs or the column that innervates the body wall, and which muscle it will innervate ([7.5.21](/7.5.21)). Along the third, radial axis, cells at the lumen divide and those that leave become neurons in the mantle ([7.5.19](/7.5.19)). Every cell of the tube thus acquires an address in a Cartesian grid of positional information, read out as a combination of transcription factors, and the specification of the extraordinary diversity of neurons is built largely from this grid.

## Summary

| Step | Mechanism | Key molecules | Clinical correlate |
|---|---|---|---|
| Neural plate shaping | convergent extension, oriented division | PCP genes (*Vangl2*, *Celsr1*) | craniorachischisis |
| Bending | MHP (notochord-induced wedging), DLHP (low BMP and Shh) | Shh, Noggin, BMP; Shroom3, actomyosin | spina bifida |
| Elevation, fusion | epidermal push, fold-tip bridges | Grhl2/3, cadherin switch E → N | anencephaly, spina bifida |
| Secondary neurulation | medullary cord, cavitation; NMPs | Sox2/Brachyury, Wnt, FGF, RA | closed caudal defects |
| A-P regions | vesicles; isthmic organizer; rhombomeres | Otx2/Gbx2, Fgf8, Wnt1, En; Krox20, Eph/ephrin, Hox | retinoid embryopathy |
| D-V pattern | roof plate BMP/Wnt vs floor plate Shh; cross-repressive code | Gli, Pax6, Olig2, Nkx2.2, Irx3, Nkx6.1 | (Shh pathway: holoprosencephaly) |

- Neurulation (Definition 18.1.1) turns the neural plate into the neural tube; primary and secondary modes (Definition 18.4.1) join in the lower spine; neuromesodermal progenitors build the caudal tube and somites from a common pool.
- Bending occurs at hinge points (Definition 18.2.1); intrinsic wedging and extrinsic epidermal forces are both required (Example 18.2.1). Closure starts at several sites in mammals; the level of a failure predicts the type of neural tube defect (Definition 18.3.1).
- Folic acid prevents most neural tube defects (Example 18.3.1: RR 0.29); about 30% are folate-resistant.
- In the two-layer lubrication model (Formula 18.5.1) interfacial tension gradients drive an irrotational flow, solved along characteristics (Formula 18.5.2) with finite-time convergence (Example 18.5.1); it yields the keyhole shape and predicts the neural groove from Eq. (18.2).
- The isthmic organizer (Definition 18.6.1) and the rhombomeres (Definition 18.6.2) refine the A-P pattern; rhombomeres are lineage compartments with a Hox code.
- The floor plate and roof plate (Definition 18.7.1) supply opposing gradients; a cross-repressive network (Formula 18.7.1) reads Shh in both level and duration and remembers it by hysteresis.

## Questions

1. A notochord from a donor chick embryo is grafted next to the dorsal half of the neural tube of a host before neural tube patterning. Predict the result for the floor plate, motor neurons and *Pax7* expression on the grafted side, and explain why a graft of floor plate gives the same result.

   **Answer** An ectopic floor plate forms in the dorsal tube next to the graft, motor neurons and ventral interneurons appear flanking it, and *Pax7* (a class I gene repressed by Shh) is lost near the graft; the pattern is a mirror-image ventral pattern centred on the graft. The notochord secretes Shh, which induces floor plate cells; these themselves express Shh (homeogenetic induction), so floor plate has the same inductive activity as notochord.

2. Isolated neural plate, separated from the epidermis, forms a median hinge point but its folds do not rise. What does this show, and what result would you expect if the notochord were also removed?

   **Answer** Wedging of the hinge-point cells is intrinsic to the plate and sufficient to furrow it, but elevation of the folds needs the extrinsic, medially directed expansion of the epidermis. Without the notochord the MHP would not form (it is notochord-induced), so the plate would stay flat in the midline; DLHPs could still form at the dorsal edges, where BMP is low and Shh absent, and indeed would be less inhibited.

3. In the MRC trial (6/593 recurrences with folic acid, 21/602 without), compute the absolute risk reduction and the number needed to treat. Why is the number needed to treat for primary prevention in a low-risk population much larger, although the relative risk is similar?

   **Answer** Risks are $6/593 = 1.01\%$ and $21/602 = 3.49\%$; ARR $= 2.48\%$, NNT $= 1/0.0248 \approx 40$. In the general population the baseline risk is about 0.1-0.2%; with the same relative risk of about 0.3 the ARR is about 0.1%, so about 1000 women must take folic acid to prevent one case. The absolute benefit scales with baseline risk, which is why it was largest in northern China (Figure B).

4. Derive Eq. (18.3) from $T_t + h_0\lvert\nabla T\rvert^{2} = 0$, and find the time at which the solution with $\boldsymbol{q}_0 = c(y, x)$ becomes singular. What is the meaning of such a singularity for the neural plate?

   **Answer** Let $\boldsymbol{w} = \nabla T$; then $\boldsymbol{w}_t + h_0\nabla\lvert\boldsymbol{w}\rvert^{2} = \boldsymbol{0}$. For irrotational $\boldsymbol{w}$, $\nabla\lvert\boldsymbol{w}\rvert^{2} = 2(\boldsymbol{w}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{w}$, so $\boldsymbol{w}_t + 2h_0(\boldsymbol{w}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{w} = \boldsymbol{0}$; multiplying by $2h_0$ gives $\boldsymbol{q}_t + (\boldsymbol{q}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{q} = \boldsymbol{0}$ for $\boldsymbol{q} = 2h_0\boldsymbol{w}$. From Example 18.5.1, $\det(I + t\nabla\boldsymbol{q}_0) = 1 - c^{2}t^{2}$, so the solution blows up at $t = 1/c$. Characteristics converging from opposite sides meet: tissue piles onto a line. In the plate this corresponds to convergence onto the midline, which the real tissue resolves by thickening and folding rather than by a singular velocity; the model is valid only before this time.

5. Shh forms an exponential gradient with decay length $\lambda = 20\ \mu\mathrm{m}$. If the Gli thresholds for the p3/pMN and pMN/p2 boundaries differ by a factor of 3 in concentration, how wide is pMN? How would the widths change if the amplitude of the gradient doubled, with fixed thresholds?

   **Answer** In a French flag readout the width is $\lambda\ln 3 \approx 22\ \mu\mathrm{m}$, a few cell diameters, consistent with the narrow pMN. Doubling the amplitude shifts every boundary dorsally by $\lambda\ln 2 \approx 14\ \mu\mathrm{m}$ but leaves the width between two boundaries unchanged, since widths depend only on threshold ratios. In the real tube the network and adaptation buffer such shifts, and duration of exposure also enters.

6. Design an explant experiment that distinguishes whether a cell reads the concentration of Shh or the integral of signalling over time. What does Figure C of the Shh model predict?

   **Answer** Culture naive intermediate neural plate explants in a range of Shh concentrations for different durations, then remove Shh (or add the Smoothened antagonist cyclopamine) and score Olig2 and Nkx2.2 at a fixed final time. If cells read concentration only, the fate depends only on dose; if they integrate, the same fate is reached by a higher dose for a shorter time or a lower dose for longer. The model predicts the latter at high doses: with a short pulse, a high level gives Olig2, a long pulse gives Nkx2.2, while very low levels never reach Nkx2.2 however long the exposure (saturation of the integral); this matches Dessaud et al. 2007.

7. How would you show that rhombomeres are compartments of cell lineage, and what role do Eph receptors and ephrins play?

   **Answer** Inject a fluorescent lineage tracer (or induce a genetic mark) into single hindbrain cells before and after the morphological boundaries appear, and map the clones a day later. Clones from cells marked early may cross future boundaries; clones from cells marked after boundary formation never do, though they spread freely within their rhombomere. Eph receptors (EphA4 in r3/r5) and ephrin-B ligands (in r2/r4/r6) are expressed in alternating segments; bidirectional repulsion when receptor-bearing and ligand-bearing cells meet sorts them into separate domains and keeps the interface sharp. Mosaic expression of dominant-negative EphA4 causes r3/r5 cells to mix into neighbouring segments.

8. A bead soaked in FGF8 is implanted into the caudal diencephalon of a chick embryo. Predict the outcome, and explain what experiment would show that FGF8 is not merely sufficient but required for the isthmic organizer.

   **Answer** The diencephalon around the bead expresses *En2*, *Wnt1* and *Fgf8* and develops ectopic midbrain (tectum) or cerebellar tissue, often a mirror-image of the normal sequence, as an isthmic graft does. Necessity is shown by loss of function: conditional removal of *Fgf8* at the isthmus in mice (or reduced dosage in hypomorphic alleles) leads to deletion of the midbrain and cerebellum through cell death; in zebrafish, the *acerebellar* mutant (*fgf8*) lacks the cerebellum and isthmus.

9. Neuromesodermal progenitors make both spinal cord and somites. Propose a lineage-tracing experiment to demonstrate their bipotency, and explain how retinoic acid and FGF/Wnt signals decide between the two fates.

   **Answer** Mark single cells in the caudal lateral epiblast near the node (dye injection in chick, or sparse genetic labelling in mouse with a Cre driver such as *T-CreERT2* at low tamoxifen) and follow the clones: a single labelled cell contributing to both neural tube and paraxial mesoderm demonstrates bipotency (retrospective clonal analysis in mouse showed such clones). Wnt and FGF from the tail bud maintain Brachyury and favour mesoderm and keep the progenitor state; retinoic acid from newly formed somites represses *Fgf8* and promotes Sox2 and neural differentiation, so cells leaving the progenitor zone anteriorly become neural as they escape the posterior FGF/Wnt gradient.

10. (Open) Many mouse neural tube defects are folate-resistant, and a fraction of human ones are as well. Suggest how one would design a study in humans to find genetic causes of folate-resistant NTDs, and why NTDs are a classic example of a multifactorial threshold trait.

    **Answer** Approach: sequence exomes or genomes of affected individuals (and parents) in populations with adequate folate intake or fortification, compare with controls, and test burdens of rare damaging variants in candidate pathways (PCP genes, Shh pathway, *GRHL*, inositol and one-carbon metabolism) and de novo variants in trios; confirm in model organisms (mouse knock-ins, explant closure assays). NTDs fit the liability-threshold model ([7.4](/7.4)): many genetic and environmental factors add to a continuous liability, and closure fails when liability exceeds a threshold; recurrence in siblings is about 2-5%, much higher than population risk but far below Mendelian ratios, and folate shifts the liability distribution so that fewer individuals cross the threshold.
