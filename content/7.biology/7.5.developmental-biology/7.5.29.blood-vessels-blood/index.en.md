The heart of the previous chapter ([7.5.28](/7.5.28)) is useless without vessels to carry blood and without blood to carry oxygen. Both arise early from the lateral plate mesoderm, at first in the yolk sac, where the embryo meets its food supply, and then inside the embryo. The vascular network is built in two steps: vessels are first assembled from dispersed precursors (vasculogenesis) and then extended, pruned and remodelled by sprouting from existing vessels (angiogenesis), under the control of a few signals, VEGF, Notch, ephrins and the mechanical force of flow. The blood is made in successive waves at different sites, and the stem cells that sustain it for life are born, surprisingly, from the endothelium of the major arteries. The chapter connects with the stem-cell biology of [7.3.24](/7.3.24), where the **hematopoietic stem cell** is defined, with **lateral inhibition** ([7.5.12](/7.5.12)), with the gene regulation of the globin loci ([7.2](/7.2)) and with the physics of flow. The development of lymphocytes and their selection belong to [7.15](/7.15), and the physiology of the circulation to [7.14](/7.14).

## 29.1. The Hemangioblast and the Blood Islands

**Definition 29.1.1 (Blood Island, Hemangioblast)** A **blood island** is an aggregate of mesodermal cells in the extraembryonic mesoderm of the yolk sac whose inner cells become primitive blood cells and whose outer cells become endothelium. A **hemangioblast** is a bipotential mesodermal progenitor that gives rise to both endothelial and hematopoietic cells.

Blood islands appear in the yolk sac at the late streak stage (mouse E7.25, human about day 16–19). The close association of blood and endothelium in them led Sabin (1920) and Murray (1932) to postulate a common precursor, the hemangioblast. Its existence was debated for decades. Cells with both potentials exist: blast colony-forming cells from differentiating mouse ES cells and from the posterior primitive streak make both endothelium and blood in culture (Choi et al. 1998; Huber et al. 2004), and single-cell lineage tracing in zebrafish found cells in the ventral mesoderm that give both (Vogeli et al. 2006). But clonal analysis of the mouse yolk sac in vivo showed that most blood island cells are already restricted to one fate when they form (Ueno and Weissman 2006), and much of the blood is now known to arise later from a specialized endothelium (29.6). The current view is that bipotential cells exist transiently in the primitive streak and posterior mesoderm, that most are committed by the time the islands form, and that the shared origin is reflected in a shared gene regulatory network: the ETS factor Etv2 (ER71), the bHLH factor Scl (Tal1), Lmo2 and Gata2, together with the VEGF receptor Flk1 (VEGFR2). Mice lacking *Etv2* or *Flk1* lack both blood and vessels; mice lacking *Scl* lack all blood and have defective yolk-sac vessels.

Inducing signals are, as for the heart, BMP from the endoderm and lateral mesoderm (BMP4 induces blood in *Xenopus* ventral mesoderm), Wnt and FGF; the heart and the blood compete for the same lateral plate mesoderm, and Wnt favours blood over heart ([7.5.28](/7.5.28)).

## 29.2. Vasculogenesis

**Definition 29.2.1 (Vasculogenesis)** **Vasculogenesis** is the formation of blood vessels de novo from mesodermal precursors (angioblasts) that differentiate into endothelial cells, aggregate into cords and form lumenized tubes, producing a primary capillary plexus.

Vasculogenesis makes the first vessels in two places at once: in the yolk sac, where the blood islands fuse into a capillary network drained by the vitelline veins, and inside the embryo, where angioblasts of the lateral and paraxial mesoderm assemble the dorsal aortae, the cardinal veins and the endocardium. Three signals are essential.

1. FGF2 promotes the formation of hemangioblasts and angioblasts from mesoderm: dissociated quail blastodisc cells form blood islands and endothelium in culture only when FGF2 is added (Flamme and Risau 1992).
2. VEGF-A, secreted by the endoderm and mesenchyme near the islands, acts through VEGFR2 (Flk1) on angioblasts, promoting their proliferation, survival and assembly into tubes. VEGF-A is one of the most dosage-sensitive genes known: mouse embryos lacking even one copy of *Vegfa* die at E11–12 with defective vessels (Carmeliet et al. 1996; Ferrara et al. 1996). *Flk1* mutants lack blood islands and organized vessels.
3. Angiopoietin-1 acting through the receptor Tie2, and PDGF-B from endothelial cells acting on PDGFRβ in pericytes and smooth muscle cells, recruit these mural cells to the endothelial tubes and stabilize them. Mice lacking *Tie2* or *Pdgfb* make vessels that are dilated, leaky and poorly covered.

The angioblasts that build the dorsal aorta of zebrafish migrate from the lateral plate to the midline beneath the notochord, guided by signals from the endoderm and the hypochord; the dorsal aorta forms first, and the posterior cardinal vein then forms partly from cells that sprout ventrally from it (Herbert et al. 2009). In amniotes, endothelial cells from the somites (sclerotome) later replace much of the dorsal aortic wall ([7.5.23](/7.5.23)). Some organs, such as the limb bud and the pancreas, also form vessels in situ by vasculogenesis from their own mesenchyme, while others, such as the brain and kidney, are invaded by sprouts from outside.

## 29.3. Angiogenesis

### 29.3.1. Sprouting

**Definition 29.3.1 (Angiogenesis)** **Angiogenesis** is the formation of new vessels from pre-existing ones, chiefly by sprouting (endothelial cells migrate and proliferate to form a new branch) and by intussusception (an existing vessel splits in two by the insertion of a tissue pillar), together with the remodelling and pruning of the resulting network into arteries, capillaries and veins.

The best-studied model is the mouse retina, whose vessels grow after birth from the optic nerve head outwards over a template of astrocytes that secrete VEGF-A in response to hypoxia (Gerhardt et al. 2003). Sprouting requires division of labour.

**Definition 29.3.2 (Tip Cell, Stalk Cell)** A **tip cell** is the endothelial cell at the leading end of a sprout, which extends many filopodia, migrates towards VEGF-A, divides little and does not form a lumen. The **stalk cells** behind it proliferate, elongate the sprout and form its lumen and junctions.

If every endothelial cell responded equally to VEGF, the existing vessel would disintegrate; instead a few cells become tips. VEGF-A signalling through VEGFR2 in an endothelial cell induces the Notch ligand Dll4. Dll4 activates Notch1 in the neighbouring cells, which reduces their expression of VEGFR2 (and of VEGFR3) and increases that of VEGFR1, a decoy receptor that binds VEGF with high affinity but signals weakly; the neighbours become less responsive to VEGF and adopt the stalk fate. Mice heterozygous for *Dll4* (it is haploinsufficient, like *Vegfa*) and retinas treated with Notch inhibitors have excessive, nonfunctional sprouting with many tip cells; mosaic analysis shows that *Notch1*-deficient cells occupy the tip position preferentially (Hellström et al. 2007; Suchting et al. 2007). The position is not fixed: in time-lapse imaging, endothelial cells overtake one another and compete for the tip continuously, the cell with the highest VEGFR2/VEGFR1 ratio winning (Jakobsson et al. 2010). This is **lateral inhibition** in a line of cells, the same logic as neural precursor selection ([7.5.12](/7.5.12)); a model shows what VEGF does to it.

**Formula 29.3.1 (A VEGF–Dll4–Notch Model of Tip Selection)** In a row of endothelial cells $i$, let $D_i$ be the Dll4 level and $N_i$ the Notch activity (scaled to their maxima), and let $G$ measure the VEGF signal. Notch reduces the cell's VEGF receptor activity, Dll4 is induced by it, and Notch is activated by the Dll4 of the two neighbours:

$$
R_i=\frac{G}{1+(N_i/k)^h},\qquad
\dot D_i=\frac{R_i^{\,n}}{1+R_i^{\,n}}-D_i,\qquad
\dot N_i=\tfrac12\,(D_{i-1}+D_{i+1})-N_i .
\tag{29.1}
$$

With $n=2$, $h=4$ and $k=0.3$, simulations of a ring of 20 cells give three regimes. At low $G$ ($\lesssim0.6$) all cells stay low: there is no tip. At intermediate $G$ the uniform state is unstable to the alternating mode, and the row resolves into alternating tip (high Dll4) and stalk (high Notch) cells, as in the lateral-inhibition model of [7.5.12](/7.5.12): the instability requires the loop gain through Dll4 and Notch, $\partial\dot D/\partial N$ times $\partial\dot N/\partial D$, to exceed one in magnitude, which happens only where $R$ is in the steep part of its response. At very high $G$ ($\gtrsim150$) VEGF signalling is so strong that Dll4 saturates in every cell; Notch is high everywhere but cannot lower $R$ below saturation, so all cells become tip-like and the contrast vanishes.

The model reproduces a key observation: very high VEGF, as in tumours or in retinas exposed to excess VEGF, disrupts the tip–stalk pattern and produces disorganized, leaky vessels, while too little produces none (Bentley et al. 2008, 2009). It also explains why VEGF must be presented as a gradient bound to the extracellular matrix: matrix-bound isoforms (VEGF164 and VEGF188 in the mouse) make a local, graded signal, and mice that express only the soluble VEGF120 isoform have fewer, wider vessels with fewer branches (Ruhrberg et al. 2002).

### 29.3.2. Guidance, Fusion and Pruning

Tip cells read guidance cues with the same receptors as axonal growth cones ([7.5.21](/7.5.21)): neuropilins (co-receptors for VEGF and semaphorins), Plexin-D1 (whose loss makes intersomitic vessels of zebrafish and mice grow into the somites instead of between them), Robo4, Unc5b (a netrin receptor), and EphB4 and ephrinB2. When two tip cells meet they fuse (anastomosis), helped by tissue macrophages that bridge them (Fantin et al. 2010); the lumen then extends into the new connection and flow begins. Flow decides which connections survive: segments with low flow regress by endothelial cells migrating away, a pruning that turns a dense primary plexus into a hierarchical network (Franco et al. 2015).

In the chick embryo the remodelling of the yolk sac plexus into arteries and veins follows the onset of flow: veins arise partly from arterial sprouts that lose connection with the arterial tree and reconnect to the venous side (le Noble et al. 2004), and ligating a vitelline artery converts arterial segments downstream to venous identity, showing that identity remains plastic under flow.

## 29.4. Arteries, Veins, Lumen and Lymphatics

### 29.4.1. Arterial and Venous Identity

Arteries and veins differ molecularly before the heart beats. Arterial endothelium expresses ephrinB2 and veins its receptor EphB4 (Wang, Chen and Anderson 1998); in mice lacking *Efnb2* or *Ephb4*, vasculogenesis occurs but angiogenic remodelling fails, and the embryos die at E10, because sorting and interaction between the two populations is needed to build the network. In zebrafish, cells are allocated to the dorsal aorta or the posterior cardinal vein by position and by a signalling cascade: Sonic hedgehog from the notochord induces VEGF in the somites, VEGF induces Notch signalling in the nearest angioblasts, and Notch, through its target *gridlock* (*hey2*), makes them arterial; reducing Notch or *gridlock* gives a smaller aorta and a larger vein, and in *gridlock* mutants blood cannot reach the trunk (Zhong et al. 2001; Lawson et al. 2001). Venous identity is actively maintained by the orphan nuclear receptor COUP-TFII (*Nr2f2*), which represses Notch signalling in veins; its loss makes veins express arterial markers (You et al. 2005). Lineage tracing in zebrafish shows that arterial and venous precursors are separate populations already in the lateral mesoderm, with different migratory timing (Kohli et al. 2013).

In peripheral tissues arteries follow nerves. In the embryonic skin of the mouse, sensory nerves and their Schwann cells secrete VEGF-A, which induces arterial differentiation of the adjacent capillaries (ephrinB2, Notch) and aligns the arteries along them; when nerves are absent, arteries do not form in the skin and the vessels are disorganized (Mukouyama et al. 2002). In return, vessels produce signals (artemin, neurotrophin-3, GDNF) that guide sympathetic axons and support neuronal survival: the two networks build each other.

### 29.4.2. Making a Lumen

Two mechanisms make the lumen. In cord hollowing, typical of large vessels such as the dorsal aorta, endothelial cells in a solid cord establish apical-basal polarity, deliver anti-adhesive proteins (podocalyxin, CD34 sialomucins) to their apical surfaces at cell-cell contacts and push apart, opening an extracellular lumen. In intracellular lumen formation, seen in some small vessels such as the zebrafish intersegmental vessels, pinocytic vacuoles fuse within a cell into a tube that connects with that of the next cell (Kamei et al. 2006). Blood pressure then expands the lumen and can also drive its extension by invagination of the apical membrane (Gebala et al. 2016).

### 29.4.3. Lymphatics

The lymphatic vessels, which return interstitial fluid to the veins and carry immune cells, develop after the blood vessels. In the mouse, at E9.5, a subset of endothelial cells in the cardinal vein expresses the transcription factor Prox1, induced by Sox18 and COUP-TFII; these cells bud from the vein, migrate towards a source of VEGF-C and form the primary lymph sacs, from which the lymphatic network sprouts (Wigle and Oliver 1999; Srinivasan et al. 2007). *Prox1* knockouts lack lymphatics; *Vegfc* knockouts have Prox1-positive cells in the vein that never leave it. Lymphatic identity is maintained by Prox1 throughout life, and lineage tracing has shown that lymphatics in the skin, heart and mesentery also have non-venous origins, from hemogenic endothelium and other mesodermal precursors. Platelets separate lymphatics from veins: at the junction of the lymph sacs with the veins, the lymphatic endothelium activates platelets through podoplanin and CLEC-2, and mice lacking either have blood-filled lymphatics. Mutations in *FLT4* (VEGFR3), *FOXC2* and *SOX18* cause human hereditary lymphedemas.

## 29.5. Shaping the Network by Flow

A vascular network is not specified vessel by vessel: everyone's circulation differs in detail. It is shaped by rules that adjust each vessel to the flow it carries. Endothelial cells sense the shear stress of flowing blood through their cilia, the PECAM-1–VE-cadherin–VEGFR2 junctional complex and ion channels such as Piezo1, and remodel to bring it towards a set point: vessels with high shear widen, vessels with low shear narrow or regress, and the transcription factor KLF2, induced by laminar shear, keeps the endothelium quiescent and anti-inflammatory. In zebrafish and mouse embryos, blocking the heartbeat or reducing blood viscosity (fewer red cells) prevents remodelling of the primary plexus. What set point would an efficient network choose?

**Formula 29.5.1 (Poiseuille Flow)** For steady laminar flow $Q$ of a fluid of viscosity $\mu$ through a rigid cylindrical tube of radius $r$ and length $\ell$, the pressure drop and the wall shear stress are

$$
\Delta p=\frac{8\mu\ell}{\pi r^4}\,Q,\qquad \tau_{\mathrm{w}}=\frac{4\mu Q}{\pi r^3}.
\tag{29.2}
$$

The hydraulic resistance $8\mu\ell/(\pi r^4)$ falls steeply with radius, and the power needed to drive the flow is $Q\,\Delta p=8\mu\ell Q^2/(\pi r^4)$. (Blood is not Newtonian and capillaries are hardly wider than red cells, but the scaling holds approximately in arterioles and larger vessels.)

**Theorem 29.5.1 (Murray's Law)** Suppose a vessel segment of length $\ell$ carrying flow $Q$ costs the power to drive the flow plus a metabolic cost proportional to the volume of blood it contains, $b\,\pi r^2\ell$, with $b$ the cost per unit volume per unit time. The total cost is minimized when

$$
Q=\sqrt{\frac{\pi^2b}{16\mu}}\;r^3,
\tag{29.3}
$$

so that the flow is proportional to the cube of the radius and the wall shear stress $4\mu Q/(\pi r^3)=\sqrt{\mu b}$ is the same in every vessel. At a branch point where flow $Q_0$ divides into $Q_1$ and $Q_2$, conservation of flow then gives

$$
r_0^3=r_1^3+r_2^3 .
\tag{29.4}
$$

**Proof** The cost per unit length is $C(r)=8\mu Q^2/(\pi r^4)+b\pi r^2$. Setting $C'(r)=-32\mu Q^2/(\pi r^5)+2b\pi r=0$ gives $Q^2=\pi^2br^6/(16\mu)$, which is (29.3); $C''>0$, so this is a minimum. Substituting into $\tau_{\mathrm{w}}$ gives $\sqrt{\mu b}$, independent of $r$. Since $Q_0=Q_1+Q_2$ and each $Q_j$ is proportional to $r_j^3$ with the same constant, (29.4) follows. $\square$

For a symmetric bifurcation the daughters have radius $2^{-1/3}r_0\approx0.79\,r_0$. Minimizing the cost of the junction also fixes the branching angles: if daughters of radii $r_1$ and $r_2$ make angles $\theta_1$ and $\theta_2$ with the parent, the optimum satisfies $\cos\theta_1=(r_0^4+r_1^4-r_2^4)/(2r_0^2r_1^2)$, which for a symmetric branch gives $\cos\theta=2^{-1/3}$, $\theta\approx37.5^\circ$, a total angle of about $75^\circ$; a small side branch leaves almost at right angles (Murray 1926; Zamir 1976). Measurements in arterial trees of many organs, and in plant xylem, roughly agree with the cube law; the large arteries deviate because flow there is pulsatile and turbulent.

The law and the developmental rule are two sides of one idea. A network that adjusts each vessel so that its shear stress returns to a fixed set point, as endothelial cells do, converges to the Murray solution without any global computation, since equal shear in every vessel is exactly the condition (29.3). Developmental remodelling is thus an algorithm that optimizes the network locally.

## 29.6. Blood Vessels in Disease

Because new vessels in the adult form by the same mechanisms, the developmental pathways are targets of therapy. Folkman (1971) proposed that tumours cannot grow beyond a millimetre or two without recruiting new vessels and that blocking angiogenesis would starve them. The anti-VEGF antibody bevacizumab and VEGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitors are used in several cancers, and anti-VEGF agents injected into the eye are the standard treatment of neovascular (wet) age-related macular degeneration and diabetic retinopathy, in which abnormal VEGF-driven vessels leak and bleed. In tumours the benefit of anti-angiogenic drugs is usually modest, partly because tumours switch to other angiogenic factors and partly because tumour vessels, already abnormal (as excess VEGF disrupts tip–stalk patterning, Formula 29.3.1), are partially "normalized" rather than removed.

The body has its own inhibitors. Endostatin, a fragment of collagen XVIII, and angiostatin, a fragment of plasminogen, released by proteolysis from the matrix and blood, inhibit endothelial proliferation and migration. The cornea, which must be transparent, is kept avascular by trapping VEGF in the matrix and by secreting soluble VEGFR1 (sFlt1); the manatee, whose cornea lacks sFlt1, is the only mammal known to have a vascularized cornea (Ambati et al. 2006). An excess of sFlt1 made by the placenta, which sequesters VEGF and placental growth factor from the maternal endothelium, is a central cause of preeclampsia, a disorder of pregnancy with hypertension and kidney damage (Maynard et al. 2003). In premature infants, the retina, whose vessels normally grow in late gestation in response to physiological hypoxia, stops vascularizing in the relatively hyperoxic air and then overgrows when hypoxia returns: retinopathy of prematurity.

## 29.7. The Waves of Hematopoiesis

### 29.7.1. Primitive and Definitive Hematopoiesis

Blood is needed long before the bone marrow exists, and the embryo makes it in successive, overlapping waves at different sites.

**Definition 29.7.1 (Primitive and Definitive Hematopoiesis)** **Primitive hematopoiesis** is the first, transient wave of blood production, in the yolk sac blood islands (or their equivalents), which makes large nucleated primitive erythrocytes expressing embryonic globins, together with macrophages and megakaryocytes, without passing through hematopoietic stem cells. **Definitive hematopoiesis** produces all adult-type blood lineages, including lymphocytes and enucleated erythrocytes expressing fetal and adult globins; its later wave arises from hematopoietic stem cells.

In the mouse the waves are:

1. Primitive (E7.25): primitive erythroid progenitors in the yolk sac blood islands; their erythrocytes circulate from E8.25, enucleate in the circulation between E12.5 and E16.5 and persist until shortly after birth.
2. Erythro-myeloid progenitors (EMPs, E8.25): made from hemogenic endothelium of the yolk sac, they seed the fetal liver and produce the first definitive erythrocytes, platelets and myeloid cells; they also give rise to tissue-resident macrophages, such as the microglia of the brain and many Kupffer cells of the liver, which persist into adulthood independently of later stem cells (Ginhoux et al. 2010; Gomez Perdiguero et al. 2015).
3. Lympho-myeloid progenitors (about E9.5) and the first transplantable hematopoietic stem cells (E10.5–11.5), born in the dorsal aorta and the umbilical and vitelline arteries and in the placenta.

The idea that the adult stem cells come from the embryo proper, not the yolk sac, came from chimeras. Dieterlen-Lièvre (1975) grafted the body of a quail embryo onto a chick yolk sac before circulation began; the blood cells of the adult chimera were of quail origin, not chick, showing that the yolk sac does not make the definitive stem cells in birds and that the intraembryonic region does. In the mouse, tissue explants from the aorta-gonad-mesonephros region of E10.5–11 embryos, cultured and transplanted into irradiated adults, repopulated all lineages for life, whereas yolk sac explants of the same age did not (Medvinsky and Dzierzak 1996).

**Definition 29.7.2 (AGM Region)** The **AGM region** (aorta–gonad–mesonephros) is the region of the midgestation embryo comprising the dorsal aorta and the surrounding mesenchyme, the genital ridges and the mesonephros, in which the first adult-type hematopoietic stem cells arise.

### 29.7.2. The Hemogenic Endothelium

Where in the AGM are stem cells made? Clusters of round hematopoietic cells attached to the floor of the dorsal aorta had been seen in many embryos since the early twentieth century. Live imaging settled their origin.

**Definition 29.7.3 (Hemogenic Endothelium, Endothelial-to-Hematopoietic Transition)** The **hemogenic endothelium** is a specialized population of endothelial cells, in the ventral wall of the dorsal aorta, the umbilical and vitelline arteries, the placenta and the yolk sac, that can give rise to hematopoietic cells. The **endothelial-to-hematopoietic transition** (EHT) is the process by which hemogenic endothelial cells lose their endothelial character and junctions, round up and leave the vessel wall as hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells, without cell division being required for the switch.

In thick slices of E10.5 mouse aorta imaged for hours, flat endothelial cells marked by a *Ly6a-GFP* (Sca-1) reporter were seen to bend, round up and bud into the lumen as hematopoietic cells (Boisset et al. 2010); in zebrafish, cells of the ventral wall of the aorta contract and bud out into the subaortic space (Bertrand et al. 2010; Kissa and Herbomel 2010). Lineage tracing with *VE-cadherin-Cre* labels most adult blood, confirming an endothelial origin (Zovein et al. 2008). The transition needs Runx1: in *Runx1* mutants hemogenic endothelium forms but no clusters bud from it, and deleting *Runx1* in VE-cadherin-expressing cells abolishes definitive hematopoiesis (Chen et al. 2009); Gata2, Notch1 (from arterial specification) and Gfi1 transcription factors are also required. The process depends on the circulation: embryos without a heartbeat (*Ncx1* mutant mice, *silent heart* zebrafish) make fewer stem cells, and shear stress and the nitric oxide it induces promote Runx1 expression (North et al. 2009; Adamo et al. 2009). The transition is a cell-state change akin to an **epithelial–mesenchymal transition**, with loss of tight junctions and reorganization of the cytoskeleton.

In humans, intra-aortic clusters appear at about days 27–40 (Tavian and Péault 2005). Making transplantable stem cells from pluripotent stem cells, by recapitulating these steps (arterial specification of endothelium, then EHT), remains difficult; the cells made in vitro usually resemble the yolk-sac waves (Ng et al. 2016; Sugimura et al. 2017 used transcription-factor programming to obtain engraftable cells).

### 29.7.3. Migration of the Stem Cells

The site of blood production changes during development. In mammals the stem cells born in the arteries enter the circulation and colonize the fetal liver (mouse from E11.5; human from about week 5–6), where they expand, about 40-fold in the mouse between E12 and E16, and produce the bulk of fetal blood; the liver niche provides SCF, thrombopoietin and IGF2, and fetal-liver stem cells divide rapidly, unlike adult ones. From about E17.5 in the mouse and week 10–11 in humans they colonize the bone marrow, attracted by CXCL12 through CXCR4, which becomes the main site after birth; the spleen is a transient site in the mouse. In zebrafish, primitive erythrocytes come from the intermediate cell mass (ICM) of the trunk; definitive stem cells, born from the aorta at about 30 hours, colonize the caudal hematopoietic tissue, then the thymus and finally the kidney marrow, the adult site.

The adult niche ([7.3.24](/7.3.24)) lies around the sinusoids of the bone marrow. Deleting *Kitl* (SCF) or *Cxcl12* from different cell types showed that the factors needed by stem cells come mainly from perivascular mesenchymal cells (Lepr- and CXCL12-expressing) and endothelial cells, not from osteoblasts as once thought (Ding et al. 2012; Ding and Morrison 2013). The niche also contains megakaryocytes, macrophages and sympathetic nerves, which regulate stem-cell quiescence and its daily rhythm of release.

## 29.8. Choosing a Lineage

The hematopoietic stem cell makes about a dozen mature cell types through intermediate progenitors whose potentials narrow step by step. The classical tree, defined by colony assays and transplantation of sorted cells, places the common myeloid and common lymphoid progenitors below multipotent progenitors. Each step is controlled by transcription factors whose loss removes particular branches and by cytokines that support survival and proliferation: erythropoietin (EPO, made mainly in the kidney in response to hypoxia) for erythrocytes, thrombopoietin for megakaryocytes, G-CSF and M-CSF for granulocytes and macrophages, IL-7 for lymphocytes. Whether cytokines also instruct the choice, rather than merely allowing it, was long debated; for G-CSF and M-CSF, single-cell imaging has shown instruction (Rieger et al. 2009). Single-cell transcriptomics and barcoding show that commitment is gradual, that many progenitors are already biased towards one lineage, and that megakaryocytes can arise almost directly from stem-cell-like cells (Notta et al. 2016; Rodriguez-Fraticelli et al. 2018). The development of T cells in the thymus, whose epithelium comes from the third pharyngeal pouch ([7.5.30](/7.5.30)), and of B cells is treated in [7.15](/7.15).

The choice between the erythroid–megakaryocytic and the myeloid branches is controlled by two antagonistic transcription factors. GATA1 activates erythroid genes and its own gene; PU.1 activates myeloid genes and its own gene; and each inhibits the other by direct protein binding. Progenitors co-express both at low levels (lineage priming), and forcing one in a committed cell of the other lineage can reprogram it (Kulessa et al. 1995).

**Formula 29.8.1 (A GATA1–PU.1 Switch)** Let $x$ and $y$ be the levels of GATA1 and PU.1. With self-activation of strength $a$, mutual repression of strength $b$, threshold $\theta$, cooperativity $n$ and decay rate $k$ (Huang et al. 2007),

$$
\dot x=\frac{a\,x^n}{\theta^n+x^n}+\frac{b\,\theta^n}{\theta^n+y^n}-kx,\qquad
\dot y=\frac{a\,y^n}{\theta^n+y^n}+\frac{b\,\theta^n}{\theta^n+x^n}-ky.
\tag{29.5}
$$

With $b=k=1$, $\theta=0.5$ and $n=4$: for $a=1$ there are five fixed points, three stable (GATA1-high, PU.1-high and a symmetric state with both at intermediate levels) and two saddles; for $a=0.5$ the central state has become a saddle and only the two committed states remain stable. The symmetric attractor represents the multipotent progenitor with low co-expression; a reduction of self-activation, as when a cytokine or a stochastic change of chromatin lowers it, destroys this attractor through a bifurcation, and the cell falls into one of the committed states. Which state it reaches depends on its position relative to the saddles, that is, on small initial biases, a picture of lineage choice as the deformation of a **Waddington landscape** ([7.3.24](/7.3.24), [7.5.10](/7.5.10)). The model shows how **bistability** with a primed state can arise from two mutually repressive, self-activating factors; it does not include the many other inputs (C/EBPα, Fli1, Klf1) that real progenitors integrate.

## 29.9. Globin Switching

Hemoglobin is a tetramer of two α-like and two β-like globin chains, encoded by two clusters: the α cluster on chromosome 16 (ζ, then α1 and α2) and the β cluster on chromosome 11 ($\varepsilon$, $^{\mathrm{G}}\gamma$, $^{\mathrm{A}}\gamma$, δ, β), each arranged in the order of developmental expression.

**Definition 29.9.1 (Globin Switching)** **Globin switching** is the developmentally regulated succession of globin genes expressed in erythroid cells: in humans from embryonic ζ and ε (Hb Gower, in yolk-sac-derived primitive erythrocytes) to fetal γ (HbF, $\alpha_2\gamma_2$, in the fetal liver) and then, around birth, to adult β (HbA, $\alpha_2\beta_2$, with a few per cent of HbA2, $\alpha_2\delta_2$).

Fetal hemoglobin binds 2,3-bisphosphoglycerate less tightly than adult hemoglobin, so it has a higher oxygen affinity and takes up oxygen from maternal blood in the placenta. The switches coincide with the changes of hematopoietic site but are not caused by them: they depend on developmental stage of the erythroid cells, and in a transgenic mouse the human β cluster switches on schedule. Each cluster is controlled by a distant enhancer, the locus control region (LCR), about 6–20 kb upstream of the ε gene, which loops to contact one promoter at a time and confers high-level expression ([7.2](/7.2)); competition among the promoters for the LCR, and gene order, favour the gene nearest to it in the embryo. The γ-to-β switch is driven above all by the zinc-finger repressor BCL11A, which binds the γ promoters and silences them in adult erythroid cells (Sankaran et al. 2008), and by LRF (ZBTB7A); variants in an erythroid-specific enhancer of *BCL11A* explain much of the inherited variation in adult HbF levels, and deletions or point mutations in the γ promoters that disrupt BCL11A or LRF binding cause hereditary persistence of fetal hemoglobin, a harmless condition.

Because γ chains can substitute for β, reactivating HbF ameliorates sickle cell disease and β-thalassaemia, which become symptomatic only as the switch to β completes in the first months after birth. Hydroxyurea raises HbF modestly, and in 2023 the first CRISPR-based therapy was approved: exagamglogene autotemcel (exa-cel), autologous hematopoietic stem cells in which the erythroid enhancer of *BCL11A* has been disrupted, which raises HbF to protective levels in most treated patients (Frangoul et al. 2021). It is a direct clinical application of developmental gene regulation.

## Summary

- Blood islands and hemangioblasts (Definition 29.1.1): bipotential cells exist transiently in the streak and posterior mesoderm; most island cells are committed early; shared Etv2–Flk1–Scl–Lmo2–Gata2 network; BMP and Wnt induce blood.
- Vasculogenesis (Definition 29.2.1): FGF2 → angioblasts; VEGF-A–VEGFR2 (haploinsufficient *Vegfa*); Ang1–Tie2 and PDGF-B–PDGFRβ recruit mural cells; dorsal aorta, cardinal veins, endocardium; some organs vascularize in situ.
- Angiogenesis (Definition 29.3.1): sprouting and intussusception; tip and stalk cells (Definition 29.3.2) chosen by VEGF→Dll4→Notch→less VEGFR2, more VEGFR1; Formula 29.3.1 gives a window of VEGF for patterning, uniform tips at very high VEGF; matrix-bound VEGF gradients; guidance (Plexin-D1, Unc5b, Robo4), anastomosis with macrophages, pruning by flow.
- Identity: ephrinB2 arteries, EphB4 veins; Shh→VEGF→Notch→*gridlock* (arterial) in zebrafish; COUP-TFII keeps veins venous; nerves induce arteries (VEGF from Schwann cells). Lumen by cord hollowing or vacuole fusion. Lymphatics: Prox1 in cardinal vein (Sox18, COUP-TFII), VEGF-C–VEGFR3, platelets separate them via podoplanin–CLEC-2.
- Flow: shear sensing (Piezo1, PECAM-1 complex, KLF2) and remodelling; Poiseuille (Formula 29.5.1) and Murray's law (Theorem 29.5.1): $Q\propto r^3$, $r_0^3=r_1^3+r_2^3$, equal shear $\sqrt{\mu b}$, symmetric half-angle 37.5°; local shear set point implements the global optimum.
- Disease: anti-VEGF therapy (tumours, macular degeneration), endostatin, angiostatin, corneal sFlt1, preeclampsia (placental sFlt1), retinopathy of prematurity.
- Hematopoiesis (Definition 29.7.1): primitive (yolk sac, E7.25), EMP (E8.25; tissue macrophages), definitive HSC (E10.5–11.5) in the AGM region (Definition 29.7.2), arteries and placenta; quail–chick and AGM transplants; hemogenic endothelium and EHT (Definition 29.7.3): live imaging, Runx1 required, flow and NO; yolk sac → fetal liver → bone marrow (CXCL12–CXCR4); perivascular niche (SCF, CXCL12).
- Lineages: HSC → MPP → CMP/CLP → MEP/GMP; TFs (Runx1, Scl, Gata2, Gata1, PU.1, C/EBPα, Ikaros, Pax5) and cytokines (EPO, TPO, G-CSF, M-CSF, IL-7); gradual commitment. GATA1–PU.1 model (Formula 29.8.1): tristable with a primed progenitor state at strong self-activation, bistable at weak.
- Globin switching (Definition 29.9.1): ζ, ε → γ (HbF) → β (HbA); LCR; BCL11A and LRF silence γ; HPFH; exa-cel disrupts the *BCL11A* erythroid enhancer for sickle cell disease and β-thalassaemia.

## Questions

1. Mouse embryos heterozygous for a null allele of *Vegfa* die at midgestation, whereas heterozygotes for most signalling genes are normal. What does this tell us about how VEGF-A acts, and how would you test whether its concentration or its spatial distribution matters?

   **Answer** VEGF-A acts in a dose-dependent way with little reserve: a 50 % reduction drops the signal below the threshold needed for endothelial survival, proliferation and assembly, so the dose is tightly matched to need (as expected for a factor whose level is also regulated by hypoxia). To test spatial distribution separately from dose, replace the endogenous gene with alleles that produce only one isoform: mice expressing only the diffusible VEGF120 have the normal amount of VEGF but no matrix-bound gradient, and they show fewer, wider vessels with reduced branching and misdirected tip cells, while mice expressing only the strongly matrix-bound VEGF188 show thin, excessively branched vessels (Ruhrberg et al. 2002). Concentration and gradient shape both matter.

2. In the model of Formula 29.3.1, explain qualitatively why the tip–stalk pattern disappears at very high VEGF and at very low VEGF. Predict the effect of an anti-VEGF antibody on a tumour whose vessels are in the high-VEGF regime.

   **Answer** Patterning needs the feedback loop (Dll4 in one cell → Notch in its neighbour → less Dll4 in that neighbour) to have a gain above one, which requires each cell's Dll4 output to be sensitive to its Notch input. At low $G$ the VEGF response $R$ is too small to induce Dll4 at all, so there is no signal to amplify. At very high $G$, $R$ remains in the saturated part of the Hill function even when Notch is high, so Dll4 is maximal in every cell and insensitive to Notch: the loop gain is near zero and all cells are tip-like. An anti-VEGF antibody that lowers $G$ from the very high regime into the window would restore alternating tip and stalk cells, giving fewer, more orderly and less leaky vessels: the "normalization" seen in tumours treated with moderate doses, with better perfusion and drug delivery for a time.

3. Design an experiment to determine whether arterial identity is fixed in the endothelial cell or can be changed by flow, and describe a result that would show plasticity.

   **Answer** In the chick yolk sac, label arterial endothelium (for example with an ephrinB2 reporter or by electroporation of a fluorescent marker into arterial cells) and then ligate a vitelline artery so that the downstream segment receives venous flow (or is perfused from the venous side); score arterial (ephrinB2, Dll4, Notch targets) and venous (EphB4, COUP-TFII) markers in the labelled cells over the next day. If labelled arterial cells downregulate ephrinB2 and express EphB4 and become part of veins, identity is plastic and controlled by flow (as le Noble et al. 2004 found). In the mouse, a complementary test is to delete *Nr2f2* in veins: venous cells then express arterial markers, showing that venous identity requires continuous repression of Notch.

4. Derive the optimal radius of a vessel carrying flow $Q$ under Murray's cost function, and show that the wall shear stress is independent of radius. If one daughter of a bifurcation carries twice the flow of the other, what are the daughter radii relative to a parent of radius 1?

   **Answer** $C(r)=8\mu Q^2/(\pi r^4)+b\pi r^2$, $C'(r)=0$ gives $r^6=16\mu Q^2/(\pi^2b)$, i.e. $Q=\sqrt{\pi^2b/(16\mu)}\,r^3$ (Theorem 29.5.1). Then $\tau_{\mathrm{w}}=4\mu Q/(\pi r^3)=4\mu\sqrt{\pi^2b/(16\mu)}/\pi=\sqrt{\mu b}$, independent of $r$. With $Q_1=2Q_2$ and $Q_0=3Q_2$: $r_1^3=\tfrac23r_0^3$, $r_2^3=\tfrac13r_0^3$, so $r_1=(2/3)^{1/3}=0.87$ and $r_2=(1/3)^{1/3}=0.69$; check $0.87^3+0.69^3=0.66+0.33=0.99\approx1$.

5. How would you show that adult hematopoietic stem cells derive from endothelium? What is the advantage of live imaging over lineage tracing for this question, and what does each fail to show?

   **Answer** Lineage tracing: activate a Cre reporter in endothelial cells only (for example *VE-cadherin-CreERT2* with tamoxifen given at E9.5–10.5, before stem cells exist) and show that a large fraction of adult blood cells, including transplantable stem cells, carry the label (Zovein et al. 2008). Its weakness is that the driver might also be expressed in a non-endothelial precursor, so labelled blood could come from cells that were never endothelial. Live imaging (Boisset et al. 2010 in mouse aortic slices, Bertrand et al. 2010 and Kissa and Herbomel 2010 in zebrafish) watches individual flat endothelial cells, integrated in the vessel wall, round up and leave as blood cells, which proves the transition directly; but it cannot show that those particular cells became long-term stem cells. Combining the two (imaging of a reporter-labelled cell followed by transplantation or single-cell tracing of its descendants) closes the gap.

6. In the GATA1–PU.1 model (Formula 29.8.1) with $a=b=k=1$, $\theta=0.5$, $n=4$, check that $(x,y)=(1,1)$ is a fixed point and that $(2,0)$ is approximately one. What would forced overexpression of PU.1 in a GATA1-high cell do?

   **Answer** At $(1,1)$: $x^4/(\theta^4+x^4)=1/(0.0625+1)=0.941$ and $\theta^4/(\theta^4+y^4)=0.059$, so $\dot x=0.941+0.059-1=0$; by symmetry $\dot y=0$. At $(2,0)$: self-activation $16/(16.0625)=0.996$, repression term $\theta^4/(\theta^4+0)=1$, so $\dot x=0.996+1-2\approx0$; $\dot y=0+1\cdot0.0625/(0.0625+16)-0=0.004\approx0$: a fixed point at about $(1.996,0.004)$. Forcing PU.1 high adds a large constant production to $\dot y$; once $y$ exceeds the separatrix (the stable manifold of the saddle near $(0.49,1.51)$), GATA1 is repressed, its self-activation collapses, and the cell moves to the PU.1-high attractor: a reprogramming of an erythroid progenitor to a myeloid fate, as observed experimentally.

7. Why does a newborn with sickle cell disease have no symptoms for several months? Explain the logic of a therapy that disrupts an enhancer of *BCL11A* rather than the *BCL11A* gene itself.

   **Answer** At birth most hemoglobin is still HbF, made from γ chains that are unaffected by the sickle mutation in β-globin; symptoms appear as the γ-to-β switch completes during the first year and HbS replaces HbF. BCL11A represses γ-globin in adult erythroid cells; removing it raises HbF. But BCL11A is also required in other lineages (B lymphocytes, the brain, hematopoietic stem cells themselves), so knocking out the gene could be harmful. The erythroid-specific enhancer is required for BCL11A expression only in erythroid cells; disrupting it in hematopoietic stem cells removes BCL11A from their red-cell progeny but leaves it in other lineages, a tissue-specific knockdown achieved by editing a regulatory element.

8. The vessels of the embryonic skin align with sensory nerves. Propose two experiments that distinguish between "nerves instruct arteries" and "arteries attract nerves".

   **Answer** (a) Remove the nerves: in mice lacking sensory neurons or Schwann cells in the skin (for example *Ngn1/Ngn2* mutants, or with nerves misrouted in *Sema3a* mutants), ask whether arteries still form and where. If arteries are missing where nerves are absent, and follow misrouted nerves, nerves instruct arterial differentiation (the result of Mukouyama et al. 2002). (b) Remove the instructive signal from nerves only: delete *Vegfa* in neurons and Schwann cells; if arteries fail to differentiate (no ephrinB2) although capillaries are present, VEGF from nerves is the signal. The converse test, removing arterial signals (artemin, NT-3) and scoring nerve routes, shows the reciprocal effect of vessels on sympathetic axons, so both directions operate at different times.

9. Estimate how much more power a vessel would dissipate if its radius were 20 % smaller than the Murray optimum, at the same flow, and what this implies for the stability of the optimum.

   **Answer** At the optimum the dissipation term is $1/3$ of the total cost and the volume term $2/3$ (from $C=D/r^4+Vr^2$ with $C'=0$: $4D/r^4=2Vr^2$). Scaling $r$ by 0.8: dissipation multiplies by $0.8^{-4}=2.44$, volume by $0.64$, so the total becomes $\tfrac13\times2.44+\tfrac23\times0.64=0.81+0.43=1.24$: 24 % above the minimum, while the power to drive the flow is 2.4 times larger. For a 20 % larger radius: $\tfrac13\times0.48+\tfrac23\times1.44=0.16+0.96=1.12$. The cost rises more steeply below the optimum than above it, so narrowing is penalized more than widening; with a shear set point, endothelial cells in a narrowed vessel feel $1/0.8^3\approx2$ times the shear and dilate, a strong restoring signal.

10. Why are tissue-resident macrophages such as microglia not replaced by adult hematopoietic stem cells, and how was this shown?

    **Answer** Microglia derive from yolk-sac progenitors (primitive macrophages and EMPs) that colonize the brain before the blood–brain barrier closes and before definitive stem cells exist, and they then maintain themselves by local proliferation throughout life, so adult bone-marrow-derived monocytes rarely enter the healthy brain. Fate mapping showed this: labelling cells expressing *Runx1* (with *Runx1-CreER*) at E7.0–7.5, before the definitive wave, labelled a large fraction of adult microglia but few blood cells (Ginhoux et al. 2010), and labelling of *Csf1r*- or *Kit*-expressing EMPs at E8.5 labelled microglia and many other tissue macrophages (Gomez Perdiguero et al. 2015); conversely, mice lacking the transcription factor *Myb*, required for definitive stem cells, still have microglia.
