Some embryos have to build a swimming or feeding larva from a few hundred cells within a day. They cannot afford long periods of signalling among large populations of equivalent cells, and they specify most of their cells early, by segregating **cytoplasmic determinants** into particular **blastomeres** during **cleavage** and by short-range signals between identified neighbours. These are the embryos in which the three modes of specification of [7.5.1](/7.5.1) were first distinguished, and in which they can still be studied cell by cell: the ascidian, where muscle is the classic case of **autonomous specification**; the spiralian embryos of snails and annelids, where the chirality of the whole body is decided by the tilt of spindles at the third cleavage; the nematode *Caenorhabditis elegans*, whose entire **cell lineage** is known; and the sea urchin, where the first gene regulatory network of an embryo was worked out. The chapter follows the cleavage patterns of [7.5.5](/7.5.5) to their consequences, and its models, a reaction-diffusion system for **cell polarity** and a Boolean network for a fate decision, introduce two tools used throughout the course. The genetics of the maternal-effect mutants that appear here is in [7.4.20](/7.4.20); **gastrulation** of these embryos is described in [7.5.9](/7.5.9).

## 6.1. Modes of Specification and How They Are Tested

Three modes were defined in [7.5.1](/7.5.1): **autonomous specification**, in which a cell inherits determinants and takes its fate whatever its neighbours; **conditional specification**, in which the fate depends on signals from other cells; and **syncytial specification**, in which nuclei in a common cytoplasm read gradients of transcription factors. Every embryo uses all three, in different tissues and at different stages. The modes are not observed directly but inferred from four kinds of experiment, which this chapter uses repeatedly.

| Experiment | Question | Result indicating autonomy | Result indicating conditional specification |
|---|---|---|---|
| Isolation: culture a blastomere alone | is the information needed for the fate inside the cell? | the cell forms what it forms in the embryo | it forms something else, or less |
| Removal: delete a blastomere | do other cells compensate? | the structures it would have formed are missing (mosaic defect) | the embryo regulates and is normal |
| Transplantation or recombination: move a cell next to new neighbours | do neighbours change the fate? | the fate is unchanged | the fate follows the new position |
| Transfer of cytoplasm, or of a candidate mRNA | is a localized component sufficient? | the recipient adopts the donor's fate | — |

Isolation tests sufficiency of what the cell contains, removal tests necessity of the cell for the whole; molecular versions of the same logic are loss of function (is the determinant necessary?) and ectopic expression (is it sufficient?).

## 6.2. Tunicates: The Yellow Crescent and macho-1

Ascidians are tunicates, the sister group of the vertebrates; their tadpole larva has a dorsal nerve cord, a notochord of exactly 40 cells and about 36 muscle cells on each side of the tail. Their cleavage is bilateral: the first furrow divides the egg into its future left and right halves, and the two sides remain mirror images, so that each blastomere has a named partner on the other side. At the 8-cell stage there are four pairs, two animal (a4.2 and b4.2) and two vegetal (A4.1 and B4.1), anterior pairs named with A, posterior with B.

In 1905 Conklin followed a yellow-pigmented cytoplasm, the yellow crescent, in the eggs of *Styela partita*. After **fertilization** it moves to the posterior-vegetal region, is segregated into the B4.1 pair at the 8-cell stage, and ends up in the cells that form the tail muscle. Isolated B4.1 blastomeres form muscle; transferring posterior-vegetal cytoplasm into other blastomeres causes them to form muscle as well. The determinant is the mRNA of *macho-1*, a zinc-finger transcription factor localized to the posterior-vegetal cortex: depleting it with antisense oligonucleotides abolishes primary muscle formation in B4.1 descendants, and injecting it into animal blastomeres causes them to form muscle (figure below). Macho-1 activates the T-box gene *Tbx6*, which activates muscle structural genes. Muscle is thus specified autonomously, by a localized maternal mRNA.

Not every ascidian tissue is specified this way. The notochord and the mesenchyme arise from A4.1 and B4.1 descendants only if they receive FGF from neighbouring **endoderm** cells at the 32-cell stage, and the secondary muscle cells near the tail tip depend on signals too. Even this paradigm of **mosaic development** is a mixture; the term "mosaic" describes the dominant mode of a tissue, not of the embryo.

## 6.3. Spiralians: Spiral Cleavage and Shell Coiling

### 6.3.1. Spiral Cleavage

**Definition 6.3.1 (Spiral Cleavage)** **Spiral cleavage** is a holoblastic cleavage in which, from the third division on, the spindles are tilted relative to the animal–vegetal axis, so that each tier of daughter cells lies over the furrows between the cells of the tier below and is displaced clockwise or anticlockwise; successive divisions alternate in the direction of tilt.

It is characteristic of the Spiralia: molluscs, annelids, nemerteans and polyclad flatworms. The first two divisions are meridional and produce four macromeres, A, B, C and D, D usually being the largest. At the third division each macromere buds off a small micromere towards the **animal pole**: the first quartet, 1a–1d. In a dextral embryo the micromeres are displaced clockwise when viewed from the animal pole, in a sinistral embryo anticlockwise. The macromeres then produce the second and third quartets (2a–2d, 3a–3d) with alternating tilt, and the fourth division of the D macromere gives 4d, the mesentoblast, which forms most of the **mesoderm** and part of the endoderm. The lineage is highly conserved across the Spiralia: the same named cells give rise to the same structures in snails and in annelids, a fact used to argue that the groups are related. The **blastula** usually has no **blastocoel** (a stereoblastula), and in many species the embryo develops into a ciliated trochophore larva. Cephalopods are the exception among molluscs: their large yolky eggs cleave discoidally ([7.5.5](/7.5.5)).

### 6.3.2. The Polar Lobe and the D Quadrant

Some spiralian eggs, such as those of the mud snail *Ilyanassa obsoleta*, extrude a bulge of cytoplasm without a nucleus just before the first division.

**Definition 6.3.2 (Polar Lobe)** A **polar lobe** is a protrusion of vegetal cytoplasm, free of nucleus, that a spiralian egg forms at the first and second cleavages and that is resorbed into only one of the daughter cells, so that its contents are inherited by the CD and then the D blastomere.

Crampton showed in 1896 that removing the polar lobe at the trefoil stage (when the egg looks like three lobes) produces a larva lacking the structures derived from the D quadrant: mesoderm (muscle, heart, intestine), shell gland, eyes, foot and velum. The lobe contains determinants, but not only for D-derived cells: removing the D blastomere at the 4-cell stage removes also the structures that other quadrants would make under its influence, such as the eyes. The D quadrant is therefore an organizer, and its descendants signal to others through the MAP kinase (ERK) pathway; in species without a polar lobe, such as many snails with equal cleavage, D is chosen among the four equivalent macromeres by contact with the animal micromeres at about the 24-cell stage, a conditional decision. Two embryos with the same lineage thus specify the same organizer, one autonomously and one conditionally.

### 6.3.3. Shell Coiling as a Maternal Effect

The coiling of snail shells is, with few exceptions, the same within a species, and the mirror-image direction is rare. In the pond snail *Lymnaea stagnalis* both forms occur. Crosses by Boycott and Diver in the 1920s, interpreted by Sturtevant, showed that coiling is controlled by one gene with the dextral allele $D$ dominant, but that the phenotype of each snail is determined by its mother's genotype, not its own: a **maternal effect** (**maternal-effect gene**, [7.4.20](/7.4.20)). The figure below shows the consequence: the phenotype of a cross appears one generation late. The direction of coiling of the adult follows from the direction of the third cleavage. When the third-cleavage blastomeres of dextral embryos were pushed mechanically into the sinistral arrangement at the 4- to 8-cell transition, normal sinistral snails developed, and vice versa: the chirality of the whole body is decided by the arrangement of eight cells.

The gene was identified by positional cloning in 2016: it encodes a **formin**, *Lsdia1*, an actin-assembling protein; the sinistral allele carries a frameshift, and the maternal mRNA is absent from sinistral eggs. Knocking out *Lsdia1* with **CRISPR–Cas9** in dextral snails produced sinistral offspring over generations, confirming the identification. The formin acts on the actin cytoskeleton in the cortex of the dividing blastomeres and gives them a helical (chiral) twist before the spindle tilts; inhibitors of formins abolish the twist. The chirality of the cytoskeleton is thus transferred to the arrangement of cells and from there to the left–right asymmetry of the snail: on the right side of a dextral embryo, and the left of a sinistral one, the signalling protein Nodal is expressed, as on the left of vertebrates ([7.5.17](/7.5.17)). In humans, mutations in the homologous formin *DIAPH1* cause a form of deafness, a reminder that the same protein serves very different ends.

## 6.4. *Caenorhabditis elegans*

### 6.4.1. An Invariant Lineage

The nematode *C. elegans* develops with an invariant **cell lineage**: every individual makes the same divisions at the same times, and every cell can be named. Sulston and his colleagues traced the whole lineage under the microscope by 1983. The embryonic lineage makes 671 cells, of which 113 die by **programmed cell death**, the first evidence that **apoptosis** is part of normal development ([7.3.25](/7.3.25)); the hatched larva has 558 cells, and the adult hermaphrodite 959 somatic nuclei.

The early lineage consists of asymmetric divisions of a stem-like P lineage, each producing one somatic founder cell and one P cell (figure below). The zygote P0 divides into a large anterior AB and a small posterior P1; P1 divides into EMS and P2; EMS into MS and E; P2 into C and P3; P3 into D and P4, which forms the germ line. The founders differ in their division rhythms and fates: E forms the whole intestine (20 cells), D only body-wall muscle, AB mostly neurons and hypodermis. The cleavage is rotational ([7.5.5](/7.5.5)): AB divides perpendicularly to the anterior–posterior axis, P1 along it. Fertilization occurs in the spermatheca of the hermaphrodite and cleavage starts in the uterus; gastrulation begins at the 26- to 28-cell stage, when the two daughters of E move into the interior by apical constriction ([7.5.9](/7.5.9)).

Each division of the P lineage is asymmetric and produces one somatic founder cell (AB, then MS and E from EMS, C, D) and one P cell; P4 forms the germ line. Bars beneath the founders show their main products in germ-layer colours (ectoderm blue, mesoderm red, endoderm yellow, germ line purple, mixed grey); numbers are the cells each founder contributes to the hatched larva, out of 558](./plots/elegans-lineage.py)

### 6.4.2. PAR Polarity

The egg has no detectable polarity before fertilization. The sperm brings a **centrosome**, which at first lies near the cortex at the point of sperm entry and defines the future posterior. It triggers local relaxation of the actomyosin cortex there; the contractile cortex flows away from it towards the anterior, carrying with it the anterior **PAR proteins** PAR-3, PAR-6 and the kinase PKC-3 (aPKC) ([7.3.15](/7.3.15)). The posterior **PAR proteins** PAR-1 and PAR-2 bind the cortex that is left. The two groups exclude each other: aPKC phosphorylates PAR-1 and PAR-2 and removes them from the membrane, while PAR-1 phosphorylates PAR-3 and PAR-2 protects PAR-1 from aPKC. Once established, by about 15 min of cortical flow, the two domains are maintained without flow until division, with the boundary near the middle of the embryo. PAR mutants were found in screens for embryos with symmetric first divisions (hence partitioning-defective).

The maintenance phase has a simple quantitative model, built on measured parameters.

**Formula 6.4.1 (Mass-Conserved Reaction–Diffusion Model of PAR Polarity)** Let $A(x, t)$ and $P(x, t)$ be the membrane concentrations of anterior and posterior PAR proteins along the cortex $0 \le x \le L$ (from the anterior to the posterior pole, $L \approx 67\,\mathrm{\mu m}$ being half the circumference). Each binds the membrane from a well-mixed cytoplasmic pool, diffuses in the membrane, dissociates, and is displaced by the other:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\partial_t A &= D_A\,\partial_x^2 A + k_{\mathrm{on}}^A A_{\mathrm{cyt}} - k_{\mathrm{off}}^A A - k_{AP}\,P^{\alpha}A,\\
\partial_t P &= D_P\,\partial_x^2 P + k_{\mathrm{on}}^P P_{\mathrm{cyt}} - k_{\mathrm{off}}^P P - k_{PA}\,A^{\beta}P,
\end{aligned}
\tag{6.1}
$$
with no-flux conditions at both poles and the cytoplasmic concentrations fixed by conservation of the total amount,

$$
A_{\mathrm{cyt}} = \rho_A - \psi\,\langle A\rangle, \qquad P_{\mathrm{cyt}} = \rho_P - \psi\,\langle P\rangle,
\tag{6.2}
$$
where $\langle\cdot\rangle$ is the mean over the membrane, $\rho_A$, $\rho_P$ the total concentrations and $\psi$ the ratio of surface to volume.

The measured values are $D_A = 0.28$ and $D_P = 0.15\,\mathrm{\mu m^2\,s^{-1}}$, $k_{\mathrm{off}}^A = 5.4\times10^{-3}$ and $k_{\mathrm{off}}^P = 7.3\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$, membrane binding $k_{\mathrm{on}}^A = 8.6\times10^{-3}$ and $k_{\mathrm{on}}^P = 4.7\times10^{-2}\,\mathrm{\mu m\,s^{-1}}$, $\psi = 0.174\,\mathrm{\mu m^{-1}}$, with $\alpha = 1$, $\beta = 2$ and antagonism constants $k_{AP} = 0.19\,\mathrm{\mu m^2\,s^{-1}}$, $k_{PA} = 2\,\mathrm{\mu m^4\,s^{-1}}$. Three features of the model explain what the embryo does.

- Bistability: with these parameters each point of the membrane, given a fixed cytoplasm, has two stable states, A-rich and P-rich, because each species, once it dominates, removes the other. This is the same logic as a toggle switch (**bistability**, [7.3.18](/7.3.18)), extended in space by diffusion.
- Fronts and their position: between an A domain and a P domain there is a front a few micrometres wide (set by $\sqrt{D/k_{\mathrm{off}}}$, about $7\,\mathrm{\mu m}$). In an infinite system a front would travel, one state invading the other; here conservation stops it. If the P domain grows, it depletes $P_{\mathrm{cyt}}$, which weakens P binding, while $A_{\mathrm{cyt}}$ rises; the front stalls where the two invasion tendencies balance. This "wave pinning" fixes the boundary near the middle of the embryo for a wide range of initial cues.
- A threshold: an initial P domain that is too small is erased, because diffusion and A binding remove it faster than it can grow; a cue above a threshold establishes stable polarity.

**Example 6.4.1** Simulating (6.1)–(6.2) on 120 grid points (figure below) from a P domain covering 40 % of the cortex gives, after 50 min, an A domain of about $1.9\,\mathrm{\mu m^{-2}}$ in the anterior half and a P domain of about $4.2\,\mathrm{\mu m^{-2}}$ in the posterior half, separated by a front at about 35 µm. Starting from domains of 10 % to 60 %, the final P domain covers 45–52 % of the cortex; starting from 3–6 %, polarity is lost. The cortical flow triggered by the centrosome is thus needed only to supply a cue above threshold; the PAR network then decides where the boundary lies.

The model also explains why polarity is robust to the amount of PAR proteins but sensitive to their ratio: changing $\rho_A$ or $\rho_P$ moves the boundary, as seen in embryos with reduced or increased dosage of PAR-2. Its limitations are known: it neglects the cortical flow itself, which also advects proteins during maintenance, and the oligomerization and clustering of PAR-3, which slow its diffusion; more complete models include both.

A: membrane concentrations of anterior (red) and posterior (blue) PAR proteins after 50 min, from a posterior cue covering 40 % of the cortex. ### 6.4.3. Asymmetric Division

The PAR domains make the first division asymmetric in three ways (**asymmetric cell division**, [7.3.21](/7.3.21)).

1. Unequal size. The posterior cortex, under PAR-2 and PAR-1, has more of the G-protein regulators GPR-1/2 and LIN-5, which anchor **dynein**; the posterior aster is pulled harder, the spindle is displaced posteriorly, and the furrow forms off-centre: AB is about 60 % of the volume.
2. Cytoplasmic gradients. PAR-1 phosphorylates the RNA-binding proteins MEX-5 and MEX-6 in the posterior, increasing their mobility there; they accumulate anteriorly, and in turn exclude from the anterior the germline proteins PIE-1 and POS-1 and the P granules.
3. Segregation of germ-line components. P granules are liquid-like condensates of RNA and protein that dissolve in the anterior and condense in the posterior cytoplasm, a gradient of phase separation set by MEX-5; at each division they go to the P cell, ending in P4. PIE-1, also inherited by the P cells, represses transcription there and keeps the germ line silent while the somatic founders activate their genes; in *pie-1* mutants P2 adopts an EMS-like fate.

The founders' fates are then set by maternal transcription factors partitioned in this way: SKN-1, a bZIP factor, in EMS (in *skn-1* mutants EMS forms no pharynx or intestine), PAL-1, a Caudal homologue, in C and D.

### 6.4.4. Cell Interactions at the Four-Cell Stage

Lineage invariance does not mean that the cells do not talk. At the four-cell stage the embryo has ABa (anterior), ABp (dorsal), EMS (ventral) and P2 (posterior). ABa and ABp are born equivalent: if their positions are swapped by pushing them with a needle, each forms what the other would have formed. The difference arises because P2 touches ABp but not ABa. P2 expresses APX-1, a Delta-like ligand, which activates GLP-1, a Notch-like receptor present on both AB cells (**Notch signaling pathway**, [7.3.19](/7.3.19)); only ABp receives the signal. In *glp-1* mutants ABp adopts the ABa fate. The same contact sets the dorsal–ventral axis: ABp is dorsal, EMS ventral.

P2 also signals to EMS. Isolated EMS cells, removed before the contact has acted, divide into two MS-like cells and make no gut; recombined with P2 on either side, they make gut on the side touching P2 (figure below). The signal is MOM-2, a Wnt protein, received by MOM-5, a Frizzled receptor (**Wnt signaling pathway**, [7.3.19](/7.3.19)), together with a parallel Src kinase pathway. Through a variant of canonical Wnt signalling, the Wnt/**β-catenin** asymmetry pathway, it lowers the nuclear level of the TCF factor POP-1 and raises that of the β-catenin SYS-1 in the daughter next to P2: that daughter becomes E and forms endoderm, the other, with high POP-1, becomes MS. The same asymmetry pathway, with POP-1 high in anterior and low in posterior daughters, is used in many later divisions of the worm: a polarized division is an elementary unit of conditional specification.

Left–right asymmetry appears at the next division: as the AB cells divide, their spindles skew so that the left daughters (ABal, ABpl) lie slightly anterior to the right ones. Reversing this skew by **micromanipulation** produces worms with mirror-image anatomy that are otherwise normal, and mutations in the maternal G-protein subunit gene *gpa-16* randomize it. As in snails, the handedness of the body begins with the chirality of cell divisions, and here too it is a property of the actomyosin cortex, which rotates chirally during division.

### 6.4.5. Heterochronic Genes and MicroRNAs

The lineage is invariant in time as well as space: each larval stage (L1 to L4) has its characteristic divisions, such as the seam cells of the hypodermis dividing asymmetrically at each larval stage and then, in the adult, fusing and making a cuticular ridge. Mutations that change the timing of these programmes without changing their content were called heterochronic.

**Definition 6.4.1 (Heterochronic Gene)** A **heterochronic gene** is one whose mutation shifts the time at which a developmental programme is executed relative to other programmes, so that a stage-specific pattern is repeated (retarded mutants) or skipped (precocious mutants).

The first two heterochronic genes, *lin-4* and *lin-14*, act as a switch. *lin-14* encodes a nuclear protein abundant in L1 that specifies L1 fates; loss of function makes the L1 programme be skipped (precocious development), while gain-of-function alleles that keep LIN-14 high repeat the L1 programme. *lin-4* loss of function also repeats the L1 programme: *lin-4* is a negative regulator of *lin-14*. In 1993 the laboratories of Victor Ambros and Gary Ruvkun showed that *lin-4* encodes no protein but a 22-nucleotide RNA complementary to sequences in the 3′ untranslated region of *lin-14* mRNA, and the gain-of-function *lin-14* alleles turned out to be deletions of exactly those sites. As *lin-4* RNA accumulates at the end of L1, it represses translation of LIN-14, and the worm moves to L2 programmes. This was the first **microRNA** ([7.2](/7.2)). A second, *let-7*, found in 2000 to control the transition from L4 to adult, is conserved from worms to humans, which showed that **microRNAs** are a general class of regulator; LIN-28, an RNA-binding protein downstream of *lin-4* that blocks *let-7* maturation, also controls developmental timing and stem-cell states in mammals. Ambros and Ruvkun received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 2024. The heterochronic pathway is a cascade of temporal switches, each made of a microRNA that rises and a target that falls, and provides a molecular mechanism for **heterochrony**, a source of evolutionary change ([7.5.39](/7.5.39)).

## 6.5. Sea Urchins

### 6.5.1. Fate Map and Regulation

The tiers of the 60-cell sea urchin embryo ([7.5.5](/7.5.5)) have well-defined fates. The animal tiers $\mathrm{an}_1$ and $\mathrm{an}_2$ form **ectoderm**, the epidermis of the larva and its ciliary band; $\mathrm{veg}_1$ forms ectoderm and some endoderm; $\mathrm{veg}_2$ forms endoderm (the gut) and the non-skeletogenic or secondary mesenchyme (pigment cells, blastocoelar cells, muscle, coelomic pouches); the large micromeres form the skeletogenic mesenchyme, and the small micromeres contribute to the coelomic pouches and the germ line. The larva is the pluteus, whose calcite skeleton gives it its shape.

**Definition 6.5.1 (Skeletogenic Mesenchyme)** The **skeletogenic mesenchyme** (primary mesenchyme) of the sea urchin consists of the descendants of the four large micromeres, which ingress into the blastocoel at the start of gastrulation and secrete the calcite spicules of the larval skeleton.

The **fate map** is precise, but cell fates are not fixed. Driesch's isolated blastomeres of the 2- and 4-cell stages formed small but complete larvae, the founding observation of **regulative development** ([7.5.1](/7.5.1)). Hörstadius in the 1930s separated the tiers of the 60-cell embryo and recombined them: an isolated animal half forms a ciliated ball of ectoderm without gut or skeleton (an animalized embryo); an animal half combined with micromeres forms a nearly normal larva, the micromeres inducing the animal cells to form gut. Micromeres transplanted to the animal pole of a whole embryo induce a second **archenteron** there. The micromeres are an organizer, specified autonomously themselves and specifying their neighbours conditionally. Removing the micromeres is compensated: some $\mathrm{veg}_2$ cells, released from a signal that normally suppresses the skeletogenic fate in them, switch fate and form a skeleton, with a delay.

### 6.5.2. β-Catenin and the Double-Negative Gate

The autonomous property of the micromeres is nuclear **β-catenin**. A maternal Dishevelled protein localized in the vegetal cortex stabilizes β-catenin in the vegetal cells, and from the 16-cell stage β-catenin enters the nuclei of the micromeres, later of the $\mathrm{veg}_2$ and $\mathrm{veg}_1$ cells. The experiments show necessity and sufficiency. Lithium, which inhibits **glycogen synthase kinase 3** and so stabilizes β-catenin everywhere, vegetalizes the embryo: animal cells form mesoderm and endoderm. Overexpressing **cadherin**, which binds β-catenin at the membrane and keeps it out of the nucleus, animalizes it: the embryo becomes a ball of ciliated ectoderm. Micromeres in which β-catenin is blocked lose their inducing activity.

In the micromeres β-catenin, together with the **maternal factor** Otx, activates *pmar1*, a **homeodomain** repressor; Pmar1 represses *hesC*, a repressor expressed in all other cells; and HesC represses the regulatory genes of the skeletogenic programme (*alx1*, *ets1*, *tbr*) and the gene for the signal Delta. Two repressions in series act as an activation restricted to the cells where the first repressor is expressed.

**Definition 6.5.2 (Double-Negative Gate)** A **double-negative gate** is a gene regulatory circuit in which a locally expressed repressor (here Pmar1) represses a globally expressed repressor (HesC) of a set of target genes, so that the targets are activated exactly where the first repressor is present, and repressed everywhere else.

The logic is shown as a Boolean network (**Boolean network**, [7.5.10](/7.5.10)) in the figure below: with $\mathrm{hesC} = \lnot\mathrm{pmar1} \land \lnot\mathrm{KD}$ and every target $= \lnot\mathrm{hesC}$, where KD indicates a knockdown of *hesC*, the targets are on only in micromeres; injecting *pmar1* mRNA into all cells, or blocking *hesC* translation with a **morpholino**, turns every cell skeletogenic, which is what is observed. The gate gives an all-or-none decision that does not depend on the level of the localized input once it exceeds a threshold, and it provides spatial control through a global repressor, which ensures that the skeletogenic genes, whose activators (such as Ets1) are present in many cells, are silent outside the micromeres.

Delta, expressed by the micromeres under the same gate, activates Notch in the adjacent $\mathrm{veg}_2$ cells, which become secondary mesenchyme through the transcription factor Gcm, while the more distant $\mathrm{veg}_2$ cells, with Wnt8 signals and nuclear β-catenin, become endoderm. The skeletogenic network downstream of the gate, with dozens of genes from *alx1* to the spicule matrix proteins, was the first gene regulatory network of an embryo to be mapped in detail, by Eric Davidson's laboratory, and remains the model for how networks of transcription factors are wired. It also explains the regulation described above: the $\mathrm{veg}_2$ cells that make a skeleton after micromere removal are those that would have received the micromere signal, and they activate *alx1* through an alternative route when it is absent.

### 6.5.3. The Oral–Aboral Axis

The second axis of the larva, from the oral (ventral) side bearing the mouth to the aboral (dorsal) side, is not fixed in the egg. In some species it is related to the plane of the first cleavage, but the relation varies, and isolated blastomeres of the 2-cell stage can form complete larvae with both sides. Its earliest known determinant is the expression of *nodal* on the future oral side by the 60- to 128-cell stage, activated by maternal Univin (a Vg1/GDF homologue) and by a redox gradient that may reflect an asymmetric distribution of mitochondria, and antagonized on the aboral side by maternal Panda. Nodal activates its own expression and that of its antagonist Lefty, which diffuses further and confines Nodal to the oral territory, an activator–inhibitor pair of the kind analysed in [7.5.12](/7.5.12). Nodal also induces BMP2/4 and Chordin on the oral side; Chordin blocks BMP signalling there, while BMP2/4 diffuses to the aboral side and specifies aboral ectoderm. Between the two, where both Nodal and BMP signalling are low, lies the ciliary band, which contains neurons. The axis is thus generated by a signalling centre with the same molecules, Nodal, Lefty, BMP and Chordin, that pattern the vertebrate embryo ([7.5.16](/7.5.16)); misexpression of *nodal* in one blastomere makes the oral side form around it, and a *nodal* knockdown gives embryos without oral or aboral ectoderm, radialized with ciliary band all round.

| Spiralians | autonomous (polar lobe) or conditional (D chosen by contact) for the D-quadrant organizer | spiral cleavage (Definition 6.3.1); quartets and 4d; polar lobe (Definition 6.3.2); shell coiling a **maternal effect**, set by the chirality of the third cleavage through the formin *Lsdia1* |

Common themes: localized maternal determinants are mRNAs or proteins tied to the cortex; the chirality of the body starts in the cortex of dividing cells; signals between identified neighbours specify fates even in "mosaic" embryos; and circuits of repressors and switches turn graded inputs into all-or-none fates.

## Questions

1. In an ascidian, isolated B4.1 blastomeres form muscle, but isolated A4.1 blastomeres do not form notochord. What does each result show, and which experiment would show that the notochord requires a signal from endoderm rather than from any neighbour?

   **Answer** B4.1 contains what it needs to form muscle: **autonomous specification** by an inherited determinant (macho-1 mRNA). A4.1 cannot form notochord alone, so notochord formation depends on interactions, conditional specification. To identify the source, recombine isolated notochord precursors at the 32-cell stage with endoderm precursors, or with ectoderm precursors, and score notochord markers; replace the endoderm by beads soaked in FGF, and block FGF signalling (MEK inhibitor or dominant-negative FGF receptor) in intact embryos. The result is that endoderm or FGF suffices and FGF signalling is necessary.

2. A dextral *Lymnaea* snail whose mother was dd is self-fertilized. What coiling will its offspring have, and what can you conclude about its own genotype?

   **Answer** Its own phenotype is dextral although its mother was dd. That is impossible: a dd mother produces only sinistral offspring. Either the record is wrong or, since coiling of an individual reflects its mother's genotype, the snail's mother must have carried $D$. Assuming the mother was in fact Dd, the snail could be Dd or dd; its offspring will show its genotype: all dextral if it carries $D$, all sinistral if it is dd. The general rule: the coiling of a snail's offspring reveals the snail's own genotype.

3. Using Formula 6.4.1, explain why the PAR boundary settles near the middle of the zygote for a wide range of initial cue sizes. How would you expect the boundary to move if the dose of PAR-2 were halved?

   **Answer** Each point of the membrane is bistable, and a front between the two states moves towards the state that is weaker locally. Because the cytoplasmic pools are finite (6.2), a growing P domain depletes $P_{\mathrm{cyt}}$ and enriches $A_{\mathrm{cyt}}$ (fewer A molecules are excluded), weakening the invasion by P; the opposite holds if the A domain grows. The front stops where the two balance, which for the measured totals is near the middle. Halving PAR-2 lowers $\rho_P$, so the balance is reached with a smaller P domain: the boundary moves posteriorly, and below a critical dose polarity fails altogether. Both predictions agree with measurements in embryos with altered PAR-2 levels.

4. Estimate the width of the PAR boundary from the parameters in 6.4.2 and compare it with the size of the embryo.

   **Answer** Within a domain a protein that strays across the front survives for about $1/k_{\mathrm{off}}$ (plus removal by the antagonist, which shortens it) and diffuses a distance $\sqrt{D/k_{\mathrm{off}}}$: for PAR-2, $\sqrt{0.15/0.0073} \approx 4.5\,\mathrm{\mu m}$, for PAR-6 $\sqrt{0.28/0.0054} \approx 7\,\mathrm{\mu m}$. The front is therefore a few micrometres to about 10 µm wide, small compared with the 50 µm length of the embryo, which is why two well-defined domains can coexist; in an embryo much smaller than $\sqrt{D/k}$ diffusion would erase the pattern.

5. An investigator finds that EMS cells isolated at the 4-cell stage and cultured alone sometimes make gut. Give two explanations, and an experiment to distinguish them.

   **Answer** (a) The EMS cell had already received the P2 signal before isolation: the signal acts during a short window in the 4-cell stage, and late isolation includes it. (b) Gut formation has a partly autonomous component, which in the intact embryo is reinforced by the signal. Isolate EMS at different times after its birth: if (a), the frequency of gut formation increases with the time EMS spent next to P2 and is zero when it is isolated immediately; if (b), it is independent of that time. A genetic test: isolate EMS from *mom-2* mutant embryos, which lack the signal; gut formation in those cells would support (b).

6. Explain why ABa and ABp are said to be "born equivalent", and what result of swapping them would have shown the opposite.

   **Answer** They are sisters from an equal division, and when their positions are exchanged each takes the fate appropriate to its new position, ABa becoming ABp-like next to P2 and vice versa. If fates had been determined at birth by inherited determinants, swapped cells would have kept their original fates in the new positions, producing an abnormal embryo with ABp-derived structures in anterior positions.

7. Write the truth table of the double-negative gate (Definition 6.5.2) with inputs pmar1 and a hesC knockdown, and explain why a single global repressor is a convenient way to restrict a programme whose activators are present in many cells.

   **Answer** With $h = \lnot p \land \lnot k$ and target $t = \lnot h$: $(p, k) = (0, 0)$: $h = 1$, $t = 0$; $(1, 0)$: $h = 0$, $t = 1$; $(0, 1)$: $h = 0$, $t = 1$; $(1, 1)$: $h = 0$, $t = 1$. So $t = p \lor k$. The targets need activators that are present in many cells (such as Ets1 and β-catenin-dependent factors); restricting each target separately would require a local activator for every one of them. A repressor that is present everywhere silences them all at once, and a single local input (pmar1) releases all of them together in the right cells: one switch controls a whole module.

8. A morpholino against *nodal* is injected into one blastomere of a sea urchin 2-cell embryo. What larva do you expect? What if *nodal* mRNA is injected into one blastomere at the 8-cell stage of a nodal-knockdown embryo?

   **Answer** The uninjected half still expresses Nodal, which induces its own expression and that of Lefty and BMP2/4 nearby; the oral side will form within the uninjected half, and the larva may be normal or slightly asymmetric, because Nodal signals across the embryo and BMP2/4 diffuses to the opposite side. If *nodal* mRNA is injected into one blastomere of an embryo otherwise lacking Nodal, the oral territory forms around the descendants of that blastomere, and the opposite side becomes aboral: the injected clone acts as a new oral organizer, showing that Nodal is sufficient to orient the axis.

9. (Open question) The four embryos of this chapter specify cells rapidly, yet each also uses conditional specification. Discuss what selective pressures might favour autonomous specification, and why no embryo is purely mosaic.

   **Answer** Autonomous specification is fast and needs few cells: an embryo that must swim or feed within a day, from a few hundred cells, cannot wait for signals to spread through large populations, and it can put each fate exactly where cleavage places the determinant. Its cost is that it cannot compensate for lost or misplaced cells, and it requires an egg in which determinants are localized precisely. Conditional specification provides error correction (as in sea urchin $\mathrm{veg}_2$ cells replacing micromeres) and coordination between tissues, and it allows the same lineage to generate fates in proportion to size. Even the most "mosaic" embryos need signals to make fates relative to neighbours (left and right AB cells, notochord next to endoderm), because a lineage alone cannot ensure that tissues fit together; a purely mosaic embryo would require a determinant for every cell type and no tolerance for variation. The balance between the two is thus a trade-off between speed and robustness, and it differs between tissues of one embryo.
