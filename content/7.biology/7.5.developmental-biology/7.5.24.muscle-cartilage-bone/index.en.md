Muscle and bone are built together. Most skeletal muscle and the axial skeleton come from the somites of [7.5.23](/7.5.23), the limb and girdle skeleton from the lateral plate mesoderm, and much of the skull from the **neural crest** ([7.5.20](/7.5.20)). This chapter follows these cells from commitment to function: how a mesodermal cell becomes a myoblast and fuses into a muscle fibre, how mesenchyme becomes cartilage and bone, how a vertebra is assembled from two somites, and how long bones grow in length at the growth plates, under local feedback and systemic hormones, until the plates close at the end of puberty. Bone is not finished at birth: it is remodelled throughout life, and an imbalance of resorption and formation causes osteoporosis. Several of the steps are known chiefly through human skeletal disorders, and the commonest of them, achondroplasia, has become a target of rational treatment. The physiology of contraction is treated in [7.14](/7.14) and the molecular motors in [7.6](/7.6); the cell biology of the **extracellular matrix** in [7.3.23](/7.3.23). Regeneration of muscle by its stem cells returns in [7.5.35](/7.5.35) and [7.5.36](/7.5.36).

## 24.1. The Origins of Skeletal Muscle

The human body has about 600 skeletal muscles, derived from three sources:

| Source | Muscles | Upstream regulators |
|---|---|---|
| Somites, dorsomedial (primaxial) myotome | deep muscles of the back, intercostal muscles | Wnt from the neural tube, Shh; Myf5 |
| Somites, migratory precursors from the ventrolateral lip of the dermomyotome | limb muscles, diaphragm, tongue (from occipital somites) | Pax3, Lbx1, c-Met; HGF from the limb mesenchyme |
| Unsegmented cranial paraxial mesoderm (cranial somitomeres) and prechordal mesoderm | muscles of the jaw, face, pharynx and larynx (pharyngeal arch muscles) and the extraocular muscles | Tbx1, Pitx2, Isl1; not Pax3 |

That limb muscles come from the somites, not from the limb bud, was shown with quail–chick chimeras: when the somites at the wing level of a chick embryo are replaced by quail somites, all the wing muscle cells carry the quail nuclear marker, while the cartilage, tendons and connective tissue of the wing are chick, from the lateral plate (Chevallier, Kieny and Mauger 1977; Christ, Jacob and Jacob 1977). The migrating precursors leave the ventrolateral lip of the dermomyotome under the control of Pax3, which induces the receptor tyrosine kinase c-Met; its ligand, hepatocyte growth factor (HGF, scatter factor), is made by the limb mesenchyme and triggers their **epithelial–mesenchymal transition** and migration. Mice lacking *Pax3* (*Splotch*), *c-Met* or *Lbx1* have no limb muscles, although their back muscles form. Head muscles have a different genetic history: they form normally in *Pax3* mutants but depend on Tbx1 and Pitx2, and they are differentially affected in muscular dystrophies (extraocular muscles are spared in Duchenne muscular dystrophy, while oculopharyngeal dystrophy affects them first). The connective tissue that surrounds each muscle comes from the lateral plate in the limbs and from the neural crest in the head, and it instructs the pattern of muscle splitting and attachment.

## 24.2. Myogenic Regulatory Factors

**Definition 24.2.1 (Myogenic Regulatory Factors)** The **myogenic regulatory factors** (MRFs) are four related basic helix–loop–helix transcription factors, MyoD, Myf5, myogenin and MRF4 (Myf6), that bind E-box sequences (CANNTG) of muscle genes as heterodimers with ubiquitous E proteins and, together with the MEF2 family, activate the skeletal muscle program. A cell that expresses MyoD or Myf5 and is committed to the muscle fate, but has not yet differentiated, is a **myoblast**.

MyoD was found as the gene whose expression could convert another cell type into muscle. Treating mouse C3H10T1/2 fibroblasts with the demethylating agent 5-azacytidine turned some of them into muscle at a frequency that suggested a single activated locus; subtractive hybridization between myoblast and fibroblast cDNA identified candidates, and one of them, transfected alone, converted fibroblasts, and later pigment, nerve, fat and liver cell lines, into cells that expressed muscle genes and fused into myotubes (Davis, Weintraub and Lassar 1987). An experiment of this kind shows sufficiency; necessity came from knockouts, which revealed redundancy and a hierarchy.

| Mutant mouse | Phenotype | Conclusion |
|---|---|---|
| *MyoD*$^{-/-}$ | nearly normal muscle; Myf5 up-regulated | redundant determination factors |
| *Myf5*$^{-/-}$ | delayed myotome, normal muscle later (rib defects from a linked enhancer) | |
| *Myf5*$^{-/-}$ *MyoD*$^{-/-}$ (with *Mrf4* also lost) | no myoblasts, no skeletal muscle; cells in muscle positions adopt other fates | Myf5, MyoD and MRF4 determine the myogenic lineage |
| *Myogenin*$^{-/-}$ | myoblasts present in normal positions but very few fibres; neonatal death | myogenin is needed for differentiation |

Thus Pax3 and Pax7 act upstream in the dermomyotome; Myf5 and MyoD (with MRF4) determine the lineage; myogenin and MRF4 drive differentiation. MyoD activates its own gene and the genes of the other MRFs, a positive feedback that makes determination self-sustaining (**bistability**, [7.3.18](/7.3.18)). Because MRFs are so powerful, they are tightly controlled: Id proteins sequester E proteins; growth factors such as FGF keep myoblasts dividing and suppress differentiation; and differentiation requires permanent withdrawal from the cell cycle, which MyoD promotes by inducing the CDK inhibitor p21 and by cooperating with the hypophosphorylated retinoblastoma protein. Muscle genes are activated in a temporal order: MyoD first marks enhancers with chromatin modifiers, and MEF2 and myogenin then drive structural genes (creatine kinase, myosin heavy chains, acetylcholine receptor subunits).

## 24.3. Myoblast Fusion and Muscle Fibres

Skeletal muscle cells do not function as individuals. Myoblasts align and fuse into multinucleate myotubes, which mature into myofibres with thousands of nuclei. Cardiac muscle and smooth muscle, by contrast, are made of single cells that do not fuse ([7.5.28](/7.5.28)). Fusion proceeds in steps.

1. Exit from the cell cycle when mitogens (FGFs) are depleted; in culture, cells that have aligned no longer incorporate thymidine.
2. Attachment to a fibronectin matrix through integrin α5β1; blocking it prevents further differentiation.
3. Recognition and alignment, mediated by cadherins (M-cadherin) and other adhesion molecules, which act only between myoblasts (but chick and rat myoblasts fuse with each other).
4. Membrane fusion, which needs Ca$^{2+}$ and two muscle-specific membrane proteins: Myomaker, needed on both partners and making membranes competent to hemifuse, and Myomixer (Myomerger), needed on at least one and completing pore formation. Mice lacking either have myoblasts but no multinucleate fibres (Millay et al. 2013; Bi et al. 2017; Quinn et al. 2017). Earlier candidates, such as the metalloproteases of the meltrin family, influence fusion but are not the fusogens. In *Drosophila*, fusion is asymmetric: founder cells attract fusion-competent myoblasts, which invade them with actin-propelled protrusions.

Fibres form in two waves. Primary fibres arise from embryonic myoblasts and form the scaffold of each muscle. Secondary fibres form later, from fetal myoblasts aligned along the primary fibres, mostly after motor neurons have formed **neuromuscular junctions** on the primaries ([7.5.21](/7.5.21)); innervation and activity influence how many secondary fibres form and their fast or slow type. By birth, a mouse has most of its adult number of fibres; afterwards muscles grow by fibre enlargement, adding nuclei by fusion of satellite cells (Section 24.4).

Muscle size is negatively controlled. Myostatin (GDF8), a member of the **TGF-β signaling pathway** family, limits the number and growth of fibres. Cattle breeds with "double muscling" (Belgian Blue, Piedmontese), whippets that are homozygous for a loss-of-function allele, and a human child homozygous for a splice mutation all have greatly increased muscle mass. Muscle length is matched to bone length: as the skeleton grows, the stretch it imposes on muscle causes new **sarcomeres** to be added in series at the ends of the fibres; a muscle immobilized in a shortened position loses sarcomeres, and one immobilized stretched gains them. Each fibre thus keeps its sarcomeres near the length of optimal overlap of actin and myosin ([7.14](/7.14)).

## 24.4. Satellite Cells

**Definition 24.4.1 (Satellite Cell)** A **satellite cell** is a muscle stem cell lying between the plasma membrane of a myofibre and its basal lamina. Satellite cells express Pax7, are mitotically quiescent in adult muscle, and are activated by injury or load to proliferate, generate myoblasts that fuse with existing fibres or form new ones, and renew themselves.

Satellite cells come from the central dermomyotome: they are cells of the somite that entered the myotome expressing Pax3 and Pax7 but not yet MRFs (lineage tracing with chick–quail chimeras and with *Pax3*-Cre and *Pax7*-Cre mice). Without Pax7, satellite cells are lost in the first weeks after birth and muscle cannot regenerate. The population is heterogeneous. About 10% are Pax7$^+$ Myf5$^-$ cells that have never expressed Myf5; when they divide perpendicular to the fibre, one daughter remains Pax7$^+$Myf5$^-$ against the basal lamina and the other, facing the fibre, becomes a Pax7$^+$Myf5$^+$ committed progenitor (Kuang et al. 2007): an asymmetric division in a **stem-cell niche**. Quiescence is actively maintained: the microRNA miR-489 represses the oncoprotein Dek in quiescent cells and is lost in the daughter that proliferates; Notch signalling from the fibre keeps cells quiescent. During postnatal growth satellite cells divide slowly and some daughters fuse with fibres, so that the number of nuclei grows with fibre size; each nucleus supplies a limited domain of cytoplasm. Exercise increases their number; with age the niche signals change and regenerative capacity declines ([7.5.37](/7.5.37)). Transplanting a single fibre with its few satellite cells into irradiated muscle regenerates many new fibres and repopulates the satellite cell pool, the classic demonstration of self-renewal (Collins et al. 2005).

## 24.5. Congenital Muscular Dystrophies

A muscle fibre survives repeated contraction because its cytoskeleton is anchored to the basal lamina. The dystrophin–glycoprotein complex links actin, through dystrophin, to the transmembrane protein dystroglycan, whose heavily glycosylated α subunit binds laminin-211 (merosin) of the basal lamina. Defects of this chain cause muscular dystrophies.

**Definition 24.5.1 (Congenital Muscular Dystrophy)** **Congenital muscular dystrophy** (CMD) is a group of mostly autosomal recessive disorders in which muscle weakness and hypotonia are present at birth or in the first months, often with joint contractures, a dystrophic muscle biopsy (fibre degeneration and regeneration, fibrosis) and, in some forms, malformations of the brain and eye.

| Form | Gene and protein | Features |
|---|---|---|
| Merosin-deficient CMD (MDC1A) | *LAMA2*, laminin α2 chain | severe weakness, white matter changes on MRI, usually normal intelligence |
| Collagen VI-related (Ullrich CMD) | *COL6A1–3* | distal joint laxity with proximal contractures |
| Dystroglycanopathies: Fukuyama CMD, muscle–eye–brain disease, Walker–Warburg syndrome | *FKTN*, *POMGNT1*, *POMT1/2*, *FKRP* and others: glycosyltransferases of α-dystroglycan | cobblestone malformation of the cortex (defective attachment of radial glia to the pial basal lamina, [7.5.19](/7.5.19)), eye defects; Fukuyama CMD is common in Japan because of an ancestral retrotransposon insertion in *FKTN* |
| *SEPN1*, *LMNA* related | selenoprotein N, lamin A/C | rigid spine; lamin A/C forms affect the nucleus |

The dystroglycanopathies show that the same glycan is needed in muscle, brain and eye, because each tissue uses α-dystroglycan to bind a basal lamina. Duchenne muscular dystrophy, caused by loss of dystrophin, is X-linked and progressive, with onset in childhood rather than at birth ([7.4](/7.4)).

## 24.6. The Origins of Bone and the Two Modes of Ossification

Three lineages make the skeleton: the paraxial mesoderm (sclerotome: vertebrae, ribs, the occipital part of the skull), the lateral plate mesoderm (the limb skeleton, the shoulder and pelvic girdles, the sternum), and the cranial neural crest (most of the facial skeleton, the jaws, part of the skull vault, the pharyngeal arch cartilages). Two modes of ossification convert mesenchyme into bone.

**Definition 24.6.1 (Intramembranous and Endochondral Ossification)** In **intramembranous ossification**, mesenchymal cells condense and differentiate directly into bone-forming **osteoblasts**, without a cartilage intermediate; this forms the flat bones of the skull vault and face and the clavicle. In **endochondral ossification**, mesenchymal cells condense and differentiate into **chondrocytes** that build a cartilage model of the future bone, which is then replaced by bone; this forms the vertebrae, ribs, limb bones and the base of the skull.

In intramembranous ossification, BMP2, BMP4 and BMP7 from the head epidermis induce neural crest-derived mesenchyme to condense and to express the transcription factors Runx2 (Cbfa1) and then Osterix (Sp7). The osteoblasts secrete osteoid, a matrix of type I collagen with proteoglycans and the bone-specific proteins osteocalcin and osteopontin, which binds calcium phosphate (hydroxyapatite) and mineralizes. The mineralizing islands (bony spicules) grow and join; the condensed mesenchyme around them becomes the periosteum; osteoblasts that become enclosed in their own matrix become osteocytes, the long-lived mechanosensing cells of bone, connected through canaliculi.

Runx2 is the master regulator of osteoblast differentiation in both modes. Mice lacking *Runx2* form a complete cartilage skeleton but no bone at all (Komori et al. 1997; Otto et al. 1997). In mice and humans, loss of one copy causes *cleidocranial dysplasia*: an autosomal dominant disorder with open fontanelles and wide cranial sutures, hypoplastic or absent clavicles (patients can bring their shoulders together in front of the chest), supernumerary teeth and short stature. Both affected bone groups are those formed by intramembranous ossification, which is the most sensitive to Runx2 dose.

## 24.7. Endochondral Ossification

Endochondral ossification proceeds in five phases.

1. Commitment. In the sclerotome, Shh from the notochord and floor plate induces *Pax1* and *Pax9* and makes the cells competent to form cartilage; a notochord grafted beside the dorsal somite converts dermomyotome into cartilage, and neural tube grafts distort the vertebrae that form around them. In the limb, condensations form where BMP signalling is high and FGF low.
2. Condensation. BMPs induce the adhesion molecules N-cadherin (needed to initiate condensation) and N-CAM (to maintain it) and the transcription factor Sox9. Sox9, with Sox5 and Sox6, activates the genes for type II collagen and aggrecan, the main components of cartilage matrix. Heterozygous loss of *SOX9* causes campomelic dysplasia, with bowed long bones, a small chest and often XY sex reversal (the second role of SOX9, [7.5.26](/7.5.26)); most affected babies die of respiratory failure because their tracheal and rib cartilage is weak.
3. Proliferation. Chondrocytes divide and secrete matrix, forming a cartilage model of the bone surrounded by a perichondrium.
4. Hypertrophy. In the centre of the model, chondrocytes stop dividing and swell, increasing their volume five- to tenfold; they switch to type X collagen and mineralize their matrix. Runx2 drives hypertrophy, and the histone deacetylase HDAC4 restrains it (overexpressed, ossification is delayed; knocked out, it is premature). Hypertrophic chondrocytes secrete VEGF, which attracts blood vessels, and Indian hedgehog (Ihh), which induces Runx2 in the adjacent perichondrial cells, so that they become osteoblasts and lay down a bone collar; the perichondrium becomes the periosteum. Mice lacking *Ihh* have no osteoblasts in the endochondral skeleton, while their intramembranous bones form normally.
5. Replacement. Blood vessels invade the mineralized cartilage, bringing osteoclasts (here often called chondroclasts), which resorb it, and osteoprogenitors from the periosteum, which deposit bone on the remnants of cartilage matrix: the primary ossification centre. Hypertrophic chondrocytes mostly die, but lineage tracing in mice shows that a fraction survive and become osteoblasts and osteocytes (Yang et al. 2014; Zhou et al. 2014). The marrow cavity forms, colonized by haematopoietic cells ([7.5.29](/7.5.29)). Inhibiting VEGF delays the invasion and the whole process.

In a long bone the primary centre forms in the shaft (diaphysis) in the embryo; in humans most primary centres appear between weeks 7 and 12. Secondary ossification centres appear later in the ends (epiphyses), from the last fetal weeks to adolescence (the distal femoral centre at about birth, which is used to estimate fetal maturity). Between each epiphyseal centre and the shaft a disc of cartilage remains, the growth plate (Section 24.9), which allows the bone to lengthen until it closes. Bone grows in width by apposition from the periosteum while osteoclasts enlarge the marrow cavity from inside.

## 24.8. The Vertebral Column: Resegmentation

A vertebra is not the descendant of one somite. Each sclerotome is divided into a rostral and a caudal half, which differ in gene expression (*Tbx18* rostrally, *Uncx4.1* caudally, set up by Mesp2 during segmentation, [7.5.23](/7.5.23#sec-23.8)) and in their cell density, and the two halves have different fates.

**Definition 24.8.1 (Resegmentation)** **Resegmentation** is the recombination of somite halves in the formation of the vertebral column: the caudal half of one sclerotome fuses with the rostral half of the next to form one vertebral body, so that the vertebrae are offset by half a segment from the somites, and the intervertebral discs form at the level of the middle of each somite.

Remak proposed resegmentation in 1855 from histology; it was demonstrated by grafting single quail somites into chick embryos, which labelled the posterior half of one vertebra and the anterior half of the next (Bagnall, Higgins and Sanders 1988; Huang et al. 2000). The offset has a functional logic. The myotome is not resegmented, so each segmental muscle spans the joint between two vertebrae and can move them; spinal nerves, whose axons and neural crest cells pass only through the rostral halves of the somites ([7.5.20](/7.5.20)), run between the pedicles of successive neural arches, which come mainly from the caudal halves, and emerge between the vertebrae; and the intersegmental arteries run along the vertebral bodies. The intervertebral disc forms from the sclerotome at the level of the boundary between the rostral and caudal halves (the annulus fibrosus, from the arthrotome of Definition 23.10.1) around a core of notochord cells (the nucleus pulposus); the rest of the notochord degenerates. Ribs grow from the costal processes of the thoracic vertebrae: their proximal parts from the central sclerotome and their distal parts from the lateral sclerotome, under Myf5/Myf6-dependent signals (PDGF, FGF) from the myotome. When the rostrocaudal polarity of the somites is disturbed, as in mutations of the clock and of Mesp2, the halves recombine irregularly and fused or hemi-vertebrae result (spondylocostal dysostosis, Definition 23.11.1).

## 24.9. The Growth Plate and the Ihh–PTHrP Loop

**Definition 24.9.1 (Growth Plate)** The **growth plate** (epiphyseal plate, physis) is the disc of cartilage between the epiphysis and the metaphysis of a growing long bone in which chondrocytes are arranged in a sequence of zones: a resting (reserve) zone of small, scattered cells; a proliferative zone of flattened cells stacked in longitudinal columns; a prehypertrophic zone; a hypertrophic zone of enlarged cells; and, at the junction with the metaphysis, the zone of vascular invasion and ossification. Bone lengthens as cells move through the zones and the hypertrophic cartilage is replaced by bone.

Each column of the growth plate is a clone: a resting-zone cell divides, its daughters divide again in the plane of the plate and then rotate and stack, building the column. Recent lineage tracing in mice found that after the secondary ossification centre forms, the resting zone contains PTHrP-expressing skeletal stem cells that renew the columns (Mizuhashi et al. 2018; Newton et al. 2019), whereas before that the plate is consumed by clones that are not renewed. Bone elongation therefore depends on the rate at which cells enter the columns, on the number of divisions in the proliferative zone, and, most of all, on the swelling of hypertrophic cells: differences in hypertrophic cell size explain much of the difference in growth rate between bones within an animal and between species (Cooper et al. 2013, comparing the jerboa's elongated metatarsals with mouse bones).

The length of the proliferative zone, and so the timing of hypertrophy, is set by a negative feedback loop between two signals (Vortkamp et al. 1996; Lanske et al. 1996; Kronenberg 2003).

- Parathyroid hormone-related protein (PTHrP) is made by periarticular chondrocytes at the ends of the bone and diffuses into the plate. Acting through the PTH/PTHrP receptor (PTH1R), it keeps chondrocytes proliferating and delays their hypertrophy.
- Cells that move far enough from the PTHrP source to escape its action stop dividing and become prehypertrophic. They make Ihh, which acts on three targets: it stimulates chondrocyte proliferation; it induces osteoblasts in the adjacent perichondrium (the bone collar); and, directly or through intermediate signals, it increases the production of PTHrP at the ends of the bone.

The loop is a negative feedback: if hypertrophy begins too close to the end, more Ihh reaches the periarticular cells, more PTHrP is made, and hypertrophy is pushed back; if the proliferative zone becomes too long, less Ihh arrives and PTHrP falls. Mouse genetics confirms each arm. Mice lacking PTHrP or PTH1R have short bones because chondrocytes hypertrophy prematurely; in humans, inactivating mutations of PTH1R cause Blomstrand chondrodysplasia, with accelerated ossification and perinatal death, and activating mutations cause Jansen metaphyseal chondrodysplasia, with delayed hypertrophy and short stature. Mice lacking *Ihh* have little PTHrP, reduced proliferation, premature hypertrophy and no bone collar. Expressing a constitutively active PTH1R in the chondrocytes of *Ihh*$^{-/-}$ mice restores the delay of hypertrophy but not proliferation, which shows that Ihh delays hypertrophy through PTHrP but promotes proliferation directly.

**Formula 24.9.1 (A Model of the Proliferative Zone)** Let $x$ be the distance from the periarticular end and suppose that PTHrP, made at $x = 0$ with amplitude $A$, diffuses and is degraded, so that $P(x) = A\,\mathrm{e}^{-x/\lambda_P}$ (the **synthesis–diffusion–degradation model** of [7.5.11](/7.5.11)). Chondrocytes proliferate where $P > \theta$ and become prehypertrophic at $x_h = \lambda_P\ln(A/\theta)$. Ihh made at $x_h$ reaches the periarticular cells attenuated to $\mathrm{e}^{-x_h/\lambda_I}$ of its source value and increases PTHrP production: $A = a_0 + a_1\mathrm{e}^{-x_h/\lambda_I}$. The length of the proliferative zone is the solution of

$$
x_h = \lambda_P\ln\frac{a_0 + a_1\mathrm{e}^{-x_h/\lambda_I}}{\theta}.
\tag{24.1}
$$

The right side decreases with $x_h$ and the left side increases, so (24.1) has a unique solution, which is stable. The feedback buffers the zone against changes in basal PTHrP production. Without feedback, $\mathrm{d}x_h/\mathrm{d}\ln a_0 = \lambda_P$; with feedback, differentiating (24.1),

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}x_h}{\mathrm{d}\ln a_0} = \frac{\lambda_P\,a_0/A}{1 + (\lambda_P/\lambda_I)(a_1\mathrm{e}^{-x_h/\lambda_I}/A)} < \lambda_P ,
$$
smaller both because only a fraction $a_0/A$ of the PTHrP is basal and because of the feedback term in the denominator. The model reproduces the mutants qualitatively: removing Ihh ($a_1 = 0$) shortens the zone; removing PTHrP ($a_0 = a_1 = 0$) gives immediate hypertrophy; a constitutively active receptor (lower effective $\theta$) lengthens it.

## 24.10. Systemic Control of Bone Growth

Bone length is controlled by hormones as well as by local signals. Growth hormone (GH), from the anterior pituitary, acts on the growth plate both directly and through insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1), made by the liver (endocrine) and by the chondrocytes themselves (paracrine). In hypophysectomized rats the growth plate shrinks and loses most of its proliferative zone; GH injections restore it, and GH injected into one tibial growth plate makes only that bone grow faster (Isaksson et al. 1982), showing a local action. Deficiency of GH or of its receptor (Laron syndrome, in which GH is high and IGF-1 low) causes proportionate short stature; excess before the plates close causes gigantism, usually from a pituitary adenoma, and excess after closure acromegaly. Thyroid hormone is also required: congenital hypothyroidism stunts growth unless treated in the first weeks.

Growth ends when the growth plates close at the end of puberty: the remaining chondrocytes are exhausted and the plate is replaced by bone. The pubertal growth spurt and the closure are both driven by estrogen, in males as well as females. A man with a homozygous inactivating mutation of estrogen receptor α was still growing at 28 years, tall, with open growth plates and osteoporosis, despite normal testosterone (Smith et al. 1994); men with aromatase deficiency, who cannot convert testosterone to estrogen, have the same phenotype, which is corrected by estrogen. Precocious puberty therefore causes tall children but short adults, and estrogen once was, and aromatase inhibitors now are, used to influence final height. The plates seem to close not by a hormone-triggered program but because estrogen accelerates the senescence of the plate, the progressive decline in proliferation and in the number and size of resting-zone cells that occurs anyway with time and with the number of divisions (Nilsson and Baron 2004).

**Formula 24.10.1 (Elongation Rate of a Growth Plate)** In steady state, if each column produces $n$ new cells per day and each cell reaches a final height $\ell$ along the axis when hypertrophic, the growth plate adds length at the rate

$$
v = n\,\ell
$$
(the contribution of matrix between cells is included in $\ell$). The total growth of a bone is the sum over its growth plates.

**Example 24.10.1 (A Human Growth Plate)** The distal femoral growth plate contributes about 1 cm per year during childhood, about $27\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ per day. If a hypertrophic chondrocyte with its matrix occupies about $30\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ along the axis, each column must produce about one cell per day. During the pubertal spurt, total height increases at a peak of about $9\,\mathrm{cm\,yr^{-1}}$ in boys (Figure B), roughly twice the prepubertal rate, so cell production or hypertrophy roughly doubles; after closure $n = 0$. In a rat, whose tibia grows several hundred micrometres per day at its fastest, columns produce several cells per day.

Several skeletal dysplasias act on the growth plate.

- *Achondroplasia*, the most common short-limbed dwarfism (about 1 in 25 000 births), is caused by the gain-of-function mutation G380R in the transmembrane domain of FGF receptor 3 (*FGFR3*). FGFR3 signalling in chondrocytes inhibits proliferation and hypertrophy (through STAT1 and the MAP kinase pathway); the mutant receptor is constitutively more active, so long bones are short while the trunk is near normal, the skull base is small (risk of foramen magnum stenosis), and the face shows midface hypoplasia. It is autosomal dominant, but about 80% of cases are new mutations, almost always on the paternal allele and more frequent with advanced paternal age: the mutation gives spermatogonial stem cells a selective advantage, so mutant clones expand in the testis with age (Goriely and Wilkie 2012). Stronger activating mutations cause hypochondroplasia (milder) and thanatophoric dysplasia (lethal). Since 2021, vosoritide, an analogue of C-type natriuretic peptide whose receptor antagonizes the MAP kinase pathway downstream of FGFR3, increases growth velocity in children with achondroplasia.
- Fibrodysplasia ossificans progressiva (FOP) is a progressive heterotopic ossification of muscles, tendons and ligaments, starting in childhood, often after minor trauma, with malformed great toes from birth. It is caused by a recurrent mutation (R206H) in the BMP type I receptor ACVR1 (ALK2), which makes the receptor hyperactive and causes it to respond to activin A as if it were a BMP (Shore et al. 2006; Hatsell et al. 2015). Ectopic endochondral bone forms from progenitor cells in connective tissue. Earlier, elevated BMP4 in lymphocytes of patients had been proposed as the cause, but it is not the primary defect.

## 24.11. Bone Remodelling

Bone is renewed throughout life: about 10% of the adult skeleton is replaced each year, in discrete units in which osteoclasts resorb a packet of bone and osteoblasts then replace it, a cycle of a few months.

**Definition 24.11.1 (Osteoclast)** An **osteoclast** is a large multinucleate cell that resorbs bone. Osteoclasts form by fusion of precursors of the monocyte–macrophage lineage (from **hematopoietic stem cells**, not from the somite or the lateral plate), attach to bone with a sealing zone, and secrete protons through a V-type ATPase and the protease cathepsin K into the enclosed space, dissolving the mineral and digesting the matrix. Their formation requires M-CSF and RANKL (receptor activator of NF-κB ligand), both made by osteoblast-lineage cells and osteocytes; osteoprotegerin (OPG), a soluble decoy receptor secreted by osteoblasts, binds RANKL and inhibits osteoclast formation.

The control of osteoclasts by cells of the osteoblast lineage couples resorption to formation, and the ratio RANKL/OPG is the main lever. In medaka fish engineered to express RANKL on demand, induction produces osteoclasts and erodes the vertebrae within days, a direct test of sufficiency. Too much resorption relative to formation causes osteoporosis. After menopause, the loss of estrogen raises RANKL and lowers OPG and the lifespan of osteoclasts is prolonged, so each remodelling cycle removes more bone than it replaces; glucocorticoids and immobilization have similar effects. The monoclonal antibody denosumab against RANKL and the bisphosphonates, which poison osteoclasts, treat osteoporosis; romosozumab, an antibody against sclerostin (an osteocyte-derived Wnt inhibitor), increases formation. Too little resorption causes osteopetrosis, dense but brittle bones with a reduced marrow cavity, anaemia and nerve compression: it follows from absent osteoclasts (mice lacking M-CSF, the *op/op* mouse, or RANK or RANKL) or from defective ones (human mutations of the proton pump subunit *TCIRG1*, the chloride channel *CLCN7* or carbonic anhydrase II), and the severe infantile form is cured by haematopoietic stem cell transplantation, which supplies normal osteoclast precursors.

**Formula 24.11.1 (Komarova Model of a Remodelling Unit)** Let $C(t)$ and $B(t)$ be the numbers of osteoclasts and osteoblasts in a basic multicellular unit and $z(t)$ the local bone mass (in percent). With autocrine and paracrine effects written as power laws (Komarova et al. 2003),

$$
\dot C = \alpha_1C^{g_{11}}B^{g_{21}} - \beta_1C,\qquad \dot B = \alpha_2C^{g_{12}}B^{g_{22}} - \beta_2B,\qquad \dot z = -k_1\max(C - \bar C, 0) + k_2\max(B - \bar B, 0),
$$
where $\alpha_i$ and $\beta_i$ are rates of production and removal, $g_{11}$ the autocrine effect of osteoclasts on themselves, $g_{21}$ the effect of osteoblasts on osteoclasts (negative: OPG exceeds RANKL), $g_{12}$ the stimulation of osteoblasts by osteoclasts (factors released from resorbed matrix), $g_{22}$ the autocrine effect of osteoblasts, $(\bar C, \bar B)$ the steady state, and $k_1$, $k_2$ the rates of resorption and formation per cell. With $\Gamma = g_{12}g_{21} - (1 - g_{11})(1 - g_{22})$, the steady state is

$$
\bar C = \Bigl(\frac{\beta_1}{\alpha_1}\Bigr)^{(1 - g_{22})/\Gamma}\Bigl(\frac{\beta_2}{\alpha_2}\Bigr)^{g_{21}/\Gamma},\qquad \bar B = \Bigl(\frac{\beta_1}{\alpha_1}\Bigr)^{g_{12}/\Gamma}\Bigl(\frac{\beta_2}{\alpha_2}\Bigr)^{(1 - g_{11})/\Gamma}.
$$
Linearizing in logarithmic variables, the Jacobian has trace $\beta_1(g_{11} - 1) + \beta_2(g_{22} - 1)$ and determinant $-\beta_1\beta_2\Gamma$; the steady state is a stable focus when the trace is negative and $\Gamma < 0$, and it loses stability in a Hopf bifurcation when the autocrine stimulation of osteoclasts $g_{11}$ rises above $1 + \beta_2(1 - g_{22})/\beta_1$.

**Example 24.11.1 (One Remodelling Cycle)** With $\alpha_1 = 3$, $\alpha_2 = 4$, $\beta_1 = 0.2$, $\beta_2 = 0.02$ (per day), $g_{11} = 0.5$, $g_{21} = -0.5$, $g_{12} = 1$, $g_{22} = 0$, the steady state is $\bar C \approx 1.06$, $\bar B \approx 212$ ($\Gamma = -1$), and the eigenvalues are $-0.06 \pm 0.02\mathrm{i}$ per day. A pulse of ten extra osteoclasts (the activation of a remodelling site) is resorbed in about two weeks, during which about 10% of local bone mass is removed; osteoblasts rise and replace it over the following months. With $k_1 = 0.24$ and $k_2 = 0.0017$ per cell and day, the bone removed is exactly replaced; if $k_2$ falls by 10% (reduced osteoblast function with age), each cycle leaves a deficit of about 1.5%, and repeated cycles cause progressive bone loss. The Hopf threshold here is $g_{11} = 1 + 0.02/0.2 = 1.1$.

## 24.12. Joints, Tendons and Mechanical Forces

Joints form within continuous cartilage condensations. At the future joint, cells stop making cartilage and become a dense interzone, marked by GDF5 (mutated in the brachypodism mouse, which has short limbs and missing joints), Wnt4 and Wnt9a; Wnt/β-catenin signalling suppresses the chondrogenic fate there, and *Gdf5*-expressing interzone cells form the articular cartilage, the synovial lining and the ligaments. The joint cavity then forms by cavitation, which requires movement: in chick embryos paralysed with neuromuscular blocking agents, joints that have begun to form fuse (Drachman and Sokoloff 1966), and mice without skeletal muscle have fused or abnormal joints. Fetal akinesia (from neuromuscular disorders or oligohydramnios) causes arthrogryposis, congenital contractures of many joints.

Tendons come from the syndetome in the trunk ([7.5.23](/7.5.23#sec-23.10)) and from lateral plate mesoderm in the limbs. Their progenitors express the bHLH factor scleraxis, induced by FGF from the myotome; TGF-β2 and TGF-β3, whose activity depends on load, maintain *scleraxis* and the tendon matrix through Smad2/3, and mice lacking both lack tendons.

Mechanical force shapes bone throughout development. Bone adapts its mass and architecture to the loads it bears, Wolff's law (1892): trabeculae align with principal stresses, bone is added where strain is high and lost where it is low (astronauts and immobilized patients lose bone). The sensing is a form of **mechanotransduction** in osteocytes, which respond to fluid flow in their canaliculi by reducing sclerostin and increasing Wnt signalling. In the embryo, several skeletal elements depend on movement: the fibular crest of the chick, which connects tibia and fibula, does not form in paralysed embryos; the patella forms in response to pressure from the quadriceps tendon; the jaws of cichlid fishes and of primates are shaped by the hardness of the food during growth. Children with cerebral palsy, who lack normal muscle forces, develop deformed bones and dislocated hips.

- Myogenic regulatory factors determine and execute the muscle fate with redundancy and positive feedback; myoblasts fuse in two waves; satellite cells (Definition 24.4.1) renew muscle after birth.
- Bone forms directly (intramembranous) or by replacement of a cartilage model (endochondral, Definition 24.6.1); Runx2 is required for all osteoblasts, Sox9 for cartilage, Ihh for the osteoblasts of endochondral bone.

- In the growth plate (Definition 24.9.1) a PTHrP–Ihh negative feedback sets the length of the proliferative zone and buffers it (Formula 24.9.1); growth rate is cell production times hypertrophic height (Formula 24.10.1, Example 24.10.1); estrogen ends growth in both sexes.
- Osteoclasts (Definition 24.11.1) and osteoblasts are coupled through RANKL/OPG; the Komarova model (Formula 24.11.1) describes a remodelling cycle and shows how a small formation deficit accumulates (Example 24.11.1).
- Mechanical forces form joints, add sarcomeres and remodel bone (Wolff's law).

## Questions

1. Describe the quail–chick chimera experiment that showed that limb muscles derive from the somites, and predict the result of grafting quail lateral plate mesoderm at the wing level instead.

   **Answer** Replace the paraxial mesoderm (somites) at the wing level of a 2-day chick embryo with the corresponding quail somites and, after the wing has developed, stain sections for the quail nuclear marker (condensed nucleolar heterochromatin, or a quail-specific antibody). All muscle fibres of the wing contain quail nuclei, while cartilage, bone, tendons and dermis are chick. Grafting quail lateral plate gives the reciprocal result: quail skeleton, tendons and connective tissue with chick muscle fibres. The muscle precursors migrate into the limb bud, where the lateral plate connective tissue patterns them.

2. Mice lacking *MyoD* have nearly normal muscle and mice lacking *Myf5* have only a delay, yet the double mutant has no skeletal muscle. What do these results show, and what further experiment showed that the double mutant cells are not simply dead?

   **Answer** MyoD and Myf5 are redundant determination factors: either suffices to establish the myoblast lineage, and in the absence of one the other is up-regulated (cross-regulation). Lineage tracing in double mutant embryos, using a reporter knocked into the *Myf5* locus, showed that the cells that would have become myoblasts are present in the positions of the myotome but adopt other fates (for example cartilage or dermis), so the factors determine fate rather than survival. (Strictly, the *Myf5* allele also disrupted *Mrf4*; MRF4 can act as a determination factor too.)

3. Myoblasts cultured in rich medium divide; when the medium is depleted, they align and fuse. Explain the molecular basis, and how you would show that fusion requires Myomaker on both cells.

   **Answer** Growth factors (FGF, IGF in high serum) activate signalling that keeps cyclin–CDK activity high and inhibits MyoD function (phosphorylation, Id proteins), so cells proliferate. When they are withdrawn, MyoD induces p21 and cell-cycle exit, myogenin and MEF2 activate differentiation genes, including those for Myomaker and Myomixer, and cells fuse. To test the requirement for Myomaker, mix wild-type myoblasts labelled with one fluorescent marker and *Myomaker*-null myoblasts labelled with another: fusion occurs among wild-type cells, but no myotubes contain both labels; null cells do not fuse with each other or with wild-type cells, whereas fibroblasts expressing Myomaker (and Myomixer) can fuse with myoblasts.

4. Explain why the clavicle and skull vault are most affected in cleidocranial dysplasia, while the limbs are nearly normal, although Runx2 is needed for all bone.

   **Answer** The clavicle and the flat bones of the skull vault form by intramembranous ossification, in which mesenchymal cells must become osteoblasts directly and early, at the start of the process, and this step is most sensitive to the dose of Runx2; one functional copy is not enough. Endochondral bones form on a cartilage scaffold, which does not need Runx2 for its initial formation, and their osteoblasts are induced later with strong Ihh signalling that up-regulates Runx2, so a half dose is largely sufficient. Complete loss in mice eliminates all bone, while the cartilage skeleton remains.

5. Using resegmentation, explain why a spinal nerve emerges between two vertebrae, and why each intercostal muscle attaches to two adjacent ribs.

   **Answer** Motor axons and neural crest cells grow only through the rostral half of each sclerotome (repelled by ephrins and semaphorins in the caudal half). The vertebral body forms from the caudal half of one sclerotome and the rostral half of the next, while the neural arch, and in particular its pedicles, comes mainly from the caudal halves. The nerve, which passed through a rostral half, therefore lies between the pedicles of two successive arches and emerges through the intervertebral foramen. The myotome of each somite is not resegmented; its muscle, half a segment out of register with the vertebrae, attaches to two vertebrae (or ribs) and spans the joint between them, so its contraction can move one relative to the other.

6. In the model of Formula 24.9.1 with $\lambda_P = 1$, $\lambda_I = 1.5$, $\theta = 1$, $a_0 = 3$ and $a_1 = 20$, compute the length of the proliferative zone and its sensitivity to $a_0$, and compare with the case without Ihh feedback.

   **Answer** Solve $x = \ln(3 + 20\mathrm{e}^{-x/1.5})$ by iteration: starting at $x = 2$, $\ln(3 + 20 \times 0.264) = \ln 8.27 = 2.11$; $x = 2.11$: $\ln(3 + 20 \times 0.245) = \ln 7.90 = 2.07$; converging to $x_h \approx 2.08$. Then $A = 8.0$, $a_1\mathrm{e}^{-x_h/\lambda_I}/A = 5.0/8.0 = 0.63$, so $\mathrm{d}x_h/\mathrm{d}\ln a_0 = (3/8.0)/(1 + 0.63/1.5) = 0.375/1.42 = 0.26$. Without feedback ($a_1 = 0$), $x_h = \ln 3 = 1.10$ and the sensitivity is 1. The feedback doubles the zone and reduces its sensitivity to the basal source about fourfold.

7. A boy with a mutation that inactivates estrogen receptor α is tall, still growing in his twenties, and has low bone density, although his testosterone is normal. Explain, and predict the phenotype of a woman with aromatase deficiency in her teens.

   **Answer** Estrogen, not androgen, drives growth-plate senescence and closure and maintains bone mass in both sexes; in males it comes from aromatization of testosterone. Without ERα the plates do not close and growth continues slowly, and bone mass is low because estrogen normally restrains osteoclasts (by lowering RANKL and shortening osteoclast lifespan). An adolescent woman with aromatase deficiency cannot make estrogen; she does not undergo breast development or the pubertal growth spurt, her growth plates remain open (tall stature with eunuchoid proportions if untreated), bone density is low, and she shows virilization from excess androgens (already at birth, from placental aromatase deficiency); estrogen replacement induces puberty and plate closure.

8. Achondroplasia is dominant, yet most patients have unaffected parents. Explain why new *FGFR3* mutations are so frequent, why they almost always arise on the father's allele, and why the mutant receptor shortens the long bones more than the trunk.

   **Answer** The G380R mutation arises at a very high rate in the male germ line because mutant spermatogonial stem cells have a proliferative advantage (the receptor is activated in them too), so mutant clones grow in the testis with age ("selfish spermatogonial selection"); the frequency of mutant sperm, and of new cases, rises with paternal age, and the mutation is on the paternal allele. In females, oocytes do not undergo this clonal selection. FGFR3 signalling slows proliferation and hypertrophy in growth plates; long bones grow mainly by endochondral growth at their plates and are affected most, whereas the vertebral bodies grow partly in other directions and the skull vault by intramembranous ossification, so the trunk and skull vault are near normal, while the skull base (endochondral) is small.

9. In the Komarova model (Example 24.11.1), at which value of $g_{11}$ does the steady state lose stability, and what does a sustained oscillation of osteoclasts and osteoblasts mean for bone? Why might a reduction of $k_2$ by 10% have a greater effect over 20 years than any single remodelling cycle suggests?

   **Answer** The trace $0.2(g_{11} - 1) + 0.02(0 - 1)$ vanishes at $g_{11} = 1.1$, while the determinant $-\beta_1\beta_2\Gamma$ stays positive ($\Gamma = g_{12}g_{21} - (1 - g_{11})(1 - g_{22}) = -0.5 + 0.1 = -0.4 < 0$ at threshold); above it the focus becomes unstable and the cells cycle without triggers, so a site would undergo repeated resorption and formation, with bone mass oscillating and drifting if formation and resorption rates per cell are not balanced. A 10% fall of $k_2$ removes about 1.5% of the local bone per cycle (Figure C); each site is remodelled every few years, but there are millions of sites and the deficits accumulate multiplicatively: after 20 cycles (say a lifetime of remodelling of trabecular bone) about $1 - 0.985^{20} \approx 26\%$ of bone mass is lost, which is the magnitude of postmenopausal bone loss.

10. (Open) Design an experiment to test whether joint cavitation requires muscle contraction or only passive movement.

    **Answer** In chick embryos (which can be manipulated in ovo), paralyse skeletal muscle with a neuromuscular blocker (decamethonium or pancuronium) from the stage when the interzone forms, and in a parallel group combine paralysis with passive movement of one limb, by a micro-actuator or by rhythmic manipulation through a window in the shell, leaving the contralateral limb immobile. Score joint cavitation, *Gdf5* and lubricin expression, and fusion of the elements. If passive movement rescues cavitation on the moved side, mechanical stimulation of the joint, not muscle-derived factors, is the cue; complementary genetic tests in mice (muscle-less *Pax3* or *Myf5;MyoD* mutants, or conditional removal of mechanosensitive channels such as Piezo1/2 or of YAP/TAZ in the interzone) would identify the transducing pathway.
