Developmental biology asks how a single cell, the fertilized egg, builds an organism of several hundred cell types arranged in organs of reproducible shape and size, and how that organism in turn sets aside the cells that will make the next generation. Nothing outside the embryo holds a blueprint, and every cell keeps the whole genome (**genomic equivalence**), so the problem breaks into a few precise questions. How do cells with the same genome become different and remember the difference (**cell memory**)? How does a cell learn where it is, by local **induction**, by reading a **morphogen** at thresholds (**positional information**), by patterns that organize themselves, or by a clock read by a moving front? How do forces generated by cells and transmitted through tissues produce shapes? How is the size of an organ controlled? How does the organism continue, through a germ line, through **stem cells** that renew adult tissues, through **regeneration**, and why does it age? And how does development respond to the environment and change in evolution? The evidence comes from two traditions that the course treats together: experiments that perturb the embryo, by removing or transplanting tissue, by making mutants and by tracing lineages, and mathematical models that make the proposed mechanisms precise, a gradient as synthesis, diffusion and degradation, a periodic pattern as a Turing instability, a clock as delayed negative feedback, a fate decision as a bistable switch, the fate of a stem cell as a **branching process**.

The chapters follow the life of the embryo. After the questions and history of the field, the **model organisms** with the experimental designs used on them, and the molecular toolkit of transcription factors and a handful of signaling families, the course goes from fertilization through cleavage to the blastula and to the invertebrates whose cells are specified early by inherited determinants. Before the embryo changes shape, a group of chapters supplies the physical and mathematical tools: adhesion and cell sorting, tissues as viscous liquids, the movements of **gastrulation** that form the **germ layers**, landscapes of stable states and **catastrophe theory**, morphogen gradients, reaction–diffusion and **lateral inhibition**, and the chemotactic aggregation of *Dictyostelium*. The body plan is then laid down twice, in *Drosophila* by maternal gradients and a cascade of segmentation genes, and in vertebrates by the **Spemann organizer**, neural induction and the patterning of the head-to-tail and left–right axes. The organs follow by germ layer: from the ectoderm the neural tube, the growth of the brain, the **neural crest**, axon guidance, the placodes, the eye and the epidermis; from the mesoderm the somites and their **segmentation clock**, muscle, cartilage and bone, the kidney, the gonads with the germ cells that colonize them, the heart, blood vessels and blood; from the endoderm the gut, lung, liver and pancreas; and the limb, patterned along three axes, as the classical system for all the mechanisms at once. After the embryo come metamorphosis and imaginal discs, growth and size control, regeneration, stem cells with embryo models and **organoids**, and aging; two chapters on health and the environment (malformations, **teratogens**, **polyphenism**, symbiosis) and on evolution close the course. The course summary gathers the answers to the six questions, the seven recurring mechanisms, a family tree of the models and a table of organs, their signals and their congenital disorders.

The cellular machinery that development deploys, signaling, the cell cycle, adhesion, apoptosis, stem cells and senescence, comes from [cell biology](/7.3); the mutants, screens, **maternal-effect genes**, imprinting and **genetic mosaics** with which developmental pathways were found come from [genetics](/7.4); enhancers, chromatin and the regulation of gene expression from [molecular biology](/7.2); and the receptors and second messengers of signal transduction from [biochemistry](/7.1). The models use the stability theory and oscillators of [ordinary differential equations](/2.4), the diffusion and Turing instability of [partial differential equations](/2.5), the generating functions and branching processes of [probability](/3.1) and the interfacial energies of [physical chemistry](/6.3). In turn, the mechanics of tissues and motors continues in [biophysics](/7.6), the switches and oscillators as network motifs in [systems biology](/7.9), and evo-devo, together with the evolution of life histories, in [evolutionary biology](/7.10); plant development belongs to [botany](/7.12). The organs built here work in [animal physiology](/7.14), the shared Toll pathway, the thymus and the blood lineages lead to [immunology](/7.15), the activity-dependent refinement of neural connections to [computational neuroscience](/7.16), and the body plans compared across phyla to [zoology](/7.17).

In olympiad training I used developmental biology only as a reference for concepts of zoology, through the Chinese translation of Gilbert's textbook. At university its standing is entirely different. It is still a very active field of research, one whose center, as I see it, lies in Europe and especially in Britain, and its paradigm accommodates the traditional and the new side by side. The traditional methods, making mutants and observing their phenotypes, or transplanting tissue and observing the consequences, remain the backbone: the organizer graft of Spemann and Mangold ([7.5.16](/7.5.16)) is still the template of an induction experiment, and large mutant screens found the segmentation genes of the fly ([7.5.15](/7.5.15)). Alongside them, mathematical modeling has become a method in its own right: Turing's reaction–diffusion mechanism ([7.5.12](/7.5.12)) is now tested against the spacing of digits and hair follicles, and the **clock-and-wavefront model** of somitogenesis ([7.5.23](/7.5.23)) was proposed two decades before the oscillating genes it predicted were found. Quantitative imaging, lineage tracing at single-cell resolution and organoids extend the same logic to human tissue. This is why these pages keep Gilbert's textbook as the framework, for its breadth of organisms and organs, include all of the models and discussions of Percus and Childress, and check the result against the principles of Wolpert's book: a model turns the question of how an embryo does something into one that can be calculated, how far a gradient reaches, at what wavelength a sheet buckles, whether a stem cell's fate is decided by chance or by control. The subject is also visual, and nearly every chapter carries several figures, many of them computed from the models.

The course is compulsory for students of the eight-year clinical medicine program, and this shapes its emphasis: the development of the germ layers and of the organs takes a large share, together with the congenital disorders that follow when an induction fails, which is where developmental biology meets the clinic ([7.5.40](/7.5.40#sec-40.5)). Finally, I regard cell biology, genetics and developmental biology as one system that tells the story from the level of the cell to the level of the individual ([7.5.40](/7.5.40#sec-40.6)). Seen from development, that system is the life cycle itself: the cell supplies meiosis, the cell cycle and the signaling pathways, genetics supplies the logic of transmission and the tools of analysis, and development uses both to build an organism whose germ line, set aside early, carries a genome, maternal products and imprints to the next generation, while everything else is rebuilt each time.
