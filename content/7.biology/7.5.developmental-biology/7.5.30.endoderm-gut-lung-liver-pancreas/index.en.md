The **endoderm** is the **germ layer** that ends up innermost ([7.5.9](/7.5.9)). In the adult it forms the epithelium of the digestive tract from the pharynx to the rectum, of the respiratory tract, and of the glands that bud from them: thyroid, thymus, parathyroids, liver, gallbladder and pancreas. In the embryo it has an earlier, less obvious role, as a source of inductive signals: it induces the heart ([7.5.28](/7.5.28)), patterns the pharyngeal arches colonized by the **neural crest** ([7.5.20](/7.5.20)), helps specify the notochord and the blood vessels, and is needed for the **germ cells** to migrate ([7.5.27](/7.5.27)). The organs it forms show, more clearly than any other, that organogenesis is a dialogue: the endodermal epithelium and the surrounding splanchnic **mesoderm** instruct each other at every step, as tissue-recombination experiments established. Three further themes run through the chapter: mechanical forces (the buckling that forms intestinal villi, the surface tension of alveoli), **branching morphogenesis** ([7.5.25](/7.5.25)), seen here in two versions, the stereotyped lung and the stochastic pancreas, and the use of developmental knowledge to make cells in culture, such as insulin-secreting β cells. The physiology of digestion and breathing belongs to [7.14](/7.14), the thymus as an immune organ to [7.15](/7.15).

## 30.1. The Gut Tube

The definitive **endoderm** is formed during **gastrulation** by epiblast cells that pass through the anterior **primitive streak** under high Nodal signalling and express Sox17 and FoxA2 ([7.5.16](/7.5.16)); in the mouse these cells do not displace the visceral endoderm as a sheet but intercalate into it, so a few visceral endoderm cells contribute to the gut (Kwon et al. 2008). *Sox17*-null mouse embryos lack definitive gut endoderm, and Sox17 drives endoderm differentiation in **embryonic stem cells**. At first the endoderm is a flat sheet on the ventral surface of the embryo (chick, human) or the outer surface of the egg cylinder (mouse). It folds into a tube from both ends: the anterior intestinal portal forms as the head fold brings the lateral anterior endoderm together ventrally, the caudal intestinal portal as the tail fold does the same posteriorly, and the two portals move towards each other, leaving the midgut open to the **yolk sac** until the yolk stalk narrows.

**Definition 30.1.1 (Primitive Gut Tube)** The **primitive gut tube** is the endodermal tube, surrounded by splanchnic **mesoderm**, formed by the folding of the embryo, which runs from the oropharyngeal membrane to the cloacal membrane and is divided into the foregut (pharynx, esophagus, stomach, proximal duodenum and the buds of the lung, liver, gallbladder and pancreas), the midgut (connected to the yolk sac by the yolk stalk; small intestine and proximal colon) and the hindgut (distal colon, rectum and the cloaca, from which the **allantois** arises).

The two ends of the tube are closed at first by membranes where endoderm touches **ectoderm** without intervening mesoderm. The oropharyngeal membrane ruptures at about day 26 in humans, so the oral cavity is lined by ectoderm (stomodeum) up to a boundary near the back of the tongue: the teeth, the major salivary glands and the anterior taste buds are ectodermal, the posterior taste buds and some glands endodermal (Rothova et al. 2012). The roof of the stomodeum forms Rathke's pouch, the anterior pituitary, which meets the infundibulum from the floor of the diencephalon. The cloacal membrane ruptures later; the cloaca is divided by the urorectal septum into the urogenital sinus and the rectum ([7.5.25](/7.5.25)).

## 30.2. Regionalization of the Gut Tube

The gut acquires its anterior-posterior pattern in two phases.

1. The **endoderm** reads the axial signals. While it is still a sheet, the endoderm is exposed to graded Wnt, FGF and BMP signals, all high posteriorly, and to retinoic acid (RA) from the **somitic mesoderm**. Posterior endoderm, exposed to high levels, expresses *Cdx2* and becomes midgut-hindgut; anterior endoderm, protected by Wnt and BMP antagonists from the anterior visceral endoderm and the head **mesoderm**, expresses *Sox2* and *Hhex* and becomes foregut. Ectopic Wnt or FGF4 in anterior endoderm posteriorizes it and represses foregut genes; inhibiting Wnt in the posterior allows foregut genes there (McLin et al. 2007). Intermediate levels specify the posterior foregut, the precursor of liver and pancreas (*Pdx1*, *Prox1*). RA is needed for the posterior pharynx, the lung, the stomach-duodenum boundary and the dorsal pancreas, and excess RA posteriorizes the pharyngeal endoderm.
2. The mesenchyme instructs the epithelium. Once the tube has formed, the splanchnic mesoderm around it, itself regionalized by a nested code of **Hox genes** (posterior **Hox genes** in the hindgut mesoderm, induced partly by Shh from the endoderm), tells the epithelium which organ to become. In recombination experiments, chick gizzard (stomach) endoderm combined with intestinal mesenchyme forms intestinal epithelium, and intestinal endoderm with stomach mesenchyme forms stomach-like glands, within limits of **competence** set by the endoderm's earlier history (Kedinger, Haffen and colleagues). Misexpressing a posterior *Hox* gene in the mesoderm of the chick midgut changes the adjacent epithelium (Roberts et al. 1998).

The transcription factors that result are mutually exclusive at boundaries. Sox2 (stomach, esophagus) and Cdx2 (intestine) repress each other and meet at the pylorus; Cdx2 is necessary and largely sufficient for intestinal identity: *Cdx2* deleted in the mouse intestine gives an epithelium with stomach and esophageal features, and ectopic Cdx2 in the stomach of adult humans is a hallmark of intestinal metaplasia, a precursor of gastric **cancer**. Nkx2-1 marks the lung and thyroid, Hhex the liver and ventral pancreas, Pdx1 the posterior stomach, duodenum and pancreas.

The gut also has to fit into the body cavity: the human intestine is several times longer than the trunk. It grows and loops in a stereotyped way. The looping pattern is set mechanically: the gut tube grows faster than the dorsal mesentery that attaches it to the body wall, and the elastic mismatch between the tube and the sheet of mesentery makes the tube buckle into loops; the loop size and number in chick, quail, zebra finch and mouse are predicted from the measured growth and stiffness of the two tissues, and cutting the mesentery away makes the gut uncoil into a straight tube (Savin et al. 2011). The human midgut grows so fast that it herniates into the umbilical cord at weeks 6–10 and rotates 270° counterclockwise about the superior mesenteric artery as it returns; malrotation and the failure of the hernia to return (omphalocele) are among the corresponding birth defects. The left-right asymmetry of looping depends on the Nodal–Pitx2 cascade acting on the dorsal mesentery ([7.5.17](/7.5.17)).

## 30.3. The Pharyngeal Arches and Pouches

**Definition 30.3.1 (Pharyngeal Arch, Pharyngeal Pouch)** A **pharyngeal arch** (branchial arch) is one of the paired, bar-shaped swellings on the sides of the embryonic pharynx, covered by **ectoderm** and lined by **endoderm**, filled with **neural crest** mesenchyme around a core of **paraxial mesoderm** and containing an arch artery and a cranial nerve. A **pharyngeal pouch** is an outpocketing of the pharyngeal endoderm between two arches, which meets an ectodermal groove (cleft) from the outside.

Vertebrates have six arches (numbered 1, 2, 3, 4 and 6; the fifth is vestigial or absent in mammals) and four pairs of pouches. In fish the pouches break through to form the gill slits; in amniotes they never open, and the arches and pouches are remodelled into structures of the head, neck and chest.

| Arch | Skeleton (neural crest) | Muscles (paraxial mesoderm) | Nerve | Artery |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 (mandibular) | Meckel's cartilage, maxilla and mandible (membrane bone), malleus, incus | muscles of mastication, anterior digastric, tensor tympani | trigeminal (V) | part of maxillary artery |
| 2 (hyoid) | stapes, styloid process, lesser horn and upper body of the hyoid | muscles of facial expression, stapedius | facial (VII) | stapedial artery (transient) |
| 3 | greater horn and lower body of the hyoid | stylopharyngeus | glossopharyngeal (IX) | common and internal carotid |
| 4 and 6 | laryngeal cartilages | pharyngeal and laryngeal muscles | vagus (X): superior and recurrent laryngeal | aortic arch, right subclavian (4); ductus arteriosus and pulmonary arteries (6) |

The pouches give the glandular and cavity derivatives:

| Pouch | Derivatives |
|---|---|
| 1 | tympanic cavity of the middle ear and the auditory (Eustachian) tube; the first cleft becomes the external acoustic meatus, and the membrane between them the tympanic membrane |
| 2 | epithelium of the palatine tonsil |
| 3 | thymus (ventral part) and inferior parathyroid glands (dorsal part), which migrate together to the chest, the parathyroids stopping behind the thyroid |
| 4 | superior parathyroid glands and the ultimobranchial body, which joins the thyroid and gives its calcitonin-producing C cells |

The thyroid itself is not a pouch derivative: it buds from the midline floor of the pharynx between the first and second arches, at the foramen caecum of the future tongue, descends in front of the trachea along the thyroglossal duct and is specified by the factors Nkx2-1, Pax8, Foxe1 and Hhex. Remnants of the duct give thyroglossal cysts, and failure of the descent an ectopic (lingual) thyroid.

The endoderm organizes the arches. In zebrafish, mutants without pharyngeal endoderm (*casanova*) form neither pouches nor arch cartilage, although the neural crest migrates; FGF3 and FGF8 from **mesoderm** and ectoderm are needed to form the pouches, and pouches are needed to segment the crest into separate arches (Crump et al. 2004). Grafts of pharyngeal endoderm into the chick head induce ectopic cartilage of the type expected from the endoderm's origin (Couly et al. 2002), and Shh from the pouch endoderm keeps the crest cells alive. The thymus epithelium requires the transcription factor Foxn1 (mutated in the athymic *nude* mouse) and attracts lymphoid **progenitors** from the blood ([7.5.29](/7.5.29), [7.15](/7.15)).

DiGeorge syndrome, the most common microdeletion syndrome (22q11.2 deletion, about 1 in 4000 births), illustrates the cooperation: hypoplasia or absence of the thymus (T-cell deficiency) and the parathyroids (hypocalcaemia), outflow-tract heart defects ([7.5.28](/7.5.28)) and a cleft palate and characteristic face. The main gene, *TBX1*, is expressed in the pharyngeal endoderm, mesoderm and ectoderm rather than in the crest; *Tbx1* mutant mice have small posterior pouches and arches and reproduce the phenotype, so a defect of the endodermal and mesodermal environment causes defects in crest-derived structures ([7.5.20](/7.5.20)).

## 30.4. The Stomach

The stomach forms from the posterior foregut, in the Sox2 domain bounded posteriorly by Cdx2 at the pylorus. Its mesenchyme expresses the homeobox gene *Barx1*, which induces the secreted Wnt antagonists sFRP1 and sFRP2; blocking Wnt in this region is what lets the epithelium become stomach, and *Barx1*-null mice develop intestinal epithelium in the stomach (Kim et al. 2005). Within the stomach, posterior (antral) identity needs RA-induced *Hnf1b*-related signals and the Wnt and FGF levels slightly higher than the anterior fundus; the pyloric sphincter forms where BMP4 from the intestinal mesenchyme induces *Nkx2-5* and *Sox9* in the adjacent smooth muscle. Infantile hypertrophic pyloric stenosis, a thickening of this muscle that blocks the outflow of the stomach in about 2 in 1000 infants, is a defect at this boundary.

The stomach dilates (the dorsal wall grows faster, making the greater curvature) and rotates 90° clockwise about its long axis (viewed from above), so that its original left side faces ventrally: this is why the left vagus nerve supplies the anterior surface and the right vagus the posterior, and why the dorsal mesentery becomes the greater omentum. It then tilts so that the pylorus moves up and to the right.

The adult stomach has distinct glands. In the corpus (body), gland units contain surface mucous (pit) cells, neck mucous cells, acid-secreting parietal cells, pepsinogen-secreting chief cells and enteroendocrine cells, renewed from **stem cells** in the isthmus; in the antrum (pylorus) the glands have Lgr5-positive stem cells at their base and gastrin-secreting G cells. After injury, differentiated chief cells can dedifferentiate and act as stem cells (Stange et al. 2013), one of several examples of plasticity in the **endoderm**, where differentiated cells regain stem-cell properties when stem cells are lost (as Paneth-cell precursors in the intestine and club cells in the airway do).

## 30.5. The Intestine and Its Villi

The intestinal epithelium starts as a smooth, pseudostratified tube. During late gestation (from about week 9 in humans, E15 in mice and E7–16 in the chick) its inner surface folds into villi, finger-like projections that increase the absorptive area about tenfold, and later the intervillus regions invaginate into crypts, where the **stem cells** of the adult intestine reside ([7.3.24](/7.3.24)).

In the chick the folds form in three steps, each coinciding with the differentiation of a layer of smooth muscle around the gut (Shyer et al. 2013). When the inner, circumferential muscle layer forms (about E8), the epithelium and its underlying mesenchyme fold into longitudinal ridges; when the longitudinal muscle forms (about E13), the ridges fold into zigzags; when a third, longitudinal muscle layer forms inside the others (about E16), the zigzags break into bumps, the villi. Removing the muscle layers, or preventing their differentiation, prevents the folding, and the patterns are reproduced in physical models in which a growing elastic layer is constrained by a stiffer one: the villi are a mechanical buckling pattern, not a chemical one. Their spacing then positions the stem cells: the tips of the villi concentrate Shh from the epithelium, which induces BMP4 in the mesenchyme clusters at the tips, and BMP inhibits stem-cell fate, so the stem cells become restricted to the regions between the villi, the future crypts (Shyer et al. 2015). In the mouse, by contrast, villi are preceded by clusters of mesenchymal cells that form a spaced pattern by a Turing-like mechanism with PDGF and BMP signalling (Walton et al. 2012, 2016): different species reach the same structure by different combinations of mechanics and signalling ([7.5.22](/7.5.22) discusses the same alternative for hair follicles).

**Formula 30.5.1 (Wavelength of Buckling on an Elastic Foundation)** Model the epithelium with its basal mesenchyme as a thin elastic plate of bending stiffness $B=Eh^3/[12(1-\nu^2)]$ (Young's modulus $E$, thickness $h$, Poisson ratio $\nu$), bonded to an elastic foundation (the outer mesenchyme and muscle) that resists a deflection $w$ with restoring force $Kw$ per unit area, and compressed by a force $P$ per unit width (from growth against the constraint of the muscle). Small deflections satisfy

$$
B\,w''''+P\,w''+K\,w=0 .
\tag{30.1}
$$

A mode $w=w_0\cos kx$ is a solution when $P=P(k)=Bk^2+K/k^2$. The flat state first becomes unstable at the minimum of $P(k)$,

$$
k^*=\Bigl(\frac KB\Bigr)^{1/4},\qquad P_{\mathrm{c}}=2\sqrt{BK},\qquad \lambda^*=\frac{2\pi}{k^*}=2\pi\Bigl(\frac BK\Bigr)^{1/4}\propto h^{3/4}.
\tag{30.2}
$$

The selected wavelength depends only on the ratio of the bending stiffness of the layer to the stiffness of its support, not on how strongly the layer is compressed: a thicker or stiffer epithelium makes fewer, wider folds, a stiffer support more, narrower ones. Beyond threshold, nonlinear terms limit the amplitude, and folds of the predicted wavelength emerge from small random roughness (figure). For a plate on a deep elastic substrate of modulus $E_{\mathrm{s}}$ instead of a Winkler foundation the result is $\lambda^*\approx2\pi h\,(E/3E_{\mathrm{s}})^{1/3}$, linear in thickness; the measured dependence of ridge spacing on the thickness and stiffness of the chick gut layers agreed with such elastic estimates (Shyer et al. 2013). The same instability makes the folds of the brain cortex and of the airway lining under smooth-muscle contraction, and in two dimensions changes in the direction of compression turn ridges into zigzags and spots, the sequence seen in the chick gut.

The epithelium also needs the enteric nervous system, from the vagal and sacral **neural crest** ([7.5.20](/7.5.20)), and, after birth, microbes: germ-free animals have shorter villi, fewer Paneth and goblet cells and an immature immune system of the gut, and bacterial products set normal stem-cell proliferation ([7.5.38](/7.5.38)). Failure of the gut lumen to recanalize after a solid stage (duodenal atresia, frequent in Down syndrome) and Hirschsprung disease (a defect of crest colonization) are the commonest intestinal malformations.

## 30.6. The Liver

### 30.6.1. Induction

The liver and the ventral pancreas arise from neighbouring cells of the ventral foregut **endoderm**, and which one forms depends on the signals each cell receives. In explants of mouse ventral foregut endoderm (E8.0), liver genes (albumin, α-fetoprotein) are turned on only when cardiac **mesoderm** is present; FGF1, FGF2 or FGF8 can replace the cardiac tissue, and inhibiting FGF receptors in the presence of cardiac mesoderm blocks liver gene expression and allows *Pdx1* and pancreatic genes instead (Jung et al. 1999; Deutsch et al. 2001). BMP from the septum transversum mesenchyme, the mesoderm into which the liver bud will grow, is also required (Rossi et al. 2001). The pancreas is thus the fate of ventral endoderm that the cardiac FGF and septum transversum BMP do not reach: a "default" for this region. Endothelial cells, the earliest vessels around the hepatic endoderm, are needed even before blood flows: *Flk1*-null embryos, without endothelium, specify hepatic endoderm but fail to form a liver bud (Matsumoto et al. 2001).

The endoderm must be competent to respond. The forkhead factors FoxA1 and FoxA2, together with GATA4 and GATA6, bind the **enhancers** of liver genes such as albumin in the endoderm before any liver gene is expressed, open the **chromatin** by displacing nucleosomes and so prepare the genes for **induction**: they are pioneer factors (Cirillo et al. 2002), and in mice lacking both *Foxa1* and *Foxa2* in the endoderm no liver bud forms (Lee et al. 2005). Hhex, induced in the ventral endoderm, is required for the hepatoblasts to leave the epithelium and invade the septum transversum; the homeobox factor Prox1 is needed for the same migration.

### 30.6.2. Hepatoblasts, Hepatocytes and Bile Ducts

From about E9 in the mouse (day 22–24 in humans) the hepatic endoderm thickens, becomes pseudostratified and buds into the septum transversum as cords of hepatoblasts, bipotential **progenitors** that will make both hepatocytes and cholangiocytes (bile duct cells), intermingling with endothelial cells and, from E10, with hematopoietic cells that make the fetal liver the main blood-forming organ ([7.5.29](/7.5.29)). The caudal part of the hepatic diverticulum forms the gallbladder and cystic duct.

The choice between the two fates is made by position. Hepatoblasts next to the portal vein mesenchyme receive Jagged1 from it, activating Notch2, and TGF-β, which is highest near the portal vein; they become cholangiocyte precursors (Sox9, HNF1β, Onecut factors), forming a single-layered ductal plate around the vein from about E13.5 in the mouse and week 8 in humans. Parts of the ductal plate become bilayered and form lumens, the future bile ducts, and the rest regresses (ductal plate remodelling). Hepatoblasts away from the portal veins differentiate into hepatocytes under the **nuclear receptor** HNF4α, Wnt–**β-catenin**, hepatocyte **growth factor** and glucocorticoids. Alagille syndrome, caused by mutations in *JAG1* or *NOTCH2*, combines paucity of bile ducts with heart defects (pulmonary stenosis, tetralogy of Fallot; [7.5.28](/7.5.28)), skeletal and eye anomalies: Notch signalling, needed in several organs, fails in all of them. Biliary atresia, an obliteration of the extrahepatic bile ducts in about 1 in 10 000–20 000 infants, is the most common reason for liver transplantation in children; its cause is unknown, but an outbreak in Australian livestock was traced to biliatresone, a plant toxin that destroys bile duct epithelium in zebrafish and mouse models (Lorent et al. 2015).

In the mature liver, hepatocytes form plates one or two cells thick, arranged in hexagonal lobules around a central vein, with portal triads (portal vein, hepatic artery, bile duct) at the corners; blood flows through sinusoids lined by fenestrated sinusoidal endothelial cells (which are mesodermal endothelium, not endoderm), and the position of a hepatocyte along the porto-central axis sets its metabolic functions (zonation, under graded Wnt signalling from the central vein). The liver's capacity to regenerate after partial hepatectomy by division of hepatocytes is discussed in [7.5.35](/7.5.35).

## 30.7. The Lung

### 30.7.1. The Lung Bud

**Definition 30.7.1 (Lung Bud)** The **lung bud** (respiratory diverticulum) is the outgrowth of the ventral foregut **endoderm**, specified by Nkx2-1, that forms the epithelium of the trachea, bronchi and alveoli; its two primary buds (bronchial buds) grow into the surrounding splanchnic mesenchyme, which forms the cartilage, smooth muscle, connective tissue and vessels of the lung.

The respiratory primordium appears in the ventral foregut opposite the fourth to sixth arch level at about day 28 in humans (E9.0–9.5 in the mouse), as a group of cells expressing Nkx2-1 (TTF-1). Its **induction** requires signals from the surrounding **mesoderm**: RA (from the lateral plate mesoderm, partly the cardiac region) and Tbx4 in the mesoderm induce Wnt2 and Wnt2b, which activate β-catenin in the ventral endoderm and are necessary and sufficient for *Nkx2-1*: in mice lacking both *Wnt2* and *Wnt2b*, or β-catenin in the foregut endoderm, no lung forms, and activated β-catenin in the foregut expands the Nkx2-1 domain into the esophagus and stomach (Goss et al. 2009; Harris-Johnson et al. 2009). BMP4 from the ventral mesenchyme represses *Sox2* in the ventral endoderm, and FGF10 from the mesenchyme at the site of budding, acting through FGFR2b on the epithelium, drives outgrowth: *Fgf10*-null mice have a trachea but no lungs (Min et al. 1998; Sekine et al. 1999). Shh from the endoderm, signalling to the mesenchyme, is needed to grow and branch the buds.

The single foregut tube then separates into a dorsal esophagus (Sox2-high) and a ventral trachea (Nkx2-1-high), by a process of septation driven by the mutual repression of Sox2 and Nkx2-1 and by BMP and Wnt from the ventral mesenchyme, while the dorsal mesenchyme keeps Wnt low by Barx1 and sFRPs. Failures of separation cause esophageal atresia with tracheoesophageal fistula (about 1 in 3500 births), in which the esophagus ends blindly and the trachea connects to the lower esophagus; mice lacking *Nkx2-1*, *Shh* or *Gli2/Gli3*, or with reduced BMP signalling, have similar defects. The human trachea is lined by an epithelium of basal **stem cells**, ciliated, secretory (club and goblet), neuroendocrine and rare tuft and ionocyte cells (the latter expressing most of the CFTR of the airway; Montoro et al. 2018; Plasschaert et al. 2018).

### 30.7.2. Branching

The bronchial tree of the mouse is remarkably stereotyped: the same branches, in the same order, form in every embryo. Reconstructing the airways of many mouse embryos at successive stages, Metzger and colleagues (2008) found that the tree is built from three geometric modes, used in a fixed sequence at each position.

**Algorithm 30.7.1 (Programmed Branching of the Lung)** Each growing tip executes one of three subroutines.

1. Domain branching: the tip grows on while daughter branches bud from it in rows, each row in one rotational domain around the parent (dorsal, ventral, lateral), at regular intervals: this lays out the scaffold of the lobes.
2. Planar bifurcation: the tip splits into two, both daughters in the plane of the parent and grandparent: repeated, it makes flat arrays at the edges and surfaces of the lobes.
3. Orthogonal bifurcation: the tip splits into two in a plane rotated by 90° about the parent axis relative to the previous branching plane: repeated alternately with planar bifurcation, it fills the interior of the lobes.

A master routine assigns the subroutines to each lineage in a fixed order (first domain branching along the main bronchi, then planar and orthogonal bifurcations), and a periodicity generator times the branches. About 4 % of the branching events varied among individuals.

The algorithm describes the geometry; the molecules generate it. FGF10 from distal mesenchyme attracts and stimulates the tip; Shh made by the tip epithelium represses *Fgf10* in the mesenchyme just in front of the tip, so the FGF10 source splits into two foci on either side and the tip bifurcates; BMP4 and Sprouty limit the response. Models of this negative feedback, a Turing-type ligand–receptor system on a growing domain (Menshykau et al. 2012), reproduce bifurcation and lateral branching. Mechanics contributes too: lung explants branch faster under higher transmural pressure, smooth-muscle differentiation around the clefts helps sculpt them (Kim et al. 2015), and in mice in which smooth muscle fails to differentiate the epithelium still branches, so muscle wrapping is not required (Young et al. 2020). The organ is instructed by its mesenchyme, as tissue recombination shows: bronchial epithelium wrapped in tracheal mesenchyme grows straight, as the trachea, while tracheal epithelium combined with distal lung mesenchyme buds and branches, as the lung (Wessells 1970; Shannon et al. 1998).

### 30.7.3. Alveoli and Surfactant

Human lung development is divided into five stages: embryonic (weeks 4–7: lung bud, trachea, lobar bronchi), pseudoglandular (weeks 5–17: branching of the conducting airways to the terminal bronchioles; the lung looks like a gland), canalicular (weeks 16–26: respiratory bronchioles, thinning of the epithelium, capillaries approach the airspaces; from about week 22 gas exchange becomes possible), saccular (week 24 to birth: terminal sacs, differentiation of alveolar cells) and alveolar (from about week 36 to adolescence: septation of the sacs into alveoli; most of the 300–500 million alveoli of the adult form after birth). In the mouse the equivalent stages run from E9.5 to about postnatal day 36.

The distal epithelium differentiates into two cell types. Alveolar type 1 (AT1) cells, large and thin (0.1–0.2 μm), cover about 95 % of the alveolar surface and, with the capillary endothelium, form the gas-exchange barrier; alveolar type 2 (AT2) cells, cuboidal, secrete surfactant and act as the stem cells of the alveolus, dividing and replacing AT1 cells after injury. In the mouse, **lineage tracing** shows that AT1 and AT2 cells arise from bipotent **progenitors** in the saccular stage and that their fates are set partly by mechanical stretch from breathing movements and by signals such as Yap/Taz in the AT1 lineage (Desai et al. 2014). The mesenchyme supplies myofibroblasts, which build the secondary septa that subdivide the sacs, lipofibroblasts, and smooth muscle recruited around the airways from FGF10-expressing distal mesenchyme under epithelial BMP4 and Shh.

**Definition 30.7.2 (Pulmonary Surfactant)** **Pulmonary surfactant** is the mixture of phospholipids (mainly dipalmitoylphosphatidylcholine) and surfactant proteins (SP-A, SP-B, SP-C, SP-D), secreted by AT2 cells as lamellar bodies, that forms a film on the liquid lining of the alveoli and lowers its surface tension, more strongly the more the film is compressed.

The surface tension of a curved air–liquid interface creates a pressure difference across it, the **Laplace pressure** ([6.3.19](/6.3.19)). For a spherical alveolus of radius $r$ with surface tension $\gamma$, by the **Young–Laplace equation**,

**Formula 30.7.1 (Laplace Pressure of an Alveolus)**

$$
\Delta p=\frac{2\gamma}{r}.
\tag{30.3}
$$

For water ($\gamma\approx70\ \mathrm{mN\,m^{-1}}$) and $r=100\ \mu\mathrm{m}$, $\Delta p\approx1.4\ \mathrm{kPa}$, about $14\ \mathrm{cm}$ of water, a large fraction of the pressure available for breathing. Worse, because $\Delta p$ falls with $r$ at constant $\gamma$, alveoli connected to the same airway are unstable: a smaller alveolus has a higher internal pressure and empties into a larger one, which then needs less pressure still, so the small ones collapse (atelectasis) and the lung inflates unevenly. Surfactant makes $\gamma$ depend on area: when the film is compressed as an alveolus shrinks, $\gamma$ falls to near $1\ \mathrm{mN\,m^{-1}}$, and when it expands $\gamma$ rises. If $\gamma$ rises with $r$ faster than linearly, $2\gamma(r)/r$ increases with $r$, the larger alveolus has the higher pressure, and connected alveoli equalize instead of collapsing (figure). Surfactant also reduces the work of breathing and the tendency of fluid to be drawn from the capillaries into the airspaces.

Surfactant is made late: in humans it reaches functional amounts at about 34 weeks of gestation. Premature infants born before then develop the respiratory distress syndrome (RDS; hyaline membrane disease), with stiff lungs, collapsing alveoli and hypoxia; it affects most infants born before 28 weeks and was a leading cause of neonatal death until two treatments derived from developmental biology. Glucocorticoids given to the mother before a preterm birth accelerate the maturation of AT2 cells and surfactant production (Liggins and Howie 1972, after the observation that cortisol-treated lambs born prematurely could breathe), and exogenous surfactant instilled into the trachea of the newborn (Fujiwara et al. 1980) reduced mortality from RDS dramatically. Mutations in the genes for SP-B, SP-C or the lipid transporter ABCA3 cause fatal or chronic lung disease in term infants. The mature lung may even signal the timing of birth: in the mouse, SP-A secreted into the amniotic fluid near term activates fetal macrophages that migrate to the uterine wall and promote the inflammatory signals of labour (Condon et al. 2004).

The cell types of the adult lung are the cells of origin of its **cancers**, which lineage-tracing studies in mice have connected to particular progenitors: adenocarcinomas mostly from AT2 cells (and club cells at the bronchioalveolar junction), squamous cell **carcinomas** from basal cells of the airways, and small-cell carcinomas from neuroendocrine cells (Sainz de Aja et al. 2021). Developmental pathways are reactivated in them: *NKX2-1* is amplified in some adenocarcinomas, *SOX2* in squamous carcinomas.

## 30.8. The Pancreas

### 30.8.1. Two Buds

The pancreas forms from two buds of the posterior foregut **endoderm** at the level of the future duodenum: a dorsal bud (mouse E9.0, human day 26) and a ventral bud next to the hepatic diverticulum. As the duodenum rotates, the ventral bud (with the bile duct) swings around behind it to lie below the dorsal bud, and the two fuse in the sixth to seventh week. The ventral bud forms the head and the uncinate process, the dorsal bud the rest; the main pancreatic duct (of Wirsung) is formed by the ventral duct and the distal dorsal duct. In about 10 % of people the ducts do not fuse (pancreas divisum), and if the ventral bud splits and its two halves migrate in opposite directions they can encircle the duodenum (annular pancreas, causing obstruction).

The two buds are induced by different neighbours. The dorsal bud forms where the notochord touches the dorsal endoderm: the notochord, through activin-βB and FGF2, represses *Shh* in this endoderm; Shh is expressed everywhere else in the gut endoderm and prevents pancreas formation, so ectopic Shh blocks the dorsal pancreas and the Hedgehog inhibitor cyclopamine causes ectopic pancreatic tissue in the stomach and duodenum (Kim, Hebrok and Melton 1997; Hebrok et al. 1998). The aorta and the vitelline veins, which contact the endoderm at the sites of the dorsal and ventral buds, are needed for *Pdx1* **induction** and bud formation (Lammert et al. 2001). The ventral bud, as described above, forms where cardiac FGF does not reach (30.6.1).

Pdx1 is required for the buds to grow: mice and humans lacking *Pdx1* have pancreatic agenesis (Jonsson et al. 1994; Stoffers et al. 1997), and **heterozygous** *PDX1* mutations cause a monogenic form of diabetes (MODY4). Ptf1a, a bHLH factor, is required for pancreatic fate itself: **lineage tracing** in *Ptf1a*-null mice shows that cells that would have formed pancreas become duodenal epithelium instead (Kawaguchi et al. 2002). The liver–pancreas decision is reversible in development: a *Pdx1* **transgene** activated in the *Xenopus* liver converts it into pancreas with exocrine and **endocrine** cells (Horb et al. 2003).

### 30.8.2. Tip, Trunk and the Endocrine Cells

The buds form a stratified epithelium that develops microlumens, which fuse into a plexus of tubes and then remodel into a branched tree. From about E12.5 in the mouse the epithelium is divided into tips, expressing Ptf1a, Cpa1 and c-Myc, and trunks, expressing Nkx6-1, Sox9 and Hnf1b, by antagonism between Ptf1a and Nkx6-1 under Notch signalling. Tips are at first **multipotent** and later become acinar cells, which secrete digestive enzymes; trunks form duct cells and the endocrine cells. Endocrine differentiation requires a transient pulse of the bHLH factor Neurogenin3 (Ngn3) in single trunk cells, selected by Notch-mediated **lateral inhibition** (as in neurogenesis, [7.5.19](/7.5.19)); *Ngn3*-null mice have no endocrine cells, and Ngn3 misexpression drives premature endocrine differentiation. Ngn3-positive cells delaminate from the epithelium, aggregate and form the islets of Langerhans, attracting vessels by secreting VEGF.

Within the endocrine lineage, Pax4 and Arx repress each other: Pax4 favours β cells (insulin) and δ cells (somatostatin), Arx α cells (**glucagon**) and PP cells (pancreatic polypeptide); ε cells (ghrelin) are a minor fetal type. Loss of *Arx* converts α cells into β and δ cells, and loss of *Pax4* the reverse; forcing *Pax4* in α cells converts them into β cells, and deleting *Dnmt1* in β cells derepresses *Arx* and converts them into glucagon-producing α cells (Dhawan et al. 2011). Mature β cells are marked by MafA, Nkx6-1 and Pdx1, and the combination of Ngn3, Pdx1 and MafA converts adult mouse acinar cells directly into β-like cells (Zhou et al. 2008). Human islets mix the cell types throughout (about 55 % β, 35 % α), whereas mouse islets have a β-cell core and an α-cell mantle.

### 30.8.3. Stochastic Branching

Unlike the lung, the pancreas branches irregularly. Clonal lineage tracing of single tip **progenitors** with multicolour reporters in the mouse showed that the clones, after E12.5, are fate-restricted (to acinar, ductal or endocrine fates, with rare tripotent cells that disappear after E15.5) and that their sizes and shapes are explained by a simple stochastic rule: tips divide and bifurcate at random and stop when they reach the vicinity of other ducts (Sznurkowska et al. 2018). The same rule describes the kidney and the mammary gland (Hannezo et al. 2017), in contrast to the programmed lung and the **reciprocal inductions** of the kidney ([7.5.25](/7.5.25)).

**Algorithm 30.8.1 (Branching-Annihilating Random Walk)** Represent the tree by its tips in the plane (or in space).

1. At each time step, every active tip turns by a small random angle and advances a fixed distance, laying down a duct behind it.
2. With probability $r_{\mathrm{b}}$ per step, a tip bifurcates into two tips at angles $\pm\theta$.
3. A tip that comes within a distance $R_{\mathrm{a}}$ of any existing duct (except its own most recent segment) terminates permanently.

Early, when ducts are sparse, the number of tips grows exponentially at rate $r_{\mathrm{b}}$; once the interior is filled, tips inside terminate as fast as they are born, only tips at the advancing edge keep branching, and the number of terminated tips catches up with the active ones. The tree fills space with a roughly uniform density of ducts set by $R_{\mathrm{a}}$ without any global instruction, and a subtree founded by one tip has an exponentially distributed size, as found for the labelled clones. The model makes falsifiable predictions, such as the fraction of terminated tips and the distribution of branch lengths between branch points, which matched the data within its simplifications (fate-restricted lineages, a constant branching rate).

B: the number of active tips rises exponentially at first; as the tree becomes crowded, tips inside terminate, only tips at the front keep branching, and the number of terminated tips rises to match the active ones](./plots/pancreas-branching.py)

### 30.8.4. Making β Cells

**Type 1 diabetes** destroys the β cells by autoimmunity, and replacing them is a major goal of regenerative medicine. Knowledge of the signals that make pancreas in the embryo made it possible to recapitulate the sequence in culture: human **pluripotent** **stem cells** are converted into definitive endoderm (activin/Nodal, a pulse of Wnt), primitive gut tube (FGF), posterior foregut (RA, Hedgehog inhibition, BMP inhibition), Pdx1-positive pancreatic progenitors (FGF, protein kinase C activation), Nkx6-1/Ngn3 endocrine precursors (thyroid hormone, γ-secretase inhibition of Notch, ALK5 inhibition) and finally glucose-responsive, insulin-secreting β-like cells, which cure diabetic mice when transplanted (Pagliuca et al. 2014; Rezania et al. 2014). Such cells have since been transplanted into people with type 1 diabetes in clinical trials, with insulin independence reported in some recipients (who need immunosuppression against rejection and autoimmunity), and islets made from a patient's own chemically **induced pluripotent stem cells** have been transplanted in a first case (Wang et al. 2024). β cells in the adult regenerate mainly by division of existing β cells (Dor et al. 2004), not from duct progenitors; under extreme β-cell loss in mice, α and δ cells can convert into insulin-producing cells (Thorel et al. 2010).

## Summary

- Roles of the **endoderm**: **inducer** (heart, notochord, vessels, arches) and epithelium of gut, airways and glands. Definitive endoderm from the anterior streak (high Nodal, Sox17, FoxA2), intercalating into the visceral endoderm. Primitive gut tube (Definition 30.1.1): foregut, midgut, hindgut; oropharyngeal and cloacal membranes; Rathke's pouch.
- Regionalization: graded posterior Wnt, FGF, BMP and RA (Cdx2 posteriorly, Sox2 and Hhex anteriorly, Pdx1 in between); mesenchyme with Hox codes instructs the epithelium (recombination experiments); Sox2–Cdx2 boundary at the pylorus; gut looping by mismatched growth of tube and mesentery; midgut herniation and 270° rotation.
- Pharynx (Definition 30.3.1): arches with crest skeleton, mesodermal muscle, nerve and artery; pouches 1 (middle ear, auditory tube), 2 (tonsil), 3 (thymus, inferior parathyroids), 4 (superior parathyroids, ultimobranchial body); thyroid from the midline floor (Nkx2-1, Pax8); endoderm and FGF3/8 organize the arches; 22q11.2 deletion (*TBX1*): thymus, parathyroid, heart and face.
- Stomach: Barx1–sFRP block Wnt; pyloric sphincter by BMP4–Nkx2-5–Sox9; 90° rotation explains vagal innervation; gland cell types; chief cells dedifferentiate after injury.
- Intestine: villi by mechanical buckling as muscle layers form (ridges, zigzags, villi in the chick); Formula 30.5.1: $\lambda^*=2\pi(B/K)^{1/4}\propto h^{3/4}$, set by stiffness ratio, not by compression; Shh–BMP at villus tips restricts **stem cells** to the future crypts; mouse uses mesenchymal clusters.
- Liver: ventral endoderm + cardiac FGF + septum transversum BMP (+ endothelium) → liver; FoxA1/2 pioneer **competence**; Hhex, Prox1 for budding; hepatoblasts → cholangiocytes near the portal vein (Jagged1–Notch2, TGF-β; ductal plate) or hepatocytes (HNF4α, Wnt, HGF); Alagille syndrome, biliary atresia.
- Lung: lung bud (Definition 30.7.1): RA, Tbx4 → Wnt2/2b → Nkx2-1; FGF10 for outgrowth; Sox2/Nkx2-1 separation of esophagus and trachea (tracheoesophageal fistula); stereotyped branching by domain, planar and orthogonal modes (Algorithm 30.7.1), FGF10–Shh feedback splits tips; mesenchyme decides branched or straight; five stages; AT1 and AT2 cells. Pulmonary surfactant (Definition 30.7.2) and the Laplace pressure $2\gamma/r$ (Formula 30.7.1): constant $\gamma$ makes alveoli unstable; surfactant's area-dependent $\gamma$ stabilizes them; RDS of premature infants, antenatal glucocorticoids, exogenous surfactant; cells of origin of lung **cancers**.
- Pancreas: dorsal bud where the notochord represses Shh, ventral bud where cardiac FGF is absent; Pdx1 (agenesis, MODY4) and Ptf1a (fate); tip (acinar) and trunk (duct, endocrine) domains; Ngn3 pulse by **lateral inhibition**; Pax4 versus Arx for β/δ versus α/PP; stochastic branching-annihilating random walk (Algorithm 30.8.1); stem-cell-derived β cells in clinical trials.

## Questions

1. Describe a tissue-recombination experiment showing that the mesenchyme determines whether the respiratory epithelium branches, and say what control is needed to interpret it.

   **Answer** Separate the epithelium and mesenchyme of an embryonic mouse lung (E11–12) with enzymes; combine bronchial (distal) epithelium with tracheal mesenchyme, and tracheal epithelium with distal lung mesenchyme, and culture the recombinants. Distal epithelium wrapped in tracheal mesenchyme grows as an unbranched tube; tracheal epithelium with distal mesenchyme buds and branches and expresses distal markers (Wessells 1970; Shannon et al. 1998): the mesenchyme is instructive. Controls: homotypic recombinants (distal epithelium with distal mesenchyme, tracheal with tracheal) must behave like the intact tissues, to show that the separation itself did not change their behaviour; and the epithelium alone (with Matrigel and FGF10 or without) defines the baseline.

2. Explain why lung and liver and pancreas all depend on signals from the surrounding **mesoderm**, but in opposite ways for liver and pancreas.

   **Answer** All three arise from foregut **endoderm** made competent by pioneer factors (FoxA1/2, GATA4/6) and need mesodermal signals to be specified and to grow out (Wnt2/2b and FGF10 for the lung, FGF and BMP for the liver, endothelial signals for liver and pancreas). Liver and ventral pancreas come from the same group of ventral cells; FGF from the cardiac mesoderm and BMP from the septum transversum push the cells to liver and away from pancreas, so the pancreas forms from the cells beyond their range. A positive signal for one fate is thus a negative one for its neighbour, and the boundary between liver and ventral pancreas is set by the range of the cardiac signal (as in explants, where removing cardiac mesoderm or inhibiting FGF receptors switches liver genes off and *Pdx1* on).

3. Using Formula 30.5.1, by what factor does the wavelength of the folds change if the epithelial layer doubles in thickness at the same Young's modulus? If instead the foundation becomes four times stiffer?

   **Answer** $\lambda^*\propto(B/K)^{1/4}$ and $B\propto h^3$. Doubling $h$ multiplies $B$ by 8 and $\lambda^*$ by $8^{1/4}=2^{3/4}\approx1.68$. A foundation four times stiffer divides $\lambda^*$ by $4^{1/4}=\sqrt2\approx1.41$, giving narrower, more closely spaced folds. In both cases the threshold force $P_{\mathrm{c}}=2\sqrt{BK}$ also changes (by $\sqrt8\approx2.8$ and by 2), but the wavelength does not depend on how far the compression exceeds the threshold, only on the stiffness ratio.

4. Experiments in the chick show that villi do not form if the smooth muscle layers are removed. Why does this argue against a purely chemical (Turing) mechanism, and what experiment would test whether mechanics is also sufficient?

   **Answer** In a Turing mechanism the spacing arises from reaction and diffusion of signals in the epithelium and mesenchyme; removing an outer muscle layer that makes none of the relevant signals should not abolish the pattern. That folding requires the muscle, and that each new muscle layer coincides with a change in pattern (ridges, zigzags, villi), points to compression by the constraining layer. To test sufficiency, replace the muscle with an artificial constraint: wrap gut tissue lacking muscle in an elastic tube or a stiff gel of known modulus, or build a synthetic bilayer (a growing or swelling gel bonded to a stiffer outer layer) and show that it reproduces the folds with the spacing predicted from measured thicknesses and moduli; Shyer et al. (2013) did both, with silicone models reproducing ridges and zigzags.

5. A premature infant born at 28 weeks develops respiratory distress. Explain the physics and the development behind it, and why two treatments work.

   **Answer** At 28 weeks the lung is in the saccular stage, with immature AT2 cells that have not yet produced enough surfactant (functional levels at about 34 weeks). Without surfactant the surface tension of the alveolar lining is near that of water, so the Laplace pressure $2\gamma/r$ needed to keep the small sacs open is high and greatest in the smallest ones; they collapse at the end of each expiration and must be reopened with great effort, and connected sacs are unstable, the small ones emptying into larger ones (Formula 30.7.1). Antenatal glucocorticoids given to the mother accelerate AT2 maturation and surfactant synthesis (the glucocorticoid surge normally does this near term); exogenous surfactant instilled into the trachea supplies the missing film directly, lowering $\gamma$ and making it area-dependent, which stabilizes the alveoli.

6. For a water-lined alveolus of radius 50 μm, compute the Laplace pressure, and compare it with an alveolus of radius 100 μm with a surfactant film at $\gamma=5\ \mathrm{mN\,m^{-1}}$.

   **Answer** Water: $\Delta p=2\times0.070/(50\times10^{-6})=2800\ \mathrm{Pa}\approx28\ \mathrm{cm}$ of water. With surfactant at $r=100\ \mu\mathrm{m}$: $2\times0.005/(100\times10^{-6})=100\ \mathrm{Pa}\approx1\ \mathrm{cm}$ of water, almost 30 times less. The normal transpulmonary pressure in quiet breathing is only a few cm of water, which shows why a lung without surfactant is so hard to inflate.

7. A child has hypocalcaemic seizures in the first week of life, recurrent infections and a heart murmur from an interrupted aortic arch. Explain which embryonic structures are affected and why one deletion affects them all.

   **Answer** Hypocalcaemia reflects absent or small parathyroid glands (pouches 3 and 4), infections reflect thymic hypoplasia (pouch 3, T-cell deficiency), and the interrupted aortic arch is a defect of the fourth arch artery, whose remodelling depends on cardiac **neural crest** and on the pharyngeal environment. This is the 22q11.2 deletion syndrome. The deleted region includes *TBX1*, expressed in the endoderm, mesoderm and **ectoderm** of the posterior pharyngeal arches; its **haploinsufficiency** reduces the posterior pouches and arches and the **second heart field**, and the crest cells that migrate through this environment cannot build the structures normally. One gene acting in the shared pharyngeal environment of several organs explains the combination.

8. In Algorithm 30.8.1, how would the tree change if the annihilation radius $R_{\mathrm{a}}$ were doubled, and how if the branching probability were doubled? Which parameter sets the final duct density?

   **Answer** Doubling $R_{\mathrm{a}}$ makes tips stop farther from existing ducts, so the space between ducts roughly doubles and the duct density (length per area in two dimensions) halves; the tree is sparser and has fewer, longer branches. Doubling $r_{\mathrm{b}}$ makes the tree branch more often and fill space faster, but tips then crowd sooner and more of them terminate, so the final density in the filled interior changes little: density is set mainly by $R_{\mathrm{a}}$ (and the step length), while $r_{\mathrm{b}}$ sets the speed of filling and the branch length between bifurcations. This separation is a testable prediction: mutations that change proliferation should alter the tempo more than the spacing.

9. Why does cyclopamine, a Hedgehog inhibitor, cause ectopic pancreatic tissue in the stomach and duodenum, while it causes holoprosencephaly in the brain?

   **Answer** In the gut endoderm Shh is expressed widely and represses pancreatic fate; the dorsal pancreas forms only where the notochord represses *Shh* in the dorsal endoderm. Blocking Hedgehog signalling everywhere removes this repression, so posterior-foregut endoderm that is competent (Pdx1-positive regions of stomach and duodenum) forms pancreatic tissue. In the head, Shh from the prechordal mesoderm and ventral forebrain is required to split the eye field and the forebrain into two halves ([7.5.18](/7.5.18), [7.5.22](/7.5.22)); blocking it gives a single fused forebrain and cyclopia. The same signal can be inhibitory in one tissue and required in another, so the teratogenic effect depends on where and when it acts.

10. Explain how a stem-cell protocol for making β cells recapitulates embryonic development, step by step, and give one reason why the cells still differ from adult β cells.

    **Answer** Each step supplies the signal the embryo uses at the corresponding stage: activin (Nodal) with a pulse of Wnt makes definitive endoderm, as in the anterior **primitive streak**; FGF makes primitive gut tube; retinoic acid with Hedgehog inhibition (mimicking notochord repression of Shh) and BMP inhibition makes Pdx1-positive posterior foregut; FGF and PKC activation expand Pdx1/Nkx6-1 pancreatic **progenitors**; Notch inhibition (γ-secretase inhibitor, mimicking release from **lateral inhibition**) with thyroid hormone and ALK5 inhibition induces Ngn3 and endocrine precursors; and further maturation gives insulin-secreting cells. They differ from adult β cells because maturation in vivo takes months and depends on cues the culture lacks (islet architecture with α and δ cells, vascular and neural contacts, postnatal nutritional changes): in-vitro cells have a less precise glucose threshold, lower insulin content and some off-target (for example enterochromaffin-like) cells, and they mature further only after transplantation.
