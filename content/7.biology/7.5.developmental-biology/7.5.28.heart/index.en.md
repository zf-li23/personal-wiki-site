The heart is the first organ to function. In a human embryo it begins to beat at about 22 days, before most other organs have been specified, and from then on the embryo depends on it: an embryo of a few millimetres can still obtain oxygen by diffusion, but growth beyond that requires a circulation. The heart develops from the splanchnic layer of the lateral plate **mesoderm**, the layer that lines the **endoderm** ([7.5.9](/7.5.9)), and its formation draws on almost every principle of the course: **induction** by the **endoderm** and inhibition by the **neural tube**, distinct **progenitor** fields added in sequence, left-right asymmetry ([7.5.17](/7.5.17)), a contribution from the **neural crest** ([7.5.20](/7.5.20)), an **epithelial–mesenchymal transition** to make the valves, and mechanics, both the forces of growth that loop the tube and the forces of blood flow that shape it. Congenital heart defects, the commonest birth defects, are failures of these steps. The blood vessels and blood are the subject of the next chapter ([7.5.29](/7.5.29)); the physiology of the adult heart and circulation belongs to [7.14](/7.14).

## 28.1. Where the Heart Comes From

After **gastrulation** the lateral plate **mesoderm** splits into two layers: the somatic (parietal) mesoderm, which with the **ectoderm** forms the body wall (somatopleure), and the splanchnic (visceral) mesoderm, which with the **endoderm** forms the gut wall (splanchnopleure); the space between them is the coelom, later divided into the pericardial, pleural and peritoneal cavities. The heart forms from the anterior splanchnic mesoderm, on both sides of the anterior intestinal portal, and comes to lie in the pericardial cavity ventral to the foregut.

The cells come from the **primitive streak**. In mouse and chick, cardiac **progenitors** are among the first mesodermal cells to ingress, from the anterior part of the streak at the early streak stage (about 50 cells in the mouse), and they migrate anterolaterally on each side. Labelling experiments in the chick show that the progenitors are already arranged in order: cells that ingress at different positions along the streak come to lie in an anterior-posterior sequence in the heart field, those destined for the outflow tract most anterior, then the ventricles, then the atria and sinus venosus, and explants taken at this stage already beat when cultured (Stalsberg and DeHaan 1969). In the mouse, clones labelled in the epiblast before gastrulation show that the progenitors of the different chambers segregate early, and the earliest cardiovascular progenitors are marked by the basic helix-loop-helix factor Mesp1 as they leave the streak (Lescroart et al. 2014).

## 28.2. Induction of Cardiogenic Mesoderm

**Definition 28.2.1 (Cardiogenic Mesoderm)** The **cardiogenic mesoderm** (heart field, heart-forming region) is the region of anterior lateral plate (splanchnic) **mesoderm** whose cells are specified to form the heart: myocardium, endocardium and their derivatives.

Three classes of experiment show how it is specified.

1. Removal. In chick and *Xenopus*, removing the anterior **endoderm** prevents heart formation from the adjacent mesoderm, while posterior endoderm cannot replace it (Nascone and Mercola 1995; Schultheiss et al. 1995).
2. Sufficiency. Anterior endoderm combined with posterior lateral mesoderm in culture induces beating heart tissue from mesoderm that would otherwise make blood and vessels; BMP2 or BMP4 beads applied to anterior **paraxial mesoderm** induce cardiac genes there.
3. Inhibition. The **neural tube** and notochord suppress heart formation: Wnt3a and Wnt8 from the neural tube and the posterior mesoderm inhibit heart and promote blood, and Noggin and Chordin from the notochord block BMP near the midline.

The combination places the heart where BMP is present and Wnt absent (Marvin et al. 2001; Schneider and Mercola 2001). The anterior endoderm both provides BMP2 and FGF8 and removes the inhibition, by secreting the Wnt antagonists Dickkopf-1 (Dkk1), Cerberus and Crescent; misexpression of Dkk1 or Crescent in posterior mesoderm induces heart genes, and Wnt8 in anterior mesoderm suppresses them. The timing is biphasic: canonical Wnt signalling promotes mesoderm formation and the expansion of cardiac **progenitors** early, then must be shut off for them to differentiate, which is why protocols that make cardiomyocytes from human **pluripotent** **stem cells** activate Wnt for a day and then inhibit it (Lian et al. 2012).

The signals act through a gene regulatory network whose core is conserved from flies to mammals.

- *Nkx2-5*, a homeobox gene, is the vertebrate homologue of *Drosophila tinman*, whose mutants lack the dorsal vessel, the fly heart. Mouse *Nkx2-5* mutants form a heart tube that fails to loop and to form chambers; in humans **heterozygous** *NKX2-5* mutations cause atrial septal defects with conduction block.
- GATA4 (with GATA5 and GATA6), T-box factors (Tbx5, Tbx20, Tbx1), Mef2c and the bHLH factors Hand1 and Hand2 cross-activate each other and *Nkx2-5* and together activate the structural genes of cardiomyocytes. The combination of Gata4, Mef2c and Tbx5 (with Hand2) is sufficient to reprogram fibroblasts directly into cardiomyocyte-like cells (Ieda et al. 2010), a test of the network's sufficiency.
- Mesp1, activated early by Eomesodermin, promotes migration from the streak and represses alternative fates (*Brachyury*, *Sox17*, *Gsc*); it is required for the heart fields to form.

## 28.3. The Heart Fields

**Definition 28.3.1 (First and Second Heart Fields)** The **first heart field** is the population of cardiac **progenitors** in the cardiac crescent that differentiates first and forms the linear heart tube, which gives rise mainly to the left ventricle and parts of the atria. The **second heart field** is the population of progenitors in the pharyngeal and splanchnic **mesoderm** medial and dorsal to the crescent, which remains proliferative and undifferentiated, is added to both poles of the tube later, and forms the outflow tract and right ventricle (from its anterior part) and most of the atria and the inflow (from its posterior part).

The second heart field was discovered in 2001 by three groups who traced cells added to the outflow tract of the chick and mouse heart after the tube had formed (Kelly et al. 2001; Mjaatvedt et al. 2001; Waldo et al. 2001). Genetic **lineage tracing** with *Isl1-Cre* in the mouse (Cai et al. 2003) labelled most of the right ventricle, outflow tract and atria but few cells of the left ventricle, and *Isl1* mutants lack the outflow tract and right ventricle, so the left ventricle is the only chamber made mainly from the first field. A retrospective clonal analysis, in which a randomly activated *nlaacZ* reporter labels a clone in about one embryo in a thousand and the clones are mapped in the fetal heart, showed that the two fields segregate as two lineages before the crescent forms (Meilhac et al. 2004). The second field is maintained proliferative by FGF8 and FGF10, Shh from the pharyngeal **endoderm** and canonical Wnt, and its differentiation is delayed by Tbx1, the gene deleted in 22q11.2 **deletion** syndrome (28.8). Retinoic acid, high posteriorly, gives its posterior part an atrial and inflow identity, which explains why excess RA expands atrial tissue at the expense of ventricle (28.8). Remarkably, the second field also contains the progenitors of the muscles of the jaw, face and neck: some clones contain both right-ventricular myocardium and branchiomeric muscle, a cardiopharyngeal lineage shared with tunicates (28.7).

Two other populations contribute.

- The proepicardium, a cluster of cells at the venous pole derived from the septum transversum and lateral mesoderm (Tbx18, Wt1), spreads over the heart surface as the epicardium; epicardial cells undergo **EMT**, enter the myocardium and form cardiac fibroblasts and the smooth muscle and pericytes of the coronary vessels.
- The cardiac **neural crest**, from the hindbrain between the otic vesicle and **somite** 3, migrates through pharyngeal arches 3, 4 and 6 into the outflow tract, where it forms the aorticopulmonary septum and smooth muscle of the arch arteries ([7.5.20](/7.5.20)).

## 28.4. The Heart Tube and the First Beat

### 28.4.1. Fusion

The two halves of the cardiac crescent are brought together at the ventral midline as the foregut folds, and they fuse into a single tube with an inner endocardium and an outer myocardium separated by an **extracellular matrix**, the cardiac jelly. The tube is suspended in the pericardial cavity by a dorsal mesocardium, through which second-field cells continue to enter. The bilateral origin was shown by cutting the ventral midline of a chick embryo, which produces two hearts, one on each side (cardia bifida; Gräper 1907; DeHaan 1959). Mutants with cardia bifida identify what drives the fusion: in the zebrafish *miles apart* mutant, the sphingosine-1-phosphate receptor S1pr2 is missing from the **endoderm** and the cardiac precursors fail to migrate to the midline; *Gata5* (*faust*) and *Sox32* (*casanova*) mutants, which lack endoderm, also have two hearts; in the mouse, loss of *Foxp4* or *Gata4* has the same effect. The endoderm thus specifies the heart, guides its precursors (in the chick along a gradient of **fibronectin**) and pulls the two fields together mechanically.

### 28.4.2. The Pacemaker

The heart begins to contract while the tube is still forming, at about the 10-somite stage in the chick and about day 22 in humans. Every cardiomyocyte of the early tube can contract spontaneously, but at different rates: rates rise from the outflow tract to the inflow, and the most rapid cells, in the sinus venosus, set the rhythm for the whole tube, while waves of depolarization travel from the venous to the arterial pole and produce a peristaltic contraction (de Jong et al. 1992). Later the pacemaker becomes the sinoatrial node, formed from Tbx18- and Shox2-positive **progenitors** at the right sinus horn that express the pacemaker channel HCN4 and are kept from becoming working myocardium by Tbx3, which represses *Nkx2-5* and the genes of fast conduction. Why does the whole tube beat at the rate of its fastest part?

**Formula 28.4.1 (A Chain of Coupled Excitable Cells)** Represent each cell $i=1,\dots,n$ by the FitzHugh–Nagumo equations, a two-variable caricature of an excitable membrane, coupled to its neighbours through **gap junctions** with conductance $D$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\dot v_i&=v_i-\tfrac13v_i^3-w_i+I+D\,(v_{i+1}-2v_i+v_{i-1}),\\
\dot w_i&=\varepsilon_i\,(v_i+a-bw_i),
\end{aligned}
\tag{28.1}
$$

where $v$ is the membrane variable, $w$ a slow recovery variable, $I$ a constant current that puts the isolated cell on a **limit cycle** (spontaneous beating; the cycle appears at a **Hopf bifurcation** as $I$ increases, [2.4](/2.4)), and $\varepsilon_i$ sets the rate of recovery and hence the intrinsic rate. Let $\varepsilon_i$ fall from the inflow to the outflow end.

Uncoupled, each cell beats at its own rate. Coupled, the chain beats at nearly the rate of its fastest cell, and each beat starts at the inflow end and travels as a wave. The mechanism is not averaging. For weakly coupled phase oscillators with an odd coupling function, such as the **Kuramoto model**, a locked chain beats at the mean of the intrinsic frequencies. Excitable cells behave differently: the fastest cell fires first, its depolarization spreads and excites the slower neighbours before their own slow depolarization reaches threshold, and each wave resets their cycle, an "overdrive" of the slower cells by the faster one. A pacemaker of a relaxation oscillator chain is therefore the region of highest frequency, provided the coupling is strong enough for the waves to propagate. The same principle applies to the **segmentation clock** ([7.5.23](/7.5.23)) and to the waves of *Dictyostelium* ([7.5.13](/7.5.13)), where the fastest centres also dominate.

## 28.5. Looping

**Definition 28.5.1 (Cardiac Looping)** **Cardiac looping** is the transformation of the straight heart tube into a helical loop, normally convex to the right (D-loop), in which the future ventricles move to the right and ventrally and the future atria dorsally and anteriorly, so that the chambers come into the arrangement needed for their later connection and septation.

Looping is the first morphological sign of left-right asymmetry in vertebrates, and its direction follows the left-sided Nodal–Pitx2 cascade ([7.5.17](/7.5.17)): in mice with **situs inversus** the loop is reversed (L-loop), and in mutants with **heterotaxy** it is random or abnormal. It is also, at least partly, intrinsic to the heart: an explanted chick heart tube still loops to the right in culture, without the rest of the embryo, and blood flow helps to complete the process.

How a straight tube makes a helix has been modelled as a buckling instability. Reconstructions of the mouse heart in three dimensions over time show that the tube grows rapidly in length, by addition of second-field cells and by growth, while its two poles stay attached to the body at fixed positions (Le Garrec et al. 2017). A rod that lengthens between fixed ends must bend.

**Formula 28.5.1 (Buckling of a Tube Growing Between Fixed Poles)** Model the tube as an elastic rod of length $L$, bending stiffness $EI$ and cross-sectional area $A$, clamped at both ends a distance $L_0$ apart. If it grows ($L>L_0$) the rod is under compression; the straight state becomes unstable when the compressive force exceeds the clamped–clamped Euler load

$$
F_{\mathrm{c}}=\frac{4\pi^2EI}{L^2},
\qquad\text{that is, at a compressive strain}\qquad
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{c}}=\frac{F_{\mathrm{c}}}{EA}=\frac{4\pi^2}{L^2}\frac{I}{A},
\tag{28.2}
$$

which for a thin-walled tube of radius $R$ ($I/A=R^2/2$) is $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{c}}=2\pi^2R^2/L^2$, small for a slender tube. Beyond threshold the rod adopts the first buckling mode, $w(x)=\tfrac12w_0\bigl(1-\cos(2\pi x/L)\bigr)$; if it is inextensible, its excess length is $\Delta L=\int_0^L\tfrac12w'^2\,\mathrm{d}x=\pi^2w_0^2/(4L)$, so the amplitude grows as $w_0=(2/\pi)\sqrt{L\,\Delta L}$. Near threshold the amplitude obeys the normal form $\mu w_0-\kappa w_0^3+h=0$, with $\mu$ the growth beyond threshold and $h$ a small bias. With $h=0$ the bifurcation is a symmetric pitchfork: the tube bends right or left with equal probability. Any small left-right asymmetry, $h\neq0$, unfolds the pitchfork, the **cusp catastrophe** of [7.5.10](/7.5.10), so that the tube always bends to the side favoured by the bias.

In the mouse the bias comes from asymmetries at the poles: at the arterial pole the right and left sides of the second field ingress at different rates and with a rotation, and at the venous pole the left side is displaced, both under the control of Pitx2 and Nodal (Le Garrec et al. 2017; Desgrange et al. 2020). The model explains why the loop is a helix rather than a planar curve (the asymmetries at the two poles act in different directions), why small asymmetries are enough, and why the direction can be randomized when the left-right pathway fails. A real heart tube is not a uniform elastic rod: its myocardium grows differentially along the outer curvature and remodels actively, and flow and the cardiac jelly add forces. The buckling model captures the geometric necessity, not all the mechanics.

## 28.6. Chambers, Septa and Valves

### 28.6.1. Chambers Balloon from the Tube

After looping, the chambers form by "ballooning": the working myocardium of the future atria and ventricles expands from the outer curvature of the tube, while the inner curvature, the atrioventricular canal, the outflow tract and the inflow retain a slowly conducting, poorly proliferating primary myocardium (Christoffels et al. 2000). Chamber myocardium expresses *Nppa* (atrial natriuretic factor), *Cx40* and *Cx43* and proliferates; primary myocardium is held in its state by Tbx2 and Tbx3, which repress the chamber program, and by BMP2 in the atrioventricular canal. The pattern of ballooning thus prefigures both the four chambers and the conduction system: the slow, primary-myocardium regions become the sinoatrial and atrioventricular nodes, and the delay between atrial and ventricular contraction originates at the atrioventricular canal.

Inside the ventricles, cardiomyocytes form trabeculae, sheets of muscle projecting into the lumen that increase the surface for oxygen exchange before coronary circulation exists. Trabeculation needs Neuregulin-1 from the endocardium acting on ErbB2 and ErbB4 receptors in the myocardium, and Notch signalling in the endocardium; later most trabeculae are compacted into the thick ventricular wall. Failure of **compaction** gives left ventricular noncompaction cardiomyopathy.

### 28.6.2. Septation and the Endocardial Cushions

The single lumen is divided by septa that grow towards the endocardial cushions, swellings of cardiac jelly in the atrioventricular canal and the outflow tract.

1. The cushions form by an endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition: under BMP2 and TGF-β from the overlying myocardium and Notch signalling within the endocardium, endocardial cells activate Snail, Slug and Twist, lose their junctions and invade the cardiac jelly as mesenchyme (the endocardial counterpart of the **epithelial–mesenchymal transition** of [7.3.23](/7.3.23)). In explants of chick atrioventricular canal on **collagen** gels, the endocardium transforms only if it is cultured with its myocardium, the classic assay of this **induction**. The cushions are later remodelled into the valve leaflets.
2. Atrial septation. The septum primum grows down from the roof of the common atrium towards the atrioventricular cushions; before it closes the gap (ostium primum), perforations open in its upper part (ostium secundum). The septum secundum then grows to its right, leaving an oval opening, the foramen ovale, whose flap valve is the septum primum: blood can pass from right to left atrium, but not back. The mesenchyme of the dorsal mesocardium (the "spina vestibuli", from the second field) closes the primary foramen.
3. Ventricular septation. The muscular septum forms at the boundary between the left-ventricle domain of *Tbx5* and the right-ventricle domain, and grows towards the cushions; moving the *Tbx5* boundary moves the septum, and removing *Tbx5* from the ventricles gives a single ventricle without a septum, as in a lizard (Takeuchi et al. 2003; Koshiba-Takeuchi et al. 2009). The membranous part of the septum, the last to close, comes from the cushions.
4. Outflow-tract septation. The single outflow vessel (truncus arteriosus and conus) is divided by the aorticopulmonary septum, formed by cardiac **neural crest** within the outflow cushions, which spirals as it grows so that the aorta comes to connect with the left ventricle and the pulmonary trunk with the right.

### 28.6.3. Coronary Vessels and the Conduction System

The coronary vessels form when the myocardium becomes too thick for diffusion from the lumen. Their endothelium has three sources: the sinus venosus, whose endothelium sprouts over and into the ventricles (Red-Horse et al. 2010), the endocardium, which contributes vessels in the inner myocardium and the septum (Wu et al. 2012; Tian et al. 2014), and the proepicardium; their smooth muscle and pericytes come from the epicardium. The coronary arteries connect to the aorta secondarily, by ingrowth of the peritruncal plexus. The conduction system, the sinoatrial node, the atrioventricular node and bundle, the bundle branches and the Purkinje network, develops from myocardium, not from nerves: the nodes from primary myocardium (28.6.1), the bundle branches and Purkinje fibres from trabecular myocardium, specified by endothelin and Neuregulin signals from the endocardium and arteries.

## 28.7. Hearts in Evolution

| Animal | Heart | Developmental difference |
|---|---|---|
| *Drosophila* | dorsal vessel, a contractile tube of about 100 cells | *tinman* (Nkx2-5) and *pannier* (GATA) specify it; Dpp from the **ectoderm**, as BMP in vertebrates |
| Tunicate (*Ciona*) | a single-chambered tube of a few hundred cells | made by two pairs of Mesp-positive founder cells (B7.5 lineage) whose **asymmetric divisions**, decided by FGF signalling, give the heart and the pharyngeal (atrial siphon) muscles: a cardiopharyngeal lineage like that of the vertebrate second field (Stolfi et al. 2010) |
| Zebrafish | two chambers, atrium and ventricle, in series | the second field contributes the outflow tract (bulbus arteriosus) and the arterial end of the ventricle but not the atrium (Zhou et al. 2011); adults regenerate the ventricle ([7.5.35](/7.5.35)) |
| Amphibians | two atria, one ventricle | an atrial septum, no ventricular septum; trabeculae keep oxygenated and deoxygenated streams partly separate |
| Reptiles (lizards, snakes, turtles) | two atria, ventricle partly divided | *Tbx5* is expressed in a gradient across the ventricle rather than with a sharp boundary; crocodilians, with a sharp boundary, have a full septum (Koshiba-Takeuchi et al. 2009) |
| Birds and mammals | four chambers, separated pulmonary and systemic circulations | full ventricular septum at the *Tbx5* boundary; a large, second-field-derived right ventricle that pumps to the lungs |

The second heart field thus expanded during vertebrate evolution with the addition of new chambers, and the separation of a pulmonary circulation, which allowed high systemic pressure with low pulmonary pressure, required both the right ventricle (from the second field) and a septum positioned by a T-box boundary.

## 28.8. Congenital Heart Disease

Congenital heart defects occur in about 8–9 of every 1000 live births, the commonest major birth defects, and in a larger fraction of miscarried fetuses. Most can now be repaired, so that more adults than children live with congenital heart disease in many countries. The defects map onto developmental steps.

- Septal defects: ventricular septal defects (VSD, the commonest single defect), atrial septal defects (ASD, often at the foramen ovale) and atrioventricular septal defects (AVSD, a failure of the cushions, frequent in Down syndrome).
- Outflow tract defects (conotruncal), arising from the second field and the cardiac crest: tetralogy of Fallot (a VSD, an aorta overriding the septum, narrowing of the right ventricular outflow, and secondary right ventricular hypertrophy; the outflow septum is displaced anteriorly), transposition of the great arteries (the aorta from the right and the pulmonary trunk from the left ventricle, as if the septum had not spiralled), persistent truncus arteriosus (no septation), double-outlet right ventricle.
- Valve defects: pulmonary and aortic stenosis, the bicuspid aortic valve (1–2 % of people, often with *NOTCH1* mutations).
- Defects of flow and growth: hypoplastic left heart syndrome, coarctation of the aorta; reduced flow through a chamber in the fetus limits its growth, and fetal intervention that opens a stenotic aortic valve can rescue left ventricular growth in some fetuses.
- Failure of the fetal shunts to close after birth: patent ductus arteriosus (common in premature infants) and patent foramen ovale (in about a quarter of adults, usually harmless).

## 28.9. The Fetal Circulation and Its Change at Birth

**Definition 28.9.1 (Fetal Circulation)** The **fetal circulation** is the arrangement of the circulation before birth, in which gas exchange takes place in the **placenta** and the lungs are bypassed by three shunts: the ductus venosus, which carries placental blood past the liver, the foramen ovale, from the right to the left atrium, and the ductus arteriosus, from the pulmonary trunk to the descending aorta; the two ventricles work in parallel rather than in series.

Oxygenated blood (saturation about 80 %) returns from the placenta in the umbilical vein; about half of it bypasses the liver through the ductus venosus into the inferior vena cava. In the right atrium the stream from the inferior vena cava is directed by a ridge (the crista dividens) through the foramen ovale into the left atrium, so that the best-oxygenated blood goes to the left ventricle and thence to the heart and brain; blood from the superior vena cava goes mainly into the right ventricle. Because the fluid-filled lungs offer a high resistance, most of the right ventricular output passes through the ductus arteriosus into the descending aorta and back to the placenta through the umbilical arteries; only about 10–25 % of the combined ventricular output passes through the fetal lungs (less in fetal sheep, more in human fetuses near term).

At birth the circulation is rearranged within minutes to hours. The first breaths expand the lungs, and the rise in oxygen tension and the release of nitric oxide and prostacyclin dilate the pulmonary arterioles, so the pulmonary resistance falls about tenfold; clamping the cord removes the low-resistance placenta, so the systemic resistance rises. Left atrial pressure, now fed by a large pulmonary venous return, exceeds right atrial pressure and presses the flap of the septum primum against the septum secundum, closing the foramen ovale functionally. The ductus arteriosus constricts in response to oxygen and to the fall of prostaglandin E2 from the placenta; it closes functionally within a day or two and anatomically within weeks, becoming the ligamentum arteriosum. Prostaglandin inhibitors (indomethacin, ibuprofen) are used to close a patent ductus in premature infants; prostaglandin E1 is given to keep it open in infants whose circulation depends on it, as in hypoplastic left heart syndrome or transposition, until surgery.

**Formula 28.9.1 (A Resistor Model of the Shunts)** Treat the pulmonary trunk and aorta as two nodes at pressures $p_1$ and $p_2$ relative to the venous side, fed by the right and left ventricular outputs $Q_{\mathrm{R}}$ and $Q_{\mathrm{L}}$, with a pulmonary resistance $R_{\mathrm{p}}$ from node 1, a systemic resistance $R_{\mathrm{s}}$ (body and placenta) from node 2, and the ductus, of resistance $R_{\mathrm{d}}$, between them. Conservation of flow at the two nodes (the analogue of **Kirchhoff's current law**) gives

$$
Q_{\mathrm{R}}=\frac{p_1}{R_{\mathrm{p}}}+\frac{p_1-p_2}{R_{\mathrm{d}}},
\qquad
Q_{\mathrm{L}}+\frac{p_1-p_2}{R_{\mathrm{d}}}=\frac{p_2}{R_{\mathrm{s}}}.
\tag{28.3}
$$

For a wide ductus ($R_{\mathrm{d}}\to0$) the two nodes are at the same pressure $p=(Q_{\mathrm{R}}+Q_{\mathrm{L}})R_{\mathrm{p}}R_{\mathrm{s}}/(R_{\mathrm{p}}+R_{\mathrm{s}})$, and the combined output divides between lungs and body in inverse proportion to their resistances: the pulmonary fraction is $R_{\mathrm{s}}/(R_{\mathrm{p}}+R_{\mathrm{s}})$. With $R_{\mathrm{p}}=8R_{\mathrm{s}}$, as in the fetus, about 11 % of the output goes to the lungs; the ductal flow $Q_{\mathrm{R}}-p/R_{\mathrm{p}}$ is positive (right to left). When $R_{\mathrm{p}}$ falls below $R_{\mathrm{s}}\,Q_{\mathrm{L}}/Q_{\mathrm{R}}$, the ductal flow reverses and becomes left to right, so a ductus that stays open after birth overloads the lungs; when the ductus closes ($R_{\mathrm{d}}\to\infty$), the pulmonary flow equals the right ventricular output. The model ignores pulsatility and the foramen ovale, but it shows why the shunts direct blood away from the lungs before birth without any valve, and why the fall of pulmonary resistance alone reverses them.

## Summary

- Origin: anterior splanchnic (lateral plate) **mesoderm**, ingressing early from the anterior streak; **progenitors** are ordered by future region already at **gastrulation**; Mesp1 marks them.
- **Induction** (Definition 28.2.1): BMP2 and FGF8 from anterior **endoderm**, Wnt inhibition by Dkk1, Cerberus and Crescent; Wnt3a/8 from the **neural tube** and Noggin/Chordin from the notochord limit the field. Network: Nkx2-5 (*tinman*), Gata4, Tbx5, Mef2c, Hand; Gata4 + Mef2c + Tbx5 reprogram fibroblasts.
- Fields (Definition 28.3.1): first heart field → crescent, tube, left ventricle; second heart field (Isl1, Tbx1, FGF8/10; RA for its posterior part) → outflow tract, right ventricle, atria; proepicardium → epicardium, coronary smooth muscle, fibroblasts; cardiac crest → outflow septum. Second field shares progenitors with head muscles (cardiopharyngeal lineage).
- Tube: fusion pulled by endoderm (cardia bifida: midline cut, *miles apart*/S1pr2, *Foxp4*); first beat at day 22; the fastest region (sinus venosus, later the sinoatrial node: Tbx18, Shox2, HCN4, Tbx3) paces the tube by overdrive (Formula 28.4.1), unlike averaging in Kuramoto chains.
- Looping (Definition 28.5.1): rightward under Nodal–Pitx2; buckling of a tube growing between fixed poles, $F_{\mathrm{c}}=4\pi^2EI/L^2$, amplitude $(2/\pi)\sqrt{L\Delta L}$, direction chosen by a small bias that unfolds the pitchfork (Formula 28.5.1).
- Chambers balloon from the outer curvature (Nppa, Tbx2/3 repress chamber fate in primary myocardium); trabeculation by Neuregulin–ErbB and Notch; cushions by endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition (BMP2, TGF-β, Notch, Twist); atrial septum primum and secundum with the foramen ovale; ventricular septum at the *Tbx5* boundary; crest-derived spiral aorticopulmonary septum; coronary endothelium from sinus venosus, endocardium and proepicardium; conduction system from myocardium.
- Evolution: *tinman* in flies, cardiopharyngeal founder cells in *Ciona*, two chambers in fish, three in amphibians, a *Tbx5* gradient and partial septum in lizards, four chambers in birds and mammals.
- Congenital heart disease (about 9 per 1000): septal, conotruncal, valve and flow defects; trisomy 21, 22q11.2 (*TBX1*), *TBX5* (Holt–Oram), *NKX2-5*, *JAG1*, teratogens; many de novo mutations.
- Fetal circulation (Definition 28.9.1): ductus venosus, foramen ovale, ductus arteriosus; lungs receive about 10–25 % of output. At birth pulmonary resistance falls about tenfold, shunts close. Resistor model (Formula 28.9.1): pulmonary fraction $R_{\mathrm{s}}/(R_{\mathrm{p}}+R_{\mathrm{s}})$ with an open ductus; ductal flow reverses when $R_{\mathrm{p}}<R_{\mathrm{s}}Q_{\mathrm{L}}/Q_{\mathrm{R}}$.

## Questions

1. Design experiments to show that anterior **endoderm** induces heart and that it acts by blocking Wnt as well as by supplying BMP.

   **Answer** Remove the anterior endoderm from a chick or frog embryo before the heart field is specified and score cardiac markers (*Nkx2-5*, cardiac **myosin**) or beating: if **induction** is required they are lost. Combine anterior endoderm with posterior lateral **mesoderm** (which normally makes blood) in culture: beating tissue and cardiac markers show sufficiency. To separate the two activities, (a) culture posterior mesoderm with BMP2 alone, with a Wnt antagonist alone (Dkk1 or Crescent protein, or cells expressing them) and with both; if both are required, only the combination should induce heart; (b) block BMP with Noggin in endoderm–mesoderm combinations, and supply Wnt8 to them: each should prevent induction. The published results (Marvin et al. 2001; Schneider and Mercola 2001) showed that Wnt antagonists induce heart genes in posterior mesoderm and that Wnt8 blocks heart formation by anterior endoderm.

2. *Isl1-Cre* labels most cells of the right ventricle and atria but few of the left ventricle. What can and cannot be concluded about the origin of the left ventricle from this experiment alone?

   **Answer** It shows that most right-ventricular and atrial cells descend from cells that expressed *Isl1* at some time, and that most left-ventricular cells did not (at levels high enough to recombine the reporter). It does not show that the left ventricle comes from a separate lineage: first-field cells might express *Isl1* transiently or weakly, or Cre efficiency might differ, and labelled and unlabelled cells could share a common **progenitor** before *Isl1* expression. Lineage separation needs clonal analysis, labelling single progenitors and asking whether clones span both regions, which Meilhac et al. (2004) did with a retrospective clonal method, finding two lineages that diverge before the crescent.

3. In the FitzHugh–Nagumo chain of Formula 28.4.1, what happens to the beating rate of the tube if the coupling $D$ is reduced to near zero, and what if the fastest cells at the inflow end are removed? Compare with a Kuramoto chain.

   **Answer** With $D\to0$ the cells decouple and each beats at its own intrinsic rate, so the tube contracts irregularly with a gradient of rates (as in panel A, grey); at intermediate $D$ the chain breaks into clusters, each paced by its own fastest cell, with conduction failures at the boundaries. If the fastest cells are removed, the next-fastest region becomes the pacemaker and the whole tube slows to its rate: the heart rate is set by the maximum, as happens when a "subsidiary pacemaker" takes over after sinoatrial node failure. In a Kuramoto chain with sinusoidal coupling the locked frequency is the mean of the intrinsic frequencies, so removing the fastest cells would lower the rate only by their contribution to the mean, and the whole chain would beat at an intermediate rate.

4. Estimate the critical strain for buckling of an early mouse heart tube of length $L=400\ \mu\mathrm{m}$ and radius $R=50\ \mu\mathrm{m}$ (thin-walled), clamped at both poles. If the tube then grows 20 % in length while its poles stay fixed, what is the amplitude of the first mode?

   **Answer** $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{c}}=2\pi^2R^2/L^2=2\times9.87\times2500/160\,000\approx0.31$: in this crude model the tube must be compressed by about 30 % before buckling, which shows that the early tube is not slender and that the threshold model is only qualitative (real looping begins with active bending as well). Taking the inextensible post-buckling relation anyway, $\Delta L=0.2\times400=80\ \mu\mathrm{m}$ with $L=480\ \mu\mathrm{m}$ gives $w_0=(2/\pi)\sqrt{480\times80}=0.637\times196\approx125\ \mu\mathrm{m}$, about a quarter of the tube length: a large bend.

5. Why does an imperfect pitchfork predict that a weak left-right signal suffices for consistent rightward looping, while a mutant without the signal loops randomly? What would happen if the bias were reversed in sign?

   **Answer** With $h=0$ the straight state loses stability symmetrically and the direction is set by random fluctuations, so each side occurs with probability 1/2, as in left-right mutants with no asymmetric Nodal (about half of the loops reversed). With $h\neq0$ the normal form $\mu w_0-\kappa w_0^3+h=0$ has a single continuous branch from small $\mu$ on the side of $h$; as growth proceeds the tube follows this branch and never reaches the other, so even a small bias yields the same direction every time, provided it exceeds the noise. Reversing $h$ (as in **situs inversus**, with Nodal on the right) reverses the loop.

6. A child has a ventricular septal defect, an aorta overriding the septum and right ventricular outflow obstruction. Name the defect and explain how a single developmental error could give all three features. Which genetic syndrome would you test for first, and why?

   **Answer** Tetralogy of Fallot (the fourth feature, right ventricular hypertrophy, follows from the obstruction). The outflow septum, formed by **neural crest** and second-field cells in the outflow cushions, is displaced anteriorly: this narrows the right ventricular outflow, leaves a gap between the outflow septum and the muscular septum (the VSD), and leaves the aorta astride both ventricles. Test for 22q11.2 deletion first, because it is the commonest genetic cause of conotruncal defects; *TBX1* **haploinsufficiency** impairs second-field proliferation and the pharyngeal environment of the cardiac crest. Associated features (thymic hypoplasia, hypocalcaemia from absent parathyroids, palatal anomalies) would support it.

7. Using Formula 28.9.1 with a wide ductus ($R_{\mathrm{d}}\to0$), $Q_{\mathrm{R}}=0.6$, $Q_{\mathrm{L}}=0.4$ (fractions of combined output) and $R_{\mathrm{p}}=8R_{\mathrm{s}}$, calculate the fraction of output through the lungs and the ductal flow. Repeat for $R_{\mathrm{p}}=0.5R_{\mathrm{s}}$ (after birth, ductus still open).

   **Answer** Fetus: the pulmonary fraction is $R_{\mathrm{s}}/(R_{\mathrm{p}}+R_{\mathrm{s}})=1/9\approx0.11$; ductal flow $=Q_{\mathrm{R}}-0.11=0.49$, right to left (about 80 % of the right ventricular output bypasses the lungs). After birth with $R_{\mathrm{p}}=0.5R_{\mathrm{s}}$: pulmonary fraction $1/1.5=0.67$; ductal flow $=0.6-0.67=-0.07$, now left to right. Since in reality the left ventricle's output rises after birth and the systemic resistance rises when the **placenta** is removed, the reversal is larger; this left-to-right shunt through a patent ductus overloads the lungs and the left heart.

8. Hypoplastic left heart syndrome is sometimes treated in the fetus by balloon dilation of a stenotic aortic valve. What developmental principle does this rely on, and what does it predict about the timing of the intervention?

   **Answer** It relies on flow-dependent growth: myocardium grows in proportion to the load and flow it receives, and a ventricle that receives little flow (because blood is diverted through the foramen ovale or cannot leave through a stenotic valve) fails to grow (the "no flow, no grow" principle). Restoring outflow restores flow through the left ventricle and allows it to grow with the fetus. The intervention must be done while the ventricle still has growth potential and before fibrosis (endocardial fibroelastosis) sets in, that is, in mid-gestation (about 21–30 weeks) when the left ventricle is still of near-normal size but its growth is failing; late intervention cannot rescue a ventricle that is already small and fibrotic.

9. Explain why mice lacking *Tbx5* only in the ventricles have a single ventricle without a septum, and what this suggests about the evolution of the four-chambered heart.

   **Answer** The muscular ventricular septum forms at the boundary between *Tbx5*-expressing (left) and non-expressing (right) myocardium, where cells with different identities meet; Tbx5 and Tbx20 act antagonistically, and the boundary localizes septal growth. Without *Tbx5* in the ventricle there is no boundary, so no septum forms (moving the boundary experimentally moves the septum). In lizards and turtles *Tbx5* is graded across the ventricle without a sharp boundary and the septum is incomplete; in crocodiles and birds it has a sharp boundary and the septum is complete. The four-chambered heart may therefore have evolved (independently in birds and mammals) by sharpening a pre-existing expression gradient into a boundary, a small regulatory change with a large anatomical effect.

10. The foramen ovale stays anatomically open in about a quarter of adults. Why is it usually harmless, and when might it not be?

    **Answer** After birth the left atrial pressure normally exceeds the right, which presses the flap (septum primum) against the septum secundum and keeps the foramen functionally closed even if the two have not fused; no blood crosses. When right atrial pressure transiently exceeds left (coughing, straining, the Valsalva manoeuvre, pulmonary embolism, diving), the flap opens and venous blood, with any clots or gas bubbles, can cross to the systemic circulation: a paradoxical embolism, which is a cause of stroke in young people and of decompression illness in divers. Closure of the foramen is considered in such patients.
