The kidneys filter the blood, recover from the filtrate almost everything the body needs, and excrete the rest; they regulate the volume and composition of the body fluids and blood pressure and secrete hormones. They develop from the intermediate mesoderm, the narrow band between the somites and the lateral plate ([7.5.23](/7.5.23#sec-23.1)), in a sequence that recapitulates, in part, the history of vertebrate excretory organs: a pronephros, a mesonephros and, in amniotes, a metanephros, the permanent kidney. The metanephros has become a paradigm of organogenesis, because it forms by a dialogue between two tissues, an epithelial tube that branches and a mesenchyme that it converts into epithelial nephrons, each tissue inducing the other: **reciprocal induction** ([7.5.3](/7.5.3)). The chapter follows this dialogue to the shape of the branched tree and the number of nephrons, which is fixed before birth in humans and influences health in adult life. The intermediate mesoderm also forms the gonads and the genital ducts, the subject of [7.5.26](/7.5.26); the physiology of the nephron is treated in [7.14](/7.14).

## 25.1. The Kidney and Its Embryonic Origin

The functional unit of the kidney is the nephron: a glomerulus, in which blood is filtered across a capillary wall covered by podocytes, and a tubule, in which the filtrate is modified on its way to the collecting duct. A nephron contains more than 10 000 cells of at least a dozen types, arranged in a fixed proximal-to-distal order: Bowman's capsule with its podocytes, the proximal tubule, the loop of Henle, the distal tubule and the connecting segment. A human kidney contains on average about 900 000 nephrons, with a range of more than tenfold among individuals (about 200 000 to 2.7 million in autopsy studies; Bertram et al. 2011). The collecting ducts, the renal pelvis and the ureter form a separate, branched drainage system. Beyond filtration, the kidney makes erythropoietin, renin and the active form of vitamin D (calcitriol).

**Definition 25.1.1 (Intermediate Mesoderm)** The **intermediate mesoderm** is the band of trunk mesoderm between the paraxial (somitic) mesoderm and the lateral plate, marked by the transcription factors Osr1, Pax2, Pax8 and Lim1 (Lhx1). It gives rise to the kidneys and their ducts, the somatic cells of the gonads and the genital ducts, and the adrenal cortex.

The intermediate mesoderm is specified along the mediolateral axis by an intermediate level of BMP signalling (Section 23.1.1): mice lacking both *Foxc1* and *Foxc2*, the transcription factors of the paraxial mesoderm, convert their paraxial mesoderm to intermediate mesoderm expressing *Pax2*. Its specification is then stabilized by signals from the paraxial mesoderm. When chick embryos were cut so that on one side the intermediate mesoderm could not contact the somites, that side formed no pronephric duct (no *Pax2*-expressing epithelium), while the other side developed normally (Mauch et al. 2000); conversely, paraxial mesoderm co-cultured with lateral plate induces pronephric tubules in it. The paraxial mesoderm is thus necessary and sufficient for kidney formation, and among its signals are Wnts, FGFs and BMPs; the intermediate mesoderm is competent to respond only at trunk levels, from about the sixth somite back, because the competence factor Hoxb4 is expressed only posterior to a level set by retinoic acid.

The response is a set of transcription factors. *Pax2* and *Pax8* are both necessary: in mice lacking both, the intermediate mesoderm does not undergo the **mesenchymal–epithelial transition** that forms the nephric duct, and no kidney structures form (Bouchard et al. 2002); Pax2 misexpressed in the presomitic mesoderm converts it to intermediate mesoderm that expresses *Lim1* and forms kidney tissue. *Lim1* is needed to convert the intermediate mesoderm into the nephric duct and later for the ureteric bud and the nephrons; *Osr1* marks the earliest intermediate mesoderm and is needed for all of its derivatives.

## 25.2. Three Kidneys

**Definition 25.2.1 (Pronephros, Mesonephros, Metanephros and Nephric Duct)** The **nephric duct** (pronephric duct, later the mesonephric or **Wolffian duct**) is an epithelial tube that forms in the anterior intermediate mesoderm and extends posteriorly to open into the cloaca. Along it, three kidneys form in anterior-to-posterior and temporal order: the **pronephros**, a few tubules at the anterior end (functional in fish and amphibian larvae, rudimentary in amniotes); the **mesonephros**, a series of tubules with glomeruli along the middle of the duct (functional in fish and amphibian adults and transiently in some amniote embryos); and the **metanephros**, the permanent kidney of amniotes, formed at the posterior end of the duct from the **ureteric bud** and the **metanephric mesenchyme**.

In the human embryo the pronephric duct appears at about day 22 in the intermediate mesoderm ventral to the anterior somites; its anterior part induces a few pronephric tubules, which never function and soon degenerate. The duct grows caudally by proliferation and migration of its tip cells and reaches the cloaca at about day 26. As it passes, it induces about 30–40 mesonephric tubules in the adjacent mesenchyme from about day 25, each with a glomerulus formed by a branch of the dorsal aorta; the anterior ones regress as posterior ones form. Whether the human mesonephros produces urine is uncertain (it probably does briefly, between weeks 6 and 10), but it has two lasting functions. Its region, the aorta–gonad–mesonephros (AGM), produces the first definitive **hematopoietic stem cells** ([7.5.29](/7.5.29)). In males, some mesonephric tubules persist as the efferent ducts of the testis and the Wolffian duct becomes the epididymis, vas deferens and seminal vesicle; in females both regress ([7.5.26](/7.5.26)). In mice and rats the mesonephros does not function as a kidney at all.

The metanephros begins at about day 28 (week 5): the ureteric bud grows out from the posterior nephric duct into the adjacent metanephric mesenchyme. The first nephrons form in week 8–9, the fetal kidney produces urine from about week 10–12 (the main source of amniotic fluid from week 16), and nephrogenesis ends at about weeks 34–36. No new nephrons form after that; growth of the kidney after birth is by enlargement of the existing nephrons.

## 25.3. Reciprocal Induction in the Metanephros

### 25.3.1. Grobstein's Experiments

The two components of the metanephros cannot develop alone. Clifford Grobstein (1953, 1955) separated the ureteric bud from the metanephric mesenchyme of mouse embryos and cultured them separately or together. Isolated, the ureteric bud did not branch, and the mesenchyme died within a day. Recombined, the bud branched and nephrons formed throughout the mesenchyme. Placing a porous filter between the two showed that the induction does not require cytoplasmic continuity; filters with pores too small for cell processes were less effective, which suggested close-range signals, and other inducers (embryonic spinal cord) could replace the bud in inducing nephrons, but not the reverse: only the metanephric mesenchyme could respond. Lineage studies with labelled cells confirmed that the two tissues give rise to distinct parts of the kidney: the ureteric bud to the collecting ducts and ureter, the metanephric mesenchyme to the nephrons (and to the stroma and part of the vasculature).

The two progenitor populations originate at different times. Cells that pass through the primitive streak early and are exposed to Wnt signalling briefly become anterior intermediate mesoderm and give rise to the nephric duct and the ureteric bud; cells that remain longer in the posterior, Wnt-dominated region become the posterior intermediate mesoderm that forms the metanephric mesenchyme (Taguchi et al. 2014). Human pluripotent stem cells reproduce this: treated with a Wnt agonist for a short time and then with FGF9, they make ureteric epithelium; treated longer, metanephric mesenchyme; and together, kidney organoids (Takasato et al. 2015; Section 25.8).

### 25.3.2. The Signals of the Dialogue

The dialogue proceeds in four steps.

1. The mesenchyme induces the bud. The metanephric mesenchyme secretes glial cell line-derived neurotrophic factor (GDNF), which binds the receptor tyrosine kinase RET together with its co-receptor GFRα1 on the nephric duct; RET is induced in the duct by retinoic acid from the surrounding stroma. GDNF signalling causes the outgrowth of the ureteric bud at the level of the mesenchyme. Mice lacking *Gdnf*, *Ret* or *Gfra1* have no kidneys (renal agenesis) or tiny rudiments and die after birth; GDNF-soaked beads placed beside the nephric duct in culture induce ectopic buds. In chimeric embryos made of wild-type cells and cells with reduced RET signalling, the cells with the most active RET move to the tip of the forming bud (Chi et al. 2009): the bud tip is built by cell competition.
2. Budding is restricted to one site. Several mechanisms keep *Gdnf* expression and the response to it confined to the posterior: the transcription factors Foxc1 and Foxc2 restrict *Gdnf* anteriorly; Slit2, acting through its receptor Robo2, keeps *Gdnf* out of the anterior mesenchyme; Sprouty1 inhibits RET signalling within the duct; and BMP4 from the mesenchyme around the duct inhibits budding, its antagonist Gremlin1 allowing it at the right place. Mutations in any of these give supernumerary or ectopic buds and duplex kidneys (*Spry1* mutants are rescued by halving the *Gdnf* dose). The expression of *Gdnf* itself requires Pax2, Eya1, Six1, the Hox11 paralogues and Sall1; mice lacking all three *Hox11* genes have no metanephros.
3. The bud rescues the mesenchyme. Factors from the bud tip, FGF2, FGF9 and BMP7 among them, prevent apoptosis of the mesenchyme, maintain its progenitors and keep them expressing the transcription factor WT1, which is needed for the competence of the mesenchyme (in *Wt1*$^{-/-}$ mice the mesenchyme dies and no bud forms).
4. The mesenchyme makes the bud branch, and the bud induces nephrons. GDNF from the cap mesenchyme continues to induce new buds at the tips; it induces *Wnt11* in the tip cells, and Wnt11 maintains *Gdnf* in the mesenchyme, a positive feedback that couples branching to progenitor maintenance; FGF10 and the transcription factors Etv4 and Etv5 downstream of RET contribute. From the stalk just behind each tip, Wnt9b induces the adjacent mesenchyme to form nephrons (Section 25.4).

## 25.4. Nephron Progenitors and the Formation of the Nephron

**Definition 25.4.1 (Cap Mesenchyme)** The **cap mesenchyme** is the layer of metanephric mesenchyme that condenses around each tip of the ureteric bud. It contains the nephron progenitor cells, which express Six2 and Cited1, renew themselves, and give rise to all epithelial cells of the nephron.

Genetic lineage tracing with a *Six2*-Cre allele showed that every epithelial cell of the nephron, from the podocytes to the distal tubule, descends from Six2-expressing cap mesenchyme, and that these cells renew themselves for the whole period of nephrogenesis (Kobayashi et al. 2008). In mice lacking *Six2*, the progenitors differentiate prematurely into ectopic renal vesicles and are exhausted, so the kidneys are small. The cap mesenchyme is therefore a self-renewing progenitor pool whose fate depends on a balance: Six2, with low β-catenin activity and FGF9/FGF20 signalling, maintains progenitors; high Wnt9b/β-catenin signalling induces differentiation. Outside the cap lie the stromal progenitors (Foxd1), which form the renal capsule, interstitial fibroblasts, pericytes and mesangial cells and signal back (retinoic acid, the atypical cadherins Fat4 and Dchs1) to promote both branching and nephron differentiation.

The nephron forms by a sequence of epithelial shapes.

1. A group of cap mesenchyme cells beside the stalk of a bud tip, induced by Wnt9b, forms a pretubular aggregate and expresses *Wnt4*, *Fgf8*, *Pax8* and *Lhx1*. Wnt4 acts in an autocrine way to complete the **mesenchymal–epithelial transition**: in *Wnt4*$^{-/-}$ mice the mesenchyme condenses but never epithelializes.
2. The aggregate becomes a renal vesicle, a polarized epithelial ball with a lumen, already polarized along the future proximal–distal axis: the part nearest the bud tip becomes distal.
3. The vesicle elongates into a comma-shaped body and then an S-shaped body, whose distal limb fuses with the ureteric epithelium, joining the lumens of the nephron and the collecting duct.
4. The three limbs of the S-shaped body are the precursors of the segments: the proximal limb forms the glomerular epithelium (podocytes and the parietal layer of Bowman's capsule), the middle limb the proximal tubule and the loop of Henle, the distal limb the distal tubule and the connecting segment. Endothelial cells, attracted by VEGF from the podocyte precursors, enter the cleft of the proximal limb and form the glomerular capillary tuft; mesangial cells follow by PDGF-B signalling. The tubules then elongate, the loop of Henle extending into the medulla.

Proximal–distal patterning uses familiar signalling pathways. Notch2 is required for all proximal segments: without it, no podocytes and no proximal tubules form; distal fates require Lhx1, Brn1 (Pou3f3) and higher β-catenin and BMP activity near the bud.

The ureteric bud forms the whole collecting system. Its first branches are remodelled into the renal pelvis and the calyces, the later ones into collecting ducts; its trunk becomes the ureter, whose wall acquires smooth muscle (induced by Shh from the urothelium and BMP4, under the control of Tbx18 in the surrounding mesenchyme). The ureter separates from the nephric duct and comes to open into the bladder, which forms from the urogenital sinus of the cloaca: the common nephric duct, the short segment between the ureter and the cloaca, is removed by apoptosis, and the ureter orifice is carried to its final position as the bladder expands (Batourina et al. 2005); the nephric duct itself then opens more caudally, into the urethra in males.

## 25.5. Branching Morphogenesis

**Definition 25.5.1 (Branching Morphogenesis)** **Branching morphogenesis** is the formation of a tree of epithelial tubes by repeated growth and splitting (or lateral budding) of the tips of an epithelium into a surrounding mesenchyme, as in the kidney (the ureteric bud), the lung, the salivary, mammary and prostate glands, and the pancreas.

In the kidney, branching is mostly terminal bifurcation: each tip splits into two, the new tips grow and split again. The human ureteric bud undergoes about 15 generations of branching before nephrogenesis ends, the first 9 or so rapidly, by week 15 (Osathanondh and Potter 1963), which would give $2^{15} \approx 33\,000$ tips if every branch continued; the actual tree is less regular. In the mouse, live imaging of organ cultures and of reporter mice shows that tips divide at regular intervals and stay spaced at a nearly constant density on the surface of the growing kidney, as if tips competed for the cap mesenchyme around them (Short et al. 2014). How is such a tree generated, and why do some branches stop?

A minimal statistical theory treats each tip as an independent random walker that can branch and can stop when it runs into existing ducts (Hannezo et al. 2017).

**Formula 25.5.1 (Branching–Annihilating Random Walk)** Each active tip moves at constant speed $v$ in a direction that changes randomly with a persistence length $\ell_p$; it branches into two active tips at rate $r_b$; and it becomes inactive (terminates) when it comes within a distance $d$ of a duct laid down by another tip. In the mean-field limit, the densities of active tips $a$ and of inactive ducts $i$ obey

$$
\partial_t a = D\nabla^2 a + r_b\,a\Bigl(1 - \frac{a + i}{n_0}\Bigr),\qquad \partial_t i = r_b\,a\,\frac{a + i}{n_0},
$$
where $D \sim v\ell_p$ is the effective diffusion of the tips and $n_0$ the density of ducts at which annihilation balances branching. The front of the active tips is a Fisher front (Proposition 23.6.1) that invades unoccupied tissue at speed $2\sqrt{Dr_b}$, leaving behind a tree of terminated branches of uniform density.

The model predicts statistics that are independent of most molecular detail: an approximately exponential distribution of branch lengths, a fraction of terminated tips that rises to a constant, and a stable ratio of active to terminated branches. Hannezo and colleagues found these features in the mouse mammary gland, where branches terminate when they approach other ducts, and extended the analysis to the kidney and other glands, where geometric constraints and tip–tip repulsion add order to the pattern. The kidney is more deterministic than a free random walk: branching is nearly bifurcating and synchronized, tips repel each other, and the growth of the organ makes space. The model is a reference that separates what follows from local rules from what needs global control.

## 25.6. Kidneys of Different Vertebrates and Two Strategies of Growth

The three kidneys are used differently in different vertebrates.

| | *Xenopus laevis* | Zebrafish (*Danio rerio*) | Mouse and human |
|---|---|---|---|
| Embryonic or larval kidney | pronephros: a single large glomus, three nephrostomes opening into the coelom, a tubule with proximal and distal segments; functional in the tadpole | pronephros: two nephrons sharing one glomerulus at the midline, functional from about 2 days after fertilization | pronephros rudimentary; mesonephros transient |
| Adult kidney | mesonephros | mesonephros, forming from about 2 weeks; new nephrons are added throughout life and after injury | metanephros |
| Nephron number | increases during larval growth | increases throughout life | fixed at the end of nephrogenesis (human: before birth; mouse: a few days after birth) |
| Growth of the kidney | new nephrons and larger nephrons | mainly new nephrons | after nephrogenesis, only enlargement (hypertrophy) of nephrons |

Despite the differences in organ, the nephron is conserved: the segments of the zebrafish pronephric nephron express the same transcription factors and transporters as the corresponding segments of mammalian nephrons, which makes the fish a model for nephron patterning and for screens of kidney toxicity and regeneration. Two strategies of growth exist: increasing the number of nephrons (fish, and amphibian larvae) or increasing their size (mammals after birth). Adult zebrafish keep a population of nephron progenitors and form new nephrons after injury, while mammals cannot: a lost nephron is not replaced.

The fixed nephron number of mammals has a clinical consequence. Brenner proposed in 1988 that individuals born with fewer nephrons are predisposed to hypertension and chronic kidney disease: the total filtration rate is shared by fewer nephrons, each of which hyperfilters, and hyperfiltration damages glomeruli, further reducing their number. Nephron number correlates with birth weight (in an autopsy series, about 250 000 more nephrons per kidney per kilogram of birth weight; Hughson et al. 2003), and preterm birth and intrauterine growth restriction reduce it; nephrons that form after preterm birth in the extrauterine environment may be abnormal.

**Formula 25.6.1 (Nephron Number and Single-Nephron Filtration)** If the number of ureteric tips $T(t)$ grows during nephrogenesis and each tip induces nephrons at a rate $\rho$ until nephrogenesis stops at time $t_{\mathrm{end}}$, the final number of nephrons per kidney is

$$
N = \rho\int_0^{t_{\mathrm{end}}} T(t)\,\mathrm{d}t,
$$
and for a total glomerular filtration rate $\mathrm{GFR}$ shared by $2N$ nephrons (two kidneys), the mean single-nephron filtration rate is $\mathrm{SNGFR} = \mathrm{GFR}/(2N)$.

**Example 25.6.1 (Fewer Nephrons, Harder Work)** With an adult GFR of $120\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}}$ and $N = 900\,000$ per kidney, SNGFR $= 120/(1.8\times10^6)\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}} \approx 67\,\mathrm{nL\,min^{-1}}$. An individual at the low end of the normal range, $N = 300\,000$, must filter about $200\,\mathrm{nL\,min^{-1}}$ per nephron to achieve the same GFR, three times as much. In the tip model, a 30% reduction in the rate of branching late in gestation, or the loss of the final weeks of nephrogenesis by preterm birth, lowers $N$ by about a third, because the tips are most numerous at the end and the integral is dominated by the last weeks (Figure A).

## 25.7. Congenital Anomalies of the Kidney and Urinary Tract

**Definition 25.7.1 (Congenital Anomalies of the Kidney and Urinary Tract)** **Congenital anomalies of the kidney and urinary tract** (CAKUT) are malformations arising from defective development of the metanephros or of the lower urinary tract: renal agenesis, hypoplasia and dysplasia (including multicystic dysplastic kidney), duplex kidney and ureter, ectopic or fused kidneys (horseshoe kidney), ureteropelvic junction obstruction, megaureter, vesicoureteral reflux and posterior urethral valves.

CAKUT are among the commonest birth defects, about 3–6 per 1000 births, and cause about 40% of chronic kidney disease in children. The developmental steps of this chapter explain their forms. Failure of the ureteric bud to form or of the mesenchyme to respond gives agenesis; reduced branching or progenitor exhaustion gives hypoplasia (a small kidney with normal structure but few nephrons); disordered induction gives dysplasia (undifferentiated tissue and cysts). An extra or ectopic bud gives a duplex kidney with two ureters; if the bud arises from an abnormal position along the nephric duct, the ureter opens at an abnormal position in the bladder, which explains why ectopic ureters are associated with reflux or obstruction (the Mackie–Stephens bud hypothesis). Bilateral agenesis or severe dysplasia, by depriving the fetus of urine and hence of amniotic fluid, causes the Potter sequence: compressed facies, limb deformities and pulmonary hypoplasia, because fetal breathing movements and lung fluid pressure require amniotic fluid ([7.5.30](/7.5.30)).

| Gene | Syndrome or phenotype | Developmental step |
|---|---|---|
| *PAX2* | renal coloboma (papillorenal) syndrome: hypoplastic kidneys, optic nerve coloboma | intermediate mesoderm, nephric duct, bud branching |
| *EYA1*, *SIX1* | branchio-oto-renal syndrome: branchial cysts, hearing loss, renal anomalies | *Gdnf* expression in the metanephric mesenchyme |
| *RET*, *GDNF* | renal agenesis (some cases; *RET* also in Hirschsprung disease, [7.5.20](/7.5.20)) | budding |
| *ROBO2*, *SLIT2* | vesicoureteral reflux, duplex systems | restriction of budding |
| *SALL1* | Townes–Brocks syndrome (anal, ear, thumb and kidney anomalies) | metanephric mesenchyme |
| *HNF1B* | renal cysts and diabetes syndrome; the most common single-gene cause of CAKUT | bud and tubule epithelium, also pancreas |
| *GATA3* | HDR syndrome (hypoparathyroidism, deafness, renal dysplasia) | nephric duct |

Most CAKUT are not monogenic: known genes explain perhaps 10–20%, copy-number variants a further fraction, and environmental factors (maternal diabetes, retinoic acid deficiency or excess, drugs that block the renin–angiotensin system in late pregnancy) contribute. Polycystic kidney diseases, in which tubules dilate into cysts because proteins of the **primary cilium** (polycystins and others, [7.3.14](/7.3.14)) are defective, are disorders of tubule maintenance rather than of kidney formation.

## 25.8. Kidney Organoids

Because the signals of kidney development are known, they can be applied in sequence to pluripotent stem cells. Protocols that mimic primitive streak exposure to Wnt and then FGF9 and retinoic acid produce three-dimensional **organoids** that contain nephrons segmented into glomerular, proximal and distal parts, connected to collecting duct-like epithelium, and surrounded by stroma and endothelium (Takasato et al. 2015; Morizane et al. 2015; Taguchi and Nishinakamura 2017, who assembled separately induced nephron progenitors and ureteric bud cells into organoids with a branched collecting system). Organoids from patients' induced pluripotent stem cells, or with genome-edited mutations, model polycystic kidney disease, podocyte disorders and the nephrotoxicity of drugs. They remain immature, resembling first- or second-trimester kidneys, lack a perfused vasculature and a drainage system, and contain off-target cell types; transplantation under the kidney capsule of mice improves their vascularization. Stem cells, embryo models and organoids are treated in [7.5.36](/7.5.36).

- The intermediate mesoderm, specified by signals from the somites, forms a nephric duct along which three kidneys form in sequence; the metanephros forms by reciprocal induction between the ureteric bud and the metanephric mesenchyme.
- GDNF–RET signalling induces a single bud at the right place, held there by several inhibitory systems; the bud keeps the mesenchyme alive and induces nephrons through Wnt9b and Wnt4.
- Six2 nephron progenitors renew themselves and form all nephron epithelia through vesicle, comma and S-shaped stages; Notch2 specifies proximal segments.
- Branching can be described by local stochastic rules of branching and termination (Formula 25.5.1); nephron number is the integral of tip number over the period of nephrogenesis (Formula 25.6.1) and is fixed before birth in humans, with consequences for adult blood pressure and kidney disease.
- Failures of these steps cause CAKUT (Definition 25.7.1); the same steps guide the construction of kidney organoids.

## Questions

1. In a chick embryo, a cut separates the intermediate mesoderm from the paraxial mesoderm on the left side only. Predict the result, and design a complementary experiment to show that the paraxial mesoderm is sufficient to induce kidney tissue.

   **Answer** On the left, no *Pax2*-expressing pronephric duct or tubules form; the right side is normal (Mauch et al. 2000). For sufficiency, combine paraxial mesoderm (somites or PSM) with lateral plate mesoderm, which normally never forms kidney, in culture or by grafting, and look for *Pax2* and *Lim1* expression and epithelial tubules in the lateral plate cells (label one tissue, for instance with quail cells, to know which formed the tubules). Pronephric tubules form; other tissues placed with the lateral plate do not induce them. The competence of the responding tissue matters: head mesoderm anterior to the sixth somite does not respond unless competence (Hoxb4) is supplied.

2. Describe Grobstein's transfilter experiment and explain what each control shows about the nature of induction in the metanephros.

   **Answer** Metanephric mesenchyme is placed on one side of a porous filter and an inducer (ureteric bud, or embryonic spinal cord) on the other; tubules form in the mesenchyme. Mesenchyme alone dies: the inducer is required (necessity of the signal for survival and differentiation). Mesenchyme with a filter shows that cell mixing is not needed, so the signal crosses the filter (secreted factors, or contact through processes in large pores). Spinal cord can replace the bud, so the signal is not unique to the bud (the mesenchyme is competent to respond to a class of signals, now known to include Wnts), but other mesenchymes do not respond to the bud, so the competence is specific. The reverse experiment (bud alone does not branch; it branches with mesenchyme or with GDNF and other factors) shows that the bud also depends on signals from the mesenchyme: the induction is reciprocal.

3. Mice lacking *Sprouty1* form multiple ureteric buds, and the phenotype is suppressed when one copy of *Gdnf* is also removed. Interpret this genetic interaction.

   **Answer** Sprouty1 is an intracellular inhibitor of receptor tyrosine kinase signalling, here of RET. Without it, the nephric duct responds to low levels of GDNF along its length and forms buds where GDNF is normally too low to induce them. Halving GDNF lowers the signal back below the threshold of the hypersensitive duct, restoring a single bud. The interaction shows that budding is set by the product of ligand level and receptor sensitivity, and that Sprouty1 acts in the GDNF–RET pathway, upstream of the budding response.

4. How would you show that all cells of the nephron derive from Six2-expressing cells, and that these cells are self-renewing?

   **Answer** Use an inducible Cre driven by *Six2* (for example *Six2*-*GFPCreERT2*) and a Cre-dependent reporter. Give tamoxifen at an early stage (E11.5) and examine kidneys at the end of nephrogenesis: labelled cells occur in podocytes, proximal tubules, loops and distal tubules, but not in collecting ducts or stroma. Labelled cells remain in the cap mesenchyme at late stages, and nephrons formed late are labelled, so the early-labelled cells persisted as progenitors and generated nephrons throughout: self-renewal. At low tamoxifen doses, clonal analysis shows single progenitors giving rise both to nephron segments and to progenitor descendants.

5. In the branching–annihilating random walk, what sets the speed at which the tree invades a large space, and why does the number of active tips grow only linearly in two dimensions at late times?

   **Answer** The active tips behave like the reactive species of a Fisher equation: they spread with effective diffusion $D \sim v\ell_p$ and multiply at rate $r_b$ until the local density of ducts saturates, so the front advances at about $2\sqrt{Dr_b}$ (Proposition 23.6.1). Behind the front, tips are annihilated by the ducts they encounter, so active tips exist only in a band of fixed width at the front; in two dimensions, a circular front of radius growing linearly with time has a perimeter, and hence a number of active tips, growing linearly with time.

6. A child is born at 28 weeks of gestation. Using Formula 25.6.1 qualitatively, explain why her nephron number may be reduced although nephrogenesis continues for a few weeks after birth, and what consequences for later life Brenner's hypothesis predicts.

   **Answer** The integral is dominated by the last weeks of gestation, when tip numbers are greatest; postnatal nephrogenesis in preterm infants continues for only about 40 days and produces fewer and partly abnormal glomeruli (in the extrauterine environment, with drugs and hypoxia), so the final $N$ is lower. With fewer nephrons, each must filter more to maintain GFR (Example 25.6.1), and glomerular hyperfiltration and hypertension damage nephrons over decades: increased risk of hypertension, proteinuria and chronic kidney disease in adulthood; birth weight and prematurity are indeed associated with these outcomes.

7. Explain why bilateral renal agenesis causes pulmonary hypoplasia, and what this shows about interactions between organ systems in the fetus.

   **Answer** From mid-gestation, amniotic fluid is mainly fetal urine. Without kidneys, the fetus is surrounded by little fluid (oligohydramnios): the uterus compresses the fetus (facial and limb deformities) and the lungs cannot expand, because their growth depends on mechanical stretch, the retention of lung fluid in the airways and fetal breathing movements against amniotic fluid. The lungs are small and the infant cannot breathe (Potter sequence). Organ systems interact not only by signals but by physiological function before birth.

8. Mutations of *HNF1B* cause renal cysts and maturity-onset diabetes of the young in the same family. Propose a developmental explanation and an organoid experiment to test it.

   **Answer** HNF1β is a transcription factor expressed in the epithelia of the ureteric bud, the tubules, the bile ducts and the pancreas; it regulates genes of cilia and polarity (including *PKHD1* and *UMOD*) and pancreatic progenitor genes. Heterozygous loss reduces branching and nephron number and disturbs tubule maintenance (cysts), and in the pancreas reduces the endocrine progenitor pool and β-cell function (diabetes). Test: make kidney organoids (and pancreatic progenitors) from iPS cells of a patient and from an isogenic corrected line (or introduce the mutation into a control line by genome editing), compare tubule formation, cyst formation (for example after forskolin, which raises cAMP), ciliary gene expression and branching of ureteric bud-like structures.

9. Compare the strategies of kidney growth in the zebrafish and the human, and explain why only one of them can replace nephrons after injury.

   **Answer** The zebrafish keeps nephron progenitors in the adult mesonephros and adds nephrons throughout life (growth by number); after injury, clusters of progenitors form new nephrons that connect to existing ducts. Humans exhaust the Six2 progenitor pool at weeks 34–36 (the cap mesenchyme undergoes a final wave of differentiation) and thereafter grow only by enlarging existing nephrons (growth by size); tubule cells can repair the epithelium after injury, but no progenitors remain to make new nephrons. Regenerative strategies (organoids, reprogramming to nephron progenitors) aim to restore the missing progenitor pool.

10. (Open) The ureteric bud must branch to fill a kidney of the right size while producing the right number of nephrons. Propose a mechanism that couples the rate of branching to the number of nephron progenitors, and an experiment to test it.

    **Answer** One coupling is through GDNF and Wnt11: progenitors in the cap make GDNF, which promotes tip division and induces Wnt11 in the tip, which in turn maintains GDNF expression in the progenitors; if each tip has a limited "share" of cap mesenchyme, branching would slow when progenitors are depleted, and nephron induction (Wnt9b) consumes progenitors. Prediction: reducing the progenitor pool (by partial genetic ablation, for instance diphtheria toxin in a fraction of Six2 cells) should reduce branching and tip number in proportion, not merely the nephron number per tip; conversely, expanding progenitors (by blocking their differentiation transiently) should increase branching. Measure tip number and spacing by live imaging of explants with a fluorescent bud reporter (for example *Hoxb7*-GFP) and progenitors (*Six2*-GFP), and compare with a model in which tip division depends on the number of progenitors per tip.
