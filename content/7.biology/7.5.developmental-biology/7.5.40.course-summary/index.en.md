The preceding thirty-nine chapters followed one animal life cycle, from the gametes and **fertilization** (7.5.4) through **cleavage**, **gastrulation** and the body axes (7.5.5–7.5.17), the organs of the three **germ layers** (7.5.18–7.5.33) and the life after the embryo (7.5.33–7.5.37) to development in health, environment and evolution (7.5.38–7.5.39), with five chapters built around mathematical models (7.5.7, 7.5.8, 7.5.10, 7.5.12, 7.5.13). This chapter adds no new mechanism. It states the six questions the course has tried to answer (40.1), extracts the seven mechanisms that recur in every chapter (40.2), sets out the experimental logic by which each was established (40.3), arranges the models in a family tree with their mathematical tools and scales (40.4), collects the organs in one table with their origin, inducers, genes and congenital disorders (40.5), places the course in one system with cell biology ([7.3](/7.3)) and genetics ([7.4](/7.4)) (40.6), and lists the questions that the chapters left open (40.7). The mathematics used throughout is developed in [2.4](/2.4) and [2.5](/2.5) (dynamical systems, diffusion and reaction–diffusion), [3.1](/3.1) and [3.2](/3.2) (probability and estimation) and [6.3](/6.3) (thermodynamics and surface tension).

## 40.1. Six Questions and the Answers of the Course

Chapter 7.5.1 listed the questions of developmental biology (Definition 1.1.1). They reduce to six, each with an answer that the course has assembled from many chapters.

| Question | The course's answer in one sentence | Main chapters and statements |
|---|---|---|
| How do cells with the same genome become different? | Cells keep the whole genome (genomic equivalence) and differ by **cell memory** of gene expression states, set by inherited **cytoplasmic determinants** or by signals and stabilized by positive feedback and chromatin | 7.5.1 (Definition 1.5.1, Definition 1.5.2), 7.5.3, 7.5.6, 7.5.15 (Definition 15.6.1) |
| How does a cell learn where it is? | By reading signals: local **induction**, **morphogen** gradients read at thresholds (**positional information**, Definition 11.1.1), self-organized patterns (Theorem 12.3.1) and clocks read by a moving front (Definition 23.7.1) | 7.5.11–7.5.17, 7.5.18, 7.5.23, 7.5.31–7.5.32 |
| How are shapes built? | By forces generated by cells and transmitted by tissues that behave as viscoelastic liquids: differential adhesion and **tissue surface tension**, actomyosin contraction, intercalation and buckling | 7.5.7–7.5.9, 7.5.18, 7.5.28, 7.5.30 |
| How is size controlled? | By intrinsic programmes of growth (**growth plate**, **organ size control**, Definition 34.4.1) coupled to systemic hormones and nutrition, with feedback that gives a set point (Proposition 34.4.1) | 7.5.24, 7.5.33–7.5.35 |
| How does the organism continue? | A germ line is set aside early (by **germ plasm** or by induction) and remade into gametes; adult tissues are renewed by **stem cells** whose fates are stochastic but balanced; some animals regenerate, all age | 7.5.26–7.5.27, 7.5.35–7.5.37 |
| How does development meet the world and history? | The environment is a normal input (plasticity, polyphenism, symbiosis) and a source of damage (teratogens, critical periods); evolution changes the regulation of a conserved toolkit in place, time, amount and kind | 7.5.38–7.5.39 |

## 40.2. Seven Recurring Mechanisms

### 40.2.1. The Mechanisms

The same few mechanisms make cells different and put them in place in every chapter. Each has a characteristic signature in experiments and a characteristic mathematical form.

|---|---|---|---|---|

### 40.2.2. How the Mechanisms Combine

No structure of the embryo uses one mechanism alone. Three combinations recur throughout the course.

- A hierarchy that refines a coarse pattern: in the *Drosophila* embryo maternal **cytoplasmic determinants** set four gradients (7.5.14), the **gap genes** read them at thresholds and repress each other into sharp domains (Formula 15.2.1), the **pair-rule genes** combine gap inputs in modular enhancers (Formula 15.3.1), the **segment-polarity genes** store the result in a bistable loop between neighbours (Formula 15.4.1), and the **Hox genes** give each segment its identity, kept by Polycomb and Trithorax (Definition 15.6.1). The neural tube (7.5.18), the limb (7.5.31–7.5.32) and the somites (7.5.23) use the same logic: a graded input, cross-repressive transcription factors, a memory.
- A gradient that seeds a self-organized pattern: pure reaction–diffusion selects a wavelength but not a position; a weak graded source selects the mode and fixes the polarity (Proposition 12.5.3, Proposition 12.8.1). Hox dose tunes the Turing wavelength of the digits (7.5.32), the dorsal BMP gradient positions the left–right Nodal cascade only after the cilia have biased it (7.5.17), and the source density stores the polarity of *Hydra* (7.5.35).
- Signals and forces in a loop: signals set the mechanical properties of cells (Snail and Twist drive apical constriction and **epithelial–mesenchymal transition**, 7.5.9; cadherin levels set **tissue surface tension**, 7.5.7), and the resulting shape changes the signals (the tube that buckles between fixed poles brings regions into contact, 7.5.28; villi form by buckling and then confine the Shh and BMP that place the stem cells, 7.5.30; dermal condensation and signalling together space the appendages, 7.5.22).

### 40.2.3. Reliability

The products of development are reproducible although each step is noisy. The course met four sources of reliability, often in the same system: negative feedback that buffers gene dosage (Formula 1.8.1) and the RA level (Formula 17.2.1); redundancy and **genetic compensation** (Definition 1.8.1); bistable switches with memory that make graded inputs all-or-none and keep the decision after the signal has gone (Proposition 11.7.1, Formula 26.2.1, Formula 29.8.1); and scaling mechanisms that keep proportions when size changes (**self-enhanced degradation**, Proposition 11.6.1; expansion–repression, Formula 16.6.1, Proposition 16.6.1). Together they are what Waddington called **canalization** (Definition 1.8.2): the **Waddington landscape** has deep valleys, and the theory of 7.5.10 explains why a description by stable valleys and their bifurcations is both possible and generic (Theorem 10.4.1, Theorem 10.7.2).

## 40.3. The Logic of Experiments

Developmental biology has kept its classical experiments and added new ones; the inference in each is the same: change one thing and see what the embryo does with it. The table lists the designs used throughout the course with what each can and cannot show.

| Design | What is done | What a result shows | Classic example | Caveat |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Isolation | culture a cell or tissue alone, in a neutral medium | development according to fate shows **specification** (Definition 1.5.1) | blastomeres of ascidians (7.5.6), animal caps (7.5.16) | "neutral" media are rarely neutral |
| Ablation, removal | kill or remove a cell or a signalling centre | loss of structures shows necessity; replacement shows regulation | Roux and Driesch (7.5.1); AER removal (Definition 31.4.1) | the remaining tissue may compensate or die |
| Grafting, transplantation | move tissue to a new place or a new host | a graft that keeps its fate is determined (**determination**); a graft that changes its host's fate is a signalling centre | Spemann–Mangold organizer (Definition 16.5.1); ZPA grafts (7.5.32); quail–chick chimeras (7.5.24) | a graft brings its own environment |
| Recombination | combine tissues from different sources, ages or species | which partner sets the type of structure (instructive) and which only allows it (permissive, Definition 3.4.2) | Nieuwkoop's recombinants (7.5.16); Grobstein's kidney (7.5.25); dermis and epidermis of appendages (7.5.22) | contact may be needed in addition to signals |
| Lineage tracing | mark a cell and follow its descendants | the cells a structure comes from (**lineage tracing**, Definition 2.5.2) and whether fate is restricted | **fate map** of the frog (7.5.9); compartments by clones (Definition 33.6.2); Six2 nephron progenitors (7.5.25) | label collisions (Formula 2.5.1); a fate map shows normal fate, not potential |
| Loss of function | mutation, knockout, knockdown, dominant negative | necessity | *bicoid* (7.5.14); **gap genes** (7.5.15); *Shh* knockout (7.5.32) | redundancy, maternal products, early lethality, **morpholino** specificity (Definition 2.8.1) |
| Gain of function | misexpression, beads, injected mRNA | sufficiency in that context | FGF beads make limbs (7.5.31); Oskar at the anterior makes pole cells (7.5.14) | sufficiency depends on competence |
| Genetic screens and epistasis | saturate mutants for one phenotype, then order them | the parts of a pathway and their order | the segmentation screens (7.5.15; [7.4.20](/7.4.20)) | the screen finds only what gives a visible phenotype |
| Quantitative imaging | measure concentrations, flows and forces in living embryos | whether the numbers fit a model: decay lengths, positional error, viscosities | Bicoid precision (Formula 11.5.1); FRAP of Nodal and Lefty (Formula 16.4.1); tissue tension (Formula 7.5.1) | measurement changes the system (tags, light) |
| Model and perturbation | write a model, predict the effect of a change, test it | whether the proposed mechanism is sufficient and which parameter matters | Turing digits with Hox dose (7.5.32); segmentation clock with changed delay (7.5.23); BMP scaling after bisection (7.5.16) | agreement does not prove the mechanism; models must make risky predictions |
| Organoids and embryo models | let stem cells self-organize in culture | what cells can build without the embryo; human-specific development | gastruloids and blastoids (Definition 36.6.1); kidney organoids (7.5.25) | an organoid is not an organ; ethical limits (7.5.36) |

The progression of the field is visible in the table: the first five designs, from Roux to Grobstein, established the phenomena (mosaic and regulative development, induction, competence, determination); genetics found the molecules; and quantitative imaging, models and organoids now test whether the molecules suffice to explain the phenomena quantitatively.

### 40.4.2. Four Recurring Mathematical Ideas

Behind the variety, four ideas account for most of the models.

1. Gradient flows and Lyapunov functions: whenever a system lowers an energy (adhesion energy in sorting, surface energy in tissue flow, the Gibbs function of a closed chemical system), it relaxes to a minimum and the qualitative outcome is decided by the shape of the energy: this is the content of Theorem 8.6.1, Theorem 12.2.1 and the landscape picture of 7.5.10. The same fact explains a negative result: a closed, isolated tissue with one diffusing component cannot hold a pattern (Theorem 11.3.2), and patterns need broken detailed balance.
2. Linear instability and the fastest-growing mode: Turing patterns (Theorem 12.3.1), chemotactic aggregation (Theorem 13.4.1), the mechanochemical condensation of the dermis (Theorem 22.5.1), the Greenspan instability of a dividing cell (7.5.8) and the KST sequence of compartments (Proposition 33.7.1) are all found by linearizing about a uniform state and asking which wavenumber grows; the answer gives the spacing, the nonlinear theory (amplitude equations, Proposition 13.6.1, Proposition 33.8.1) gives the final form.
3. Thresholds on exponentials give logarithms: a threshold $T$ on an exponential profile $C_0\mathrm{e}^{-x/\lambda}$ lies at $x = \lambda\ln(C_0/T)$; hence boundaries shift by $\lambda\ln 2$ when the dose doubles (Definition 11.4.1), the number of pole cells grows as the logarithm of the Oskar dose (Formula 27.2.1), the age of menopause depends logarithmically on the ovarian reserve (Formula 27.7.1), and a two-zone FGF–RA front moves logarithmically with its source (Proposition 23.6.2). Logarithmic dependence is the mathematical reason why many developmental outputs are robust to large changes in gene dosage.
4. Bistability with memory: positive feedback with cooperativity gives two stable states separated by a fold; a transient signal can flip the switch, and the new state persists after the signal is gone (Proposition 11.7.1). The course met this circuit in the Nanog–Gata6 decision (Formula 5.6.1), the gap genes (Formula 15.2.2), the segment-polarity loop (Formula 15.4.1), the neural tube code (Formula 18.7.1), the gonad (Formula 26.2.1) and blood lineages (Formula 29.8.1). It is the molecular form of determination.

### 40.4.3. Scales

The physical scales of development constrain which mechanisms are possible. A paracrine signal with $D \approx 1$–$10\,\mu\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$ crosses $100\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ in $L^2/D \approx 10^3$–$10^4\,\mathrm{s}$ but a centimetre only in weeks (Theorem 11.4.1); tissue flows at about $10\,\mathrm{nm\,s^{-1}}$, a millimetre a day, at Reynolds numbers near $10^{-14}$ (7.5.8). Hence morphogen fields are typically less than a millimetre or about a hundred cell diameters across, large structures are patterned early when they are small and then grow (limbs, 7.5.31; the whole body plan, 7.5.17), and the long time scales of the organism (growth, reproduction, aging) are set not by diffusion but by cell kinetics and systemic hormones.

Three regularities run through the table. The same signalling families (Wnt, BMP, FGF, Hedgehog, Notch, RA; 7.5.3) induce almost every organ, so that the identity of an organ is set less by its inducer than by the competence of the responding tissue and the combination and timing of signals. Many syndromes affect several organs at once because the organs share a tissue of origin or a regulator: the neural crest (face, heart outflow, gut innervation, pigment), the pharyngeal endoderm with *TBX1* (thymus, parathyroids, heart, face), *TBX5* in the heart and the forelimb. And the most sensitive period for structural malformations is the embryonic period, when these inductions take place (7.5.2, Definition 38.4.2).

## 40.6. Cell, Inheritance and Development as One System

Cell biology, genetics and developmental biology describe one system at three levels: the cell provides the machinery, genetics provides the logic of transmission and the tools of analysis, and development uses both to build an organism that will make the next generation. The genetics course drew the correspondence row by row ([7.4.26](/7.4.26#sec-26.4)); seen from development, the system is the life cycle itself.

![The animal life cycle with the chapters of this course at each stage: gametes (7.5.26–7.5.27), zygote (7.5.4), blastula (7.5.5–7.5.6), gastrula and body plan (7.5.9, 7.5.14–7.5.17), organs (7.5.18–7.5.33) and the adult (growth, renewal and aging, 7.5.34–7.5.37). The dashed chord is the germ line, set aside early and carried to the next generation (7.5.27). Beside each stage, filled boxes give the cellular machinery it uses (cell biology, 7.3): meiosis; Ca²⁺ signals and the restart of the cell cycle; cycles without growth, polarity and the Hippo pathway; adhesion, EMT and actomyosin; signalling, differentiation and apoptosis; stem cells, senescence and cancer. Open boxes give the genetic logic that analyses it (genetics, 7.4): segregation, recombination and imprinting; maternal-effect genes; maternal and zygotic mutants; screens, epistasis and homeotic genes; mosaics and sex chromosomes; the two hits of cancer and heritability](./plots/life-cycle-system.py)

Read around the ring, the figure makes three points.

- Development is where the levels meet: each stage of the life cycle is explained by cellular machinery acting in a particular place and time. **Meiosis** ([7.3.21](/7.3.21)) becomes **gametogenesis** (7.5.27); the cell cycle ([7.3.20](/7.3.20)) becomes cleavage and its timer (Formula 5.2.1); signalling pathways ([7.3.18](/7.3.18), [7.3.19](/7.3.19)) become induction and morphogen gradients; adhesion and junctions ([7.3.23](/7.3.23)) become sorting and gastrulation (7.5.7, 7.5.9); **apoptosis** ([7.3.25](/7.3.25)) carves digits (7.5.32) and matches neurons to targets (7.5.21); stem cells and the **stem-cell niche** ([7.3.24](/7.3.24)) become renewal and regeneration (7.5.35–7.5.36); **cellular senescence** and telomeres ([7.3.25](/7.3.25)) become aging (7.5.37).
- Genetics supplies both the inputs and the methods: the egg's axes are the products of **maternal-effect genes** ([7.4.20](/7.4.20)); the parental genomes are made unequal by **genomic imprinting** ([7.4.18](/7.4.18)), set in the germ line (7.5.27); sex chromosomes start gonadal development ([7.4.6](/7.4.6), 7.5.26). The methods are genetic: screens found the segmentation genes (7.5.15), **mitotic recombination** and **genetic mosaics** ([7.4.9](/7.4.9)) revealed compartments (7.5.33), and **epistasis analysis** ordered the pathways.
- The circle closes in the germ line: Weismann's separation of germ line and soma (7.5.1) is the reason development and heredity can be studied separately at all, and it is also why the soma ages while the germ line continues (7.5.37). What passes between generations is a genome, a set of maternal products and a pattern of imprints; everything else is rebuilt each generation, which is why changes in development are the material of evolution (7.5.39).

## Questions

1. A morphogen forms an exponential gradient with decay length $\lambda = 100\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ in a field $500\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ long. Two target genes have thresholds that differ by a factor of 6. How far apart are their boundaries? By how much does each boundary move if the source strength doubles, and how would self-enhanced degradation change the answer?

   **Answer** For $C = C_0\mathrm{e}^{-x/\lambda}$ the boundary of threshold $T$ is at $x = \lambda\ln(C_0/T)$ (Definition 11.4.1), so two thresholds differing by a factor 6 are $\lambda\ln 6 = 179\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ apart. Doubling $C_0$ moves every boundary by $\lambda\ln 2 = 69\,\mu\mathrm{m}$, about 14 % of the field. With self-enhanced degradation the profile is a power law $C = 6D/[k(x+\varepsilon)^2]$, and doubling the source shifts a boundary only by about $0.21\varepsilon$ (Proposition 11.6.1), so the pattern becomes much less sensitive to dose.

2. An activator–inhibitor system has the Jacobian $J = \left[\begin{smallmatrix}1 & -1\\ 2 & -1.5\end{smallmatrix}\right]$ and diffusivities $1$ (activator) and $d$ (inhibitor). Show that the uniform state is stable without diffusion, find the critical ratio $d_c$ for a Turing instability and the critical wavenumber.

   **Answer** $\operatorname{tr} J = -0.5 < 0$ and $\det J = -1.5 + 2 = 0.5 > 0$, so the uniform state is stable. With diffusion the determinant of $J - k^2\operatorname{diag}(1, d)$ is $h(k^2) = d k^4 - (d f_a + g_h)k^2 + \det J$ with $f_a = 1$, $g_h = -1.5$. Instability requires $\min h < 0$, i.e. $d - 1.5 > 2\sqrt{0.5\,d}$ (Theorem 12.3.1). With $s = \sqrt d$: $s^2 - \sqrt2\,s - 1.5 = 0$, $s = (\sqrt2 + \sqrt8)/2 = 2.121$, so $d_c = 4.5$. At threshold $k_c^2 = (d f_a + g_h)/2d = 3/9 = 1/3$ (Formula 12.3.1), i.e. a wavelength $2\pi/k_c = 10.9$ in units of $\sqrt{D_a/\text{rate}}$. Figure B of 40.4.2 shows the dispersion curves.

3. In the clock-and-wavefront model, somite length is $S = vP$ (Formula 23.7.1). In a zebrafish embryo the clock period is 30 min and somites are about $50\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ long. What is the front speed? A mutation lengthens the transcriptional delay so that the period increases by 20 %, with the front speed and the total duration of somitogenesis unchanged. Predict the somite length and number.

   **Answer** $v = S/P = 50\,\mu\mathrm{m}/30\,\mathrm{min} = 1.7\,\mu\mathrm{m\,min^{-1}}$. With $P' = 1.2P$, $S' = 1.2S = 60\,\mu\mathrm{m}$, and since $N = \Theta/P$ (Formula 23.7.1), $N' = N/1.2$, about 17 % fewer somites. The period of a delayed negative feedback oscillator is about $2(T + 1/b + 1/c)$ (Formula 23.3.2), so a longer delay lengthens it, which is the logic of the experiments in 7.5.23.

4. A tissue is removed from a donor embryo and grafted to another position in a host. Describe the possible outcomes and what each shows about specification, determination and signalling. How would you distinguish an instructive from a permissive signal?

   **Answer** If the graft develops according to its original fate in the new place, it was determined (7.5.1); if it develops according to the new place, it was not yet determined there (it may still have been specified, which an isolation in neutral medium tests, Definition 1.5.1). If the graft changes the fate of host cells (labelled to tell donor from host), it is a signalling centre, as the organizer (Definition 16.5.1) and the ZPA (7.5.32). An instructive signal determines which fate the responder adopts, so different inducers give different structures from the same responder; a permissive signal is only needed for the responder to express a fate it already has, so different inducers give the same structure (Definition 3.4.2). The test is a recombination of one responder with several inducers, as with dermis and epidermis in 7.5.22.

5. A stem cell divides symmetrically into two stem cells with probability $p = 0.3$, asymmetrically with probability $r = 0.5$, and into two differentiated cells with probability $q = 0.2$. Is the process subcritical, critical or supercritical? What is the probability that a clone started by one stem cell dies out? What values of $p$ and $q$ give homeostasis?

   **Answer** The offspring generating function is $f(x) = q + rx + px^2$, with mean $m = r + 2p = 1.1 > 1$: supercritical. The extinction probability is the smallest root of $f(x) = x$ in $[0, 1]$ (Theorem 36.4.3): $0.3x^2 - 0.5x + 0.2 = 0$, roots $1$ and $2/3$, so $2/3$. Homeostasis needs $m = 1$, i.e. $p = q$; then individual clones still die out with probability 1, and the survivors grow, which is the neutral drift of 7.5.36 (Theorem 36.4.4).

6. By Murray's law (Theorem 29.5.1) a vessel of radius $r_0$ branching symmetrically gives two daughters of radius $r_0/2^{1/3}$. How many symmetric generations separate a vessel of radius $10\,\mathrm{mm}$ from a capillary of radius $4\,\mu\mathrm{m}$? What does the result say about how the vascular tree is built?

   **Answer** After $n$ generations $r_n = r_0 2^{-n/3}$, so $n = 3\log_2(r_0/r_n) = 3\log_2(2500) = 33.9$, about 34 generations. A tree of that depth cannot be specified branch by branch; it is built by local rules (sprouting from a plexus, tip selection by Dll4–Notch, Formula 29.3.1, and remodelling by shear stress), and the shear set point implements the global optimum locally (7.5.29).

7. Why does a 22q11.2 deletion cause defects of the thymus, the parathyroid glands, the outflow tract of the heart and the face together? Give another example of a single gene with effects in two organs that are not obviously related, and explain it in the same way.

   **Answer** The deleted region contains *TBX1*, required in the pharyngeal endoderm and mesoderm; the pharyngeal pouches form the thymus and the parathyroids, the pharyngeal mesoderm contributes to the second heart field, and the pharyngeal environment guides the cardiac and cranial neural crest that build the outflow septum and the facial skeleton (7.5.20, 7.5.28, 7.5.30). The organs share a tissue of origin and a signalling environment. Another example is *TBX5*: Holt–Oram syndrome combines septal defects with malformed forelimbs because Tbx5 specifies the forelimb field and patterns the heart (7.5.28, 7.5.31); this is mosaic pleiotropy in the sense of Definition 38.3.1.

8. A diffusible signal has $D = 10\,\mu\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$. Estimate how long it takes to establish a gradient over $100\,\mu\mathrm{m}$, $1\,\mathrm{mm}$ and $1\,\mathrm{cm}$. What do the numbers imply for the size of embryonic fields and for how large structures such as the limb are patterned?

   **Answer** The time scale is $L^2/D$ (Theorem 11.4.1 gives the local accumulation time, of the same order): $10^4/10 = 10^3\,\mathrm{s} \approx 17\,\mathrm{min}$; $10^6/10 = 10^5\,\mathrm{s} \approx 28\,\mathrm{h}$; $10^8/10 = 10^7\,\mathrm{s} \approx 4\,\mathrm{months}$. Gradients therefore pattern fields of at most about a millimetre (some 100 cells) within the hours to days available, and large structures are patterned while they are small and then grow: the limb bud is a fraction of a millimetre when its axes are specified (7.5.31–7.5.32), and the body plan is laid out in an embryo a millimetre long (40.4.3).

9. A gene with positive autoregulation follows $\dot x = s + 2x^4/(1 + x^4) - x$. Show that it has two stable states for small $s$, and explain why a transient signal can switch it on permanently. Name three decisions in the course that are thought to work this way.

   **Answer** Steady states satisfy $s = x - 2x^4/(1 + x^4)$. At $s = 0$ the roots include $x = 0$ (stable, since the derivative of the right-hand side is $-1$ there) and an upper root near $x = 1.84$ (stable), separated by an unstable root near $x = 1$. The lower branch ends at a fold at $s \approx 0.38$; a signal above it drives $x$ to the upper branch, which extends back to $s = 0$, so the gene stays on after the signal is removed (Proposition 11.7.1, figure C of 40.4.2). Examples: the SOX9 side of the toggle switch of the gonad after a pulse of SRY (Formula 26.2.1), the neural tube code with hysteresis in the response to Shh (Formula 18.7.1), and the segment-polarity loop that stores the pair-rule pattern (Formula 15.4.1).

10. Mortality in humans doubles about every 8 years. By how many years would a treatment that halves mortality at all adult ages extend median life span? Why does the answer not depend on the age at which mortality starts to rise?

    **Answer** Under the Gompertz law $\mu(x) = B\mathrm{e}^{\gamma x}$ with $\gamma = \ln 2/8 = 0.087\,\mathrm{yr^{-1}}$, halving $B$ is equivalent to shifting the curve by $\ln 2/\gamma = 8$ years (Definition 37.1.2, Example 37.1.1), so the median life span increases by about 8 years. The shift is the same at all ages because multiplying an exponential by a constant is a translation in time; the Makeham term $A$ and the starting age do not enter as long as the Gompertz term dominates.

11. Design an experiment that tests whether a proposed reaction–diffusion mechanism explains the number of digits, rather than merely being consistent with it. What predictions distinguish it from a positional-information model?

    **Answer** A Turing mechanism predicts that the number of condensations changes with the width of the field at fixed wavelength, and that changing a kinetic parameter changes the wavelength (7.5.32: reducing distal Hox dose increases digit number with thinner digits; in the chamber model the number $n$ follows $(n\pi/b)^2 = r/D + \lambda^2/a^2$, Proposition 32.5.1). A positional-information model predicts a fixed number of fates set by thresholds, with digit size scaling with the field. Experiments: widen or narrow the autopod (for example by changing Shh or Gli3 activity) and count digits and measure their spacing; vary BMP or Wnt activity in culture and measure the wavelength; compare with model predictions computed before the experiment. Agreement with risky predictions supports the mechanism; agreement only with the final pattern does not.

12. Explain, with examples from the course, the statement that cell biology, genetics and development form one system, and say what development adds to the other two.

    **Answer** Cell biology describes the machinery (signalling, adhesion, the cytoskeleton, the cell cycle, meiosis, death and senescence), genetics the transmission of genes and the tools to analyse them (maternal effects, mosaics, screens, epistasis, imprinting), and development uses both: induction is a signalling pathway acting between tissues at a particular time, gastrulation is adhesion and actomyosin acting on sheets, cleavage is a cell cycle running on maternal stocks (40.6). Development adds space and time: which cells receive which signal where and when, and how the result is remembered. The germ line, set aside in development, carries the genome to the next generation, so development also connects heredity to evolution (7.5.39).
