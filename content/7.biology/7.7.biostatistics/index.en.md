Biostatistics answers questions of the following kind in biomedicine: whether a drug works, whether an exposure causes disease, how likely a person with a positive test is to be truly ill, how long a patient can expect to live. None of these answers can be read off a single individual, because individuals differ greatly and even two measurements on the same person disagree; biostatistics is the methodology for reaching reliable conclusions in the presence of such variation. Its questions fall by purpose into description, estimation, comparison, association and adjustment, causation, and prediction, and the choice of method depends on two things: the structure of the data, that is, the type of the outcome variable and which of the dimensions "individuals × variables × time" are present; and how the data were obtained, whether from a probability sample, from an experiment with **randomization**, or from plain observation. This course is a compact, self-contained version of [statistics](/3): each method comes with its intuition, conditions of use, formulas, code and a biomedical example, so it can be read without the statistics courses, and heavier derivations link to the theorems there. It is also closer to medicine than the statistics courses are: epidemiologic studies, diagnosis and screening, clinical trials and **meta-analysis** are core topics here.

The order of the course is the order of data shapes. The foundations start from the data matrix, the scales of measurement and descriptive statistics, treat diagnostic tests through conditional probability, and lead from probability distributions and sampling distributions to confidence intervals and hypothesis tests. Next comes **cross-sectional data**, measured once, with methods arranged by the type of the outcome: one-sample and two-sample t methods for continuous outcomes, rank methods for skewed and ordinal outcomes, contingency tables for categorical outcomes, correlation and regression for a continuous predictor, the nonlinear standard curves and dose–response curves common in the laboratory, and analysis of variance for comparing many groups together with the principles of experimental design (replication, randomization, blocking, and one of the commonest laboratory mistakes, **pseudoreplication**). The third part adds study design and time: effect measures and biases in observational studies; logistic regression and generalized linear models for adjusting for many variables at once; **potential outcomes**, **causal diagrams** and **propensity scores** for deciding when adjusted associations can be called causal; then **survival data** with **censoring**, **longitudinal data** with within-subject correlation and mixed models, and the sample size, interim analyses and evidence synthesis of randomized controlled trials. The last part turns to omics data, in which variables far outnumber individuals: distances, clustering and principal components, thousands of simultaneous tests and the **false discovery rate**, models for RNA-seq counts based on the **negative binomial distribution**, and the building and honest evaluation of prediction models. Only a few real research settings run through these chapters, each raising a new question on every return: the East Boston paradox in which children who smoke seem to have better lung function, followed from boxplots and two-group comparisons to a multiple regression in which a coefficient changes sign, to **confounding** and causal diagrams; the Doll–Hill case-control study and the British doctors cohort; the Physicians' Health Study of aspirin and the meta-analyses that followed; low-dose CT screening for lung cancer; laboratory assays such as ELISA and enzyme kinetics; and the airway transcriptome of airway smooth muscle cells treated with dexamethasone. The course summary reorganizes these methods into a map for choosing a method by research question, outcome type and design.

The mathematical foundations come from the statistics courses: [probability theory](/3.1), [statistical inference](/3.2), [linear regression analysis](/3.3), [multivariate statistical analysis](/3.4) and [statistical computing and software](/3.5). The biological background comes from other courses: ELISA and enzyme kinetics from [biochemistry](/7.1), while the statistics of GWAS, the correction for population stratification and Mendelian randomization are treated fully in [genetics](/7.4) and are linked from the relevant places here. There is no clear border between biostatistics, bioinformatics and systems biology, and the three courses divide the ground as follows: statistics is the common foundation of the other two; [bioinformatics](/7.8) handles linear and semi-linear structures, namely sequences, alignments, genomes, variants and phylogenetic trees, where the E-value of a database search is a relative of the multiple testing here; [systems biology](/7.9) handles omics data and the networks built from them, and the general mathematics of networks lives in [network science](/1.4.7). The same kind of data is shared among the courses: the statistical principles of RNA-seq (normalization, negative binomial generalized linear models, false discovery rate) are in [7.7.20](/7.7.20), the experiment and the complete analysis pipeline in [7.9.2](/7.9.2), both using the same airway data with identical numbers; dimension reduction, clustering and pseudobulk analysis of single-cell and spatial omics also rest on the high-dimensional methods of this course.

Among the readers I had in mind in writing this course are students who take part in biology olympiads; when I studied biostatistics at that stage, I used a Chinese textbook. It is not wrong to see biostatistics as a condensed statistics, but it has its own use: for these readers it should be an abridged statistics that is also a self-consistent system, usable at the bench at any moment, without first reading the whole of statistics. Biostatistics is closer to medicine than to statistical theory; one of the two traditional biostatistics courses I took was offered by a medical school, and its questions came from patients, cohorts and laboratories. I therefore wanted biomedicine to be the theme of this course, not merely the background of its examples: instead of piling up many unrelated examples, I deliberately reduced their number so that a few of them become an undercurrent, each reused as often as possible to tie together more knowledge, as smoking and health runs from a descriptive plot all the way to causal inference and survival analysis.

Another approach that I find fresh is to divide the course by data type, as 40880033, offered by a statistics department, does: cross-sectional data, survival data, longitudinal data, modern high-dimensional biological data, plus modern statistical methods such as large-scale inference and variable selection. I attended only the first lecture of that course, but its way of dividing the material is worth borrowing: traditional biostatistics is organized by method, while organizing by the shape of the data shows why each method appears where it does. The content of this course is mainly traditional biostatistics, with a structure that also takes this approach into account, and the last few chapters look at omics data from a statistical point of view. I am also willing to regard statistics as the foundation for learning bioinformatics: new methods for omics keep appearing in large numbers, yet the multiple testing, batch effects, overdispersion and **overfitting** they face again and again are problems that statistics has already discussed here.
