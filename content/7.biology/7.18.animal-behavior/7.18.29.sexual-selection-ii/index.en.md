In the previous chapter ([7.18.28](/7.18.28)) we saw why males usually compete for females and how they compete, by fighting, sneaking and outnumbering the sperm of their rivals. This chapter turns to the other half of Darwin's theory, mate choice. That females choose is now beyond doubt; why they choose has been one of the most debated questions of evolutionary biology. A female may gain resources from her mate (food, a territory, care for her young), or she may gain only sperm and yet be very choosy, in which case the benefits must be indirect, carried by the genes of her offspring. Or her preference may be a byproduct of a sensory system that evolved for another purpose, which males exploit, and against which females may then evolve resistance.

The theoretical models of these processes, Fisher's runaway (**Fisherian runaway**), good genes and the handicap principle, and **sensory bias**, were developed in [7.10.13](/7.10.13#sec-13.2.3); here we concentrate on the behavioral evidence, the experiments that distinguish the hypotheses and the rules females use to choose. The second part of the chapter deals with the conflicts of interest between males and females over whether to mate and whose sperm should fertilize the eggs, which produce some of the most bizarre adaptations in the animal kingdom. Conflict over parental care is treated in [7.18.32](/7.18.32), and the role of courtship signals in mating sequences in [7.18.40](/7.18.40).

## 29.1. Evidence for Mate Choice

### 29.1.1. Experiments

Darwin had little firm evidence that females choose, and nearly a century passed before anyone tested the idea experimentally. Malte Andersson's (1982) study of the long-tailed widowbird (*Euplectes progne*) in Kenya is the classic. Males of this sparrow-sized bird grow tails up to half a meter long, which they display in a slow, cruising flight over their grassland territories when a female passes; they do not use the tails in contests with other males. Andersson mapped the territories of 36 males, counted the nests (females) on each, and divided the males at random into four groups. In one group he cut the tails to about 14 cm and glued the removed pieces onto the tails of a second group, lengthening them by about 25 cm on average; the third group was left untouched, and the fourth had its tails cut and glued back at the original length, to control for the handling. Before the manipulation the groups did not differ in the number of females they had attracted. Afterward, males with lengthened tails attracted significantly more new females than controls or shortened males. Shortened males did not display less or lose their territories, so the difference was caused by female choice. The key result was that females preferred tails longer than any natural tail: the tail length of the population must be held in check by natural selection, presumably by the costs of flying with a long tail.

Such costs have been measured in the barn swallow (*Hirundo rustica*). Males with experimentally lengthened tail streamers paired sooner and were preferred by females seeking extra-pair copulations, but they caught smaller prey, grew poorer feathers at the next molt and paired later the following year (Møller 1988, 1989). Song can be selected in the same way. Male sedge warblers (*Acrocephalus schoenobaenus*), which sing long, varied songs on arrival from Africa and stop singing as soon as they pair, acquire mates sooner the larger their repertoire, and females brought into breeding condition with estradiol in the laboratory responded more strongly to playbacks of larger repertoires (Catchpole 1980; Catchpole and colleagues 1984).

### 29.1.2. Multiple Ornaments

Males often carry several ornaments, which may be directed at different receivers. Males of the red-collared widowbird (*Euplectes ardens*) have both a long tail and a red collar. Long-tailed males attract more females, but the size and color of the collar predict success in defending a territory against rival males, not mating success (Pryke and colleagues 2001, 2002). The collar is a signal in male–male competition, the tail a signal to females. Within the species, males with larger or redder collars have shorter tails, and across the genus *Euplectes* there is a similar inverse relation between tail length and carotenoid coloration: widowbirds, with large, dispersed territories where female choice dominates, have long tails and little color, while bishops, which breed in dense colonies with intense male–male competition, have short tails and extensive red or yellow plumage (Andersson and colleagues 2002). A trade-off in allocation between two signals, together with the relative strength of the two kinds of sexual selection, may have shaped the diversity of the genus.

### 29.1.3. The Hypotheses

The benefits that females may gain by choosing, and the processes that may produce preferences without benefits, are summarized below. They are not mutually exclusive: a preference that first evolved through a sensory bias may later be maintained by good genes, and direct and indirect benefits often go together (Kokko and colleagues 2003).

| Hypothesis | Females gain | Prediction | Example (section) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Direct benefits | Food, territory, care, protection, fertility | Preferred males provide more resources; female fecundity or survival increases | Hangingflies, bullfrogs (29.2) |
| Fisherian runaway | Attractive sons | Genetic correlation between preference and trait | Stalk-eyed flies (29.3.1) |
| Good genes, handicaps | Viable offspring of both sexes | Ornament reflects condition; offspring of ornamented males survive better | Peacocks, sticklebacks (29.3.2) |
| Compatible genes | Offspring with complementary genotypes | Preference depends on the female's own genotype | Sticklebacks' MHC, crickets (29.3.3) |
| Sensory bias | Nothing (preference evolved in another context) | Preference older than the trait | Swordtails, water mites (29.4) |
| Chase-away | Nothing; resistance to manipulation | Females resist exaggerated traits; preference weaker where trait is present | Platyfish, wolf spiders (29.4) |

## 29.2. Direct Benefits

The simplest reason for choosiness is that males differ in what they offer.

**Definition 29.2.1 (Direct Benefits)** **Direct benefits** of mate choice are material benefits that increase the survival or fecundity of the choosing female herself, or of her current offspring, independently of the genes of the male: food, access to a territory or nest site, parental care, protection from predators or harassment, and the fertility of the male's ejaculate.

Selection on preferences for direct benefits is direct selection on the preference genes, through the female's own reproductive success, and it is therefore strong and easy to establish. Many examples involve food given by the male.

**Definition 29.2.2 (Nuptial Gift)** A **nuptial gift** is material other than sperm that a male transfers to a female during courtship or mating and that she consumes or uses: prey or other food items, glandular secretions, a nutritious part of the spermatophore (the *spermatophylax* of katydids), defensive chemicals, or the male's own body.

The hangingfly *Hylobittacus apicalis* gives a clear example of how a gift is used. A male catches an insect and releases a pheromone to attract a female; she feeds on the prey while they copulate. Randy Thornhill (1976) found that the duration of copulation increased with the size of the prey, and that the number of sperm transferred increased with the duration of copulation: females terminated copulation early when the prey was small, before the male had transferred a full ejaculate, and a male needed to copulate for about 20 minutes for full sperm transfer. A female that mates with males offering large prey gains food for her eggs and avoids the risks of hunting herself; a male with a small gift fertilizes fewer eggs.

Gifts may protect rather than nourish. Males of the moth *Utetheisa ornatrix* sequester pyrrolizidine alkaloids from their larval food plants and transfer them to the female in the spermatophore; she incorporates them into her eggs, which become distasteful to predators. The male's courtship pheromone is derived from the same alkaloids, so its concentration tells the female how much protection she will receive, and females prefer males whose pheromone signals a large load (Dussourd and colleagues 1991). In the Mormon cricket and many other katydids the spermatophylax is a substantial meal ([7.18.28](/7.18.28#sec-28.1.5)), and in redback spiders the male offers his own body ([7.10.13](/7.10.13#sec-13.2.3)).

Resources and care are the other main kinds of direct benefit. Male North American bullfrogs (*Lithobates catesbeianus*) defend territories in ponds where females lay their eggs. Some territories are warmer, so the eggs develop faster, and have fewer predatory leeches; females prefer these territories, and the largest males hold them (Howard 1978). Choice of a male here is choice of a site, and female choice and male–male competition point in the same direction. In species with paternal care, females can choose males that will be good fathers: male sedge warblers with larger repertoires feed their young more (Buchanan and Catchpole 2000), and redder male house finches (*Haemorhous mexicanus*), whose color depends on dietary carotenoids, bring more food to the nest (Hill 1991). Females may also gain from multiple mating itself: by acquiring enough sperm, by obtaining several gifts, by gaining help from several males (the polyandrous Galápagos hawks and dunnocks of [7.18.30](/7.18.30)), or by reducing the risk of infanticide from males that might have fathered her young.

## 29.3. Indirect Benefits

In lekking birds, bowerbirds and many other species, a female receives nothing from her mate but sperm, yet she chooses carefully. Female satin bowerbirds visit several bowers, watch the full courtship of many males over several weeks, and finally mate with one, often the same male that most other females choose (Uy and colleagues 2001); males with better decorated bowers obtain more matings, and removing the decorations reduces a male's success (Borgia 1985). In such cases a preference can evolve only if it changes the genes of the offspring, by indirect selection.

### 29.3.1. Fisherian Runaway

Fisher's hypothesis is that females gain attractive sons. If some females prefer males with a long tail, their offspring inherit both the genes for a long tail and the genes for the preference, so the two become genetically correlated (in **linkage disequilibrium**). Selection on males for the tail then drags the preference along, and the stronger preference intensifies selection on the tail, until natural selection on the tail balances sexual selection ([Theorem 13.2.1](/7.10.13#thm-13.2.1)).

The essential prediction, a **genetic correlation** between trait and preference, has been tested by artificial selection. Stalk-eyed flies (*Teleopsis dalmanni*, formerly *Cyrtodiopsis dalmanni*) of Malaysia have eyes on long lateral stalks, much longer in males, and males with the widest eye span roost with the largest groups of females at night. Wilkinson and Reillo (1994) selected one laboratory line for long male eye span and another for short eye span. After 13 generations the preference of females had changed as a correlated response: females of the long line preferred males with long eye spans, and females of the short line preferred males with short eye spans. Similar correlations between the strength of a trait and the strength of preference for it have been found among populations of guppies (Houde and Endler 1990) and within populations of three-spined sticklebacks, in which daughters of red fathers preferred red males while daughters of dull fathers did not (Bakker 1993).

These results show that the runaway process *can* operate but not that it is the only process at work. To support Fisher's hypothesis alone, one would have to show that the preferred trait indicates nothing about viability, a negative result that is hard to establish. Moreover, the runaway does not require the trait to be uninformative, and it can operate alongside good genes (Iwasa and colleagues 1991). A more serious difficulty is theoretical.

**Proposition 29.3.1 (A Cost of Choice Removes the Line of Equilibria)** In the model of [Theorem 13.2.1](/7.10.13#thm-13.2.1), add direct selection against the preference, with gradient $-k\bar y$ ($k > 0$), so that females with stronger preferences pay a cost (in time, energy or predation risk) that grows with the strength of their preference. With additive genetic variance $H$ of the preference,

$$
\Delta\bar z = \tfrac12 G\beta, \qquad \Delta\bar y = \tfrac12\left(B\beta - Hk\bar y\right).
$$
Then the only equilibrium is $\bar z = \theta$, $\bar y = \theta$ (natural selection optimum, no preference beyond it); in particular the line of equilibria disappears.

**Proof** At an equilibrium $\Delta\bar z = 0$, so $\beta = 0$ (assuming $G > 0$). Then $\Delta\bar y = 0$ requires $Hk\bar y = 0$, that is $\bar y = 0$ when $\theta$ is set to 0. Substituting into $\beta = (\bar y - \bar z)/\nu^2 - (\bar z - \theta)/\omega^2 = 0$ gives $-\bar z(1/\nu^2 + 1/\omega^2) = 0$, so $\bar z = 0$. $\square$

With a cost, populations near the line of equilibria slide slowly down it toward the naturally selected optimum, the preference eroding as it goes (Pomiankowski, Iwasa and Nee 1991). The decay is slow when the cost is small, because the indirect selection through the genetic covariance nearly balances it, but it is inexorable, and real preferences do have costs: female sage grouse travel between leks, female bowerbirds spend weeks visiting bowers. Preferences maintained only by Fisherian benefits are therefore expected to be fragile unless something keeps the male trait away from its natural optimum, such as a mutational bias toward smaller ornaments (males in poor condition from new mutations), which brings us to good genes.

### 29.3.2. Good Genes and Handicaps

The **good genes hypothesis** holds that ornaments reveal heritable viability, so that the offspring of both sexes of ornamented males survive better. Amotz Zahavi's (1975) **handicap principle** proposed why such ornaments can be reliable: they are costly, and only males of high quality can afford to bear them. Zahavi's original version, in which a male carries a fixed handicap and survives in spite of it, was rejected by early models: a handicap that reduces the survival of all males equally reduces the advantage of the genes for quality as much as it reveals them. The versions that work, formalized by Alan Grafen (1990), are condition-dependent: each male expresses the ornament to the degree that is optimal for him, and because the marginal cost of a larger ornament is lower for males in good condition, males in good condition develop larger ornaments.

**Example 29.3.1 (A Condition-Dependent Handicap)** Let a male of quality $q > 0$ choose ornament size $s \geq 0$. The mating benefit $b(s) = 1 - \mathrm{e}^{-s}$ is the same for all males (females respond to the ornament, not to quality directly), and the survival cost $c(s, q) = s^2/(2q)$ is smaller for high-quality males. The male's net payoff $b(s) - c(s, q)$ is maximal where $b'(s) = \partial c/\partial s$, that is

$$
\mathrm{e}^{-s^*} = \frac{s^*}{q}.
$$
The left-hand side decreases and the right-hand side increases with $s^*$, so there is a unique solution, and since the right-hand side decreases with $q$, $s^*(q)$ increases with quality. A female who prefers larger ornaments therefore chooses males of higher quality. The essential assumption is that the marginal cost $\partial c/\partial s = s/q$ decreases with quality; without it, all males would choose the same ornament and the signal would carry no information. The general theory of costly signals is developed in [7.18.39](/7.18.39).

A second theoretical problem is the *lek paradox*: if females consistently choose the best males, the additive genetic variance in fitness should be used up, as in artificial selection, and choice should cease to pay. Current views offer several answers. Deleterious mutations enter every genome continually, so it always pays to avoid mates that carry many of them. If ornaments depend on condition, and condition depends on almost every physiological process, a large part of the genome contributes to variation in ornaments (Rowe and Houle 1996). Females may prefer different traits in different years: in lark buntings (*Calamospiza melanocorys*), the trait that predicted male success changed from year to year (Chaine and Lyon 2008). And coevolution with parasites keeps changing which genes are good. Hamilton and Zuk (1982) proposed that bright plumage advertises heritable resistance to parasites that are themselves evolving; their prediction that species with more blood parasites are brighter was supported in their original survey but not consistently in later ones ([7.10.13](/7.10.13#def-13.2.5)).

Two case studies test good genes directly.

- *Peacocks.* In a feral population of Indian peafowl (*Pavo cristatus*) at Whipsnade, England, Marion Petrie found that the number of eyespots in a male's train predicted his mating success, and that removing eyespots reduced success in the following year (Petrie and colleagues 1991). She then mated females at random to males with trains of different quality, collected the eggs and reared the chicks under standard conditions. Chicks of males with larger eyespots grew faster and, when released into the park, survived better to two years of age (Petrie 1994). Because the mothers were assigned at random, the effect cannot be attributed to better females choosing better males. Females may, however, invest more in eggs fertilized by attractive males (as female mallards lay larger eggs after mating with attractive males), a confound that random mating does not remove. In a population in Japan, females showed no preference for more elaborate trains (Takahashi and colleagues 2008), and other studies found that eyespot number varies too little among males to be used: the train may be necessary but not decisive, while displays and vocalizations may be assessed instead.
- *Sticklebacks.* In spring, male three-spined sticklebacks develop red throats, build nests and court females with a zigzag dance; the male alone cares for the eggs and fry for about ten days. Milinski and Bakker (1990) offered females a choice between two males under white light and under green light, which makes the red invisible: females preferred the redder male under white light but showed no preference under green light, although the males courted equally in both. Redness signaled condition; when preferred males were infected with a parasite, their condition and their redness declined, and females no longer preferred them under white light. Choosing red males thus gives females a healthy partner and a better father, a direct benefit. Genetic benefits come in addition. Females prefer the odor of males whose genes of the **major histocompatibility complex** (MHC) complement their own, giving offspring an intermediate, optimal number of MHC alleles, and favor alleles that confer resistance to the prevailing parasites (Reusch and colleagues 2001; Eizaguirre and colleagues 2009). In the stickleback, all four assumptions of the Hamilton–Zuk hypothesis have been shown: parasites reduce fitness, resistance is heritable, it is signaled by the ornament, and females prefer the more ornamented males.

Other evidence points the same way. Male satin bowerbirds with better bowers carry fewer ectoparasitic feather mites (Doucet and Montgomerie 2003), and males that solve cognitive problems more quickly have higher mating success (Keagy and colleagues 2011). Female crickets (*Teleogryllus oceanicus*) approach artificial songs modeled on the songs of males with strong immune responses (Tregenza and colleagues 2006). In the gray tree frog (*Hyla versicolor*), the offspring of males that call with longer calls, which females prefer, grew faster and survived better than their maternal half-siblings sired by males with short calls (Welch and colleagues 1998), a design that controls for female effects by splitting the eggs of each female between two males.

### 29.3.3. Compatible Genes, Extra-Pair Mating and Polyandry

Good genes are good for every female; compatible genes are good only for some. A female's best mate may depend on her own genotype, as with the complementary MHC alleles of sticklebacks and the avoidance of related mates, whose offspring suffer **inbreeding depression**.

Many females mate with more than one male, even though one mating usually provides enough sperm. In socially monogamous songbirds, 10–40 percent of the offspring are often sired by males other than the social mate ([7.18.30](/7.18.30)). Females actively seek these copulations, and they seek males whose ornaments are more elaborate than those of their social partners: larger song repertoires in great reed warblers, longer tails in barn swallows, brighter ultraviolet crowns in blue tits and larger white forehead patches in collared flycatchers. A comparative study of 73 bird species found that sexual dichromatism was unrelated to the social mating system but increased strongly with the rate of extra-pair paternity (Owens and Hartley 1998), suggesting that male plumage evolved to attract other males' females. In blue tits and collared flycatchers, extra-pair young survived better than their within-pair half-siblings raised in the same nest, evidence of a genetic benefit.

The strongest test controls for female investment. In the brown antechinus (*Antechinus stuartii*), a small marsupial in which both sexes breed only once and males give no care, females usually mate with several males. Fisher and colleagues (2006) mated females in the laboratory three times, either three times with the same male or once each with three different males, and released mothers and litters into the wild. Litter sizes at birth were the same, but polyandrous females weaned about three times as many surviving young, and monandrous females did better when their single partner was a male who did well in sperm competition: the advantage of polyandry was that it allowed the best sperm competitor to fertilize the eggs, a form of choice after mating.

Choice after mating, **cryptic female choice** ([7.10.13](/7.10.13#rem-13.2.1)), can be powerful. Female feral fowl (*Gallus gallus*) eject, by cloacal contractions, the sperm of subordinate males that have forced copulations on them (Pizzari and Birkhead 2000). Female field crickets (*Gryllus bimaculatus*) mated to a sibling and a non-sibling, in either order, had the same hatching success as females mated to two non-siblings, and higher success than females mated to two siblings, because they preferentially stored the sperm of the unrelated male in their spermatheca (Tregenza and Wedell 2002; Bretman and colleagues 2009).

Extra-pair mating has costs, which limit it. In collared flycatchers, males whose mates were removed for an hour during the fertile period, simulating a trip to seek extra-pair copulations, later reduced their feeding of the chicks (Sheldon and colleagues 1997). And the best sire for sons may not be the best for daughters: in the red deer of Rum, males with high lifetime reproductive success fathered daughters of low fitness (Foerster and colleagues 2007), a sign of intralocus **sexual conflict** (Section 29.6).

## 29.4. Sensory Bias and Chase-Away Selection

A preference may exist before the trait it favors, as a byproduct of the receiver's sensory system, and males may evolve traits that exploit it. The evidence for **sensory bias** comes from phylogenies in which the preference is older than the trait ([Definition 13.2.3](/7.10.13#def-13.2.3)). In swordtails (*Xiphophorus*), males of many species have an elongated lower tail fin, the sword, but the related platyfishes of the same genus and the outgroup genus *Priapella* lack it. Alexandra Basolo (1990, 1995) attached plastic swords to males of the swordless platyfish *Xiphophorus maculatus* and of *Priapella olmecae*, and females preferred them to unmanipulated males. The preference thus probably predates the sword. A striking case of sensory exploitation involves the water mite *Neumania papillator*, which hunts copepods by waiting with its legs raised and detecting the vibrations of the prey. A courting male vibrates his legs at the frequency produced by a copepod, and females respond by orienting toward and clutching at him, as they do to prey; hungry females respond more strongly, which suggests that the courtship signal exploits the predatory response (Proctor 1991).

Sensory exploitation need not benefit the female, and if mating with exploiting males is costly, females are selected to resist.

**Definition 29.4.1 (Chase-Away Selection)** **Chase-away selection** (Holland and Rice 1998) is sexually antagonistic coevolution in which a male trait that exploits a female's sensory bias induces females to mate at a suboptimal rate or with suboptimal males, selection then favors females with a higher threshold of response to the trait, and that in turn favors males with a more exaggerated trait, producing escalating male traits and increasing female resistance.

The chase-away model makes a distinctive prediction: unlike Fisher's model, in which preference and trait evolve together, it predicts that females should be *less* responsive to the trait in species where the trait has evolved than in species where it is absent. Both swordtail and wolf spider examples fit. Female preference for swords is stronger in the swordless platyfishes and *Priapella* than in the swordtails themselves. In *Schizocosa* wolf spiders, tufts of bristles on the forelegs of courting males have stronger effects on the receptivity of females in species whose males lack tufts than in species whose males have them (McClintock and Uetz 1996). In *Drosophila melanogaster*, females prefer large males, but mating with them shortens their lives (Pitnick and García-González 2002). The chase-away model leads naturally to the general theory of sexual conflict in Section 29.6.

## 29.5. How Females Choose

### 29.5.1. Sampling Rules

To choose, a female must sample potential mates, and sampling costs time, energy and exposure to predators. How should she search? Two simple rules have been compared (Janetos 1980; Real 1990). Under the *best-of-n* rule, she inspects a fixed number $n$ of males and returns to the best. Under a *sequential threshold* rule, she inspects males one at a time and accepts the first one whose quality exceeds a threshold $T$.

**Proposition 29.5.1 (The Optimal Acceptance Threshold)** Let male quality $X$ have a continuous distribution with density $f$ and tail $\bar F(T) = \mathbb{P}(X > T)$, and let each inspection cost $c$. A female using the sequential threshold rule with threshold $T$ obtains the expected net payoff

$$
V(T) = \frac{\int_T^\infty x f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x - c}{\bar F(T)},
$$
and the threshold that maximizes $V$ satisfies $T^* = V(T^*)$: she should accept any male whose quality exceeds the expected payoff of continuing to search.

**Proof** The number of males inspected is geometric with success probability $\bar F(T)$, with mean $1/\bar F(T)$, and the accepted male's expected quality is $\mathbb{E}[X \mid X > T] = \int_T^\infty xf(x)\,\mathrm{d}x/\bar F(T)$; subtracting the expected cost $c/\bar F(T)$ gives $V(T)$. Writing $N(T) = \int_T^\infty xf\,\mathrm{d}x - c$, we have $N'(T) = -Tf(T)$ and $\bar F'(T) = -f(T)$, so

$$
V'(T) = \frac{-Tf(T)\bar F(T) + f(T)N(T)}{\bar F(T)^2} = \frac{f(T)}{\bar F(T)}\left[V(T) - T\right],
$$
which is zero where $V(T) = T$; it is positive for $T < T^*$ and negative above, so $T^*$ is the maximum. The condition says that at the optimum, rejecting a male of quality just below $T^*$ and searching on gives exactly what accepting him would. $\square$

The threshold rule is generally better than the best-of-n rule, because it stops early when a good male happens to be met first; and with both rules, females should be less choosy when sampling is more costly. Real females often use mixtures: female sage grouse and satin bowerbirds revisit several males before choosing, as under best-of-n, while females in species where searching is risky tend to accept males above a threshold. Female choosiness does vary with costs as predicted: female sand gobies, for example, are less choosy when predators are present, and in many species females late in the breeding season, with little time left, accept males they would earlier reject.

### 29.5.2. Learning and Copying

Preferences are not fixed. Many are learned early in life by **sexual imprinting** on the parents ([7.10.11](/7.10.11)), as in cross-fostered zebra finches and gulls that later prefer partners resembling their foster parents. Females can also learn from other females. In *mate-choice copying*, a female's preference for a male increases after she has seen another female choose him. Lee Dugatkin (1992) showed this in guppies: a female that had preferred one of two males switched her preference to the other male after watching him court a model female, unless the difference in color between the males was large. Copying can save sampling costs, especially for young, inexperienced females, and has been described in sage grouse, black grouse and Japanese quail, among others ([7.18.46](/7.18.46)).

### 29.5.3. Male Choice and Female Ornaments

When males invest heavily in offspring, they too should choose, and females may evolve ornaments. Sex-role reversal is the extreme case ([7.18.28](/7.18.28#sec-28.1.5)); in many monogamous birds in which both sexes care, both sexes are ornamented and both choose. In crested auklets (*Aethia cristatella*), both sexes grow forehead crests in the breeding season, and in experiments with models both sexes displayed most to members of the other sex with longer crests (Jones and Hunter 1993). Females of some primates that live in multimale groups develop large sexual swellings that vary in size and color during the cycle and signal fertility; in olive baboons, females with larger swellings reared more offspring (Domb and Pagel 2001), although later studies questioned this relation. Females may compete for the dominant male, or for matings with several males, which gives each male some chance of paternity and so reduces the risk of infanticide. Alpine accentors (*Prunella collaris*), songbirds living in multimale groups in which a male helps to feed a brood only if he has mated with its mother, have similar displays: females present a swollen red cloaca to males and sing to attract them during the fertile period (Nakamura 1990; Langmore and colleagues 1996).

## 29.6. Sexual Conflict

### 29.6.1. Conflict over Mating

Males and females share an interest in producing offspring but rarely agree on how to do it. A **sexual conflict** ([Definition 13.2.7](/7.10.13#def-13.2.7)) exists whenever the optimal outcome of an interaction differs between the sexes, and it leads each sex to evolve traits that bias the outcome toward its own interests, a process of *sexually antagonistic coevolution* (Parker 1979). Because a male's reproductive success usually increases with the number of his matings and a female's does not, conflict over whether to mate is the commonest form.

The conflict can be represented by the optimal mating rates of the two sexes. A female gains from the first matings (sperm, gifts) but loses from additional ones (time, energy, injury, infection, predation), so her fitness is maximal at an intermediate mating rate $m_f$; a male gains from every additional mating with her as long as each increases his share of paternity. A male trait that increases the mating rate, "persistence", is then favored even when it lowers female fitness, and a female trait that reduces it, "resistance", is favored in females; both traits have costs. Because each trait is favored only as long as the other sex has not countered it, both can escalate to costly levels while the mating rate itself changes little, and populations or species can come to rest at different levels of armament with similar mating rates (Arnqvist and Rowe 2005).

The water striders (Gerridae) studied by Göran Arnqvist and Locke Rowe provide the best evidence for such coevolution. A male water strider pounces on a female and tries to secure a mating by holding her; superfluous matings are costly for females, which carry the male around, feed less and are more exposed to predators, so females struggle violently to dislodge males, often by flipping over and pushing the male against the water surface. Males of different *Gerris* species have evolved grasping genital segments of different elaboration, and females have evolved abdominal spines that help them resist. Across 15 species, the levels of male grasping armament and female anti-grasping armament were closely correlated: species with well-armed males had well-armed females, and the balance between the two, not the absolute armament of either sex, predicted the mating behavior (Arnqvist and Rowe 2002).

Other examples of conflict over mating:

- *Scorpionflies.* Male *Panorpa* that cannot obtain a nuptial gift may force a female to copulate, holding her with a clamp on the dorsal abdomen, the notal organ (Thornhill 1980). The female obtains no food and must forage herself, which is dangerous: many scorpionflies die in spider webs, where they steal prey ([7.18.28](/7.18.28#sec-28.3.2)).
- *Waterfowl.* Unlike most birds, male ducks have a phallus, which in different species ranges from 1.5 to 40 cm, and its length increases with the frequency of forced extra-pair copulations. Females of species with long, elaborate phalluses have elaborate vaginas with dead-end pouches and spirals that coil in the opposite direction to the male's phallus, which make intromission without female cooperation difficult (Brennan and colleagues 2007).
- *Bed bugs.* Male bed bugs (*Cimex lectularius*) pierce the female's abdominal wall with their intromittent organ and inject sperm into her body cavity, *traumatic insemination*, which bypasses her genital tract and the choice it could exercise; females have evolved a special organ, the spermalege, at the site of piercing, which reduces the damage and infection.

### 29.6.2. Conflict after Mating

The male ejaculate is not only a vehicle for sperm. In *Drosophila melanogaster*, the seminal fluid contains more than 100 accessory gland proteins (Acps) that incapacitate rival sperm, protect the male's own sperm, increase the female's rate of egg laying and reduce her willingness to remate. Some of these benefits to the male are paid by the female: in an experiment in which females were exposed to males genetically engineered to lack the main Acps, to normal males, or to males that courted but could not mate, females exposed to Acps died sooner, with a median life span of 21 days against 29 days in the other treatments (Chapman and colleagues 1995). Males of *Heliconius* butterflies transfer an *anti-aphrodisiac* odor at mating that makes the female unattractive to other males (Gilbert 1976). Female counteradaptations include sperm ejection, sperm storage under their own control (Section 29.3.3) and complex reproductive tracts that act as obstacle courses for sperm.

Who wins such conflicts? Theory predicts a continual chase rather than a final victory (Parker 1979), and experimental evolution supports it. William Rice (1996) allowed male *Drosophila* to evolve for 41 generations against females that were prevented from evolving (by supplying fresh females from a stock each generation): the males became better at sperm competition and at inducing females to lay eggs, and the females mated to them died sooner. Holland and Rice (1999) then compared lines kept for 47 generations either with intense sexual selection (three males per female) or with enforced lifelong monogamy, in which a male's success depends entirely on his mate's success. Males of the monogamous lines evolved to be less harmful, courting and mating less, and their mates lived longer and produced more offspring; females of the monogamous lines in turn evolved lower resistance, so that they suffered more when mated to males from the sexually selected lines. Male adaptations are normally kept in check by female counteradaptations, and vice versa. Signs of the chase are found in molecular evolution: seminal proteins and other reproductive proteins evolve unusually fast, a possible engine of reproductive isolation ([7.10.13](/7.10.13#sec-13.2.5)).

## Summary

- Experiments show that females choose: widowbirds prefer tails longer than the natural maximum; sedge warblers prefer larger repertoires; ornaments have survival costs (barn swallows). Different ornaments can serve female choice and male–male competition (red-collared widowbirds).
- Why females choose:

  | Benefit | Key evidence |
  |---|---|
  | Direct benefits (Definition 29.2.1), nuptial gifts (Definition 29.2.2) | Hangingflies, *Utetheisa* alkaloids, bullfrog territories, better fathers |
  | Fisherian runaway | Correlated response of preference to selection on eye span (stalk-eyed flies); erodes if choice is costly (Proposition 29.3.1) |
  | Good genes, condition-dependent handicaps (Example 29.3.1) | Peacock offspring growth and survival; stickleback redness, condition and MHC; gray tree frog calls |
  | Compatible genes, cryptic female choice | MHC complementarity; antechinus polyandry; cricket sperm storage; fowl sperm ejection |
  | Sensory bias, chase-away (Definition 29.4.1) | Swordtails and *Priapella*; water mites; weaker preferences where the trait exists |

- Females sample mates by best-of-n or threshold rules; the optimal threshold equals the expected payoff of continued search (Proposition 29.5.1), and choosiness falls with sampling costs. Preferences can be learned by imprinting and copying. Males choose and females are ornamented when males invest heavily.
- Sexual conflict arises whenever optima differ between the sexes: over mating rate (water striders, scorpionflies, ducks, bed bugs) and over fertilization (*Drosophila* seminal proteins, anti-aphrodisiacs). Persistence and resistance escalate in sexually antagonistic coevolution, shown by experimental evolution in *Drosophila*; intralocus conflict makes the best sire for sons a poor sire for daughters.

## Questions

1. In Andersson's widowbird experiment, why were two control groups needed? What would have been concluded if only shortened and lengthened males had been compared?

   **Answer** The untouched controls measure the success of normal males; the cut-and-reglued controls show whether the handling and the gluing itself affected success. Comparing only shortened and lengthened males would show that tail length affects success but not in which direction relative to normal: the difference could arise because shortening reduced success (preference for normal or long tails) or lengthening increased it (preference for supernormal tails). The key finding, that lengthened males did better than normal males, could not have been established, and any effect could have been an artifact of handling.
2. A biologist finds that female house finches prefer redder males and that redder males feed their nestlings more. Design an experiment to test whether females also gain good genes.

   **Answer** Separate genetic from parental effects by cross-fostering: swap clutches or hatchlings between nests of red and dull males shortly after laying, so that each chick is raised by a foster father unrelated to it. Measure growth, immune response and survival of the chicks in relation to the color of the genetic father and of the foster father. Good genes predict an effect of the genetic father's color; direct benefits predict an effect of the foster father's color. Also control for differential maternal investment (for example by measuring egg size and yolk carotenoids, or by using split clutches from the same female), since females might invest more in eggs fertilized by attractive males.
3. Show that under the threshold rule of Proposition 29.5.1 a female should accept a male whose quality exceeds the expected payoff of continued search, and explain why the threshold should fall when a predator is present.

   **Answer** At the optimal threshold $V'(T) = [f(T)/\bar F(T)](V - T) = 0$, so $T^* = V(T^*)$: a male of quality $x$ should be accepted if $x$ exceeds what she can expect by rejecting him and searching on, which is $V$. A predator raises the cost $c$ of each inspection, which lowers $V(T)$ for every $T$ and hence the optimum $V(T^*)$; since $T^* = V(T^*)$, the threshold falls: she becomes less choosy. This is what is observed in sand gobies and guppies when predators are present.
4. Using Proposition 29.3.1, explain why preferences maintained solely by Fisherian benefits are unstable, and name two factors that could stabilize them.

   **Answer** With any direct cost of choice, the only equilibrium is the natural optimum of the trait with no preference, so preferences and exaggerated traits decay, slowly if the cost is small. Preferences can be stabilized if they also bring direct benefits (offsetting the cost), if they bring good-genes benefits that persist because genetic variance in condition is renewed by mutation or parasite coevolution, or if a mutational bias continually pushes the male trait below its naturally selected optimum, so that selection through the preference keeps acting.
5. How would you distinguish chase-away selection from Fisherian runaway using comparative data on a genus of fishes in which some species have a male ornament and others do not?

   **Answer** Map the ornament and the strength of female preference for it (measured, for example, by preferences for males with artificial ornaments in all species) onto a phylogeny. Under Fisherian runaway, preference and trait coevolve: species with the ornament should have stronger preferences than species without it. Under chase-away, the trait evolves by exploiting a preexisting preference and females then evolve resistance: preference should be present in ornament-less outgroups (the bias predates the trait) and be weaker in species with the ornament. Additional evidence for chase-away would be fitness costs to females of mating with highly ornamented males.
6. (Open question) Female water striders resist mating attempts that males persist in. Explain how an experiment that manipulates the cost of resistance could test the sexual conflict hypothesis for mating rates.

   **Answer** The hypothesis predicts that females mate at a rate that balances the cost of extra matings against the cost of resisting. If resistance is made more costly or harassment more intense (for example by increasing the density of males, or by reducing female mobility), females should accept more matings ("convenience polyandry") even though their fitness falls, while if males are given less opportunity to harass (shelters for females, lower male density), mating rates should fall toward the female optimum. Measuring female feeding, survival and egg production across treatments would show whether the realized mating rate is above the female optimum, as sexual conflict predicts. Manipulating male grasping structures (for example, ablating spines) or female abdominal spines and measuring mating success would test whether these armaments shift the outcome, as found in comparative and experimental work on *Gerris*.
