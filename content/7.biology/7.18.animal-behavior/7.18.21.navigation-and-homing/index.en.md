A homing pigeon released in unfamiliar country hundreds of kilometers from its loft circles once or twice and flies off toward home. A garden warbler hatched in Germany flies alone, at night, to West Africa on its first autumn migration and returns the next spring to within a few hundred meters of its birthplace. A salmon returns after years at sea to the stream in which it hatched, and a green turtle to an island 2 000 km from its feeding grounds. A desert ant, after a meandering search for food 200 m from its nest, turns and runs straight home. These achievements require that the animal determine a direction and, in many cases, its position relative to a goal, using information that we cannot perceive directly and that it must integrate with an internal clock.

The study of navigation became experimental in the 1950s, when Gustav Kramer discovered that caged migratory birds orient in the direction of migration and that they use the sun as a compass, and Karl von Frisch showed that bees use the pattern of polarized light in the sky. This chapter begins with concepts and methods (21.1–21.2), then treats the compasses of birds, the sun, the stars and the Earth's magnetic field (21.3), the maps that allow true navigation and the inherited programs of young migrants (21.4), the homing of salmon and sea turtles (21.5), and the navigation of insects over small distances, path integration and landmarks (21.6), ending with the question whether animals have cognitive maps (21.7). The physics and molecular mechanisms of magnetoreception are in [7.6.19](/7.6.19), the neural representation of head direction, place and path integration in mammals and insects in [7.16.44](/7.16.44) and [7.16.46](/7.16.46), the clock that compensates for the sun's movement in [7.18.18](/7.18.18#sec-18.6.2), and the migrations themselves in [7.18.20](/7.18.20). Spatial memory and the role of the hippocampus follow in [7.18.42](/7.18.42).

## 21.1. Orientation and Navigation

### 21.1.1. Compasses and Maps

**Definition 21.1.1 (Navigation)** **Navigation** is the process by which an animal determines and maintains a course toward a goal, which requires information about the direction of the goal relative to its own position and a means of keeping a chosen direction. *Orientation* in the narrower sense is the ability to move in a particular compass direction, without necessarily knowing the location of a goal.

Donald Griffin (1955) distinguished three levels of performance:

| Type | Ability | Information needed | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Piloting | Reaching a goal by following familiar landmarks or a learned route | Memory of landmarks | Homing over familiar ground; pigeons following roads |
| Compass orientation | Moving in a constant direction without reference to landmarks | A compass | Young migrants on their first migration; displaced juveniles |
| True navigation | Moving toward a goal from an unfamiliar place | A map, to determine position relative to the goal, and a compass | Experienced pigeons and adult migrants after displacement |

**Definition 21.1.2 (True Navigation)** **True navigation** is the ability to head toward a goal from a place the animal has never visited, without using cues emanating from the goal itself and without information collected on the outward journey. It requires a "map sense" by which the animal determines its position relative to the goal from local environmental cues.

Gustav Kramer (1953) proposed that homing pigeons and migrants solve true navigation in two steps, the *map-and-compass model*: at the release site the bird first uses a map to determine the direction of home ("home lies to the north-west"), and then uses a compass to fly in that direction. The two steps involve different information and can be studied separately: compass mechanisms by manipulating the cues (shifting the sun with mirrors, rotating a planetarium sky, changing the magnetic field) and map mechanisms by displacing animals to unfamiliar places. The distinction proved fruitful because birds turned out to have several compasses and possibly several maps, which they combine.

Movements in which the animal is guided by stimuli coming from the goal itself, such as a moth flying up an odor plume or a female cricket walking to a calling male, are not navigation in this sense but taxes ([7.18.12](/7.18.12)), and movements that retrace the outward path or use information collected on it, such as **path integration** (21.6), are a form of navigation that does not require a map.

**Definition 21.1.3 (Homing)** **Homing** is the return of an animal to a specific place, usually its nest, roost, breeding site or territory, after having left it voluntarily or after experimental displacement.

Homing ranges from the return of a limpet to its home scar on a rock after feeding, by following its own mucus trail, to the return of shearwaters over thousands of kilometers. A Manx shearwater (*Puffinus puffinus*) taken from its burrow on an island off Wales in 1953, flown to Boston and released, was back in its burrow 12.5 days later, having crossed 5 000 km of the Atlantic, which shearwaters of that population never cross (Mazzeo 1953).

### 21.1.2. Methods

The study of navigation relies on a few experimental paradigms:

- *Displacement experiments* move animals to a known release site, often by a route that deprives them of information (in darkness, in a rotating drum, under anesthesia), and record where they go: vanishing bearings (the direction in which a released bird disappears from view through binoculars), homing times and success, recoveries of ringed birds, and today complete GPS tracks.
- *Cue-conflict and manipulation experiments* change one cue while keeping others: mirrors that shift the apparent sun, clock shifts that change the expected position of the sun, planetarium skies, Helmholtz coils that change the magnetic field, anosmia (temporary loss of smell), magnets glued to the head.
- *Orientation cages* test caged migrants, which in the migration season hop and flutter in the direction in which they would migrate (migratory restlessness, [7.18.20](/7.18.20#def-20.5.1)). The most widely used is the funnel introduced by Stephen and John Emlen.

**Definition 21.1.4 (Emlen Funnel)** An **Emlen funnel** (Emlen and Emlen 1966) is an orientation cage for small birds consisting of an inverted cone of blotting paper or typewriter correction paper with an ink pad (or a smooth surface) at its center and a screen on top, through which the bird can see the sky but not the horizon. A bird in migratory restlessness repeatedly jumps from the base onto the sloping wall, leaving footprints or scratches; the distribution of marks around the funnel, counted in sectors, gives the bird's preferred direction for that night.

The data from such experiments are directions, and they are analyzed with the statistics of **circular data** ([3.6.7](/3.6.7#sec-7.5)). For a sample of headings $\theta_1, \dots, \theta_n$ the mean vector has components $\bar C = \frac{1}{n}\sum \cos\theta_i$, $\bar S = \frac{1}{n}\sum\sin\theta_i$, its direction is the mean direction $\bar\theta$ and its length $r = \sqrt{\bar C^2 + \bar S^2}$, between 0 (no preferred direction) and 1 (all identical), measures the concentration. The Rayleigh test asks whether $r$ is larger than expected from a uniform distribution, with test statistic $z = nr^2$; the V test asks whether the headings are concentrated around an expected direction. Each bird contributes one mean heading (from its marks in one night or from several nights), and the second-order analysis tests whether the birds of a group agree.

## 21.2. The Sun Compass

### 21.2.1. Kramer's Starlings

Kramer (1950) placed European starlings (*Sturnus vulgaris*) in a circular cage with six windows through which they could see the sky. In the migration season they fluttered in the direction of migration, but only when they could see the sun; under overcast skies they were disoriented. With mirrors that deflected the image of the sun by 90°, the birds shifted their direction by 90°. They were using the sun as a compass. Since the sun's azimuth changes during the day, a bird that keeps a constant direction must change its angle to the sun at the rate the azimuth changes; Kramer's birds did so, and starlings trained to find food in a particular compass direction, with an artificial "sun" (a lamp) fixed in position, changed their chosen direction relative to the lamp during the day by about the rate at which the real sun moves (Kramer 1953). The compass is time-compensated by the circadian clock.

**Definition 21.2.1 (Sun Compass)** A **sun compass** is a mechanism of orientation that uses the azimuth of the sun (or, in insects, also the pattern of polarized light it produces in the sky) as a directional reference. Because the sun's azimuth changes over the day, the compass is *time-compensated*: the angle between the animal's heading and the sun is adjusted with the time of day according to an internal circadian clock.

### 21.2.2. Clock Shifts

The decisive test of time compensation is the clock-shift experiment. Klaus Schmidt-Koenig (1958) kept homing pigeons for several days under an artificial light–dark cycle shifted by six hours relative to the natural day, which shifts their circadian clock. When released under a sunny sky, pigeons whose clock had been advanced six hours deviated from the home direction by about 90° counterclockwise, and pigeons whose clock had been delayed by six hours deviated by about 90° clockwise, as predicted if they misread the time by six hours and the sun moves about 15° per hour ([7.18.18](/7.18.18#sec-18.6.2) for the geometry). Under overcast skies, clock-shifted pigeons homed normally, because they were using another compass that does not depend on time.

![Model: clock-shift experiments with homing pigeons (simulated vanishing bearings, after Schmidt-Koenig 1958). Pigeons are released around noon at a site from which home lies due north (dashed line). A: controls vanish homeward. B: birds whose clock was advanced by 6 h interpret the noon sun as an evening sun in the west and deviate about 90° counterclockwise. C: birds delayed by 6 h deviate about 90° clockwise. Each dot is one bird; the arrow is the mean vector; the Rayleigh test is given below each panel.](./plots/clock-shift.py)

Clock-shift experiments have shown time-compensated sun compasses in many animals: migrating and homing birds, monarch butterflies, honeybees and ants, sandhoppers that find the shore line by the sun, fish and turtles. Young pigeons must learn to use the sun compass: they learn the course of the sun at their home loft during their first weeks of flying, and pigeons that had seen the sun only in the afternoon could not use it correctly in the morning (Wiltschko and colleagues 1981). Pigeons rely on the sun compass whenever it is available, even over familiar terrain, but older, experienced pigeons are less deflected by clock shifts over familiar ground, where they can also use landmarks.

## 21.3. Star and Magnetic Compasses

### 21.3.1. The Star Compass

Most songbirds migrate at night. Franz and Eleonore Sauer showed in the 1950s that caged warblers oriented correctly under a clear night sky and in a planetarium, but it was Stephen Emlen (1967, 1970) who analyzed how. He tested indigo buntings (*Passerina cyanea*), which migrate at night between eastern North America and Central America, in Emlen funnels under the sky of a planetarium.

- Under a planetarium sky that matched the natural sky, the birds oriented in the seasonally appropriate direction: south in autumn, north in spring. When the planetarium sky was rotated, the birds' headings rotated with it.
- When Emlen blocked out parts of the sky, orientation was lost only when the region within about 35° of the celestial pole, around the Pole Star and the constellations near it (the Big Dipper, Cassiopeia, Cepheus), was covered. The birds did not use a single star: they could orient with any of several constellations of the northern sky.
- The star compass is not time-compensated. Clock-shifted buntings oriented normally under the planetarium sky, because the axis of rotation of the sky, and the configurations of stars around it, indicate north at any time of night.
- The star compass is learned. Young buntings raised without a view of the sky were not oriented. Young buntings raised under a planetarium sky that rotated, in the nestling period before their first migration, about the star Betelgeuse in Orion instead of the Pole Star, later oriented away from Betelgeuse in autumn as if it were the Pole Star: they had learned that the center of rotation of the sky is north (Emlen 1970).

**Definition 21.3.1 (Star Compass)** A **star compass** is a mechanism of orientation by which night-migrating birds determine geographic north from the pattern of stars around the center of rotation of the night sky. In birds it is learned in the young bird by observing the rotation of the sky, and it is not time-compensated.

Learning the center of rotation rather than a particular star makes the star compass robust: the Pole Star is not exactly at the pole and moves over millennia with the precession of the Earth's axis, and the constellations visible change with latitude. The rotation axis is always geographic north.

### 21.3.2. The Magnetic Compass

Caged European robins (*Erithacus rubecula*) oriented in their migratory direction in closed rooms without any view of the sky, and in 1966 Wolfgang Wiltschko showed that their direction changed when the magnetic field around the cage was changed with Helmholtz coils. Wolfgang and Roswitha Wiltschko (1972) then found that the robins' compass is an **inclination compass** ([7.6.19](/7.6.19#def-19.3.2)): it does not respond to the polarity of the field, but to the axis of the field lines and their inclination with respect to gravity. Reversing the vertical component of the field reversed the birds' heading, as did reversing the horizontal component, while reversing both left it unchanged. The bird distinguishes "poleward" (where the field lines point downward) from "equatorward", not magnetic north from south.

The inclination compass has properties that point to its mechanism: it works only within a narrow window of field intensity around the local value, to which the bird can adapt within an hour; it needs short-wavelength light; and it is disrupted by weak radio-frequency fields of megahertz frequencies. These properties are explained by the **radical pair mechanism** in **cryptochrome** proteins of the retina (cryptochrome 4 in birds), and the compass information is processed in a visual area of the forebrain, cluster N; the physics is developed in [7.6.19](/7.6.19#sec-19.4). The magnetic compass has been found in birds, newts, sea turtles, salmon, spiny lobsters, mole rats, bats and insects; **magnetoreception** is therefore widespread, but its mechanisms may differ: mole rats have a polarity compass that works in darkness, consistent with magnetite-based receptors.

An inclination compass has one problem: at the magnetic equator the field lines are horizontal, and a bird crossing it cannot distinguish poleward from equatorward. Garden warblers that were exposed in the laboratory to a horizontal field, simulating the crossing of the magnetic equator, and then to the inclined field of the Southern Hemisphere, reversed their heading and headed southward toward the magnetic "equatorward" of the new field, as birds wintering in southern Africa must do (Wiltschko and colleagues 1992); a horizontal field acts as a cue to reverse the response.

### 21.3.3. Calibration Among Compasses

Birds have several compasses and must keep them consistent; one compass calibrates another. Young birds calibrate their magnetic compass with the rotation of the sky before their first migration; during migration, many songbirds recalibrate their magnetic compass each evening from the pattern of polarized light near the horizon at sunset, whose symmetry axis indicates geographic south–north independent of magnetic declination (Muheim, Phillips and Åkesson 2006). Which cue is the reference varies with age, species and experimental conditions, and conflicting results have been reported for decades, but the principle is general: a reliable cue calibrates the others, so that the bird can use whichever is available.

## 21.4. Maps and True Navigation

### 21.4.1. Juveniles and Adults: Perdeck's Starlings

Do migrants navigate to a goal, or do they merely fly in a constant direction for a certain time? A. C. Perdeck (1958) answered with one of the largest experiments in behavioral biology. Between 1948 and 1957, he and his colleagues caught about 11 000 starlings that were migrating in autumn west-southwest through the Netherlands from their breeding areas around the Baltic Sea to wintering areas in southern England, Belgium and northern France. The birds were ringed, transported by airplane to Switzerland, 600 km to the southeast, and released. The recoveries of ringed birds in the following winter showed a striking difference. Adults, which had migrated before, flew northwest, correcting for the displacement, toward their normal wintering area. Juveniles on their first migration flew west-southwest, as if they had not been displaced, and ended up in southern France and Spain, in a region shifted from the normal one by the displacement.

![Perdeck's displacement experiment (schematic: regions placed by approximate latitude and longitude, no coastlines). Starlings migrating west-southwest through the Netherlands (normal route, gray) were caught and released in Switzerland. Adults (blue) corrected for the displacement and flew northwest to the normal wintering area; juveniles (orange) kept the normal direction and ended in southern France and Spain.](./plots/perdeck.py)

The juveniles used compass orientation, an inherited program of direction and distance ("vector navigation", [7.18.20](/7.18.20#sec-20.5.1)); the adults used true navigation, which requires a map that they had acquired during their first migration. The result has been confirmed with modern tracking. White-crowned sparrows caught on migration in Washington State and flown 3 700 km east to New Jersey were followed with radio transmitters: adults headed west-southwest toward their normal wintering area, juveniles continued south, parallel to their normal route (Thorup and colleagues 2007). Adult Eurasian reed warblers displaced 1 000 km east from the Baltic coast to Moscow oriented in caged tests toward their breeding grounds, correcting for the displacement (Chernetsov, Kishkinev and Mouritsen 2008).

### 21.4.2. The Young Cuckoo's Program

The common cuckoo (*Cuculus canorus*) is a **brood parasite** ([7.18.32](/7.18.32)): its young are raised by songbird hosts that do not migrate to the same places, and the adult cuckoos leave Europe in summer, weeks before the young fledge. Nonetheless the young cuckoos migrate alone, at night, to the wintering area of their population in the forests of central Africa. Satellite tracking has shown that juvenile cuckoos from Denmark travel to the same general wintering region as the adults of their population, along a similar route (Vega and colleagues 2016). How can they? Since no adult cuckoo shows them the way, and their foster parents winter elsewhere, the information must be inherited: an innate program of the timing of departure, the direction (or a sequence of directions) and the distance or duration of travel, read with compasses that the young bird calibrates itself. The program has evolved by natural selection, because cuckoos whose inherited program led them to unsuitable areas did not survive or reproduce. It is the same kind of program as in the blackcaps whose inheritance has been analyzed by cross-breeding ([7.18.20](/7.18.20#sec-20.5.2)). Adult cuckoos, like adult starlings, have learned a map: experienced cuckoos displaced experimentally from Denmark to Spain corrected their routes toward their usual stopover and wintering areas (Willemoes and colleagues 2015).

### 21.4.3. Map Cues: Smell and Magnetism

What constitutes a bird's map is the most contested question in the field. A useful map requires at least two environmental gradients that vary over space in different directions; by comparing the values at its current position with remembered values at home, the bird can compute the direction of home (a bicoordinate grid map). Two kinds of cue are supported.

- *Olfactory cues.* Floriano Papi and colleagues (1972) found that pigeons made anosmic (by sectioning the olfactory nerves, or temporarily by zinc sulfate or local anesthesia of the nasal mucosa) homed poorly from unfamiliar release sites, though not from familiar ones. Pigeons raised in lofts with deflector panels that turned the wind by 90° deflected their vanishing bearings accordingly (Baldaccini and colleagues 1975), as if they had learned which odors come with winds from which direction. The olfactory map hypothesis (Papi; Wallraff 2005) proposes that young pigeons learn the distribution of atmospheric odors around their loft, carried by winds, and at a release site recognize from the local odor mixture the direction in which they have been displaced. The hypothesis was long contested, because anosmia could affect motivation, but GPS-tracked pigeons that had their olfactory nerves cut were impaired whereas birds whose trigeminal (magnetosensitive) nerve was cut were not (Gagliardo and colleagues 2006, 2009), and anosmic shearwaters released at sea failed to orient home while magnetically disturbed ones did not (Gagliardo and colleagues 2013). Olfactory navigation is now accepted for pigeons and some seabirds, though its importance in other birds is uncertain.
- *Magnetic cues.* The intensity and inclination of the geomagnetic field vary systematically over the Earth: inclination is $0^\circ$ at the magnetic equator and $90^\circ$ at the magnetic poles, and total intensity varies from about $25$ to $65\,\mu\mathrm{T}$, with gradients that are not parallel. A position can therefore in principle be estimated from the local combination of intensity and inclination. Magnetic anomalies affect the orientation of released pigeons (Walcott 1991). The best evidence for a magnetic map comes from sea turtles and fish (21.5).

**Definition 21.4.1 (Magnetic Map)** A **magnetic map** is a mechanism by which an animal derives information about its geographic position, or about the direction of a goal, from the local values of parameters of the geomagnetic field, such as total intensity, inclination and declination, that vary predictably over the Earth's surface.

Pigeons over familiar terrain also use visual landmarks: GPS tracks of pigeons released repeatedly from the same site show that each bird develops an idiosyncratic route that it follows faithfully, often along roads, rivers and other linear features (Biro, Meade and Guilford 2004). Proposals that pigeons use low-frequency sound (infrasound) produced by ocean waves and topography as a map cue (Hagstrum 2013) remain speculative.

## 21.5. Salmon and Sea Turtles

### 21.5.1. Salmon Homing and Olfactory Imprinting

Pacific salmon hatch in freshwater streams, spend months to years there as fry and parr, transform into smolts that migrate to the sea, feed for one to several years in the ocean, often thousands of kilometers away, and return to spawn in their natal stream. Tagging studies showed that most adults return to the stream, even the tributary, where they were released or hatched; straying rates are usually a few percent. Transplantation experiments located the learning in time: salmon transferred to another stream before the smolt transformation returned to the stream of release, not of birth, whereas those transferred after it returned to their natal tributary.

**Definition 21.5.1 (Olfactory Imprinting Hypothesis)** The **olfactory imprinting hypothesis** (Hasler and Wisby 1951) states that salmon learn the characteristic odor of their home stream during a sensitive period, chiefly during the smolt transformation, and as adults use this memorized odor to find the stream when they return from the sea, choosing at each fork of the river system the branch that carries the familiar odor.

Arthur Hasler was led to the hypothesis by von Frisch's discovery of the extraordinary olfactory sensitivity of fishes and by Lorenz's work on imprinting. The evidence for it is of four kinds.

1. *Discrimination.* Fish trained by reward and punishment learned to discriminate the water of two streams; the ability was lost when the nasal sacs were cauterized or when the organic fraction was removed from the water. Electrodes in the olfactory bulb of returning adults recorded strong responses to home-stream water and weak responses to water from other streams.
2. *Plugging the nose.* Adult coho salmon captured at a fork in a river system and released downstream returned to the branch in which they were captured, but fish with their nasal openings plugged chose branches at random (Wisby and Hasler 1954).
3. *Artificial imprinting.* Hasler and Scholz exposed hatchery coho smolts to a low concentration of morpholine, a chemical not found in natural waters, while controls were raised without it; both groups were marked and released into Lake Michigan. When, 18 months later during the spawning season, morpholine was metered into one stream, Oak Creek, most fish recaptured there were from the morpholine-exposed group; in a year when no morpholine was added, few fish of either group entered the creek. Smolts imprinted on morpholine or on β-phenylethyl alcohol chose the stream scented with "their" chemical, and ultrasonic tracking of imprinted fish in Lake Michigan showed that they stopped and milled in an area scented with morpholine but swam through it when it was unscented (Hasler and Scholz 1983).
4. *Hormonal control.* Imprinting coincides with a surge of thyroxine at the smolt transformation; presmolts treated with TSH, which raises thyroid hormones, while exposed to an artificial odor later chose the stream with that odor, whereas untreated presmolts did not. Increases in thyroid hormone are also caused by environmental changes (new water, changes in temperature or flow), and Scholz proposed that salmon imprint sequentially on a series of odors along their downstream migration, which they retrace in reverse on their return (the *sequential imprinting hypothesis*).

The natural odors that salmon imprint on probably include the bouquet of dissolved organic compounds, among them amino acids, of each stream, and population-specific odors of conspecifics; a role for the odors of juveniles of the same population (pheromones) has been proposed. Olfactory imprinting explains homing within the river system, but not how salmon find the river mouth from the open ocean. There the evidence points to a magnetic map. Sockeye salmon of the Fraser River, returning from the Gulf of Alaska, can reach the river either north or south of Vancouver Island; the proportion using the northern route varied from year to year with the drift of the geomagnetic field, as predicted if the fish seek the magnetic signature that the coast near the river mouth had when they left as smolts (Putman and colleagues 2013). Juvenile Chinook salmon exposed in the laboratory to magnetic fields typical of the northern and southern limits of their oceanic range oriented in the directions that would take them back toward the center of their feeding area (Putman and colleagues 2014). Salmon thus appear to imprint on both the odor of their stream and the magnetic field of its mouth, an idea called *geomagnetic imprinting*.

### 21.5.2. Sea Turtles

Female loggerhead turtles (*Caretta caretta*) of the North Atlantic nest on beaches in Florida and the southeastern United States; the hatchlings emerge at night and must reach the sea, cross the coastal waters and enter the North Atlantic gyre, in which juveniles travel for years around the ocean before returning to coastal feeding grounds and, as adults, to nest near their natal beach. Kenneth and Catherine Lohmann analyzed the cues at each stage.

- *On the beach* the hatchlings crawl toward the brightest, lowest horizon, which on a natural beach is the open sea; artificial lights behind beaches disorient them and kill many.
- *In the surf* they swim into the waves, which near the shore move toward the beach, so swimming into them leads seaward. In laboratory tanks hatchlings oriented into wave motion, detected by its orbital accelerations, and in the dark (Lohmann, Swartz and Lohmann 1995).
- *Offshore*, beyond the waves, they keep their seaward direction with a magnetic compass. In the open ocean they respond to the magnetic field as to a map: hatchlings exposed in the laboratory to magnetic fields replicating those at locations along the edge of the North Atlantic gyre swam in directions that, at each location, would keep them within the gyre and away from dangerous cold waters or the coast (Lohmann and colleagues 2001). Juvenile green turtles exposed to fields of locations 337 km north or south of their feeding ground swam in the direction that would take them home (Lohmann and colleagues 2004).

The nesting sites of loggerheads along the coast of Florida change from year to year in a way that tracks the drift of the magnetic field: where the magnetic signatures of neighboring coastal areas converged, nesting densities increased, and where they diverged, nesting densities decreased (Brothers and Lohmann 2015). Turtles, like salmon, appear to imprint on the magnetic field of their natal area. The navigation of green turtles from the coast of Brazil to Ascension Island, a target 8 km across after a journey of 2 200 km, probably combines a magnetic map for the long approach with odors carried downwind from the island over the final tens of kilometers (Lohmann and colleagues 2008).

## 21.6. Navigation by Insects

### 21.6.1. Path Integration in Desert Ants

Desert ants of the genus *Cataglyphis* forage alone in the heat of the Saharan day for insects killed by the heat, in flat terrain where trails would be blown away and odors evaporate. A forager searches along a tortuous path that may take it 200 m from the nest, and when it finds food it turns and runs straight back to the nest. Rüdiger Wehner and colleagues showed that it does so by **path integration** ([7.16.44](/7.16.44#def-44.4.1)): during the outward journey the ant continuously updates a vector pointing home by integrating the directions and distances of all its movements. Ants captured at a feeder and displaced to unfamiliar ground ran off the vector they had computed, in the correct compass direction and for the correct distance, and then began a systematic search, in loops of increasing size centered on the fictive nest position (Wehner and Srinivasan 1981).

![Model: path integration in the desert ant (wikibehav simulation). A: an outward search path (blue, older parts paler) from the nest to food (star), and the straight home run (orange) along the integrated home vector. B: an ant displaced at the feeder runs the same home vector from the release point to a fictive nest and then searches in expanding loops. C: errors accumulate: 40 ants that made the same outward trip but registered each step with a small heading error (10° s.d. per step, plus an individual compass bias of 4° s.d. and a distance error of 8 %) end their home runs in a cloud around the nest; near the nest, ants switch to landmarks and odors.](./plots/path-integration.py)

Path integration needs a compass and an odometer.

- *The compass* is a sky compass: the ants use the pattern of polarized light in the sky (21.6.3), detected by specialized ommatidia in the dorsal rim of the compound eye, together with the position of the sun and the spectral gradient of the sky. Covering the dorsal rim areas impairs orientation.
- *The odometer* counts steps. Wittlinger, Wehner and Wolf (2006) let ants walk from the nest to a feeder 10 m away and then lengthened the legs of some ants with pig bristles ("stilts") and shortened those of others by partially cutting them ("stumps"); in the test channel, ants on stilts overshot the nest and ants on stumps undershot it, by amounts matching the change in stride length. The ants were counting steps, a pedometer. Ventral optic flow contributes as well, and ants integrate the ground distance of paths over hills, measuring the horizontal projection.

Path integration accumulates errors, so the home vector is only approximately correct after long, tortuous trips; ants compensate by using landmarks and, close to the nest, by systematic search. The neural circuit of path integration in insects, in the central complex of the brain, has been modeled as a ring attractor driven by compass and speed inputs ([7.16.44](/7.16.44#sec-44.4.2)).

### 21.6.2. Landmarks and Views

Near the goal, insects use visual landmarks. Niko Tinbergen showed in the 1930s that digger wasps (*Philanthus*) find their nest burrow by landmarks: after the wasp left, he moved a ring of pine cones that had surrounded the nest, and on return the wasp searched at the center of the displaced ring. Cartwright and Collett (1983) trained honeybees to find sugar water at a fixed position relative to landmarks and changed the landmarks in tests: the bees searched where the retinal image of the landmarks best matched the image they had experienced at the feeder, as if they had stored a *snapshot* and moved so as to reduce the difference between the current view and the snapshot. View-based navigation of this kind, in which an animal learns the panoramic views along a route and steers to match them, explains much of the route-following of ants in cluttered environments without requiring a map (Collett, Chittka and Collett 2013). Ants and bees perform *learning flights* and *learning walks* when they first leave the nest, turning back to view it from different directions and storing views for the return.

### 21.6.3. Polarized Light and the Bee's Compass

Karl von Frisch found in the 1940s that honeybees dancing on a horizontal comb in the hive, where the dance usually points toward the food directly, need to see only a patch of blue sky to orient correctly, and that the orientation changed when a polarizing filter over the comb was rotated. Sunlight scattered in the atmosphere is partially polarized, and its e-vectors form a pattern of concentric circles around the sun; the pattern is symmetric about the solar meridian and indicates the sun's position even when the sun itself is hidden. Insects detect it with ommatidia of the dorsal rim area, whose photoreceptors are sensitive to ultraviolet light and arranged with orthogonal microvilli, making them polarization analyzers. Because the pattern moves with the sun, the polarization compass is time-compensated like the sun compass. The use of this compass to communicate direction in the waggle dance of the honeybee is described in [7.18.40](/7.18.40).

## 21.7. Do Animals Have Cognitive Maps?

Edward Tolman (1948) proposed that rats learning mazes acquire a **cognitive map**: an internal representation of the spatial relations among places, which allows them to plan new routes, including shortcuts, rather than a chain of responses to stimuli. The discovery of place cells in the rat hippocampus (O'Keefe and Dostrovsky 1971), and later of grid cells, head direction cells and boundary cells, gave the idea a neural basis in mammals ([7.16.46](/7.16.46)). In what sense other animals, particularly insects, have cognitive maps is controversial.

- *For maps*: honeybees tracked with harmonic radar after displacement to unfamiliar release sites within their familiar foraging range flew home by novel shortcuts, and bees whose path integration vector pointed one way and whose landmark memory pointed another sometimes flew directly toward the hive or toward a feeder they had visited, as if they could compute new routes between known places (Menzel and colleagues 2005).
- *Against*: many apparent shortcuts can be explained by simpler mechanisms, namely path integration combined with view matching: if the views around the goal are visible from the release site, or similar enough to familiar views, the animal can steer by them without a map. Simulations show that the radar-tracked flights of bees can be reproduced by such mechanisms (Cheung and colleagues 2014). The behavioral evidence for a map in insects is therefore ambiguous, and the definition of a cognitive map, a metric representation that supports computation of novel routes, sets a high bar that few experiments can test cleanly.

The same debate concerns birds and other vertebrates over large distances: an adult migrant that corrects for displacement shows true navigation, but whether its map is a representation of the spatial layout of the world or a set of rules relating gradient values to directions (a "grid map" of a few cues) is unknown. The cognitive abilities involved in spatial memory, the specialization of the hippocampus in food-storing birds and the evidence for maps in mammals are discussed in [7.18.42](/7.18.42).

## Summary

- Navigation (Definition 21.1.1) requires information about the direction of a goal; orientation in the strict sense keeps a direction. Griffin's levels: piloting, compass orientation and true navigation (Definition 21.1.2), which requires a map; Kramer's map-and-compass model separates the two steps. Homing (Definition 21.1.3) is the return to a specific place.
- Methods: displacement, cue-conflict and clock-shift experiments, and orientation cages such as the Emlen funnel (Definition 21.1.4), analyzed with **circular data** statistics (mean vector, Rayleigh and V tests).
- The sun compass (Definition 21.2.1) is time-compensated by the circadian clock: mirrors shift Kramer's starlings, and clock shifts of 6 h deflect pigeons by about 90°; it is learned in young pigeons. The star compass (Definition 21.3.1) of night migrants uses the center of rotation of the sky, is learned before the first migration (buntings raised under a sky rotating about Betelgeuse) and is not time-compensated. The magnetic **inclination compass** of birds reads the axis and inclination of field lines, depends on light and is disrupted by radio-frequency fields (radical pairs in cryptochrome). Compasses calibrate one another, often with sunset polarized light.
- Juvenile migrants use an inherited clock-and-compass program (Perdeck's juvenile starlings flew parallel to their route after displacement; young cuckoos reach their population's wintering area without guidance); adults use true navigation. Map cues: atmospheric odors (anosmic pigeons and shearwaters fail from unfamiliar sites) and magnetic intensity and inclination (Definition 21.4.1); landmarks and learned routes over familiar ground.
- Salmon home by olfactory imprinting (Definition 21.5.1) on the odor of their natal stream during the smolt stage, supported by discrimination, nose plugging, artificial imprinting with morpholine and β-phenylethyl alcohol and the thyroid hormone surge; in the ocean they use a magnetic map and imprint on the field of the river mouth. Sea turtle hatchlings orient to the light horizon, the waves and the magnetic field; juveniles use magnetic maps, and nesting sites track the drift of the field.
- Desert ants navigate by **path integration**, with a sky compass and a step-counting odometer (stilts and stumps), and use landmarks and views near the goal; bees and ants store snapshots and use polarized light as a compass. Whether insects have **cognitive maps** remains debated: novel shortcuts can often be explained by path integration and view matching.

## Questions

1. A young pigeon raised in a loft is released 50 km away on an overcast day and homes successfully. Which compass might it have used, and how would you test your hypothesis?

   **Answer** Under overcast skies the sun compass is unavailable, so the pigeon probably used its magnetic compass (and, over familiar ground, landmarks). Tests: release pigeons under overcast skies with small magnets attached to the head or back (magnetic disturbance) and others with nonmagnetic brass bars of the same mass; if the magnet-carrying birds are disoriented under overcast but not under sunny skies, the magnetic compass is the backup (Keeton 1971 obtained this result). Alternatively, fit birds with small Helmholtz coils that reverse the vertical component of the field: under overcast skies their vanishing bearings should be reversed relative to controls. Release from an unfamiliar site to exclude piloting by landmarks.

2. (Calculation) In a test of 12 robins in Emlen funnels, the mean headings were 10°, 350°, 30°, 20°, 340°, 0°, 50°, 330°, 15°, 5°, 45° and 300°. Compute the mean direction and the mean vector length, and apply the Rayleigh test ($p \approx \mathrm{e}^{-z}$ for a first approximation).

   **Answer** Sum of cosines: $\cos 10^\circ + \cos 350^\circ + \cos 30^\circ + \cos 20^\circ + \cos 340^\circ + \cos 0^\circ + \cos 50^\circ + \cos 330^\circ + \cos 15^\circ + \cos 5^\circ + \cos 45^\circ + \cos 300^\circ = 0.985 + 0.985 + 0.866 + 0.940 + 0.940 + 1 + 0.643 + 0.866 + 0.966 + 0.996 + 0.707 + 0.5 = 10.394$. Sum of sines: $0.174 - 0.174 + 0.5 + 0.342 - 0.342 + 0 + 0.766 - 0.5 + 0.259 + 0.087 + 0.707 - 0.866 = 0.953$. So $\bar C = 0.866$, $\bar S = 0.079$, $r = \sqrt{0.866^2 + 0.079^2} = 0.870$, and the mean direction is $\arctan(0.079/0.866) \approx 5^\circ$. The Rayleigh statistic is $z = nr^2 = 12 \times 0.757 = 9.08$, and $p \approx \mathrm{e}^{-9.08} \approx 10^{-4}$ (the exact small-sample correction gives a similar value). The birds are significantly oriented, with a mean heading of about 5°, close to north, as expected for spring migration in Europe.

3. In Perdeck's experiment, the juvenile starlings flew in their normal direction after displacement. Would you predict the same result for juvenile cuckoos? What experiment would test whether young cuckoos have only a vector program or also a sense of the goal?

   **Answer** Young cuckoos migrate alone, with no experience of their wintering area, so by the same argument as for starlings they should have only an inherited vector program (direction, sequence of directions and duration), and after displacement they should fly a route parallel to the normal one and end up in a correspondingly displaced area. The test is to displace juvenile cuckoos before their first migration, for example from Denmark to a site several hundred kilometers east or south, fit them with satellite transmitters and compare their tracks with those of undisplaced juveniles and of displaced adults. A vector program predicts parallel routes; any compensation toward the population's normal stopover or wintering region in juveniles would indicate an inherited sense of the goal, perhaps based on magnetic map parameters (as in sea turtle hatchlings, which respond to the magnetic signatures of places they have never visited).

4. How do the experiments with morpholine-imprinted salmon exclude the possibility that the fish simply returned to the stream of release because of other cues, such as the general water chemistry of Oak Creek?

   **Answer** The experiment had two essential controls. First, imprinted and unimprinted fish of the same genetic stock were raised identically and released at the same place, so any attraction to Oak Creek itself would affect both groups equally; yet in the years when morpholine was added to the creek, the recaptured fish were mostly from the imprinted group. Second, in a year in which no morpholine was added, few fish of either group were caught in Oak Creek, and the two groups were caught in similar numbers. Together these show that the attraction depended on the combination of early exposure and the presence of morpholine, not on any other property of the creek. Ultrasonic tracking added a third line: imprinted fish stopped in a morpholine-scented area of the lake but swam through it when it was not scented or when another chemical was used, and unimprinted fish were not affected by morpholine.

5. Desert ants that have been carried from the feeder to an unfamiliar place run off their home vector and then search. Why is a systematic search with loops of increasing size a good strategy, and what does it say about the ant's estimate of its own error?

   **Answer** Path integration errors accumulate, so when the home vector has been run off the nest lies somewhere in a region around the fictive position, with a probability density that is highest at the center and decreases outward. A search that starts at the center and expands in loops returns repeatedly to the most probable region while gradually covering less probable areas, which minimizes the expected time to find the nest for such a distribution. The size of the loops scales with the length of the outward journey: ants that have walked farther, and whose estimate is therefore less certain, search more widely (Merkle, Knaden and Wehner 2006). The search pattern therefore reflects an implicit estimate of the uncertainty of the path integrator, an example of near-optimal behavior under uncertainty.
