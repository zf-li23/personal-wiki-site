The three previous chapters treated signals in general: what they are for ([7.18.37](/7.18.37)), how they are produced and transmitted ([7.18.38](/7.18.38)) and why they are reliable ([7.18.39](/7.18.39)). This chapter surveys signals by the social problems they solve, following the four contexts distinguished by Bradbury and Vehrencamp. In *conflict*, two individuals compete for a resource that only one can have, and both gain if the contest is settled without a fight. In *courtship*, the sexes must find, assess and synchronize with each other, while their interests partly diverge. In *social integration*, individuals that cooperate, care for young or live together need to recognize each other and to coordinate their actions. And *environmental signals* inform others about predators and food, external conditions that the sender has detected. Each context sets a different degree of conflict between sender and receiver, and therefore a different solution to the problem of reliability.

The chapter draws on topics treated elsewhere in the course: contests and territories ([7.18.25](/7.18.25), [7.18.26](/7.18.26)), sexual selection ([7.18.28](/7.18.28), [7.18.29](/7.18.29)), family conflict and begging ([7.18.32](/7.18.32)), kin recognition ([7.18.33](/7.18.33)), groups and vigilance ([7.18.27](/7.18.27)), antipredator defenses ([7.18.24](/7.18.24)) and social insects ([7.18.36](/7.18.36)). Here the emphasis is on the signals themselves. It ends with two broader themes: communication as a network in which every exchange has an audience, and the question of what animal signals mean, which leads to social cognition ([7.18.45](/7.18.45)) and language ([7.18.47](/7.18.47)).

## 40.1. Signals in Conflict

### 40.1.1. Signals of Fighting Ability

Contests are the context of maximal conflict: both contestants want the other to retreat. Yet both gain if the likely loser retreats without a fight, so signals that reveal fighting ability, the **resource-holding potential** of each contestant ([7.18.26](/7.18.26#sec-26.4)), are favored when they are reliable. Three kinds of signal carry such information.

- *Indices of size*: lateral displays in which fishes spread their fins and present the flank, the parallel walks of red deer, the deep croaks of toads and the formants of deer roars ([7.18.39](/7.18.39#sec-39.3)). Amplifiers such as erected crests, spread gill covers and raised hair make the body look larger and differences in size easier to judge.
- *Signals of stamina*: repeated, energetically demanding displays such as the roaring rate of red deer, the calling rate of frogs and the tail beating of cichlids, whose rate can be sustained only by individuals in good condition.
- *Weapon displays*: the claw waving of fiddler crabs, the antler displays of deer, the meral spread of stomatopods. Weapons displayed are not always weapons used: male fiddler crabs (*Uca annulipes*) that lose their large claw regrow a claw of similar length but lighter and weaker, which is used to threaten as effectively as an original claw although it would lose a fight (Backwell and colleagues 2000), a bluff maintained, like that of molting stomatopods, by the rarity of the bluffers.

### 40.1.2. Signals of Aggressive Motivation

Contestants differ not only in fighting ability but also in how much they value the resource and how willing they are to escalate. Signals of motivation are subject to the strongest temptation to exaggerate, and their reliability depends on the risk they create for the sender.

Songbirds give a well-studied example. A male song sparrow (*Melospiza melodia*) has a repertoire of about eight to ten song types, many shared with his neighbors. When a neighbor sings, a male can reply with the same type (*type matching*), with a different type that both share (*repertoire matching*), or with an unshared type. Playback experiments showed that type matching is a more aggressive reply than repertoire matching, which is more aggressive than an unshared type, and that males use these options as a graded series of escalating threats that allow the neighbor to escalate or back down (Beecher and colleagues 2000). Matching requires that the singer share the type with the neighbor, an **index signal** of being an established neighbor ([7.18.39](/7.18.39#sec-39.3)). Black-capped chickadees (*Poecile atricapillus*) similarly match the pitch of a rival's song (frequency matching).

The best predictor of attack is often not a loud signal but a quiet one. In several songbirds, males about to attack an intruder sing *soft songs* at low amplitude: in song sparrows, soft song was the only display that predicted whether a male would attack a taxidermic mount (Searcy, Anderson and Nowicki 2006). Low amplitude restricts the signal to the opponent and allows it to be given only at close range, where the sender is exposed to attack, a *proximity* guarantee of reliability ([7.18.39](/7.18.39#sec-39.2)).

### 40.1.3. Badges of Status

In many species that live in groups, individuals carry visible patches of color whose size or shape varies among individuals and is correlated with dominance.

**Definition 40.1.1 (Status Signal)** A **status signal**, or badge of status, is a morphological trait, usually a patch of color, that varies among individuals of the same sex and age class, is correlated with their dominance or fighting ability, and is used by others to decide whether to challenge them.

Sievert Rohwer (1975) introduced the idea for Harris's sparrows (*Zonotrichia querula*), whose wintering flocks contain birds with black bibs of very different extent; dark birds were dominant. Badges are cheap to produce, so their honesty requires social costs: when Rohwer dyed the bibs of subordinates black, they were attacked by dominants and did not rise in rank unless they were also injected with testosterone, which made them behave aggressively, consistent with the idea that a badge must be backed by behavior. In paper wasps, subordinates whose faces were painted to look dominant were attacked more ([7.18.39](/7.18.39#sec-39.5)).

The best-known badge, the black bib of male house sparrows (*Passer domesticus*), illustrates how a textbook example can fail. Early studies found that males with larger bibs were dominant (Møller 1987), and the result was repeated in many textbooks. A meta-analysis of all available studies, including many unpublished data sets, found that the overall correlation between bib size and dominance was weak and not significantly different from zero, with evidence of publication bias toward positive results (Sánchez-Tójar and colleagues 2018). The house sparrow is now a cautionary case for the study of signals: a correlation reported in a few populations, with small samples, does not establish a signal function.

### 40.1.4. Ownership, Victory and De-escalation

Territorial owners advertise their presence and their claim ([7.18.26](/7.18.26#sec-26.2.2)). Ownership signals typically carry individual identity, which allows neighbors to recognize each other and to reduce aggression between familiar neighbors (the **dear enemy effect**, [7.18.26](/7.18.26#sec-26.5)), and boundaries are negotiated by countersinging or, in mammals, by the overmarking of each other's scent marks.

Contests end with signals. The winner of a contest may perform a *victory display*, as when fighting crickets sing and jerk their bodies after a rival retreats; such displays may intimidate the loser or advertise the victory to bystanders (Section 40.5). The loser gives signals of de-escalation: submission, retreat, appeasement. Darwin (1872) pointed out that submissive displays are often the opposite in form of threat displays, his *principle of antithesis*: a threatening dog stands tall with raised hackles, ears forward and tail up, a submissive dog crouches with ears flat and tail down. Antithesis maximizes the difference between signals with opposite meanings, which makes them easy to discriminate.

## 40.2. Courtship Signals

### 40.2.1. Mate Attraction and Assessment

Mate attraction signals answer several questions for the receiver: is the sender of my species, of the opposite sex, receptive, and of high quality? These questions favor different designs. Species identity favors stereotyped components under stabilizing selection, so that the signal differs clearly from those of related species; quality favors variable components under directional selection, such as call rate, call duration and song repertoire size. Many courtship signals combine both: in the gray treefrog (*Hyla versicolor*), the pulse rate of the call, which differs from that of the sibling species *H. chrysoscelis*, is under stabilizing preference by females, whereas the call duration, which is costly, is under directional preference (Gerhardt 1991). The evolution of such signals by sexual selection, and the hypotheses of **good genes**, **Fisherian runaway** and **sensory bias**, are treated in [7.10.13](/7.10.13) and [7.18.29](/7.18.29).

### 40.2.2. Courtship Sequences and Synchronization

Courtship is often a sequence of signals exchanged between the sexes, each step a response to the partner's last action. In the three-spined stickleback, the male's zigzag dance is followed by the female's head-up posture, the male leads her to the nest, she enters, he quivers against her tail, and she spawns ([7.18.3](/7.18.3)). Such chains allow each partner to assess the other repeatedly and to synchronize the release of gametes, which is crucial in species with external fertilization.

Courtship signals also synchronize physiology. In ring doves (*Streptopelia risoria*), Daniel Lehrman showed that the courtship of the male stimulates the growth of the female's ovary and that the sequence of courtship, nest building and incubation is a chain of hormonal and behavioral interactions between the partners. Mei-Fang Cheng (1992) later found that a female's ovarian development is stimulated most by her own nest coos, given in response to the male's courtship: the male's signal acts partly through the female's own vocal response, a form of *self-communication*.

### 40.2.3. Sex-Role Reversal

When males invest more in offspring than females, as in pipefishes and seahorses, where males brood the eggs, and in jacanas and phalaropes, where males incubate and rear the young, females compete for mates, males are choosy, and the signals are reversed: females are larger or more brightly colored and court the males. In the pipefish *Syngnathus typhle*, females develop a striped ornament during courtship, and males prefer more ornamented females ([7.18.28](/7.18.28)). Role reversal confirms that signal elaboration follows the intensity of competition for mates rather than sex as such.

## 40.3. Social Integration

### 40.3.1. Recognition

Cooperation, care and group life require that individuals direct their help to appropriate partners: offspring rather than unrelated young, mates rather than strangers, group members rather than intruders. Every recognition system has three components (Reeve 1989; Bradbury and Vehrencamp 2011): the *production* of a label or signal by the individual to be recognized; its *perception* and comparison with a *template* of the appropriate class, which the recognizer has acquired by learning (familiarity, the association with a place) or by phenotype matching against itself or its relatives ([7.18.33](/7.18.33)); and an *action* decided by a threshold rule.

The decision is a problem of **signal detection theory**. The labels of desirable and undesirable individuals overlap, so a recognizer that accepts all individuals within a certain distance of its template accepts some undesirable individuals (acceptance errors) and rejects some desirable ones (rejection errors). Hudson Kern Reeve's (1989) *optimal acceptance threshold* model predicts that the threshold should be more permissive when undesirable individuals are rare or when rejecting a desirable individual is costly, and stricter when intruders are common or costly. Guard honey bees at the nest entrance accept more nonnestmates when nectar is abundant and robbing is rare, and become stricter when robbing increases (Downs and Ratnieks 2000), as the model predicts. Recognition is also an arms race with mimics such as brood parasites, which evolve to match the hosts' templates ([7.18.32](/7.18.32)).

### 40.3.2. Signatures of Individuals

When the cost of helping the wrong individual is high and the young of many parents mingle, selection favors signals that make individuals identifiable and receivers that discriminate finely. The comparison of related species living in different social situations has shown this clearly. Bank swallows (*Riparia riparia*) nest in dense colonies where fledglings from many burrows mix, whereas northern rough-winged swallows (*Stelgidopteryx serripennis*) nest solitarily. The calls of bank swallow chicks are more variable among individuals, carrying more information about identity, and bank swallow parents discriminate the calls of their own chicks from others, whereas rough-winged swallow parents do not (Beecher 1982).

King penguins (*Aptenodytes patagonicus*) breed without nests in colonies of tens of thousands; parents returning from the sea find their chick in a crèche by its call, and both parents and chicks recognize each other's calls in the din of the colony. The calls use the two sides of the syrinx to produce two voices whose beat pattern is individually distinctive, and playback experiments showed that chicks recognize their parents even when the call is degraded by noise (Aubin and Jouventin 1998; Jouventin, Aubin and Lengagne 1999). Mexican free-tailed bats (*Tadarida brasiliensis*) raise their pups in caves where millions of pups cluster at densities of thousands per square meter; a mother returning from foraging nevertheless finds and nurses her own pup most of the time, using its location, its call and its smell (McCracken 1984).

**Definition 40.3.1 (Signature Whistle)** A **signature whistle** is an individually distinctive whistle, developed by each bottlenose dolphin in its first year or two of life, that it uses to broadcast its identity and maintain contact with others (Caldwell and Caldwell 1965).

Dolphins produce their signature whistles mainly when separated from their group or reuniting with it, and they occasionally copy the signature whistles of others. Wild bottlenose dolphins responded to playback of copies of their own signature whistle by calling back, but not to the whistles of other individuals, which suggests that a copy can be used to address a particular individual, somewhat like a name (King and Janik 2013). The whistles of dolphins are learned (**vocal learning**, [7.18.17](/7.18.17#sec-17.4)), which allows them to be distinctive.

### 40.3.3. Pairs and Duets

In species with long-term pair bonds, partners often perform joint displays.

**Definition 40.3.2 (Duet)** A **duet** is a coordinated vocal display of two individuals, usually a mated pair, in which the partners alternate their notes in a precisely timed sequence (antiphonal duet) or sing simultaneously.

Duets are common in tropical birds with year-round territories and long pair bonds, and occur also in gibbons, titi monkeys, some frogs and insects. Their precision can be remarkable: in plain-tailed wrens (*Pheugopedius euophrys*) of the Andes, groups of males and females alternate syllables so rapidly and regularly that the duet sounds as if sung by one bird, with each sex singing its own syllables (Mann, Dingess and Slater 2006). Neural recordings showed that the song system of each partner responds more strongly to the duet than to its own syllables alone, so that the duet is coordinated by mutual auditory feedback between two brains (Fortune and colleagues 2011).

Duets have been proposed to serve joint territory defense, guarding of the mate against rivals, maintenance of contact in dense vegetation and advertisement of the pair's commitment (Hall 2004). The hypotheses are not exclusive and have different predictions. If duets defend territories, pairs should duet more in response to playback of a rival pair, which they do in many species; if duets guard mates, a partner should answer the other's song more readily when same-sex rivals are present or when the partner is fertile, as in some wrens and in magpie-larks (*Grallina cyanoleuca*), where pairs that coordinate their duets more precisely are more threatening to rivals (Hall and Magrath 2007).

### 40.3.4. Signals between Parents and Offspring

Offspring signal their needs by begging, and parents adjust their provisioning; whether begging is an honest signal of need or a scramble among siblings is discussed in [7.18.32](/7.18.32). Parents also signal to offspring. Directive signals keep mobile young together, warn them of predators and lead them to food. A domestic hen calls her chicks to a food item with a rapid series of food calls while picking up and dropping the food (tidbitting), and she gives food calls more readily when the chicks peck at unpalatable food, which redirects their attention to palatable items (Nicol and Pope 1996), one of the few cases in which a parental signal might function as teaching ([7.18.46](/7.18.46)).

### 40.3.5. Appeasement, Reconciliation and Group Labels

Members of stable groups have repeated conflicts but also depend on each other. Subordinates give submissive signals that reduce the aggression of dominants, and dominants give reassurance signals. Frans de Waal and Angeline van Roosmalen (1979) found that chimpanzees that had fought were more likely to make friendly contact with each other shortly afterward than at other times, often by kissing, embracing or grooming; such *reconciliation* has since been found in many primates and other social mammals, and it is more common between partners whose relationship is valuable. Allogrooming reduces tension and maintains relationships, and the ritualized greeting ceremonies of spotted hyenas, which involve the presentation of the erect pseudopenis or penis and are initiated mostly by subordinates, reaffirm relationships after separation in a fission–fusion society ([7.18.39](/7.18.39#sec-39.1.2)).

Large groups cannot be integrated by individual recognition. Social insect colonies use shared chemical labels, blends of cuticular hydrocarbons that all members acquire by contact and exchange, and attack individuals whose labels differ from the colony template ([7.18.36](/7.18.36)). Birds that live in flocks often share group-specific contact calls: budgerigars (*Melopsittacus undulatus*) placed in new groups converged on a shared call within a few weeks (Farabaugh, Linzenbold and Dooling 1994).

## 40.4. Environmental Signals

### 40.4.1. Signals to Predators

Prey signal to predators at several stages of an attack ([7.18.24](/7.18.24)): **aposematism** declares unprofitability before an attack, detection signals tell a stalking predator that it has been seen, and condition signals tell a pursuing predator that the prey is hard to catch.

**Definition 40.4.1 (Pursuit-Deterrent Signal)** A **pursuit-deterrent signal** is a signal given by prey to a predator that indicates that the predator has been detected or that the prey is able to escape, and so discourages the predator from attacking or pursuing (Woodland, Jaafar and Knight 1980).

The stotting of gazelles, the push-up displays of lizards and the tail flagging of deer are examples ([7.18.24](/7.18.24#sec-24.6.3)). Pursuit-deterrent signals can be reliable because both parties benefit: the predator saves a futile chase and the prey saves an escape. Their honesty is guaranteed when they are indices of escape ability (a lizard cannot perform many push-ups without the endurance to run) or when they are costly to prey in poor condition. A striking case is the song of skylarks (*Alauda arvensis*) chased by merlins (*Falco columbarius*): larks that sang during the chase, and those that sang well, were less likely to be caught, and merlins were more likely to give up chasing a singing lark, which suggests that song during escape signals the lark's condition (Cresswell 1994).

### 40.4.2. Alarm Calls

**Definition 40.4.2 (Alarm Call)** An **alarm call** is a vocalization given by an animal that has detected a predator and that causes others to take evasive or defensive action.

Alarm calls pose an evolutionary puzzle, because a caller that draws attention to itself appears to benefit others at its own expense. Several kinds of benefit to the caller have been proposed and supported in different species.

- **Kin selection**. Paul Sherman (1977) studied Belding's ground squirrels (*Urocitellus beldingi*) in the Sierra Nevada. Females are philopatric and live near their mothers, sisters and daughters, whereas males disperse. When a terrestrial predator such as a weasel or coyote appeared, adult females called more often than males, and females with close relatives living nearby called more often than females without; callers were stalked and chased more often than noncallers. The pattern fits kin selection ([7.10.16](/7.10.16)): calling is favored when the benefit to relatives, weighted by relatedness, exceeds the cost to the caller. Belding's ground squirrels have a second call, a whistle given to hawks, which is given by squirrels that are themselves fleeing and probably benefits the caller by causing a general scramble for cover in which the hawk is less likely to catch it (Sherman 1985).
- *Benefits to mates and offspring*. Many animals call more when their mates or young are near.
- *Reciprocity and sentinels*. In groups with sentinels ([7.18.27](/7.18.27#sec-27.2.4)), alarm calls are given by individuals that are already safe, and other group members may return the service.
- *Manipulation and selfish benefits*. A caller may induce others to move in a way that reduces its own risk, for example by causing a flock to take flight together (the **dilution effect**), or may warn the predator that it has been detected, which makes the call partly a pursuit-deterrent signal.

Alarm calling also depends on who is listening. Roosters gave more aerial alarm calls to a model hawk flying overhead when a hen was present in the next cage than when they were alone, and fewer when the audience was a bird of another species, a bobwhite quail (Karakashian, Gyger and Marler 1988).

**Definition 40.4.3 (Audience Effect)** An **audience effect** is a change in the probability or form of a signal that depends on the presence or kind of potential receivers.

### 40.4.3. Referential Signals

Do alarm calls tell listeners only that the caller is frightened, or do they say something about the predator? Thomas Struhsaker (1967) noticed that vervet monkeys (*Chlorocebus pygerythrus*) in Kenya give acoustically distinct alarm calls to leopards, martial eagles and pythons, and that others respond differently to each: to leopard alarms by running into trees, to eagle alarms by looking up and running into dense bushes, to snake alarms by standing on their hind legs and looking down at the ground. Robert Seyfarth, Dorothy Cheney and Peter Marler (1980) played back recorded alarm calls in the absence of predators and found that the monkeys responded with the escape appropriate to the predator class of the call: the call alone was enough.

**Definition 40.4.4 (Referential Signal)** A **referential signal**, more precisely a *functionally referential* signal, is a signal that is produced specifically in response to a particular class of external stimuli, such as a type of predator or food, and that elicits in receivers, even in the absence of the stimulus and of other contextual cues, a response appropriate to that class (Macedonia and Evans 1993).

![Functionally referential alarm calls of vervet monkeys (schematic, after Seyfarth, Cheney and Marler 1980). Rows: the call played back from a hidden loudspeaker; columns: the escape responses of the listeners; shading: how often each response follows each call (illustrative, high on the diagonal). Leopard alarms send monkeys into trees, where leopards cannot follow them easily; eagle alarms make them look up and run into bushes, out of reach of a stooping eagle; snake alarms make them stand up and scan the ground. Because the responses differ and are appropriate to the predator, the calls specify the predator class rather than only the caller's level of fear](./plots/vervet-alarms.py)

Referential alarm calls have since been described in many animals. Meerkats (*Suricata suricatta*) give different calls to aerial predators, terrestrial predators and snakes, and within each class the calls vary with urgency, combining predator type and urgency in one signal (Manser 2001). Chickens give different calls to aerial and ground predators, and hens respond appropriately to playbacks (Evans, Evans and Marler 1993). Japanese tits (*Parus minor*) give a distinctive "jar" call when they see snakes; tits hearing it search the ground and tree trunks and approach a stick moved like a snake more often than after other calls, which suggests that the call evokes a search image of a snake in the listener (Suzuki 2018). Japanese tits also combine calls: a sequence meaning roughly "scan for danger" followed by one meaning "approach" elicits both behaviors in that order, whereas the reversed order does not, a simple compositional rule (Suzuki, Wheatcroft and Griesser 2016).

Listeners also learn the calls of other species. Diana monkeys (*Cercopithecus diana*) in the Taï Forest respond to the eagle and leopard alarms of crowned eagles and of other monkeys, and to the alarm calls of guinea fowl, which call when they see leopards or humans (Zuberbühler 2000). Such responses are learned, while the production of a species' own alarm calls appears to be largely innate and refined by experience: young vervets give eagle alarms to many birds, including harmless ones, and gradually restrict them to martial eagles and a few other raptors.

### 40.4.4. Mobbing and Victim Signals

Mobbing calls, in contrast to most alarm calls, attract others to the predator ([7.18.27](/7.18.27#sec-27.2.5)). Black-capped chickadees encode the danger posed by a predator in their "chick-a-dee" mobbing call: the number of "dee" notes at the end of the call is larger for small, agile raptors such as pygmy owls, which are dangerous to chickadees, than for large raptors that rarely catch them, and playbacks with more dee notes elicited stronger mobbing (Templeton, Greene and Davis 2005).

Prey that have been caught often give loud distress calls, which may startle the predator or attract other predators that interfere with the attack. Injured fish release from their skin an alarm substance (*Schreckstoff*, discovered by Karl von Frisch) to which other fish, including other species, respond by hiding and shoaling. Whether the alarm substance is a signal is doubtful: it is released only when the skin is damaged, so the sender gains little, and the epidermal cells that contain it may function primarily in immunity against parasites and pathogens, so that the response of others is eavesdropping on a cue (Chivers and colleagues 2007). Social insects, in contrast, have true alarm pheromones that recruit nestmates to defend the colony ([7.18.38](/7.18.38#sec-38.4.1)).

### 40.4.5. Food Signals

Food calls impose immediate costs on the sender, which shares a resource it has found, and their benefits are similar to those of alarm calls: kin selection, mating benefits, reciprocity, or help in exploiting a resource. Food signals are much rarer than alarm signals, and senders are more selective about when and to whom they give them. Roosters give food calls with tidbitting more often when a hen is present than when alone or with another male (Marler, Dufty and Pickert 1986; Evans and Marler 1994); they sometimes call without food, and hens respond less to roosters that do so often. Common ravens (*Corvus corax*) in the forests of Maine recruit other ravens to carcasses by loud "yells": the callers are young, wandering birds that cannot feed at a carcass defended by a territorial pair, and by recruiting a crowd they overwhelm the owners' defense (Heinrich 1988).

### 40.4.6. The Information in the Honey Bee Dance

The **waggle dance** of honey bees ([7.17.38](/7.17.38)) is the most elaborate food signal known, and its information content can be analyzed quantitatively. A forager that has found a rich source performs, on the vertical comb, a series of waggle runs separated by return loops. The angle of the waggle run relative to the vertical encodes the direction of the food relative to the sun's azimuth ([7.18.21](/7.18.21#sec-21.6.3)), and the duration of the waggle run increases roughly linearly with the distance to the food, at about a second of waggling per kilometer, with differences among races. Sources closer than a few tens of meters are indicated by "round dances" without a clear direction. Distance is not measured directly but by the optic flow experienced during the flight, so that bees flying through a narrow tunnel lined with patterns dance as if the food were much farther away than it is (Srinivasan and colleagues 2000).

The code is imprecise. The direction of successive waggle runs scatters around the mean, more for near than for far sources: for close sources, the runs diverge by tens of degrees. Towne and Gould (1988) proposed that this scatter is "tuned" to spread recruits over the area of a flower patch, which covers a larger angle when it is near; later analyses suggest that the scatter is at least partly a mechanical constraint of the dance (Tanner and Visscher 2010). John Haldane and Helen Spurway (1954) were the first to calculate the information in the dance in bits ([7.18.37](/7.18.37#sec-37.4.2)).

Do recruits use the information? The claim was disputed for decades by Adrian Wenner and colleagues, who argued that recruits find food by the odor carried by the dancer. Two lines of evidence settled the question. A robotic bee whose waggle runs could be programmed recruited bees to the direction and distance indicated by the robot (Michelsen and colleagues 1992), and tracking of individual recruits by harmonic radar showed that they flew directly in the direction and approximately to the distance indicated by the dance they had followed, and then searched around the end point (Riley and colleagues 2005). The value of the information depends on the environment: disorienting the dances of colonies by diffusing the light on the comb reduced the colonies' foraging success in tropical habitats with patchy, rich resources but not in temperate habitats where resources are more evenly distributed (Dornhaus and Chittka 2004), an example of the dependence of the value of information on the decision environment ([7.18.37](/7.18.37#sec-37.3.5)).

## 40.5. Communication Networks

Most signals reach more than one receiver. A song broadcast in a territory is heard by the neighbor to whom it is directed, by other neighbors, by females and by predators; a contest between two males is watched by others. Peter McGregor (2005) emphasized that communication typically takes place in a *network* of several signalers and receivers within signaling range of each other, which changes the costs and benefits of signaling.

Receivers in a network can eavesdrop on interactions between others and gain information that they could not obtain in a one-to-one exchange: relative information about two individuals at once, without the cost of interacting. Female black-capped chickadees whose mates had "lost" a song contest with a simulated intruder (a playback that overlapped and matched the male's songs, outsinging him) were more likely to seek copulations with other males than females whose mates had "won" (Mennill, Ratcliffe and Boag 2002). Male Siamese fighting fish (*Betta splendens*) that had watched two others fight approached the winner more cautiously than the loser (Oliveira, McGregor and Latruffe 1998).

Signalers in turn respond to their audiences. First-order audience effects, such as the alarm calls of roosters, depend on the presence of the intended receiver; second-order effects depend on the presence of eavesdroppers: male fighting fish displayed differently to a rival when a female was watching (Doutrelant, McGregor and Oliveira 2001). The structure of social relationships through which such information spreads can be described by **social network analysis** ([7.18.27](/7.18.27#sec-27.8)).

## 40.6. What Do Animal Signals Mean?

When a vervet gives a leopard alarm, does it mean "leopard!", "climb a tree!", or "I am terrified"? Wynne and Udell emphasize that the question has two parts, which should be kept apart. From the receiver's perspective, a signal carries information if it changes the receiver's estimate of the world, and functionally referential signals show that receivers can extract specific information about external events. From the sender's perspective, the question is whether the sender intends to inform, that is, whether it takes into account what the receiver knows. **Morgan's canon** ([7.18.1](/7.18.1)) cautions against assuming intention where simpler mechanisms suffice.

The evidence on intention is mixed. Vervet mothers do not alarm call more when their infants are unaware of a danger than when they have already seen it, which suggests that vervets do not take others' knowledge into account (Cheney and Seyfarth 1990). Wild chimpanzees, in contrast, were more likely to give alarm calls to a model snake when group members arriving had not seen it than when they had, and continued calling until the others were safe (Crockford and colleagues 2012), which suggests some sensitivity to what others know ([7.18.45](/7.18.45)). Seyfarth and Cheney (2003) concluded that in many primates, listeners are more sophisticated than signalers: listeners extract rich information about the world and the social relations of callers, while callers give calls that are relatively fixed and reflect their own state and perception. Human language, which depends on both speakers and listeners modeling each other's minds, may have evolved from this asymmetry ([7.18.47](/7.18.47)).

## Summary

- Conflict signals:

  | Information | Signals | Reliability |
  |---|---|---|
  | Fighting ability | Size indices and amplifiers; stamina displays; weapons (regrown fiddler crab claws are bluffs) | Index, handicap |
  | Aggressive motivation | Song type matching (a graded escalation), frequency matching, soft song | Proximity risk, informational index |
  | Dominance | **Status signals** (Definition 40.1.1): Harris's sparrows, paper wasps; house sparrow bibs not supported by meta-analysis | Social costs, behavior backing |
  | Ownership, outcome | Territorial song and marks, victory displays, submission (principle of antithesis) | Mixed |

- Courtship signals combine stereotyped species-identity components and variable quality components (gray treefrogs), form sequences that synchronize partners and physiology (ring doves, the female's own coos), and are reversed when males invest more (pipefishes).
- Recognition is a threshold decision on labels compared with templates (optimal acceptance thresholds, guard bees). Identity signals evolve where young mingle: bank versus rough-winged swallows, king penguins, free-tailed bats, dolphin **signature whistles** (Definition 40.3.1).
- **Duets** (Definition 40.3.2), precisely coordinated in plain-tailed wrens, serve joint defense, mate guarding and contact. Parents use directive signals; reconciliation, allogrooming and greetings integrate groups; colonies share chemical labels and flocks shared calls.
- Environmental signals: **pursuit-deterrent signals** (Definition 40.4.1) are honest when they indicate escape ability (stotting, push-ups, skylark song); **alarm calls** (Definition 40.4.2) benefit callers by kin selection (Belding's ground squirrel females), mates, reciprocity or manipulation, and show **audience effects** (Definition 40.4.3); **referential signals** (Definition 40.4.4) specify predator classes (vervets, meerkats, chickens, Japanese tits, with simple syntax); listeners learn heterospecific alarms.
- Mobbing calls encode danger (chickadee dee notes); *Schreckstoff* is probably a cue; food calls are rarer and more selective (roosters, ravens).
- The **waggle dance** encodes direction and distance (optic flow odometry); its scatter is larger for near sources; recruits use it (robotic bee, harmonic radar); its value is greatest in patchy environments.
- Communication networks allow eavesdropping on interactions (chickadee females, fighting fish) and produce audience effects.
- Receivers extract referential information; evidence that senders intend to inform is limited (vervets) but present in chimpanzees.

## Questions

1. Why is soft song a reliable predictor of attack in song sparrows, whereas loud song is not?

   **Answer** Loud song can be given from a distance at little risk and is used in many contexts (advertisement, mate attraction), so it is weakly correlated with the intention to attack. Soft song is audible only at close range, so a male can give it effectively only near the opponent, where he is exposed to attack; giving it without the willingness to fight would be risky. The reliability is thus guaranteed by proximity, and soft song is produced mostly when a male is about to escalate.
2. A meta-analysis found no overall relation between bib size and dominance in house sparrows. Give three reasons why early studies may have reported a relation, and describe how a signal function of the bib could be tested experimentally.

   **Answer** (1) Small samples produce large chance correlations, and studies that found positive correlations were more likely to be published (publication bias); (2) bib size may correlate with dominance in some populations or seasons for incidental reasons (age, condition) without being used as a signal; (3) measurement methods differed and dominance was scored in different contexts. An experimental test manipulates the bib (enlarging it with dye or reducing it) in birds of known rank, in a design blind to treatment, and records whether opponents change their behavior (fewer challenges to enlarged birds) and whether manipulated birds receive extra aggression when their behavior does not match (social costs); replication across populations with preregistered analyses avoids the earlier problems.
3. In Belding's ground squirrels, a female with three sisters nearby ($r = 0.5$) can give an alarm call that raises her own risk of death by 0.05 and reduces each sister's risk by 0.04. Is calling favored? What if the listeners are three unrelated males?

   **Answer** By Hamilton's rule, calling is favored if $rNb > c$: $0.5 \times 3 \times 0.04 = 0.06 > 0.05$, so calling is favored. With unrelated listeners, $r = 0$ and the inclusive fitness benefit is zero, so calling is not favored by kin selection (it could still be favored by other benefits, such as making the predator abandon the hunt). This matches Sherman's finding that females with kin nearby called more than females without kin and more than dispersing males.
4. What observations and experiments are needed to show that a call is functionally referential rather than an indicator of the caller's level of fear?

   **Answer** (1) Production specificity: the call is given to a particular class of stimuli (for example, aerial predators) and not to others, independently of the caller's arousal; urgency should be varied within each predator class to separate class from urgency (as in meerkats). (2) Perception specificity: playback of the call in the absence of the predator and of other cues elicits a response appropriate to that class (looking up, running into bushes), different from responses to calls for other classes. (3) Responses should not be explained simply by acoustic features that signal urgency (for example, loudness); habituation–dishabituation experiments, in which listeners habituated to one call of a class transfer the habituation to another call with the same referent but not to an acoustically similar call with a different referent, test whether listeners categorize calls by referent.
5. A dancing honey bee indicates a food source 2 km away. If the angular error of a single waggle run has a standard deviation of 6°, estimate the information about direction in one run, and explain why recruits follow several runs.

   **Answer** For a normal distribution of angular error with standard deviation $\sigma$, the entropy is $h = \log_2(\sigma\sqrt{2\pi\mathrm{e}})$ (in degrees), so $h = \log_2(6 \times 4.13) = \log_2 24.8 \approx 4.6$ bits, and the information is $\log_2 360 - 4.6 \approx 8.5 - 4.6 = 3.9$ bits, close to 4 bits. Following $n$ independent runs and averaging reduces the error by $\sqrt n$, which adds about $\frac12\log_2 n$ bits (one extra bit for four runs); recruits follow several runs, and their search after arrival compensates for the remaining error.
6. (Open question) Design an experiment to test whether a songbird's duets function in joint territory defense or in mate guarding.

   **Answer** Playback design with marked pairs on territories: (1) play back duets of a rival pair, solo songs of a rival male and solo songs of a rival female; the joint defense hypothesis predicts that pairs respond with coordinated duets and joint approach mainly to duets (a rival pair), whereas the mate guarding hypothesis predicts that each sex answers its partner's song most when a same-sex rival is heard (the female answers more after a rival female's song). (2) Vary the female's fertility stage: mate guarding predicts more answering by males during the female's fertile period. (3) Temporarily remove one partner and test whether the other's singing and the frequency of intrusions change. (4) Measure the precision of duet coordination and test whether more precise duets deter intruders more effectively.
