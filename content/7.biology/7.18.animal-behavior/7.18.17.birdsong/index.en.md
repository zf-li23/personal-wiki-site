Few behaviors have been studied at as many levels as the song of birds. A male white-crowned sparrow on a Californian hillside sings a whistled phrase of two seconds that differs audibly from the song of males a few kilometers away; he learned it as a nestling from the males around him, practiced it for months the following spring, and produces it with a set of brain nuclei found only in birds that learn their vocalizations. The same song keeps rivals off his territory and attracts females, who judge from it something about his quality and origin. Birdsong therefore allows all four of Tinbergen's questions ([7.18.1](/7.18.1)) to be asked of one behavior, and the answers inform each other: knowing why females attend to song predicts which brain areas encode their preferences, and knowing how song is learned explains why its dialects change over time.

This chapter follows song from the sound to its function. It begins with the description of sounds by spectrograms and with the geographic variation of song (17.1); turns to the development of song, the classic experiments of Marler, Konishi and Baptista that separated the roles of genes, acoustic experience and social interaction (17.2); and then to the mechanisms of production and learning, from the **syrinx** to the song system of the forebrain, where song learning can be described as **reinforcement learning** in a **basal ganglia** circuit (17.3). Section 17.4 asks how often vocal learning evolved and what birdsong shares with human speech, and 17.5 asks why learning song is worth its cost. The neural circuit of song is summarized from a computational point of view in [7.16.36](/7.16.36#sec-36.6) and the basal ganglia and dopamine in [7.16.39](/7.16.39) and [7.16.40](/7.16.40); here they are expanded into a complete account of the behavior. The anatomy of the syrinx is in [7.17.64](/7.17.64#sec-64.6), sensitive periods in general in [7.18.5](/7.18.5), the physics of sound signals in [7.18.38](/7.18.38), neighbor recognition in [7.18.26](/7.18.26), mate choice in [7.18.29](/7.18.29), cultural transmission in [7.18.46](/7.18.46) and the evolution of language in [7.18.47](/7.18.47).

## 17.1. Describing Song

### 17.1.1. Sound, Oscillograms and Spectrograms

A sound is a propagating disturbance of pressure. Its simplest description is the *oscillogram* (waveform), the pressure, or the voltage of a microphone, against time; it shows the amplitude envelope of a sound and its temporal pattern but hides its frequency content. Since the 1950s, when the Sona-Graph analyzer made it possible, bird sounds have been described by spectrograms.

**Definition 17.1.1 (Spectrogram)** A **spectrogram** (sonogram) is a representation of a sound as the distribution of its energy over frequency and time: the signal is divided into short, overlapping time windows, the power spectrum of each window is computed by the Fourier transform, and the spectra are displayed side by side with frequency on the vertical axis, time on the horizontal axis and intensity as darkness or color.

The windowed Fourier transform implies a trade-off that every user of spectrograms meets. A window of duration $\Delta t$ resolves frequencies only to about $\Delta f \approx 1/\Delta t$: a window of $11.6\,\mathrm{ms}$ (256 samples at $22.05\,\mathrm{kHz}$) gives a resolution of about $86\,\mathrm{Hz}$. Long windows show the harmonics of a tone sharply but smear fast frequency modulations; short windows show the modulations but blur the frequency. Songs of birds are often modulated within a few milliseconds, and analysis settings are chosen to match the question.

On a spectrogram a pure tone is a horizontal line, a frequency sweep a slanted line, a harmonic sound a stack of parallel lines at integer multiples of the fundamental frequency, and a noisy sound a band of diffuse darkening. Bird vocalizations are traditionally divided into *calls*, usually short, simple and innate signals given by both sexes in specific contexts (alarm, contact, begging, flight calls), and *songs*, longer and more complex vocalizations, usually given by males in the breeding season, that defend territories and attract mates. The distinction is one of convenience: in many tropical species both sexes sing, and some calls are learned. Songs are described hierarchically. The smallest continuous trace is a *note* (element); one or more notes that are always produced together form a *syllable*; repeated syllables form a *trill*, and syllables and trills form *phrases* and the whole *song*. A male's distinct songs are his *song types*, and the set of song types is his *repertoire*. Repertoire sizes range from one song type in the white-crowned sparrow and the zebra finch to a dozen in the song sparrow and more than a thousand in the brown thrasher.

### 17.1.2. Variation and Dialects

Peter Marler, then a young ecologist studying British lakes, noticed in the 1950s that chaffinches (*Fringilla coelebs*) around each lake sang a slightly different version of the species' song. Geographic variation of this kind turned out to be widespread in birds that learn their songs.

**Definition 17.1.2 (Dialect)** A song **dialect** is a variant of a species' song shared by the birds of a local population and differing from the variants of neighboring populations, often with sharp boundaries between the areas where different variants are sung.

The classic case is the white-crowned sparrow of the San Francisco Bay area. Marler and Tamura (1964) showed that males in Marin, north of the Golden Gate, in Berkeley, east of the Bay, and at Sunset Beach to the south each sing their own version of the song: the introductory whistles are similar, but the terminal trills differ consistently among the three populations, while songs within a population are nearly identical. Dialects can be stable for decades: in some populations of the white-crowned sparrow the same dialect was recorded with only modest changes over 30 years, as human dialects persist over generations.

Three non-exclusive proximate hypotheses could explain dialects:

| Hypothesis | Cause of the differences | Prediction |
|---|---|---|
| Genetic differences | Populations differ in genes that shape song development | Dialect groups are genetically distinct; song develops without exposure to adults |
| Acoustic stimulus | Young birds copy the songs they hear | A young bird reared with recordings of a foreign dialect sings that dialect |
| Social interaction | Young birds copy the adults they interact with | Live tutors are copied in preference to recordings or unseen singers |

The genetic hypothesis fares poorly at the scale of dialects. Six dialect groups of white-crowned sparrows in the northern part of the range show little genetic differentiation (Soha and colleagues 2004; Poesel and colleagues 2017). At a larger scale, however, the migratory Puget Sound subspecies (*Z. l. pugetensis*) and the resident Nuttall's **subspecies** (*Z. l. nuttalli*) form distinct genetic clusters in genome-wide markers, and males respond more strongly to playback of the song of their own subspecies (Lipshutz and colleagues 2017). Heritable differences thus exist between subspecies, but local dialects are learned (17.2).

If dialects are learned, how do they arise? Douglas Nelson proposed that they are products of **cultural evolution**: small copying errors, analogous to mutations, are occasionally learned by other birds and spread, so that the song of a population drifts over time (Nelson and colleagues 2004). Recordings of white-crowned sparrows over 30 years and of savannah sparrows (*Passerculus sandwichensis*) over 31 years documented the addition and loss of particular song elements and the replacement of one song type by another (Williams and colleagues 2013). Song also changes with the environment. In San Francisco, the minimum frequency of white-crowned sparrow song rose between 1969 and 2005 as traffic noise, which is concentrated at low frequencies, increased (Luther and Baptista 2010); urban great tits (*Parus major*) similarly sing higher minimum frequencies than forest birds (Slabbekoorn and Peet 2003). Whether such changes are learned adjustments, individual flexibility or selection on song is discussed with the transmission of signals in [7.18.38](/7.18.38).

## 17.2. The Development of Song

### 17.2.1. Isolation, Tape Tutors and Deafening

Marler and his students tested the hypotheses by hand-rearing white-crowned sparrows taken from nests as eggs or a few days after hatching (Marler 1970).

- Birds reared in acoustic isolation began to sing at the normal age, about 150 days, but never produced more than an *isolate song*: a simplified, twittering vocalization with some species-typical features (whistled tones, a typical duration) but none of the structure of wild song.
- Birds that heard tape recordings of white-crowned sparrow song between about 10 and 50 days of age, and then nothing for months, also began to sing at about 150 days. Their first songs were incomplete, but by about 200 days they sang a full song that copied the recorded version: a bird from Marin that heard Berkeley songs sang the Berkeley dialect.
- Birds that heard recordings only after day 50 did not learn them.

The experiments support the acoustic stimulus hypothesis and show that learning is restricted to a **critical period** early in life (a [sensitive period](/7.18.5#def-5.2.1) in the terms of the previous chapters), well before the bird sings. The memory of the song is stored for months without being used. Masakazu Konishi (1965) then showed that birds must also hear themselves. White-crowned sparrows deafened after hearing tutor song but before they began to sing produced songs even more abnormal than isolate song, and so do young zebra finches deafened before learning. A deafened bird cannot use its memory: the memorized song must be compared with auditory feedback from the bird's own attempts.

**Definition 17.2.1 (Song Learning)** **Song learning** is the acquisition of a song by imitation of the songs of other individuals. It proceeds in two phases: in the **sensory phase** the young bird memorizes songs heard during a sensitive period, forming an auditory *template*; in the **sensorimotor phase** it practices and gradually matches its own vocal output to the template using auditory feedback, through *subsong* (soft, variable, unstructured), *plastic song* (recognizable but variable syllables, often more than will be retained) and finally **crystallized song**, which is stable for the rest of the season or life.

The template is partly innate. An isolate song has species-typical features, so even without any model the bird has a crude specification of what to sing; experience refines it. During the plastic phase, birds of many species sing more syllable types than they will keep, and they drop most of them at crystallization, preferentially keeping those that match the songs of their territorial neighbors (Marler and Peters 1982; Nelson 1992). Song development is therefore *selective overproduction*: the bird first generates variety and then retains what fits its template and its social environment.

The two phases are separated in time in the white-crowned sparrow and other temperate migrants, which memorize songs as nestlings in summer and sing for the first time the following spring. In the zebra finch, a domesticated Australian finch whose males learn one song in their first three months, they overlap: memorization takes place between about 20 and 65 days, subsong starts at about 35 days, and the song crystallizes at about 90 days. Because it learns fast and breeds well in captivity, the zebra finch became the main laboratory species for the neurobiology of song.

![Timelines of song learning in two species (typical ages). In the zebra finch the phases overlap: memorization from about day 20 to 65, subsong from about day 35, crystallization at about 90 days. Species differ in when they can learn. *Age-limited* (closed-ended) learners such as the zebra finch and the white-crowned sparrow learn only during a juvenile sensitive period and sing the same song all their life. *Open-ended* learners keep learning as adults: canaries (*Serinus canaria*) add new syllables every year, after a period in late summer and autumn in which their song becomes plastic again, and European starlings, parrots and mockingbirds learn throughout life. These differences correspond to differences in the seasonal plasticity of the song system (17.3.5).

### 17.2.2. Innate Predispositions

A young bird does not learn every sound it hears. White-crowned sparrows that heard only recordings of song sparrow (*Melospiza melodia*) song during the sensitive period developed isolate songs, as if they had heard nothing; birds that heard both species learned only the white-crowned sparrow song (Marler 1970; Konishi 1985). The perceptual system of the young bird is predisposed to accept the sounds of its own species. Marler and Peters (1977) found the same in swamp sparrows (*Melospiza georgiana*): when young birds heard synthetic songs in which swamp sparrow syllables were arranged in the temporal pattern of song sparrow song, they copied the syllables but sang them in the swamp sparrow's own pattern of a simple trill; song sparrows, which have more complex songs, learned syllables of either species but arranged them in the song sparrow pattern. Each species' innate template specifies different features: in the swamp sparrow, the acoustic structure of syllables, in the song sparrow, the temporal organization of the song.

These predispositions are the "instincts to learn" of the modern synthesis of instinct and learning ([7.18.3](/7.18.3#sec-3.5)): what is inherited is not the song but the rules for acquiring it, a mechanism that directs learning toward appropriate models and makes the outcome reliable.

### 17.2.3. Social Tutors

Tape-tutor experiments exclude social interaction, which in nature is part of every learning experience. Luis Baptista, noting that wild white-crowned sparrows occasionally sing the songs of other species, placed hand-reared young sparrows where they could see and hear a live adult of another species, the strawberry finch (*Amandava amandava*), and hear but not see adult white-crowned sparrows. The young sparrows learned the song of the strawberry finch, their social tutor, and not the song of their own species that they could only hear (Baptista and Petrinovich 1984). Live tutors also extended the sensitive period: birds exposed to live tutors after 50 days still learned. Social interaction can therefore override the acoustic predisposition. In zebra finches, juveniles tutored by a live adult copy song more accurately than those that hear the same song through a loudspeaker, and social tutoring activates catecholaminergic neurons of the midbrain that modulate auditory and song areas (Chen and colleagues 2016). Young birds learn more from a tutor who responds to them.

Natural cross-fostering shows the same distinction between innate and learned vocalizations.

**Example 17.2.1 (Galahs Raised by Pink Cockatoos)** In Australian woodlands, pink cockatoos (*Lophochroa leadbeateri*) sometimes take over tree holes in which galahs (*Eolophus roseicapilla*) have laid eggs, and raise the galah chicks. Rowley and Chapman (1986) recorded the calls of such fostered galahs. Their begging and alarm calls were identical to those of galahs reared by galahs, but their contact calls, which keep flocks together, resembled those of their cockatoo foster parents. In the galah, begging and alarm calls develop without a model, whereas contact calls are learned from the birds with which the young associate. The same species can thus have innate and learned vocalizations, and the learned ones are those that identify social partners.

:::

Social feedback during the sensorimotor phase also shapes song. Brown-headed cowbirds (*Molothrus ater*) are brood parasites (**brood parasitism**, [7.18.32](/7.18.32)), so a young male never hears his father in the nest. Females do not sing, but they respond to songs they find attractive with a brief *wing stroke*, a fluttering display. West and King (1988) found that young males repeated the song variants that elicited wing strokes and dropped the others, and males housed with females of another population developed songs typical of the females' population. Females thus act as tutors without singing, by reinforcing selectively the songs that males try out, an example of learning guided by the reactions of a receiver.

## 17.3. Mechanisms of Song

### 17.3.1. The Syrinx and the Production of Sound

Birds produce sound with the **syrinx** at the junction of the trachea and the two primary bronchi ([7.17.64](/7.17.64#sec-64.6)). In songbirds each bronchus contains a pair of soft tissue folds, the medial and lateral labia, which are set into vibration by the expiratory airflow, as the vocal folds of mammals are: the labia open under air pressure and close by their elasticity and the Bernoulli effect, a *myoelastic–aerodynamic* mechanism that high-speed imaging confirmed in species from songbirds to pigeons (Elemans and colleagues 2015). The pressure of the air sacs drives the sound, and up to seven pairs of syringeal muscles, innervated by the tracheosyringeal branch of the hypoglossal nerve, control the tension of the labia (and hence the frequency) and the opening of each side.

Because each side of the syrinx is an independent sound source, songbirds can produce two notes at once, and many species switch rapidly between the sides. Fernando Nottebohm (1971) found that cutting the left tracheosyringeal nerve of a canary abolished most syllables, whereas cutting the right had little effect; in canaries the left side produces most of the song, and in other species the dominant side or the division of labor differs (brown thrashers sing low frequencies with the left and high frequencies with the right side). Singing is coordinated with breathing: canaries produce trills of more than 20 syllables per second by inserting a *minibreath*, a brief inspiration, between successive syllables.

The sound produced by the syrinx is filtered by the vocal tract. Songbirds open their beaks wider for higher frequencies, and the oropharyngeal cavity changes in volume with each note so that the vocal tract resonance tracks the fundamental frequency and suppresses harmonics; the pure, whistled tones of many songs are the result. This motor apparatus sets limits on what a bird can sing. A trill whose syllables sweep a wide range of frequencies requires the beak and syrinx to move through the whole range in every syllable, so fast trills must be narrow in frequency. Jeffrey Podos (1997) showed that, across species of emberizid sparrows, trills fall below an upper-bound line in the plane of trill rate and frequency bandwidth; among Darwin's finches, species with large, deep beaks adapted for crushing seeds sing slower and narrower trills (Podos 2001), so the ecology of feeding affects the evolution of song through the mechanics of the beak.

### 17.3.2. The Song System

**Definition 17.3.1 (Song System)** The **song system** is the set of interconnected, anatomically discrete forebrain and brainstem nuclei that are specialized for the production and learning of song in songbirds (and, in a corresponding arrangement, in parrots and hummingbirds). It consists of a **motor pathway**, from HVC through the robust nucleus of the arcopallium (RA) to the brainstem motor neurons of the syrinx and the respiratory premotor nuclei, and an **anterior forebrain pathway** (AFP), from HVC through Area X, the dorsolateral nucleus of the medial thalamus (DLM) and the lateral magnocellular nucleus of the anterior nidopallium (LMAN) back to RA.

HVC is a proper name (originally an abbreviation of "hyperstriatum ventrale, pars caudalis", later read as "high vocal center"). Its neurons project either to RA (HVC$_{\mathrm{RA}}$ neurons) or to Area X (HVC$_{\mathrm{X}}$ neurons). RA projects to the tracheosyringeal part of the hypoglossal nucleus (nXIIts), whose motor neurons innervate the syringeal muscles, and to the respiratory premotor nuclei of the brainstem. Auditory input reaches the forebrain through Field L, the avian analog of the primary auditory cortex, and is processed further in the caudomedial nidopallium (NCM) and the caudomedial mesopallium (CMM). The avian pallium has no layered cortex, but its nuclei correspond to cortical layers and areas in their connections and cell types ([7.14.46](/7.14.46#sec-46.8.3)); in this sense RA resembles the layer-5 neurons of the mammalian motor cortex that project to brainstem motor neurons.

![The song system of an oscine songbird in a schematic sagittal view of the brain (anterior to the left; not to scale). Motor pathway (blue): HVC → RA → nXIIts → syringeal muscles, with RA also driving the respiratory premotor nuclei. Anterior forebrain pathway (teal): HVC → Area X (basal ganglia) → DLM (thalamus) → LMAN → RA; it is needed for learning but not for singing a crystallized song. Auditory areas (pink): Field L → NCM and CMM, where the memory of tutor song is thought to be stored and auditory feedback is analyzed. Dopaminergic neurons of the VTA (green) project to Area X.](./plots/song-system.py)

The motor pathway is necessary for singing. Lesions of HVC or RA, or cutting the pathway from RA to nXIIts, abolish learned song in adults; a bird still gives its innate calls, which depend on other circuits (Nottebohm and colleagues 1976). The activity of the pathway explains how a song is generated in time. In zebra finches singing their single song motif, each HVC$_{\mathrm{RA}}$ neuron fires one brief burst of a few spikes at one precise moment of the motif, and different neurons fire at different moments, so that the population tiles the song like the ticks of a clock (Hahnloser, Kozhevnikov and Fee 2002). RA neurons, receiving these bursts, fire in more complex patterns that relate to the acoustic features of syllables. Long and Fee (2008) tested where the clock lies by cooling small regions of the brain of singing birds: cooling HVC by a few degrees slowed the whole song uniformly, syllables and gaps alike, by up to 45 %, whereas cooling RA had little effect on timing. HVC therefore sets the tempo of song, and RA translates the time signal into motor commands.

### 17.3.3. The Anterior Forebrain Pathway and Learning by Trial and Error

The AFP is a loop through the **basal ganglia** and thalamus, homologous to the cortico-basal ganglia-thalamocortical loops of mammals ([7.16.40](/7.16.40)). Area X contains striatal and pallidal neurons, DLM is a thalamic nucleus, and LMAN is a pallial (cortical) nucleus that projects to RA. Lesions of LMAN or Area X in juvenile zebra finches disrupt song learning, whereas the same lesions in adults leave crystallized song nearly unchanged (Bottjer, Miesner and Arnold 1984; Sohrabji, Nordeen and Nordeen 1990; Scharff and Nottebohm 1991). Juveniles with LMAN lesions crystallize an abnormal, simplified song prematurely. The AFP therefore serves learning, not production.

What does it contribute? Juvenile song is variable from rendition to rendition, and Ölveczky, Andalman and Fee (2005) found that inactivating LMAN pharmacologically in singing juveniles immediately reduced this variability: the song became more stereotyped. LMAN injects variability into the motor pathway, the motor equivalent of trial and error. Learning then requires a signal that evaluates each rendition. Tumer and Brainard (2007) gave adult zebra finches a burst of white noise whenever they sang one syllable at a pitch below (or above) a threshold; within hours the birds shifted the pitch of that syllable to avoid the noise, and the shift required the AFP. Dopaminergic neurons of the ventral tegmental area that project to Area X provide the evaluation. When singing birds heard distorted auditory feedback on a particular syllable, these neurons decreased their firing; when the expected distortion was omitted, they increased it (Gadagkar and colleagues 2016). They signal whether a rendition was better or worse than expected: a performance prediction error like the reward prediction error of **dopamine** neurons in mammals ([7.16.39](/7.16.39)).

The circuit implements **reinforcement learning** by perturbation. Let the motor command for a syllable be a vector $\boldsymbol{m}$ of acoustic parameters (pitch, duration, spectral shape), let each rendition add a random perturbation $\boldsymbol{\xi}$ from LMAN, and let the bird compare the result with its template $\boldsymbol{t}$ and compute an evaluation $R$, highest for a perfect match. If the command is changed in the direction of perturbations that gave a better than expected outcome,

$$
\boldsymbol{m} \leftarrow \boldsymbol{m} + \alpha\,(R - \bar R)\,\boldsymbol{\xi},
\tag{17.1}
$$
where $\bar R$ is a running average of the evaluation and $\alpha$ a learning rate, then on average the command climbs the gradient of $R$. For perturbations $\boldsymbol{\xi} \sim \mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0}, \sigma^2 I)$ small enough that $R(\boldsymbol{m} + \boldsymbol{\xi}) \approx R(\boldsymbol{m}) + \nabla R \cdot \boldsymbol{\xi}$, the expected change is $\alpha\,\mathbb{E}[(\nabla R\cdot\boldsymbol{\xi})\,\boldsymbol{\xi}] = \alpha\sigma^2\,\nabla R$. Learning therefore needs both variability ($\sigma > 0$) and an evaluation that depends on the outcome. Rule (17.1) is the REINFORCE rule of machine learning, and in the bird its terms have identifiable carriers: $\boldsymbol{\xi}$ in LMAN input to RA, $R - \bar R$ in the dopamine signal to Area X, and the update in the plasticity of corticostriatal and LMAN–RA synapses (Fee and Goldberg 2011). The model also accounts for the observation that LMAN carries a learned bias as well as noise: after pitch learning, LMAN activity initially pushes the pitch in the learned direction, and the change is consolidated into the motor pathway over about a day (Andalman and Fee 2009).

![Model: song learning as reinforcement learning (Eq. (17.1)), for one syllable described by two standardized acoustic features. A: the trajectory of the motor command from a subsong starting point toward the memorized template. B: distance from the template against the number of renditions practiced. An intact bird (blue, exploratory variability $\sigma = 0.3$) converges within about 1500 renditions. With LMAN inactivated (orange, $\sigma = 0.06$) the command moves in the same direction but about 25 times more slowly, since the expected step is proportional to $\sigma^2$. A deafened bird (red) has variability but no evaluation and does not learn. Learning rate $\alpha = 0.02$; evaluation noise s.d. 0.2.](./plots/song-rl.py)

A juvenile zebra finch sings its motif several hundred thousand times before it crystallizes, enough practice for such a slow, sampling-based algorithm. The template itself, the target against which renditions are evaluated, is thought to be stored in auditory areas. In NCM, the expression of the immediate early gene *ZENK* (*egr-1*), which codes for a transcription factor induced by neural activity, increases when a bird hears song, and in adult zebra finches the strength of this response to the tutor's song correlates with how much of the tutor song the bird copied (Bolhuis and colleagues 2000); during singing, *ZENK* is induced instead in the song nuclei themselves (Jarvis and Nottebohm 1997).

### 17.3.4. Neurons That Recognize the Bird's Own Song

Neurons of the song system respond to sound as well as driving song. Daniel Margoliash (1983) found that neurons in HVC of anesthetized white-crowned sparrows respond more strongly to playback of the bird's own song than to the songs of other individuals of the same dialect, and that some respond only to a particular sequence of its syllables. Such selectivity develops during the sensorimotor phase and is a candidate mechanism for comparing feedback with the template. In swamp sparrows, which have several song types, individual HVC neurons that project to Area X respond to playback of only one of the bird's song types and fire in a corresponding pattern when the bird sings that type (Mooney 2001; Prather and colleagues 2008). These neurons thus represent the same song type in perception and in production, like the *mirror neurons* of the primate premotor cortex, and could link what a bird hears from its neighbors to what it can sing, as is needed for song matching (17.5.3). The auditory responses of these neurons are gated by behavioral state: they are strong in sleeping or anesthetized birds and suppressed in awake birds, and during singing a copy of the motor command (an **efference copy**) may allow the comparison of intended and heard song.

### 17.3.5. Sex Differences, Seasons and New Neurons

In zebra finches and canaries only males sing, and Nottebohm and Arnold (1976) found that HVC and RA are several times larger in males than in females, and Area X is small or absent in females: the first sex difference in the size of brain nuclei found in any vertebrate. Gurney and Konishi (1980) showed that estradiol given to female zebra finch hatchlings masculinizes the song system, and such females sing when given testosterone as adults, an example of the organizational and activational effects of steroid hormones treated in [7.18.19](/7.18.19). Hormones are not the whole story: a zebra finch that was genetically male on its right side and female on its left side had a more masculine song system on the male side, in a common hormonal environment (Agate and colleagues 2003), so sex chromosome genes in brain cells also contribute ([7.16.36](/7.16.36#sec-36.6)).

In seasonal breeders, the song system changes with the seasons. HVC and RA of male canaries are about twice as large in spring, when the birds sing stable songs, as in late summer and autumn, when song becomes plastic and new syllables are learned (Nottebohm 1981); the changes are driven by testosterone, which rises with day length ([7.18.18](/7.18.18)), and similar seasonal growth occurs in wild white-crowned sparrows and other species that do not learn new songs as adults, so it supports seasonal song production more generally. Goldman and Nottebohm (1983) discovered that new neurons are added to HVC of adult canaries throughout life, born in the ventricular zone and migrating into the nucleus, where many become HVC$_{\mathrm{RA}}$ neurons. This discovery of **adult neurogenesis** in a functional circuit overturned the dogma that the adult vertebrate brain makes no new neurons, and stimulated the search that found it in the mammalian hippocampus.

Is a large HVC the cause or the consequence of learning many songs? Male marsh wrens (*Cistothorus palustris*) tutored with either a handful of songs or as many as 45, and sedge warblers (*Acrocephalus schoenobaenus*) reared in isolation or with tutors, developed song nuclei of the same size (Brenowitz and colleagues 1995; Leitner and colleagues 2002). Learning more does not enlarge the nucleus; across species, those with large repertoires have large HVCs (DeVoogd and colleagues 1993), which suggests that the capacity is built first and then filled.

## 17.4. The Evolution of Vocal Learning

### 17.4.1. Vocal Learners

**Definition 17.4.1 (Vocal Learning)** **Vocal learning** (vocal production learning) is the ability to modify the acoustic structure of one's vocalizations as a result of experience with the sounds of others, typically by imitation. It is distinguished from *usage learning* (learning in which context to give an innate vocalization) and *comprehension learning* (learning the meaning of others' vocalizations), which are widespread among animals.

Vocal communication is universal among birds and mammals, but vocal learning is rare. Among birds it is known in three groups: the oscine passerines (songbirds, about 5 000 species, nearly half of all birds), the parrots (about 400 species) and the hummingbirds (about 360 species); in the suboscine passerines, whose songs develop normally in birds deafened young, it has been found in the bellbirds (*Procnias*, Cotingidae), whose males change their songs as adults (Kroodsma and colleagues 2013). Among mammals, vocal learners include humans, cetaceans (the songs of humpback whales change from year to year across ocean basins, dolphins learn signature whistles), pinnipeds (a harbor seal named Hoover imitated human speech), several bats, and elephants (an African elephant imitated the sound of trucks); nonhuman primates, despite their cognitive abilities, have at most limited control over the acoustic structure of their calls. Vocal learning is therefore not a consequence of intelligence; it is a specialization of the vocal motor system.

### 17.4.2. How Many Origins?

Mapping vocal learning onto a phylogeny is an exercise in **ancestral state reconstruction**, whose result depends on both the tree and the character data. On the phylogeny accepted in the 1990s, each of the three avian groups of vocal learners was nested among nonlearners, and the most parsimonious reconstruction (the one requiring the fewest changes) was three independent origins rather than one origin followed by four losses (Jarvis and colleagues 2000). Genome-scale phylogenies changed the picture: parrots turned out to be the sister group of the passerines, and falcons the sister group of both (Hackett and colleagues 2008; Jarvis and colleagues 2014; Prum and colleagues 2015). On this tree two scenarios are about equally parsimonious: three gains (hummingbirds, parrots, oscines), or two gains (hummingbirds, and the common ancestor of parrots and passerines) followed by losses in the suboscines and the New Zealand wrens. The discovery of vocal learning in bellbirds adds either a further gain within the suboscines or a regain.

Scenario (1): three independent gains (green bars marked 1); scenario (2): two gains, one in the hummingbirds and one in the common ancestor of parrots and passerines (marked 2), and losses in the New Zealand wrens and suboscines (red crosses). The brain gives independent evidence. Mapping the induction of *ZENK* by singing and hearing, Jarvis and colleagues (2000) found that parrots, hummingbirds and songbirds all have seven comparable forebrain song nuclei, in similar positions and with similar connections, and that birds that do not learn their vocalizations have none of them; their calls are controlled from the midbrain and brainstem. The similarity could indicate a common origin or **convergent evolution** constrained by a shared ancestral motor circuit. Parrots add a complication: each of their song nuclei has a "core" surrounded by a "shell" with its own connections, absent in songbirds, and the shell may underlie the parrots' exceptional capacity for imitation (Chakraborty and colleagues 2015). One hypothesis holds that vocal learning circuits arose repeatedly by duplication of the surrounding motor pathways that control movements of the body (Feenders and colleagues 2008).

### 17.4.3. Birdsong and Human Speech

The parallels between song learning and the development of human speech were pointed out by Marler and are now detailed (Doupe and Kuhl 1999):

| Feature | Songbirds | Humans |
|---|---|---|
| Learning from adult models | Tutor song; dialects | Language of the community; accents |
| Sensitive period | Memorization in a juvenile window (age-limited learners) | Phonetic perception tuned to the native language in the first year; second languages learned late keep an accent |
| Predispositions | Selective learning of conspecific song | Preferential attention to speech |
| Practice stage | Subsong, plastic song | Babbling, from about 7 months |
| Auditory feedback | Needed to learn and, in many species, to maintain song | Needed to learn speech; deafness impairs speech development |
| Social tutoring | Live tutors more effective | Infants learn phonemes from live speakers but not from video |
| Forebrain circuits | Motor pathway with a direct projection from RA to vocal motor neurons; AFP through the basal ganglia | Laryngeal motor cortex with a direct projection to the nucleus ambiguus; cortico-basal ganglia loops |

A key anatomical feature of vocal learners is the direct projection from a forebrain motor area to the brainstem motor neurons of the vocal organ: RA to nXIIts in songbirds, the laryngeal motor cortex to the nucleus ambiguus in humans. Nonhuman primates and nonlearning birds have at most sparse direct projections. Pfenning and colleagues (2014) compared gene expression across the brains of birds and humans and found that the song nuclei of the three avian groups of vocal learners and the human speech areas share specialized expression of dozens of genes, many involved in axon guidance, that distinguish them from the surrounding motor areas and from the corresponding areas of nonlearners: RA resembles the laryngeal motor cortex, and Area X a part of the human striatum active in speech. Vocal learning in birds and humans is an example of deep convergence, in which independently evolved circuits recruit the same genes.

The transcription factor FOXP2 links the two systems. A dominant point mutation in *FOXP2* causes in the members of a London family (the "KE family") a severe disorder of speech, a developmental verbal dyspraxia affecting the sequencing of orofacial movements, together with structural differences in the striatum (Lai and colleagues 2001). In zebra finches, *FoxP2* is expressed in Area X, more strongly during the period of song learning; in canaries, more strongly in the months when song is plastic; and singing itself regulates its expression (Haesler and colleagues 2004; Teramitsu and White 2006). Knocking down *FoxP2* in Area X of juvenile zebra finches by RNA interference made their imitation of tutor song incomplete and inaccurate, much like the speech of affected humans (Haesler and colleagues 2007). FOXP2 is not a "language gene": it is highly conserved and acts in many tissues. It appears to be required for the plasticity of basal ganglia circuits that learn motor sequences, and both songbirds and humans use such circuits for vocal learning. Song and speech differ in one crucial respect: speech is combinatorial and meaningful, whereas the elements of song have no meaning of their own; the question how far song has a syntax, and whether animals can learn recursive grammars, is discussed in [7.18.47](/7.18.47).

## 17.5. The Adaptive Value of Song Learning

### 17.5.1. A Costly Ability

Learning song requires time, a large brain region and a long dependence on appropriate tutors, and an isolated or misled young bird ends up with a poor song. Innately specified songs, as in suboscines, pigeons and chickens, work without these costs. The cost of the brain itself is considerable: songbirds and parrots pack neurons in the pallium about twice as densely as primates, so that a raven or a macaw has as many forebrain neurons as a monkey with a much larger brain (Olkowicz and colleagues 2016), and neural tissue is among the most expensive tissues to maintain ([7.18.8](/7.18.8#def-8.1.2)). Several ultimate hypotheses propose benefits that outweigh these costs (Nowicki and Searcy 2014):

| Hypothesis | Proposed benefit of learning | Typical evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Environmental (acoustic) adaptation | Learning lets songs match the transmission properties of the local habitat | Song structure differs between habitats |
| Recognition | Shared, learned songs identify neighbors, group members or individuals | Type matching; group-specific calls |
| Information sharing | Learning expands the vocal repertoire where living with kin favors sharing information | Larger repertoires in cooperative breeders |
| Sexual selection: male–male competition | Learned repertoires allow graded threat signals to rivals | Responses to matched songs; territorial tenure |
| Sexual selection: female choice | Learned song reveals male quality or developmental history | Female preferences; developmental stress effects |
| Geographic matching | Dialects advertise local origin and allow **assortative mating** and **local adaptation** | Female preference for local dialect |

### 17.5.2. Adapting to the Local Environment

Sounds degrade differently in different habitats. In dense vegetation, high frequencies are absorbed and scattered and rapid repetitions are blurred by reverberation, so slow, low-frequency, tonal songs transmit best; in open habitats, wind and temperature gradients cause amplitude fluctuations that favor rapid modulations ([7.18.38](/7.18.38)). Great tits in dense forests sing pure, low-frequency whistles, whereas those in open woodlands use higher frequencies and more complex songs (Hunter and Krebs 1979), and white-crowned sparrows in more densely vegetated sites sing slower trills of lower frequency (Derryberry 2007). A young bird that learns from adults in its own habitat acquires a song already tested by local transmission, and learning allows it to track changes in the environment, such as the rise of urban noise, faster than genetic evolution could.

### 17.5.3. Neighbors and Rivals

A territorial male deals with the same neighbors for years, and learning their songs allows him to address them individually. Young white-crowned sparrows and song sparrows learn much of their final repertoire in their first breeding season from the males on adjacent territories, and keep in their crystallized repertoire the song types they share with them (Nordby and colleagues 1999). Song sparrows use shared songs to graded effect (Beecher and colleagues 2000):

- *Type matching*: replying to a neighbor's song type with the same type, an aggressive signal.
- *Repertoire matching*: replying with another type from the repertoire shared with that neighbor, an intermediate signal.
- *Unshared reply*: replying with a type the neighbor does not sing, a conciliatory signal.

In playback experiments, territory owners responded most aggressively to a type match, less to a repertoire match and least to an unshared song (Burt and colleagues 2001). The ability pays: the time a male song sparrow keeps his territory increases about fourfold as the number of song types he shares with his neighbors rises from fewer than five to more than twenty (Beecher and colleagues 2000). The benefit depends on stable neighborhoods. Sedge wrens (*Cistothorus platensis*) of the North American Great Plains are nomadic, breeding at different sites during the summer; they improvise songs on the general species pattern and have no dialects. In Costa Rica and Brazil, where sedge wrens hold territories all year, males learn the songs of their neighbors and form dialects (Kroodsma and colleagues 1999). Learning thus evolves where it allows males to communicate specifically with long-term neighbors, the situation in which the dear enemy effect ([7.18.26](/7.18.26)) also arises.

### 17.5.4. Groups and Information

In species that live in stable groups, learned vocalizations identify group members. In cooperatively breeding birds that live in large groups of relatives and nonrelatives, such as noisy miners (*Manorina melanocephala*) and superb starlings (*Lamprotornis superbus*), calls identify individuals, whereas in species with small family groups they more often signal only group identity (Keen and colleagues 2013); birds that move into a new group as adults learn its calls. Across 97 species, cooperative breeders have larger vocal repertoires, especially of alarm and contact calls, than other birds (Leighton 2017), as the information-sharing hypothesis predicts.

Learned vocal sequences can also have structure that carries information. Chestnut-crowned babblers (*Pomatostomus ruficeps*) combine two meaningless acoustic elements in different orders to produce two calls with different functions (Engesser and colleagues 2015), and Japanese tits (*Parus minor*) respond appropriately to a combination of an alarm call and a recruitment call only when the two are given in the natural order (Suzuki, Wheatcroft and Griesser 2016). Song itself often has rules of sequence. The song of the Bengalese finch, a domesticated form of the white-rumped munia (*Lonchura striata*), consists of syllables whose order varies according to transition probabilities that a finite-state model describes well, and it is more complex than the song of the wild munia; Okanoya (2004) proposed that domestication relaxed natural selection while female preference for complex sequences continued. The study of such sequence rules, the "syntax" of song, and of how they are learned and represented in HVC is an active field; their relation to human grammar is discussed in [7.18.47](/7.18.47).

### 17.5.5. Female Choice

Females are the main audience of song in many species, and in many species they also sing: in a comparative analysis of more than 1 000 songbird species, female song was present in about 71 % of those with data, and ancestral-state reconstruction indicated that female song was present in the common ancestor of songbirds (Odom and colleagues 2014). The northern temperate pattern, in which only males sing, is derived. Female song functions in territory defense and in competition with other females, and the evolution of song is therefore not explained by male competition and female choice alone.

Where males sing to females, song plays a role in mate attraction. A male Cassin's finch (*Haemorhous cassinii*) whose mate is removed sings much more, whereas introducing a female to an unpaired male reduces his singing (Sockman and colleagues 2005). What can a female learn from a *learned* song? Three answers have been proposed.

1. *Local origin.* According to the geographic matching hypothesis, a male who sings the local dialect announces that he was raised locally, and a female who prefers the local dialect obtains mates whose genes are adapted to the area (Baker and Cunningham 1985). Near Tioga Pass in California, male white-crowned sparrows singing the local dialect had lower infections with the blood parasite *Haemoproteus* and fathered more offspring than males with foreign songs (MacDougall-Shackleton and colleagues 2002). But elsewhere females do not prefer their fathers' dialect, males can change dialect when they move, and dialect groups are not genetically distinct (17.1.2), so the evidence is mixed. Female swamp sparrows reared in the laboratory prefer unfamiliar local songs to foreign songs, so some sparrows can recognize local song in general (Anderson and colleagues 2014).
2. *Repertoire size.* Females of sedge warblers and great reed warblers prefer males with large repertoires, which in great reed warblers obtain more extra-pair fertilizations and have offspring that survive better (Hasselquist, Bensch and von Schantz 1996), and song-rich male sedge warblers feed their young more (Buchanan and Catchpole 2000). A review of the many tests, however, found consistent evidence for a preference for larger repertoires in only a minority of species (Byers and Kroodsma 2009), so repertoire size is not a universal target of choice.
3. *Developmental history and performance.* The developmental stress hypothesis (Nowicki, Peters and Podos 1998) starts from the timing of song learning: the song nuclei grow and song is memorized in the first weeks of life, when nestlings are growing rapidly and are most vulnerable to food shortage, disease and parasites. A male's song quality could therefore reveal how well he coped with early stress, which is otherwise hard to assess. Nowicki and colleagues (2002) hand-reared swamp sparrows and fed half of them 70 % of the food the controls ate for about two weeks. The restricted birds caught up in body mass, but as adults they had a smaller HVC and copied the tutor songs less accurately. In playback tests, female song sparrows gave more copulation solicitation displays to accurately copied songs than to poorly copied ones. Females can also assess performance directly: female swamp sparrows prefer trills close to the upper performance limit of Podos (Ballentine, Hyman and Nowicki 2004), and the performance limit is a mechanical constraint that a male in poor condition cannot fake. In this sense learned song is an honest signal of quality in the sense of the **handicap principle** ([7.18.39](/7.18.39)).

Female preferences are themselves partly learned. Female zebra finches reared without hearing high-performance songs do not prefer them as adults (Chen and colleagues 2017), while their preference for complex songs survives developmental stress. The neural basis of preference shows how proximate and ultimate analyses inform each other. In female canaries, whose HVC is small but present, lesions of HVC abolished the preference for conspecific over heterospecific song (Brenowitz 1991). In female zebra finches, which never sing and have a tiny HVC, preferences survive HVC lesions and depend on auditory areas such as CMM (MacDougall-Shackleton and colleagues 1998). Asking what females use song for led to the question of where in their brain they evaluate it, and the answer differs with how much females of the species sing.

### 17.5.6. An Integrated View

Birdsong illustrates how the levels of explanation fit together. Selection for communication with neighbors, mates and group members in particular social and acoustic environments favored the ability to learn; learning required a forebrain circuit with a direct projection to the vocal motor neurons, a basal ganglia loop for trial-and-error refinement and an auditory memory; the timing of learning, during rapid growth in the nest, makes song an indicator of developmental history; and the imprecision of copying produces dialects that evolve culturally. Each mechanism constrains the function and each function explains the mechanism, which is why the integrative study of song, from genes to geographic variation, has been so productive.

## Summary

- Song is described by oscillograms and spectrograms (Definition 17.1.1); songs are built of notes, syllables, trills and phrases, and a male's song types form his repertoire. Local populations of many songbirds sing dialects (Definition 17.1.2), which are learned rather than genetic at the local scale and change by **cultural evolution** and in response to noise.
- Song learning (Definition 17.2.1) has a sensory phase, in which the tutor song is memorized during a **critical period** (white-crowned sparrow: days 10–50), and a sensorimotor phase of subsong and plastic song leading to crystallized song; isolates sing abnormal songs with species-typical features, and birds deafened before singing cannot use their memory. Age-limited and open-ended learners differ.
- Learning is guided by innate predispositions (selective learning of conspecific syllables or temporal patterns) and by social interaction (live tutors, cowbird females' wing strokes); fostered galahs learn contact calls but not begging and alarm calls (Example 17.2.1).
- The two-sided **syrinx** produces sound by a myoelastic–aerodynamic mechanism; the vocal tract filters it, and a motor performance limit trades trill rate against bandwidth.
- The song system (Definition 17.3.1): the motor pathway HVC → RA → nXIIts produces song, with HVC as a sparse clock; the anterior forebrain pathway (Area X, DLM, LMAN), a **basal ganglia** loop, is needed for learning: LMAN injects variability, VTA **dopamine** signals performance errors, and learning follows a perturbation rule (Eq. (17.1)) whose rate is proportional to the variance of exploration. NCM holds tutor memories; HVC neurons are selective for the bird's own song and some mirror song types in hearing and singing.
- Song nuclei are larger in males of species where only males sing, are organized by estradiol and sex chromosome genes, grow seasonally under testosterone, and recruit new neurons in adults (**adult neurogenesis**).
- Vocal learning (Definition 17.4.1) occurs in oscines, parrots, hummingbirds (and bellbirds) and in humans, cetaceans, pinnipeds, bats and elephants. On current phylogenies it evolved two or three times in birds. Vocal learners share forebrain nuclei, a direct forebrain-to-vocal-motor projection and convergent gene expression; FOXP2 is required in Area X for accurate imitation, as in human speech.
- Learning song is costly; its benefits include acoustic adaptation to habitat, recognition of neighbors (song sparrow type matching; tenure rises fourfold with shared songs) and group members, information sharing in cooperative breeders, and female choice for local dialect, repertoire size, accurate copying (developmental stress hypothesis) and vocal performance. Female song is widespread and ancestral.

## Questions

1. A spectrogram computed with a window of 5 ms shows a syllable as a smooth downsweep from 6 to 3 kHz, whereas one computed with a window of 40 ms shows a set of short horizontal segments. Explain the difference and say which representation is more faithful.

   **Answer** The frequency resolution of a windowed Fourier transform is about the reciprocal of the window duration: $1/5\,\mathrm{ms} = 200\,\mathrm{Hz}$ for the short window and $25\,\mathrm{Hz}$ for the long one, while the time resolution is the window duration itself. If the syllable sweeps 3 kHz in, say, 30 ms, its frequency changes by $3000/30 = 100\,\mathrm{Hz}$ per ms; within a 40 ms window it covers the whole range, so the long window spreads the energy over many frequency bins and, with overlapping windows, renders the sweep as a staircase of blurred segments. The 5 ms window follows the sweep because the frequency changes by only 500 Hz within a window, comparable to the resolution of 200 Hz. For a fast frequency-modulated sound the short window is more faithful; for a steady harmonic tone the long window would be better. Neither is the "true" picture: the choice must match the time scale of the modulation.

2. Design an experiment, with the necessary controls, that distinguishes the acoustic stimulus hypothesis from the social interaction hypothesis for the learning of dialects in a species of sparrow.

   **Answer** Hand-rear young males from one dialect area (A) in individual sound-isolation boxes and assign them at random to four groups during the sensitive period: (1) loudspeaker playback of dialect B; (2) a live adult male of dialect B in an adjacent cage, visible and able to interact; (3) a live, visible adult male of dialect B together with playback of dialect A from a loudspeaker; (4) no song (isolate controls). Record all males the following spring, when songs have crystallized, and score the similarity of their songs to dialects A and B blind to treatment, using spectrographic cross-correlation. The acoustic stimulus hypothesis predicts that groups 1 and 2 learn B equally well and that group 3 learns a mixture or the song presented more often; the social interaction hypothesis predicts that group 2 learns B better than group 1 and that group 3 learns B, the song of the social tutor. Group 4 shows the isolate song, the baseline against which learning is measured. Equal amounts of song exposure in all groups are needed, so playback must be matched to the singing rate of the live tutors.

3. (Calculation) In the perturbation rule (17.1), assume a single acoustic parameter $m$, a template $t$, an evaluation $R = -(m + \xi - t)^2$ and a perfect baseline $\bar R = -(m - t)^2$. Show that the expected change of $m$ per rendition is $-2\alpha\sigma^2(m - t)$ and find how many renditions are needed to halve the error $\lvert m - t\rvert$ for $\alpha = 0.02$ and $\sigma = 0.3$. How does the answer change if LMAN is inactivated so that $\sigma = 0.06$?

   **Answer** With $e = m - t$, $R - \bar R = -(e + \xi)^2 + \mathrm{e}^2 = -2e\xi - \xi^2$. The update is $\Delta m = \alpha(-2e\xi - \xi^2)\xi$, and since $\mathbb{E}[\xi] = 0$, $\mathbb{E}[\xi^2] = \sigma^2$ and $\mathbb{E}[\xi^3] = 0$ for a normal perturbation, $\mathbb{E}[\Delta m] = -2\alpha\sigma^2 e$. The mean error therefore decays as $e_k = e_0(1 - 2\alpha\sigma^2)^k \approx e_0\,\mathrm{e}^{-2\alpha\sigma^2 k}$, and it halves after $k_{1/2} = \ln 2/(2\alpha\sigma^2)$ renditions. For $\alpha = 0.02$ and $\sigma = 0.3$: $k_{1/2} = 0.693/(2 \times 0.02 \times 0.09) \approx 193$ renditions. With $\sigma = 0.06$, $\sigma^2$ is 25 times smaller and $k_{1/2} \approx 4800$ renditions. Reducing exploratory variability slows learning in proportion to its variance, which is the logic of the LMAN inactivation experiments; it also explains why juveniles with LMAN lesions crystallize an imperfect song: the song stops improving long before the end of the sensitive period.

4. Why do the results of isolation experiments not show that the song of the isolate is "the innate component" of the normal song?

   **Answer** An isolate is not a normal bird minus experience; it is a bird that developed in an abnormal environment. Its song reflects the innate predispositions and the motor capacities of the species, but also the effects of social deprivation and of practicing without any model, which may shape it in ways that never occur in nature (for example by crystallizing its own improvisations). Conversely, innate predispositions also act in normal development by selecting what is memorized and retained, so the innate contribution is present in the normal song in a form the isolate does not show. As argued for the instinct–learning dichotomy in 7.18.3, the right question is which features of the song differ between birds that differ in a specified experience (or gene), not which part of a song is innate.

5. The developmental stress hypothesis predicts that song quality is an honest indicator of a male's early condition. List the links of the argument and the evidence for each, and suggest an observation in the field that could refute the hypothesis.

   **Answer** (a) The song system develops and song is learned during the nestling and fledgling period, when growth is fast and food is limiting: supported by the timing of the sensory phase and of HVC growth. (b) Early stress impairs the development of song nuclei: shown by food restriction of swamp sparrows and song sparrows (smaller HVC or RA) and by corticosterone treatment. (c) Impaired nuclei reduce song quality: food-restricted swamp sparrows copied tutor songs less accurately; stressed zebra finches sing shorter, simpler songs. (d) Females prefer high-quality songs: female song sparrows displayed more to accurately copied songs; swamp sparrow females prefer high-performance trills. (e) Early condition predicts adult quality (survival, parental care, immune function), which makes the preference adaptive: supported weakly and indirectly. A refuting observation would be a population in which naturally stressed males (for example from late or large broods, measured by nestling growth) sing songs that do not differ from those of well-fed males in copying accuracy or performance, or in which females mating with high-quality singers gain nothing in offspring condition or survival.

6. (Open question) The song system of parrots has a core and a shell; songbirds have only the core. Propose comparative and experimental studies that could test whether the shell underlies the greater capacity of parrots for imitation.

   **Answer** Comparatively, measure the relative size of the shell in parrot species that differ in imitative ability (budgerigars, which imitate readily, versus species with small learned repertoires), controlling for body size and phylogeny with phylogenetic comparative methods; the hypothesis predicts a positive correlation. Developmentally, determine when the shell matures relative to the onset of vocal imitation. Experimentally, in budgerigars trained to imitate novel sounds, make selective lesions or chemogenetic inactivation of the shell versus the core: the hypothesis predicts that shell inactivation impairs the learning of new sounds and the imitation of heterospecific sounds while leaving already learned contact calls, which depend on the core, intact. Recording neurons in the shell during imitation learning should reveal activity related to novel vocal targets. Finally, gene expression profiling of the shell versus the core could identify specializations comparable to those shared by vocal learners (Pfenning and colleagues 2014).
