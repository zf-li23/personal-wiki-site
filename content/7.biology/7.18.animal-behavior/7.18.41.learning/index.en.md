Animals change their behavior as a result of experience: a snail stops withdrawing from a harmless touch, a rat avoids a food that once made it ill, a pigeon learns which key to peck, a chickadee remembers where it hid thousands of seeds. Learning is the subject of the next seven chapters, which treat animal cognition in the tradition of **comparative psychology** ([7.18.1](/7.18.1)) but with the question that runs through this course: why did a particular capacity evolve in a particular species? This chapter covers the elementary forms of learning: habituation, classical and operant conditioning, and the evolved constraints and specializations that shape what each species learns easily. The cellular mechanisms of these forms (*Aplysia*, the mushroom body of *Drosophila*) are in [7.16.38](/7.16.38), the prediction-error theory of conditioning and its connection to dopamine and **reinforcement learning** in [7.16.39](/7.16.39), and the physiological classification of memory in [7.14.46](/7.14.46#sec-46.5). Learning restricted to a sensitive period, **filial imprinting** and song learning, was treated in [7.18.5](/7.18.5) and [7.18.17](/7.18.17); memory and spatial cognition follow in [7.18.42](/7.18.42), concepts, time and number in [7.18.43](/7.18.43), and learning from others in [7.18.46](/7.18.46).

## 41.1. Learning as an Adaptation

### 41.1.1. What Learning Is

Not every change in behavior is learning. A marathon runner slows in the second half because the muscles tire; a person entering a dark cinema sees better after a few minutes because the retina adapts; a young bird flies better each week partly because its muscles and nervous system mature. These changes are excluded from the definition.

**Definition 41.1.1 (Learning)** **Learning** is a relatively persistent change in an animal's behavior, or in its potential for behavior, that results from experience, excluding changes caused by fatigue, sensory adaptation, maturation, injury or disease. **Memory** is the retention of the information acquired, which allows it to affect later behavior.

The phrase "potential for behavior" matters because learning is often not expressed when it occurs. A rat that explores a maze without reward learns its layout, but shows this only when food is later placed in the goal box (latent learning, Section 41.6.1), and a young songbird memorizes a tutor's song months before it sings it ([7.18.17](/7.18.17)). The distinction between learning and performance means that a failure to perform is never proof of a failure to learn, a caution that recurs whenever species are compared.

Learning is a mechanism of development, and the old opposition between "innate" and "learned" behavior has been replaced by the question of how genes and experience interact ([7.18.3](/7.18.3), [7.18.5](/7.18.5)). Two facts make this plain. First, the capacity to learn is itself inherited: it depends on genes that build sensory systems, synapses and plasticity mechanisms, and it can be changed by artificial selection (Section 41.5.4). Second, what is learned, when and how easily is species specific: rats learn tastes that predict illness in one trial, pigeons learn to peck for food without being taught, and bees learn colors that predict nectar but not colors that signal an escape route (Section 41.5.2).

### 41.1.2. When Does Learning Pay?

If learning is an adaptation, natural selection should favor it only under some conditions. A fixed (innate) behavior is reliable, immediate and cheap, but it is right only if the environment is the same as the one in which the behavior evolved. Learning can track an environment that changes, but it requires sampling, during which the animal makes mistakes, and it requires neural machinery that costs energy to build and run. David Stephens (1991) analyzed this trade-off with a model in which the environment varies on two time scales.

**Formula 41.1.1 (Value of Learning)** Let the best action match one of two states of the environment. Within a lifetime of $T$ periods, the state persists from one period to the next with probability $p$; the state at birth equals the parent's state at birth with probability $q$. A fixed strategy, which performs throughout life the action that was best at the parent's birth, has mean payoff (the fraction of periods with the right action)

$$
W_{\mathrm{fixed}} = q\,m(p) + (1 - q)\bigl(1 - m(p)\bigr), \qquad m(p) = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\frac{1 + (2p - 1)^t}{2},
$$
where $m(p)$ is the probability that the state at time $t$ is still the state at birth. A learner that samples the state and always performs the action that was best in the previous period, at a cost $c$ per period, has mean payoff $W_{\mathrm{learn}} = \bigl(\tfrac12 + (T - 1)p\bigr)/T - c$.

Learning is favored when the environment is predictable within a lifetime ($p$ high), so that what is learned remains useful, but unpredictable between generations ($q$ low), so that the information cannot be stored by natural selection in the genome. When both kinds of predictability are high, a fixed behavior does as well without the cost; when the environment changes too fast for experience to be informative ($p$ close to $\tfrac12$), neither strategy can track it and the cheaper one wins.

A: the region of the plane of within-life predictability $p$ and between-generation predictability $q$ in which learning gives a higher payoff than a fixed behavior (shaded for a cost $c = 0.05$; the dashed line is the boundary for $c = 0.15$). Innate behavior is favored where experience carries no information ($p$ near $\tfrac12$) and where both $p$ and $q$ are close to 1 (top right corner). B: payoffs along two slices: the learner (green) and a fixed behavior when the environment is highly predictable across generations ($q = 0.95$, blue) or not ($q = 0.6$, dashed red). A higher cost of learning shrinks the region in which it pays.](./plots/learning-value.py)

The model explains a pattern seen across many systems. Food preferences, the location of resources and the identity of neighbors and mates change on the scale of a lifetime and are learned; responses to stimuli that have been constant over evolutionary time, such as the shape of a predator or the smell of a conspecific, are often innate (sign stimuli, [7.18.3](/7.18.3)). Within the same system, predispositions and learning are frequently combined: a young bird has an innate bias toward the song of its species and learns the local dialect ([7.18.17](/7.18.17)).

### 41.1.3. Kinds of Learning

Learning is conventionally divided into nonassociative forms, which involve a single kind of stimulus, and associative forms, in which the animal learns a relation between two events. The categories are tools for analysis, not natural kinds: one episode of learning in the wild usually involves several of them.

## 41.2. Nonassociative Learning

### 41.2.1. Habituation

**Habituation** is the simplest and most widespread form of learning ([7.14.46](/7.14.46#sec-46.5.1)): the decline of a response to a stimulus that is repeated without consequence. Herbert Jennings (1906) described it in the single-celled ciliate *Stentor*, which contracts into its tube when struck by a jet of water but, after a few repetitions, stays extended and keeps feeding. The ragworm *Nereis* withdraws into its burrow when a shadow passes, and stops doing so after a few dozen shadows; nematodes stop reversing to repeated taps on their dish; incubating birds stop leaving the nest when a car passes on a nearby road. The adaptive value is obvious: an animal that responded to every rustle of leaves would have no time to feed, and each unnecessary escape costs energy and attracts attention.

Habituation must be distinguished from sensory adaptation (a fatigued receptor) and from motor fatigue. Three of its properties make the distinction possible, and together with several others they form the standard list of its characteristics (Thompson and Spencer 1966, revised by Rankin et al. 2009):

- *Stimulus specificity*: the response to a different stimulus is not reduced. A sea slug habituated to touch of the siphon still withdraws its gill fully when its mantle is touched, and the gill muscle is not fatigued.
- *Dishabituation*: a strong or novel stimulus of another kind restores the habituated response at once. If the sensory receptors were adapted, an unrelated stimulus could not restore it.
- *Spontaneous recovery*: after a rest without stimulation, the response returns, at least partly.
- The response declines approximately exponentially toward an asymptote; repeated series of habituation training produce progressively faster habituation and, after spaced sessions, long-term habituation lasting days.
- Weak stimuli habituate faster and more completely than strong ones, and very intense stimuli may not habituate at all.
- Habituation is faster at short interstimulus intervals (ISIs), and recovery after short-ISI training is also faster, so that responses tested long after training can be stronger after short-ISI than after long-ISI training. This *rate sensitivity* (Davis 1970, startle responses in rats) shows that habituation is not a single decaying trace.

Rate sensitivity is explained by models in which the stimulus passes through several stages of habituation with different time constants. In the cascaded-integrator model of John Staddon and Jennifer Higa (1996), each stage subtracts a slowly decaying memory of its recent input from that input and passes the remainder on. At short ISIs the fast stage removes most of the stimulus before it reaches the slow stage, so habituation is quick but also quickly lost; at long ISIs the fast stage decays between stimuli and the slow stage does the work, giving slow but durable habituation.

In the gill-withdrawal reflex of *Aplysia*, short-term habituation is a depression of transmitter release at the synapses from sensory to motor neurons, and long-term habituation involves a loss of synaptic contacts ([7.16.38](/7.16.38#sec-38.3.1)). In vertebrates, habituation of the startle response occurs in the brainstem pathway, while habituation to complex stimuli such as a particular song or face involves the forebrain.

In nature, habituation lets animals ignore what is common and harmless while remaining alert to the rare and dangerous. Territorial animals habituate to the songs of established neighbors and respond less to them than to strangers, one mechanism of the **dear enemy effect** ([7.18.26](/7.18.26)). Habituation to people is the basis of field studies of primates and of wildlife tourism, and it reduces the **flight initiation distance** of animals in parks and cities ([7.18.24](/7.18.24)); its cost is that habituated animals may approach people, vehicles or traps too closely. Because habituation is stimulus specific, predators that vary their approach, or alarm signals that differ from familiar sounds, escape it.

### 41.2.2. Sensitization and Dishabituation

Sensitization is the opposite process ([7.14.46](/7.14.46#sec-46.5.1)): after a strong or noxious stimulus, responses to many other stimuli increase for minutes to days. A shock to the tail of *Aplysia* makes the gill withdrawal reflex to a light touch larger and longer; a person who has heard a loud bang startles at a door closing. The dual-process theory of Philip Groves and Richard Thompson (1970) proposed that every stimulus engages two independent processes, a decremental one specific to the stimulus pathway (habituation) and an incremental one in a general arousal system (sensitization), and that the observed response reflects their sum. Dishabituation is then sensitization superimposed on habituation, which is why a novel stimulus restores the habituated response. In *Aplysia*, sensitization is produced by serotonin released from facilitatory interneurons onto the terminals of the sensory neurons, which enhances their transmitter release through cyclic AMP and protein kinase A; repeated shocks recruit gene expression and synaptic growth, the first example in which the transition from short- to long-term memory was traced to molecules ([7.16.38](/7.16.38#sec-38.3.2)).

## 41.3. Classical Conditioning

### 41.3.1. Pavlov's Procedure

Ivan Pavlov, studying digestion in dogs with surgically exteriorized salivary ducts, noticed that the dogs salivated before food reached their mouths, at the sight of the attendant who usually fed them. He called this "psychic secretion" and turned his laboratory to analyzing it. The analysis has four elements: an *unconditioned stimulus* (US, food in the mouth) that elicits an *unconditioned response* (UR, salivation) without training; a *conditioned stimulus* (CS, the sound of a metronome or a buzzer, a light) that initially elicits no such response; repeated pairing of the CS shortly before the US; and the *conditioned response* (CR) that the CS comes to elicit. (The Russian terms are better translated as "unconditional" and "conditional": the response to the CS is conditional on training.) This is **classical conditioning** ([7.16.38](/7.16.38#sec-38.2)), also called Pavlovian conditioning; the outcome occurs whatever the animal does.

The CR is often similar to the UR, but not always. In *Aplysia*, a light touch of the mantle paired with a tail shock comes to elicit gill withdrawal, but the CR is weaker and shorter than the UR and never includes the release of ink that accompanies a strong shock (Carew, Hawkins and Kandel 1983). In rats, a CS that predicts an injection of morphine comes to elicit responses opposite to the drug's effects, such as increased sensitivity to pain; this compensatory CR contributes to drug tolerance, and rats tolerant to heroin are more likely to die of a large dose given in an unfamiliar environment, where the compensatory CR is absent, than of the same dose in the familiar one (Siegel et al. 1982). The general rule is that the CR prepares the animal for the US: salivation and insulin release before food, sexual arousal before a mating opportunity, protective responses before pain.

Several variants of the procedure reveal how timing matters. In *delay conditioning* the CS is still present when the US begins; in *trace conditioning* a gap separates the end of the CS from the US, and learning is weaker the longer the gap; *simultaneous* and *backward* pairing (US before CS) usually produce little excitatory conditioning. If the CS is then repeatedly presented without the US, the CR declines: *extinction*. Extinction is not forgetting but new learning that inhibits the old: the CR returns after a rest (spontaneous recovery), when the CS is presented outside the context in which extinction took place (renewal), or after an unsignaled US (reinstatement) (Bouton 2004). The return of extinguished fear is a central problem in the treatment of anxiety disorders ([7.16.37](/7.16.37#sec-37.2.3)).

### 41.3.2. Conditioning across the Animal Kingdom

No animal that has been tested thoroughly has failed to show classical conditioning. Some examples, chosen to show its natural functions:

- Mollusks: *Aplysia* (above); the pond snail *Lymnaea* learns in one trial to associate a touch of the lips with sucrose, and the CR (feeding movements) lasts for weeks.
- Insects: a honeybee restrained in a tube extends its proboscis when sugar water touches its antennae; after a few pairings of an odor with sugar, the odor alone elicits proboscis extension (Bitterman et al. 1983). This *proboscis extension response* is the main preparation for the neurobiology of insect learning, alongside olfactory conditioning in *Drosophila* ([7.16.38](/7.16.38#sec-38.4)). In the field, bees learn the color, odor and shape of a flower in one to three visits.
- Fish: male blue gouramis (*Trichopodus trichopterus*) that received a light signal 10 s before a rival appeared performed their aggressive display earlier and won more fights than males for which light and rival were unpaired (Hollis 1984). When the same signal predicted the arrival of a female, conditioned males courted rather than attacked her, spawned sooner and fathered many more young than unconditioned males (Hollis et al. 1997).
- Birds: male Japanese quail exposed to a CS that predicts access to a female approach the CS, copulate more quickly and release more sperm and seminal fluid than control males (Domjan, Blesbois and Williams 1998).
- Mammals: meerkats (*Suricata suricatta*) learn that the alarm calls of fork-tailed drongos (*Dicrurus adsimilis*), which follow foraging meerkats, predict predators, and flee at them. Drongos exploit this by giving false alarms when a meerkat has caught a large prey item and stealing it as the meerkat flees; when a drongo's false alarms become frequent, the meerkats' response extinguishes, and drongos then switch to mimicking the alarm calls of other species, including meerkats, which restores the response (Flower, Gribble and Ridley 2014).

These cases show why Karen Hollis (1997) argued that conditioning should be understood as a preparatory mechanism: a signal that predicts a predator, a rival, a mate or food lets the animal meet the event with its physiology and behavior already in place.

### 41.3.3. What Is Learned

What association does conditioning create? In one view, the CS becomes connected to the response (stimulus–response, S–R learning); in another, to a representation of the US (stimulus–stimulus, S–S learning), which then evokes the response. The two can be distinguished by changing the value of the US after conditioning. Holland and Rescorla (1975) conditioned rats with a tone followed by food; afterward, in the absence of the tone, the food was paired with high-speed rotation, which made it aversive. In a later test the tone elicited much less conditioned activity than in control rats, although the tone itself had never been paired with rotation. The tone had therefore become associated with a representation of the food, whose value had changed. In *sensory preconditioning*, two neutral stimuli are first presented together; when one of them is then paired with a US, the other also elicits the CR, again showing that associations form between stimuli without any response being reinforced. Both S–S and S–R associations exist, and their balance depends on the preparation; in higher-order conditioning, for example, the second-order CS is often associated with the response evoked by the first.

The form of the CR depends on the US and on the CS. Brown and Jenkins (1968) found that hungry pigeons begin to peck a key that is lit for a few seconds before free food, although pecking has no effect on the food: *autoshaping*. Pigeons peck the key with an open beak, as if eating, when the US is food, and with a closed beak and swallowing movements, as if drinking, when it is water (Jenkins and Moore 1973). A CS that is a localized object tends to elicit approach and contact (sign tracking), whereas a diffuse CS such as a tone elicits approach to the food site (goal tracking). The CS engages the behavior system of the US, its natural sequence of search and consumption, at the stage appropriate to its distance from the US in space and time (the behavior-systems view of William Timberlake).

### 41.3.4. Contingency, Prediction Error and Attention

Pavlov thought that conditioning depended on contiguity, the closeness of CS and US in time. Experiments of the 1960s showed that contiguity is not enough.

- *Contingency*. Robert Rescorla (1967) gave one group of rats tones that were followed by shocks and another group the same number of tones and shocks distributed independently of each other (the truly random control). The CS–US pairings that occurred by chance in the second group produced no conditioning: what matters is that the CS predicts the US, that the probability of the US is higher in its presence than in its absence.
- *Blocking*. Leon Kamin (1968) first conditioned rats to a noise followed by shock, then presented the noise together with a light, followed by the same shock. A test of the light alone showed almost no fear, whereas rats trained only with the compound feared the light.
- *Overshadowing*. When two stimuli are conditioned together from the start, the more salient one acquires most of the association, as Pavlov had found.
- *Conditioned inhibition*. If a stimulus X is presented with an excitatory CS A only on trials without the US (A+ and AX− trials), X acquires the ability to suppress the CR, shown by a reduced response when X is added to another excitatory CS (summation test) and by slow acquisition when X is later paired with the US (retardation test).

**Definition 41.3.1 (Blocking)** **Blocking** is the failure of a stimulus to acquire an association with a US when it is presented in compound with another stimulus that already predicts that US. **Overshadowing** is the reduced conditioning of a stimulus when it is trained in compound with a more salient stimulus, compared with conditioning of the same stimulus alone.

These results are explained by the **Rescorla–Wagner rule** ([Definition 39.1.1](/7.16.39#def-39.1.1) in 7.16.39): learning is driven by the prediction error, the difference between the US received and the US predicted by all stimuli present, and the stimuli present share that error. A blocked stimulus is not learned about because the US is already predicted. The rule also explains contingency if the experimental context is treated as a cue present on every trial: in the truly random group, the context comes to predict the shocks and the CS, which adds no further prediction, loses the strength it gained at first. Conditioned inhibitors acquire negative strength because they are present when a predicted US fails to occur. The prediction error of the rule has a neural counterpart in the firing of dopamine neurons ([7.16.39](/7.16.39#sec-39.3)), and its extension to time within a trial is **temporal-difference learning**.

Some results cannot be explained by error correction alone. In *latent inhibition* (Lubow and Moore 1959), a CS that has been presented many times without consequence is conditioned more slowly when it is later paired with a US. The Rescorla–Wagner rule predicts no effect of preexposure, because the prediction error is zero when nothing is predicted and nothing happens. Theories of attention explain latent inhibition by letting the associability of a stimulus, its rate of learning $\alpha$, change with experience. Mackintosh (1975) proposed that animals attend to stimuli that are good predictors; Pearce and Hall (1980) proposed the opposite, that attention goes to stimuli whose consequences are uncertain, so that a stimulus followed by surprising events keeps a high $\alpha$ and one followed reliably by nothing (or by a fully predicted US) loses it.

**Definition 41.3.2 (Latent Inhibition)** **Latent inhibition** is the retardation of conditioning to a stimulus that has previously been presented repeatedly without consequence.

**Formula 41.3.1 (Pearce–Hall Associability)** In a simple hybrid of the Rescorla–Wagner and Pearce–Hall models, the associative strength $V$ of a CS changes on each trial by $\Delta V = \kappa\,\alpha_n(\lambda - V)$, and its associability is updated as

$$
\alpha_{n+1} = \gamma\,\lvert\lambda_n - V_n\rvert + (1 - \gamma)\,\alpha_n,
$$
where $\lambda_n$ is the US received on trial $n$ ($0$ if none), $\kappa$ a learning rate and $\gamma \in (0, 1]$ the weight of the most recent trial.

Latent inhibition is adaptive for the same reason as habituation: a stimulus that has long predicted nothing is unlikely to start predicting something important. It is found in rats, goldfish, honeybees and humans, and it is reduced in some animal models of schizophrenia, in which irrelevant stimuli fail to lose their salience.

## 41.4. Operant Conditioning

### 41.4.1. The Law of Effect and Shaping

Edward Thorndike (1898) placed hungry cats in puzzle boxes that could be opened from the inside by pulling a loop or pressing a lever, with food outside. On the first trials a cat struggled, clawed and squeezed at random until it happened to operate the latch; over successive trials the time to escape fell gradually, with no sudden improvement that would suggest insight. Thorndike concluded that responses followed by a "satisfying state of affairs" are stamped in (the law of effect), the principle of **operant conditioning** ([7.16.38](/7.16.38#sec-38.2)), also called instrumental conditioning: the consequence of an action changes its future frequency. B. F. Skinner (1938) replaced the discrete trials of the puzzle box with the free-operant chamber, in which a rat presses a lever or a pigeon pecks a key at any time and responses are recorded automatically.

A consequence that increases the frequency of the response it follows is a reinforcer; one that decreases it is a punisher. Each can consist of adding a stimulus (positive) or removing one (negative), so that four contingencies result:

| | Behavior becomes more frequent | Behavior becomes less frequent |
|---|---|---|
| Stimulus added | Positive reinforcement: a woodpecker taps a soft spot and finds a grub | Positive punishment: a wolf pup that sniffs a porcupine is pierced by quills |
| Stimulus removed | Negative reinforcement: a nestling's submissive posture stops a sibling's pecking | Negative punishment: a squirrel that caches in sight of others loses its cache |

"Positive" and "negative" refer to adding or removing, not to pleasantness, and a consequence is a punisher only if the behavior actually becomes less frequent. A stimulus in whose presence a response is reinforced, such as a light that signals that lever presses now pay, is a *discriminative stimulus*; the three-term contingency of discriminative stimulus, response and consequence describes most trained behavior. Stimuli paired with a primary reinforcer such as food become conditioned (secondary) reinforcers: the click of the clicker used by animal trainers is a CS for food that can be delivered at the exact moment of the desired action.

Responses that an animal never performs cannot be reinforced, but they can be built by *shaping*: reinforcing successive approximations to the target. Skinner taught pigeons to play a form of table tennis, rewarding first a peck at a fixed ball, then at a ball in different positions, then at a rolling ball, and finally only pecks that sent the ball past an opponent (Skinner 1962). Modern training of guide dogs, rescue dogs, zoo animals for veterinary care and laboratory animals for cognitive tests relies on shaping and positive reinforcement. Operant principles also explain unwanted behavior in captive animals: an olive baboon in a zoo that bit itself and pulled out its hair was reinforced by the attention of well-meaning keepers; when the keepers ignored the self-injury and attended instead to a healthy alternative behavior (lip smacking), the self-injury almost disappeared, as shown by a reversal (ABAB) design (Dorey et al. 2009).

### 41.4.2. Schedules of Reinforcement

In nature, actions are rarely rewarded every time. Skinner and Charles Ferster (1957) studied how the rule linking responses to reinforcers controls the pattern of responding, which they recorded as cumulative records: the total number of responses plotted against time, so that the slope is the response rate.

**Definition 41.4.1 (Schedule of Reinforcement)** A **schedule of reinforcement** is the rule that determines which responses are reinforced. In a *ratio* schedule, reinforcement depends on the number of responses: after every $n$th response (fixed ratio, FR $n$) or after a number of responses that varies around a mean $n$ (variable ratio, VR $n$). In an *interval* schedule, the first response after a time interval has elapsed since the last reinforcer is reinforced: a fixed interval (FI $t$) or an interval that varies around a mean $t$ (variable interval, VI $t$). Reinforcement of every response is continuous reinforcement (FR 1).

Each schedule produces a characteristic pattern. Ratio schedules produce high rates, because the rate of reinforcement increases with the rate of responding; FR schedules produce a pause after each reinforcer followed by a rapid run, whereas VR schedules, in which any response may be reinforced, produce high steady rates (the schedule of gambling machines). Interval schedules produce lower rates, because responding faster does not bring the next reinforcer sooner: on FI schedules the rate accelerates through the interval in a "scallop", the basis of the study of timing ([7.18.43](/7.18.43)), and on VI schedules it is moderate and steady. Behavior reinforced intermittently is also more resistant to extinction than behavior reinforced continuously (the partial reinforcement extinction effect), because the absence of reward is less of a change from training.

When two schedules are available at once, as two keys each with its own VI schedule, animals distribute their responses in proportion to the reinforcers obtained from each, a regularity discovered by Richard Herrnstein (1961) with pigeons.

**Formula 41.4.1 (Matching Law)** On concurrent schedules with response rates $B_1, B_2$ and obtained reinforcement rates $R_1, R_2$, *strict matching* is

$$
\frac{B_1}{B_1 + B_2} = \frac{R_1}{R_1 + R_2},
$$
and the *generalized matching law* (Baum 1974) is $\log(B_1/B_2) = s\log(R_1/R_2) + \log b$, where $s$ is the sensitivity to the reinforcement ratio ($s < 1$: undermatching, typical values 0.8–0.9) and $b$ a bias toward one alternative.

Matching is the individual-level counterpart of the **ideal free distribution** of a group of foragers among patches ([7.18.25](/7.18.25)): mallards fed with bread at two points of a pond distribute themselves in proportion to the rates of supply (Harper 1982), and undermatching in individuals corresponds to the observed underuse of rich patches by groups. Matching is not always optimal (on concurrent VR schedules, exclusive choice of the better alternative maximizes reward, and animals usually achieve it), and whether matching results from optimization, from a local rule of moving to the currently better alternative (melioration), or from a learning rule of the reinforcement-learning type is still debated.

### 41.4.3. What Is Learned

Skinner (1948) gave hungry pigeons food every 15 s regardless of what they did and reported that each bird developed an idiosyncratic ritual, turning counterclockwise or tossing its head, as if the coincidence of an action with food had reinforced it ("superstition"). John Staddon and Virginia Simmelhag (1971) repeated the experiment with detailed records and found a different picture: early in each interval the birds performed varied *interim* activities, but as the time of food approached, all of them pecked near the magazine, a *terminal* response elicited by the expectation of food. Contiguity of action and reward is important (in many tasks a delay of a few seconds abolishes learning unless the response is marked by a signal), but animals also learn the contingency between actions and outcomes: free rewards delivered independently of responding reduce the response, as the truly random control reduces classical conditioning.

Does the animal learn that the action produces a particular outcome, or only to perform the action in the situation? Christopher Adams and Anthony Dickinson (1981) trained rats to press a lever for sugar pellets and then, outside the chamber, made the pellets aversive by pairing them with illness. Rats with limited training then pressed less, even in an extinction test without pellets, so their pressing was controlled by the current value of the outcome: it was **goal-directed behavior**. After extensive training, the pressing became insensitive to devaluation, a habit triggered by the situation. The two modes depend on different parts of the striatum and correspond to model-based and model-free reinforcement learning ([7.16.38](/7.16.38#sec-38.5), [7.16.40](/7.16.40)).

Operant learning is found in as many taxa as classical conditioning. Queen triggerfish learn to press a rod for food in about five rewards; *Aplysia* learns to perform a biting movement that is followed by stimulation of the esophageal nerve (Brembs et al. 2002); fruit flies learn to avoid one side of a chamber that is heated when they enter it, or to fly in a direction that keeps a heat beam off (Wolf and Heisenberg 1991). In nature, operant learning combines with classical conditioning: a blue jay that vomits after eating a monarch butterfly avoids monarchs afterward ([7.18.24](/7.18.24#sec-24.3.1)), the warning coloration acting as a discriminative stimulus, and it generalizes the avoidance to similar butterflies, the basis of Müllerian and Batesian mimicry.

## 41.5. Biological Constraints and Adaptive Specializations

### 41.5.1. Conditioned Taste Aversion

The behaviorist program assumed that any stimulus could be associated with any consequence equally well (equipotentiality). John Garcia and Robert Koelling (1966) tested this with "bright-noisy-tasty water": rats drank water flavored with saccharin while each lick also produced a flash of light and a click. One group was then made ill with X-rays or lithium chloride, another received electric shocks to the feet. In tests, the cues were offered separately. Rats made ill avoided the saccharin taste but not the bright-noisy water; rats shocked avoided the bright-noisy water but not the taste. The association depended on the combination of cue and consequence: tastes are readily associated with internal malaise, external audiovisual cues with pain on the skin.

**Definition 41.5.1 (Conditioned Taste Aversion)** **Conditioned taste aversion** (the Garcia effect) is the avoidance of a taste that has been followed by illness. It is typically acquired in a single pairing, with delays between taste and illness of up to several hours, is stronger for novel tastes, and is resistant to extinction.

![Selective association in conditioned taste aversion (schematic). A: the design and the shape of the result of Garcia and Koelling (1966). Rats drank water that was flavored with saccharin and produced a flash and a click at each lick; one group was then made ill, the other received foot shocks. In tests of each cue alone (bars: aversion, the reduction of drinking relative to baseline; illustrative values), illness had conditioned an aversion to the taste, shock to the audiovisual cue. B: the range of CS–US intervals over which conditioning occurs depends on the system (schematic curves on a logarithmic time axis): eyelid conditioning works best at about half a second, conditioned fear over seconds to about a minute, and taste aversion over minutes to several hours.](./plots/taste-aversion.py)

The long delay is as important as the selectivity: in most conditioning preparations a delay of a few seconds between CS and US abolishes learning, but a toxin takes time to act. The ecology of the rat explains both features. Norway rats are dietary generalists that live in a familiar area and encounter many new foods, some toxic; they cannot vomit. They sample a new food in a small amount and, if they later become ill, avoid it (Garcia et al. 1974). The comparison of bats supports this interpretation. Three insect-eating and fruit-eating bat species that eat diverse foods formed strong aversions to a novel flavor (cinnamon or citric acid) followed by an emetic injection, even with a delay; the common vampire bat (*Desmodus rotundus*), which feeds only on blood, did not, whether the injection was immediate or delayed (Ratcliffe et al. 2003). A specialist that has no toxic foods to learn about has lost, or never evolved, the specialized learning.

Taste aversion has practical uses. Patients receiving chemotherapy develop aversions to foods eaten before treatment, which can be redirected to a "scapegoat" flavor given just before it (Bernstein 1985). Northern quolls (*Dasyurus hallucatus*), Australian marsupial predators that die from eating the toxic invasive cane toad (*Rhinella marina*), were fed small toad sausages laced with a nausea-inducing drug before release into areas the toads were invading; the trained quolls avoided toads and survived longer than untrained ones (O'Donnell, Webb and Shine 2010). Rats exposed to a weak odor followed by illness acquire little aversion to the odor, but if the odor is presented together with a taste, the aversion to the odor becomes strong (taste-potentiated odor aversion; Rusiniak et al. 1979), the opposite of overshadowing. The enhanced learning of a color in the presence of the odor of pyrazine by chicks, which contradicts the Rescorla–Wagner rule ([7.18.24](/7.18.24#sec-24.3.2)), is a similar potentiation; both suggest that cues belonging to a natural category of signals (the taste of a food, the odor and color of a defended insect) are learned as a unit, and that warning signals evolved to exploit this feature of predators' learning.

### 41.5.2. Preparedness

Martin Seligman (1970) generalized these findings: for each species, associations can be placed on a continuum from *prepared* (learned in one or a few trials) through *unprepared* to *contraprepared* (learned with great difficulty or not at all).

**Definition 41.5.2 (Preparedness)** **Preparedness** is the degree to which an animal is predisposed by its evolutionary history to learn a particular association between a stimulus, a response and a consequence.

Many examples fit the concept. Laboratory-reared rhesus macaques that saw a video of another monkey reacting with fear to a toy snake acquired a lasting fear of snakes, but monkeys that saw the same fear reactions edited onto video of flowers acquired no fear of flowers (Cook and Mineka 1989, 1990). Honeybees readily learn colors that signal food, but not colors that signal the way out of a box, which explains an old dispute about whether bees see colors at all (Menzel and Erber 1978). Rats easily learn to avoid a shock by running or freezing, but slowly by pressing a lever; Robert Bolles (1970) argued that avoidance learning is easy only when the required response is one of the species' innate defense reactions (fleeing, freezing, fighting). A pigeon can be trained to peck, hop, swallow or fly for food, but no pigeon has ever been house-trained. Preparedness does not imply that the prepared association is innate: the monkeys learned their fear of snakes, but learning was channeled toward the stimuli that were dangerous in the evolutionary past.

### 41.5.3. Instinctive Drift and Autoshaping

Keller and Marian Breland, students of Skinner who founded a company that trained animals for advertisements and shows, described the "misbehavior of organisms" (1961). A raccoon trained to put a coin into a piggy bank for food learned the task with one coin, but with two coins it began to rub them together and dip them in and out of the bank for minutes, delaying its reward, a behavior never reinforced. Pigs trained to carry wooden tokens to a bank came to drop and root them along the way, more so as training continued. The intruding behaviors were the species' food-handling actions (raccoons rub and wash food items, pigs root), elicited by objects that had become signals of food.

**Definition 41.5.3 (Instinctive Drift)** **Instinctive drift** is the progressive intrusion of species-typical behavior related to the reinforcer into a trained operant response, even when the intruding behavior delays or prevents reinforcement.

Autoshaping (Section 41.3.3) makes the same point in the laboratory. If key pecks *cancel* the food that follows the lit key, pigeons continue to peck on a large proportion of trials, losing food they could otherwise have had (negative automaintenance; Williams and Williams 1969). Behavior that looks operant can be controlled by Pavlovian signals, and the two forms of conditioning interact in all natural learning. The modern synthesis is that learning uses general associative mechanisms, found throughout the animal kingdom, operating on inputs that each species selects and weighs according to its ecology, and producing responses drawn from its behavior systems. Whether some forms of learning go further, as distinct modules for particular problems, is the debate about adaptive specializations taken up for spatial memory in [7.18.42](/7.18.42).

### 41.5.4. Costs and Comparative Tests

The model of Section 41.1.2 assumes that learning is costly; experiments confirm it. Frédéric Mery and Tadeusz Kawecki (2002) selected lines of *Drosophila melanogaster* for the ability to learn to avoid an oviposition medium previously associated with the bitter taste of quinine; within about 15 generations the selected lines learned faster and remembered longer than control lines. Their larvae were poorer competitors for food than larvae of control lines (Mery and Kawecki 2003), and flies forced to form long-term memory by spaced training subsequently died sooner when deprived of food and water (Mery and Kawecki 2005). In guppies (*Poecilia reticulata*) artificially selected for larger brain size, the large-brained females learned a numerical discrimination task better, but they had smaller guts and fewer offspring (Kotrschal et al. 2013), the trade-off expected if brain tissue is expensive ([7.18.8](/7.18.8)).

If learning is an adaptation, related species should differ in what they learn according to the problems they face. Comparisons of this kind are informative only if they are matched for perception, motivation and the task's fit to each species' behavior, a difficulty that runs through the comparative study of cognition (Macphail 1987 argued that, once such "contextual variables" are controlled, no differences in general intelligence among vertebrates remain). Some clear cases:

- *Faces of paper wasps*. Females of the paper wasp *Polistes fuscatus* found colonies together and compete for dominance; their faces vary greatly among individuals, and they recognize one another. In a T-maze in which one arm was associated with a mild shock and the other with a picture of a conspecific face, *P. fuscatus* females learned the discrimination quickly, and they learned faces better than other patterns of the same complexity. Females of *P. metricus*, which found nests alone and have uniform faces, learned faces no better than chance (Sheehan and Tibbetts 2011). Face learning, a specialization once thought to be unique to mammals, evolved in a social insect.
- *Taste aversion in bats* (Section 41.5.1): generalists learn, the blood-feeding specialist does not.
- *Learned song*: western marsh wrens, which learn about 100 song types, have song-control nuclei about 25 % larger than eastern marsh wrens, which learn about 40 (Canady, Kroodsma and Nottebohm 1984).
- *Spatial memory* in food-storing birds and in species with sex differences in ranging behavior ([7.18.42](/7.18.42), [7.18.23](/7.18.23#sec-23.5)).

### 41.5.5. Neural Substrates of Different Forms of Learning

Different forms of learning use different brain systems, which is itself evidence that learning is not one process. Brenda Milner's studies of the patient H.M., whose medial temporal lobes had been removed in 1953, showed that he could not form new memories of facts and events, but he learned a mirror-drawing task at a normal rate over days while denying each time that he had done it before. Memory was thereafter divided into **declarative memory**, dependent on the hippocampus and medial temporal lobe, and nondeclarative memory, which includes skills and habits (striatum), priming (neocortex), classical conditioning of emotional responses (**amygdala**) and of skeletal responses (cerebellum), and habituation and sensitization (reflex pathways) ([7.16.42](/7.16.42), [7.14.46](/7.14.46#sec-46.5.2)). In all of these, short-term memory requires modification of existing proteins, and long-term memory requires gene expression and new synaptic growth, with long-term potentiation as a major mechanism of synaptic change ([7.16.38](/7.16.38#sec-38.3.3)). In insects, the mushroom bodies play a role in olfactory and visual learning comparable to that of the vertebrate pallium. The evolution of learning thus involved both conserved molecular machinery (cyclic AMP signaling, CREB) and the elaboration of circuits specialized for particular kinds of information.

### 41.6.1. Latent Learning and Learning Sets

Edward Tolman and Charles Honzik (1930) ran three groups of rats daily in a complex maze. One group always found food in the goal box, one never did, and one found food from the eleventh day on. The never-rewarded rats made many errors, but when food was introduced for the third group, its errors fell within one or two days to the level of the group rewarded from the start. The rats had learned the maze while exploring it without reward; the reward changed their performance, not their learning.

**Definition 41.6.1 (Latent Learning)** **Latent learning** is learning that occurs without apparent reinforcement and is not expressed in behavior until an incentive makes it useful.

Latent learning was a central argument of Tolman's case that rats acquire a **cognitive map** rather than a chain of reinforced responses ([7.16.46](/7.16.46#sec-46.1.1)); its ecological equivalent is the exploration by which animals learn the layout of their home range before they need it ([7.18.42](/7.18.42)). Harry Harlow (1949) gave rhesus monkeys hundreds of two-choice discrimination problems, each with a new pair of objects. Learning of each problem became faster, until after a few hundred problems the monkeys solved a new problem after a single trial, keeping a rewarded choice and switching after an unrewarded one. They had acquired a strategy that applies to the whole class of problems.

**Definition 41.6.2 (Learning Set)** A **learning set** is an improvement in the rate of learning across a series of problems of the same type, reflecting acquisition of a rule or strategy common to them ("learning to learn").

Learning sets were used for a time to rank species by intelligence, with primates first and rats and cats far behind; the ranking proved to depend heavily on the sensory modality of the problems (rats form learning sets rapidly with odors, Slotnick and Katz 1974), another warning about comparisons that ignore each species' perceptual world.

### 41.6.2. Play

Young mammals and birds chase, wrestle, leap and manipulate objects in ways that resemble serious behavior but have no immediate purpose. Gordon Burghardt (2005) proposed five criteria by which *play* can be recognized: the behavior is not fully functional in the context in which it appears; it is spontaneous, voluntary and apparently rewarding; it differs from the serious form (exaggerated, incomplete, or in an unusual sequence); it is repeated, but not stereotyped; and it occurs only when the animal is healthy, fed and relaxed. By these criteria play occurs in most mammals and in parrots and corvids, and also in some reptiles (turtles batting balls), fish (cichlids pushing a thermometer) and even bumblebees, which roll small balls without any reward (Dona et al. 2022).

Social play is often marked by signals that it is not serious, such as the play bow of dogs and the open-mouth "play face" of primates, and it involves self-handicapping and role reversal, a stronger animal letting a weaker one win. Its functions are uncertain. Hypotheses include training of motor skills (in mice, rats and cats, the period of play coincides with the period of synapse formation in the cerebellum; Byers and Walker 1995), development of social skills and bonds, and "training for the unexpected", practicing recovery from loss of control (Špinka, Newberry and Bekoff 2001). Rats deprived of play as juveniles show deficits in social behavior as adults, but direct evidence that play increases survival or reproduction is scarce, and play has costs: young fur seals and gazelles at play are more often killed by predators. Play therefore occupies an unusual place: a behavior clearly shaped by evolution, rich in learning opportunities, whose precise contribution to learning has not been demonstrated.

## Summary

- Learning (Definition 41.1.1) is a persistent change in behavior or behavioral potential produced by experience; performance can lag behind learning. Learning pays when the environment is predictable within a lifetime but not between generations (Formula 41.1.1), and it has costs.
- **Habituation**, the decline of response to a harmless repeated stimulus, is stimulus specific, shows dishabituation and spontaneous recovery, and is rate sensitive, which suggests several stages with different time constants. Sensitization increases responses after a strong stimulus.
- In **classical conditioning** the CR prepares the animal for the US (gouramis, quail, drug tolerance); extinction is new learning. What is learned is often a CS–US (S–S) association, shown by US devaluation; the response form depends on the US (autoshaping).
- Conditioning depends on contingency, not mere contiguity; blocking, overshadowing (Definition 41.3.1) and conditioned inhibition follow from the prediction error of the **Rescorla–Wagner rule**; latent inhibition (Definition 41.3.2) requires changes of attention (Pearce–Hall, Formula 41.3.1).
- In **operant conditioning**, consequences (reinforcement and punishment, positive and negative) change the frequency of actions; shaping builds new responses. Schedules of reinforcement (Definition 41.4.1) produce characteristic patterns; choice between concurrent schedules follows the matching law (Formula 41.4.1), the individual counterpart of the **ideal free distribution**. Devaluation separates **goal-directed behavior** from habits.
- Learning is biologically constrained: conditioned taste aversion (Definition 41.5.1) is selective, acquired in one trial over hours, and absent in the blood-feeding vampire bat; preparedness (Definition 41.5.2) channels learning (monkeys and snakes, bees and colors); instinctive drift (Definition 41.5.3) and negative automaintenance show Pavlovian control of operant behavior.
- Learning abilities evolve: selection improves learning in *Drosophila* at a cost; social paper wasps learn faces and solitary ones do not; different forms of learning use different brain systems (Section 41.5.5).
- Latent learning (Definition 41.6.1) and learning sets (Definition 41.6.2) show that animals learn structure without reinforcement; play is widespread, but its role in learning remains uncertain.

## Questions

1. How would you show that the decline of a response to a repeated stimulus is habituation rather than sensory adaptation or muscular fatigue?

   **Answer** Test stimulus specificity and dishabituation. If a different stimulus that uses the same effector still elicits the full response, the muscles are not fatigued; if a strong stimulus of another modality (or applied elsewhere on the body) immediately restores the response to the original stimulus, the receptors for that stimulus cannot be adapted, because the restoring stimulus does not act on them. Spontaneous recovery after rest and the faster rehabituation in later sessions (savings) are also characteristic of habituation. In *Aplysia*, recording from the sensory neurons shows that their spikes are unchanged while transmitter release at their synapses declines, locating habituation centrally.
2. In the model of Formula 41.1.1, why does a fixed behavior beat learning when $p$ is close to $\tfrac12$, even though it is right only half the time?

   **Answer** When the state changes at random from period to period, the previous state carries no information about the next one, so the learner's choice is also correct only half the time. Both strategies then have a mean payoff close to $\tfrac12$, but the learner pays the cost $c$. Learning can only exploit regularities that persist long enough for experience to predict the future.
3. Rats are first trained with a tone followed by shock until they fear the tone; then a light is added and the compound is followed by a stronger shock. Predict what the light acquires, using the Rescorla–Wagner rule.

   **Answer** In the second phase the US is larger than predicted, so $\lambda$ exceeds the strength of the tone and the prediction error $\lambda - V_{\mathrm{tone}}$ is positive. The light (and the tone) gain strength in proportion to this error, so the light acquires some excitatory strength: blocking is partial and "unblocking" occurs to the extent that the US is surprising. If the second-phase shock were the same as in the first, the light would acquire almost nothing (blocking); if it were weaker, the negative error would give the light inhibitory strength.
4. Why are ratio schedules associated with higher response rates than interval schedules with the same rate of reinforcement?

   **Answer** On a ratio schedule the rate of reinforcement is proportional to the rate of responding, so faster responding is directly rewarded. On an interval schedule the reinforcer becomes available at a time independent of responding, and once the animal responds often enough to collect it soon after it becomes available, further responses bring no extra reward; moreover, on VI schedules, long interresponse times are more likely to be reinforced, because the reinforcer has had more time to become available. Both features favor lower rates on interval schedules.
5. Explain the adaptive logic of conditioned taste aversion: why should the association be selective, tolerate long delays, and be stronger for novel foods?

   **Answer** Illness caused by food is a consequence of something eaten, not of a sight or sound encountered at the time, so associating it with tastes avoids spurious associations with the many external stimuli present. Toxins act after absorption, minutes to hours after eating, so a learning mechanism with a short delay window would miss the cause. Familiar foods that have been eaten safely are unlikely to be the cause of a new illness, whereas a novel food is a likely culprit; learning that is concentrated on novel tastes assigns the blame correctly. The absence of the mechanism in the vampire bat, which eats only blood, supports the argument.
6. (Open question) Design an experiment to test whether a bird species that caches food has a learning ability specialized for its ecology, and list the controls needed to interpret a difference from a non-caching relative.

   **Answer** Compare a caching species with a related non-caching species on two tasks: a spatial memory task (for example, remembering which of several locations held food after a delay, in a room-sized arena) and a non-spatial control task of matched difficulty (remembering the color of a stimulus), both trained by the same operant procedure. An adaptive specialization predicts a species difference in the spatial task but not in the control task (an interaction of species and task). Controls: equal motivation (hunger level, preference for the reward), perceptual ability to discriminate the stimuli, familiarity with the apparatus and the experimenter, and the use of several populations or species pairs, so that the difference is not due to one arbitrary pair; the analysis should take phylogeny into account. A difference in performance on one task alone could reflect motivation, attention or perception rather than learning.
