Whenever an animal's behavior now reflects an experience in the past, memory is involved. Every form of learning of [7.18.41](/7.18.41) implies memory, and so does every case of homing and navigation in [7.18.21](/7.18.21). This chapter treats memory as an object of study in its own right: how long animals remember, how much, what interferes with remembering, and whether some animals remember particular past episodes as we do. Spatial memory receives most attention because it is the best case for comparing species: animals differ greatly in the spatial problems they face, and food-storing birds, whose ecology and hippocampus were introduced in [7.18.23](/7.18.23#sec-23.5), have made it possible to ask whether memory is specialized by natural selection. The cellular basis of memory and the hippocampal representation of space are in [7.16.42](/7.16.42), [7.16.44](/7.16.44) and [7.16.46](/7.16.46), and working memory as a property of neural circuits in [7.16.45](/7.16.45); here the emphasis is on behavior and comparison.

## 42.1. Studying Memory in Animals

### 42.1.1. Memory in Natural Problems

Many foraging problems require memory. The amakihi (*Chlorodrepanis virens*), a Hawaiian honeycreeper, feeds on the nectar of mamane flowers, which take about an hour to refill; birds avoid flower clusters that they have recently emptied (Kamil 1978). Rufous hummingbirds remember which artificial flowers they have emptied and when each will be refilled ([7.18.43](/7.18.43)). A nutcracker that stores pine seeds in autumn must find them months later, and a female cowbird that parasitizes nests must remember where hosts are building and return when they have begun to lay. Memory also supports social life: elephants recognize the contact calls of about a hundred other individuals, and in families led by older matriarchs the group distinguishes the calls of familiar and unfamiliar elephants better (McComb et al. 2001).

The duration of memory in simple conditioning depends on the system. In eyelid conditioning the US must follow the CS within about a second, in taste aversion it can follow several hours later ([7.18.41](/7.18.41#sec-41.5.1)). In operant learning, a delay of a few seconds between response and reward prevents learning, apparently because the animal does other things in the meantime and cannot identify which action produced the reward: marking the correct response with a brief light or tone allows rats to learn with much longer delays (Lieberman, McIntosh and Thomas 1979).

### 42.1.2. Methods

Three procedures, each usable with many species, dominate laboratory studies of memory.

**Definition 42.1.1 (Delayed Matching to Sample)** In **delayed matching to sample** (DMTS), a sample stimulus is presented and removed, and after a retention interval the subject must choose, among two or more comparison stimuli, the one identical to the sample. In *delayed non-matching to sample* the non-matching stimulus is correct. Accuracy as a function of the retention interval measures the persistence of memory for the sample.

In the standard version for pigeons, the center one of three keys is lit white; a few pecks turn it to the sample color (red, say); after more pecks it goes dark for the retention interval; then the side keys light red and green in random positions, and a peck at the red key is rewarded. The same logic, with pictures on touch screens, sounds or objects, is used with monkeys, dolphins, bees and humans.

![Delayed matching to sample. A: one trial on a three-key panel for pigeons: a white ready signal on the center key; the sample (red), which the bird must peck; a dark retention interval of duration $d$; the two comparison stimuli on the side keys (position randomized), of which a peck at the one matching the sample is rewarded. B: forgetting in DMTS (illustrative curves of the shape found by Grant 1975): accuracy falls toward chance (50 %) within seconds, and faster when trials follow each other closely (intertrial interval, ITI, 4 s rather than 20 s), because the sample of the previous trial interferes with the current one. The curves are $0.5 + 0.45\,\mathrm{e}^{-d/\tau}$ with $\tau = 3\,\mathrm{s}$ and $9\,\mathrm{s}$.](./plots/dmts.py)

The *radial-arm maze* (Olton and Samuelson 1976) has eight or more arms radiating from a central platform, each baited once at the end; the animal must collect all the food without re-entering an emptied arm, which requires remembering which arms it has visited. In the *Morris water maze*, a rat swims in a pool of opaque water to find a submerged platform located only by distant cues in the room ([7.16.42](/7.16.42#sec-42.3.1)). Field tests use the animals' own behavior: caches made by birds in aviaries, food hidden by an experimenter, or the natural sequence of visits to flowers.

## 42.2. Short-Term Memory

Short-term memory is a store of limited capacity that holds information for seconds to minutes; in humans and other primates it is usually studied as **working memory**, the active maintenance and manipulation of information in the service of a current task ([7.16.45](/7.16.45#sec-45.1)). In animals, the distinction between short-term and long-term memory is operational: it refers to what the animal remembers within a trial, as opposed to what it retains from day to day.

### 42.2.1. Duration and Interference

In DMTS with colors, untrained pigeons forget the sample within a few seconds; with extensive training (17 000 trials in one study), they remember it for up to a minute (Grant 1976), and monkeys and dolphins for a few minutes. Memory in the radial maze lasts much longer. Rats removed from an eight-arm maze after four choices and returned after a delay completed the maze almost without error after delays of up to four hours, with errors becoming frequent only after eight hours or more (Beatty and Shavalia 1980); pigeons collecting food from eight sites in a room remembered which they had emptied for about an hour (Spetch and Honig 1988). The difference shows that the "duration of short-term memory" is not a fixed property of a species but depends on the material and the task, a caution for all comparisons.

Much forgetting is due to interference rather than decay. *Proactive interference* is the disruption of memory by earlier information: in DMTS, accuracy is lower when the intertrial interval is short, because the sample of the previous trial is confused with the current one, and rats run several times a day in the same radial maze make more errors in later runs. *Retroactive interference* is disruption by later information: turning on the house light during the retention interval impairs matching in pigeons, monkeys and dolphins (Grant 1976). Memory is also partly under the animal's control. In *directed forgetting* (Maki and Hegvik 1980), each sample is followed by a "remember" cue, after which comparison stimuli appear, or a "forget" cue, after which normally no test follows; on occasional probe trials with a test after a forget cue, pigeons perform worse, as if they had stopped maintaining the sample. Whether this reflects active forgetting or merely the absence of rehearsal is debated; designs in which the forget cue signals that another, different test will follow (so that the bird remains engaged) still show the effect (Kaiser, Sherburne and Zentall 1997).

### 42.2.2. Capacity and Coding Strategies

Rats rarely re-enter an arm in an eight-arm maze. Controls show that they do not follow a fixed sequence of turns, do not mark visited arms by odor, and use distant cues in the room: if the maze is rotated after the first choices, they avoid arms in the places they have visited, not the physical arms. Accuracy falls with more arms, but remains above chance with 17 and even 24 arms, where rats start to adopt simplifying strategies such as entering adjacent arms in turn.

![Working memory in the radial-arm maze. A: an eight-arm maze seen from above, with five arms still baited (yellow food wells) and three already visited (empty wells, tinted arms). B: the number of different arms entered in the first eight choices. For a rat choosing arms at random with replacement, the exact distribution (computed by recursion on the number of arms already entered) has mean $8\bigl(1 - (7/8)^8\bigr) = 5.25$; trained rats (illustrative typical values) enter seven or eight different arms. C: predictions for the interruption experiment of Cook, Brown and Riley (1985) in a 12-arm maze: rats removed after their $k$th choice and returned after a delay. If the rat remembers the arms already visited (retrospective coding), its memory load during the delay, and hence its errors after it, should increase with $k$; if it remembers the arms still to visit (prospective coding), they should decrease; if it switches from one code to the other halfway, they should peak at $k = 6$, as observed.](./plots/radial-maze.py)

The interruption experiment of Robert Cook, Michael Brown and Donald Riley (1985) revealed a flexible coding strategy. If rats remembered only where they had been, interrupting them after ten choices in a 12-arm maze should be the most disruptive; if they remembered only where they still had to go, interrupting after two choices should. Errors after the delay were in fact greatest for interruptions after six choices and smaller for earlier and later ones, as expected if the rats remembered visited arms early in a trial and switched to remembering unvisited arms later, keeping the load below about six items.

Comparisons of capacity across species are difficult because performance depends on training. Young chimpanzees trained to touch numerals in ascending order after they had been masked outperformed university students when the numerals were displayed for only a fraction of a second (Inoue and Matsuzawa 2007); with comparable practice, however, humans reached similar levels (Silberberg and Kearns 2009, Cook and Wilson 2010). In primates, as in humans, the capacity of visual working memory appears to be a few items.

### 42.2.3. Serial Position Effects

When people recall a list, they remember the first items (primacy) and the last items (recency) better than those in the middle.

**Definition 42.2.1 (Serial Position Effect)** The **serial position effect** is the dependence of memory for an item in a list on its position: better retention of the first items (*primacy*) and of the last items (*recency*) than of items in the middle.

Recency is found in many species tested with lists: dolphins recognized the last sounds of a list of six better than earlier ones (Thompson and Herman 1977), and rhesus monkeys shown sequences of pictures judged whether a probe picture had been in the list with almost perfect accuracy for the last item, less for the first and least for the middle ones (Castro and Larsen 1992). Anthony Wright and colleagues (1985) trained pigeons, rhesus monkeys and humans on the same list-memory task with four pictures and varied the delay before the probe. In all three species, recency dominated at short delays and primacy at long delays, with a U-shaped curve at intermediate delays; the time scale differed (seconds for pigeons, tens of seconds for monkeys, up to a minute or more for people). Rats in a radial maze showed the same change with delay, recency at delays up to a few minutes and primacy at delays of 16 minutes or more (Bolhuis and van Kampen 1988).

![Serial position effects in list memory and their change with the retention interval (illustrative model of the pattern found by Wright et al. 1985 in pigeons, monkeys and humans). A list of four items is followed after a delay $d$ by a probe. Accuracy at position $i$ is $0.5 + 0.45\max(R, F)$, with a recency component $R = \exp\bigl(-0.7(4 - i) - d/\tau_R\bigr)$ that decays with the delay and a primacy component $F = 0.85\bigl(1 - \mathrm{e}^{-d/\tau_F}\bigr)\mathrm{e}^{-0.9(i - 1)}$ that develops with it ($\tau_R = 3\,\mathrm{s}$, $\tau_F = 2\,\mathrm{s}$, pigeon time scale). Immediately after the list only the last items are remembered well; after a long delay the first ones are.](./plots/serial-position.py)

The two effects have different explanations. Recency reflects the persistence of the last items in short-term memory; primacy is usually attributed to the transfer of the first items to a longer-lasting store, or to their freedom from proactive interference, since no items precede them. That the balance shifts with the delay in a similar way in birds, rodents, monkeys and humans suggests that these are general properties of memory, not consequences of verbal rehearsal. A computational account of primacy and recency in working memory is in [7.16.45](/7.16.45#sec-45.5.3).

## 42.3. Long-Term Memory

### 42.3.1. Capacity and Duration

Animals can store large amounts of arbitrary information. Pigeons trained to discriminate 160 photographs (80 positive and 80 negative, assigned arbitrarily) remembered the discrimination after two years without testing (Vaughan and Greene 1984). In a study lasting three to five years, pigeons and baboons were trained on ever-increasing sets of pictures, each associated arbitrarily with a left or right response; the pigeons learned between about 800 and 1200 associations before their performance stopped improving, the baboons between 3500 and 5000 and were still learning when the study ended (Fagot and Cook 2006). Pigeons that had learned which of eight cups in an aviary hid food remembered it after ten months (Balda and Wiltschko 1995), and Clark's nutcrackers retrieve caches after six months or more in the laboratory ([7.18.23](/7.18.23#sec-23.5.1)).

Memories can survive large changes of the brain. Larvae of the tobacco hornworm moth (*Manduca sexta*) conditioned in their last larval stage to avoid an odor paired with shock still avoided it as adult moths, after the metamorphosis in which much of the nervous system is rebuilt; conditioning in earlier larval stages was retained to the end of larval life but not into the adult (Blackiston, Silva Casey and Weiss 2008).

### 42.3.2. Metamemory

Does an animal know what it remembers? Robert Hampton (2001) gave rhesus monkeys a DMTS task in which, after the sample had disappeared, they could either take the test, with a large reward for a correct choice and none for an error, or decline it and receive a small guaranteed reward. The monkeys declined more often after long delays, and on trials in which they had to take the test, their accuracy was lower than on trials in which they had chosen to take it, so their decisions to decline were based on how well they remembered on that particular trial. On occasional trials without any sample, they declined almost always. This *metamemory*, monitoring of one's own memory, has been found in monkeys, apes and dolphins; pigeons tested in similar ways have generally failed the critical tests (Sutton and Shettleworth 2008). Whether such results require awareness of memory or can be explained by learned responses to internal cues correlated with the strength of memory is debated, and illustrates the use of **Morgan's canon** ([7.18.1](/7.18.1)).

## 42.4. Spatial Memory in Food-Storing Birds

### 42.4.1. How Caches Are Found

The ecology of caching and the first proof that cachers remember their sites, the eye-patch experiment of Sherry, Krebs and Cowie (1981), were described in [7.18.23](/7.18.23#sec-23.5.1). Further experiments analyzed what is remembered.

- *Precision*. Marsh tits given mildly radioactive sunflower seeds stored them in the field. The experimenters located the caches with a Geiger counter and placed control seeds 10 cm or 1 m away; over the next days the birds took their own seeds much more often than the nearby controls (Cowie, Krebs and Sherry 1981).
- *Own caches*. Two Clark's nutcrackers allowed to cache separately in the same aviary each recovered its own caches and not the other's (Vander Wall 1982). Nutcrackers forced to cache in holes chosen by the experimenter still found their seeds 10 to 15 days later, so they did not rely on a preference for particular kinds of site (Kamil and Balda 1985).
- *Landmarks*. When Vander Wall shifted all the large objects (logs, rocks) on one side of an aviary by 20 cm after the nutcrackers had cached, they searched 20 cm from their caches on that side, in the direction of the shift, but accurately on the undisturbed side: caches were located by their relation to nearby landmarks. Black-capped chickadees caching in four identical artificial trees relied on large cues on the walls of the room more than on small cues next to each site, and when the room cues were rotated, they searched at correspondingly rotated sites (Sherry 1984, Herz et al. 1994).
- *Content*. Nutcrackers open their bills wider when probing for caches of larger seeds (Moller et al. 2001), and scrub jays remember what they cached, as shown below.

### 42.4.2. The Adaptive Specialization Hypothesis

Food-storing species, which depend on memory for thousands of locations, were the test case for an evolutionary prediction: natural selection should improve the cognitive abilities that a species' ecology requires, and only those.

**Definition 42.4.1 (Adaptive Specialization Hypothesis)** The **adaptive specialization hypothesis** states that cognitive abilities, and the brain structures that support them, are shaped by natural selection according to the ecological problems a species (or sex, or population) faces, so that species that depend more on a particular kind of learning or memory perform better in it, but not necessarily in other cognitive tasks.

The evidence comes from four kinds of comparison.

- *Between species*. Among four North American corvids, Clark's nutcrackers and pinyon jays, which store tens of thousands of seeds and have special pouches or expandable esophagi for carrying them, performed better in a spatial DMTS task on a touch screen than Mexican jays and scrub jays, which store less; in a color version of the same task, the order was different and the nutcracker did not excel (Olson et al. 1995). Storing parids and corvids have a larger hippocampus relative to the forebrain than non-storing relatives ([7.18.23](/7.18.23#sec-23.5.2)).
- *Between populations*. Black-capped chickadees from Alaska, where winters are long and food is scarce, cached more, retrieved caches more accurately in the laboratory and solved spatial problems faster than birds from Colorado, and had larger hippocampi with more neurons (Pravosudov and Clayton 2002). Mountain chickadees from high elevations, with harsher winters, learned the locations of rewarding RFID-controlled feeders, but made more errors than birds from low elevations when the rewarded location was reversed (Croston et al. 2017), suggesting a trade-off between persistent spatial memory and cognitive flexibility; in the same populations, spatial learning performance predicted survival over the first winter (Sonnenberg et al. 2019) and is partly heritable (Branch et al. 2022).
- *Between the sexes*. In polygynous meadow voles (*Microtus pennsylvanicus*), males range over areas several times larger than females and make fewer errors in mazes; in monogamous prairie voles (*M. ochrogaster*), in which the sexes share a range, they perform equally (Gaulin and FitzGerald 1989). In the brood-parasitic brown-headed cowbird, only females search for host nests and remember their locations for days; females have a larger hippocampus than males, whereas in non-parasitic relatives, red-winged blackbirds and common grackles, there is no sex difference (Sherry et al. 1993), and female cowbirds outperform males in a spatial memory task (Guigueno et al. 2014). In pinyon jays, males, which retrieve caches to feed incubating females and young, made fewer errors than females after retention intervals of two to four months (Dunlap et al. 2006).
- *Within individuals*. Hippocampal neurogenesis in chickadees and the growth of the hippocampus in young tits depend on the experience of storing ([7.18.23](/7.18.23#sec-23.5.2)).

The hypothesis has been criticized on two grounds (Bolhuis and Macphail 2001). The behavioral differences between storers and non-storers are often small, inconsistent across tasks, or explicable by differences in attention, motivation and strategy rather than memory capacity: storers rely more on spatial than on featural cues (Clayton and Krebs 1994), which may reflect a difference in what they attend to rather than how well they remember. Second, some phylogenetic comparisons of hippocampal volume have not been replicated with larger samples and different methods. The defense is that cognitive specialization need not mean a superior general capacity, but a different weighting of information, and that the correlated variation within species (populations, sexes, seasons) is hard to explain otherwise. The debate has made comparisons more rigorous, with matched tasks, several species pairs and phylogenetic methods, and the most convincing current evidence comes from studies of individual variation in wild populations, where cognition, survival and heritability can be measured together.

## 42.5. Episodic-Like Memory

### 42.5.1. What, Where and When

Endel Tulving (1972) distinguished *episodic memory*, the memory of personally experienced events in their context of place and time, from *semantic memory*, knowledge of facts; both are forms of **declarative memory** ([7.16.42](/7.16.42#sec-42.1.1)). Tulving later added that episodic recollection involves a conscious re-experiencing of the event, which cannot be tested in animals without language. Nicola Clayton and Anthony Dickinson (1998) proposed behavioral criteria instead: remembering what happened, where and when, in an integrated way that can be used flexibly.

**Definition 42.5.1 (Episodic-Like Memory)** **Episodic-like memory** is memory of a specific past event that integrates its content (what), its location (where) and its time (when or how long ago), retrieved as a single representation and used flexibly to guide behavior; it is the behavioral counterpart in animals of human episodic memory, without the criterion of conscious recollection.

Western scrub jays (*Aphelocoma californica*) cache many kinds of food, some of which decay quickly. Clayton and Dickinson let jays cache peanuts in one side of a tray of sand and wax-moth larvae ("wax worms", a preferred food) in the other side, on two occasions separated by 120 h, and allowed them to search the tray 4 h after the second caching episode. Jays that had learned in training that worms became inedible after 124 h but were fresh after 4 h searched first where they had cached worms if the worms had been cached 4 h earlier, but where they had cached peanuts if the worms had been cached 124 h earlier. Jays for which worms had always been fresh searched for worms in both cases. All food had been removed before the tests, so no cues from the food could guide the search: the jays remembered which food was where and how long ago it had been cached, and combined this with what they had learned about the decay of each food.

![Episodic-like memory in western scrub jays: the design and the shape of the result of Clayton and Dickinson (1998). A: on two occasions a jay cached peanuts in one side and wax worms (its preferred food) in the other side of a sand-filled tray with distinctive landmarks; after 4 h or 124 h it could search the tray, from which all food had been removed. Later experiments extended the result. Jays that learned after caching that worms decayed faster than they had assumed adjusted their search accordingly, which shows that the "when" information is used flexibly, not as a learned rule tied to the time of caching (Clayton, Yu and Dickinson 2003). Similar what–where–when memory has been reported in black-capped chickadees, magpies, rats, meadow voles, bees and cuttlefish. In rats, memory for several episodes can be replayed in the order in which they occurred, and the replay depends on the hippocampus (Panoz-Brown et al. 2018). Dogs trained to imitate their owner on the command "do it" reproduced an action demonstrated a minute or an hour earlier even when, in a preceding phase, they had learned to expect a different command after demonstrations, so that they had no reason to encode the action for later imitation; memory formed incidentally, without anticipation of a test, is a further criterion of episodic memory (Fugazza, Pogány and Miklósi 2016).

The hippocampal "barcodes" recorded in chickadees caching seeds, sparse population patterns specific to each caching event and reactivated at retrieval ([7.18.23](/7.18.23#sec-23.5.3)), are a candidate neural mechanism for this kind of memory, in which a representation of an event is linked to, but separate from, the map of places.

### 42.5.2. Planning for the Future

If episodic memory allows a person to re-experience the past, it may also allow imagining the future ("mental time travel", Suddendorf and Corballis 1997). Caching is behavior for the future, but it could be a fixed response triggered by present conditions. Caroline Raby and colleagues (2007) tested scrub jays in an apparatus with three compartments. On alternate mornings, the jays were confined either to a compartment in which they always received breakfast or to one in which they never did. When later given food in the evening, with access to both compartments, the jays cached more in the compartment in which they had experienced no breakfast. In a second experiment, the jays received peanuts for breakfast in one compartment and dog kibble in the other; in the evening they cached kibble preferentially in the "peanut" compartment and peanuts in the "kibble" compartment, providing variety for the morning. The jays were not hungry when they cached, so they were acting for a motivational state they did not currently have, which had been proposed as a hallmark of human planning (the Bischof-Köhler hypothesis). Jays sated on one food cache mainly another, but when they had learned that they would be sated on that other food at the time of recovery, they switched to caching the food they would then want (Correia, Dickinson and Clayton 2007). Similar evidence of planning has been reported for great apes, which select and keep a tool needed later in another room (Mulcahy and Call 2006), and for ravens. Critics have argued that some of these results can be explained by associative learning about places and foods; the debate parallels that about episodic memory itself. Caching birds also take account of what others have seen of their caches ([7.18.45](/7.18.45)).

## 42.6. Spatial Cognition

### 42.6.1. Frames of Reference

An animal can specify a location in two kinds of frame. In an *egocentric* frame, positions are defined relative to its own body ("turn left after the tree"); in an *allocentric* frame, relative to external features, independent of the animal's position (the nest is between the boulder and the stream). The two support different mechanisms:

- **Path integration** (dead reckoning; [7.18.21](/7.18.21#sec-21.6.1), [7.16.44](/7.16.44#sec-44.4.1)): the continuous updating of the vector to a starting point from self-motion signals. It works without landmarks and accumulates errors with distance. A ferret that had gone blind could still run straight to its cage in a familiar room; desert ants return to their nest along the integrated vector even when displaced to unfamiliar ground.
- *Route following*: a learned sequence of movements or of responses at successive landmarks. Lorenz's water shrews ran along fixed trails and jumped over stones that had been removed; when they landed unexpectedly flat, they stopped and explored as if in unfamiliar terrain.
- *Beacon homing*: approaching a stimulus at or next to the goal, the simplest use of a cue, which can be acquired by classical conditioning.
- *Landmark use*: locating a goal from its distance and direction relative to one or more landmarks that are not at the goal.

### 42.6.2. Landmarks

Tinbergen's digger wasps searched for their nest in the center of a displaced ring of pine cones ([7.18.21](/7.18.21#sec-21.6.2)). How the position of a goal is computed from several landmarks was analyzed in gerbils by Thomas Collett, Bruce Cartwright and Beverley Smith (1986). Gerbils trained to find a sunflower seed at a fixed distance and direction from a single landmark searched at the correct position relative to the landmark when it was moved, so they had learned a vector from the landmark to the goal, which requires knowing directions as well as distances (in this case from cues outside the arena). Trained with two identical landmarks, with the seed between and to one side of them, they searched in the right place; when the landmarks were moved to twice their original separation, they searched in two places, each at the correct vector from one of the landmarks, not in a single compromise position. Each landmark had been associated with its own vector to the goal. Pigeons, black-capped chickadees and Clark's nutcrackers, by contrast, often average the positions indicated by different landmarks, weighting each by its reliability and distance to the goal (Cheng 1989; Cheng and Sherry 1992; Gould-Beierle and Kamil 1996), much as in the optimal combination of cues.

### 42.6.3. The Geometry of Space

Ken Cheng (1986) trained rats to find food in one corner of a rectangular box with distinctive panels (differing in pattern, odor and brightness) in each corner. When the rats were disoriented before each trial, they chose the correct corner and the diagonally opposite corner, which has the same geometric relation to the walls (a long wall on the left, a short wall on the right), about equally often, although the panels should have distinguished them. Rats could learn to use the panels, but they relied on the shape of the space first. Cheng proposed a *geometric module*, an encapsulated system that reorients the animal by the shape of its surroundings and ignores non-geometric features. Young children disoriented in a rectangular room make the same rotational errors and begin to use a colored wall at about the age at which they acquire spatial language (Hermer and Spelke 1994); chicks, fish, pigeons and monkeys use geometry too.

Later work qualified the modular interpretation. Many species, including disoriented fish and chicks, use features as well as geometry when the features are large or close to the goal, and their reliance on each depends on the size of the space and on experience (Cheng and Newcombe 2005). Ants walking in a rectangular arena also make rotational errors, and so does a model that simply steers to match the panoramic view of the goal stored during training (Wystrach et al. 2011): the visual scene in a rectangular box is similar from diagonally opposite corners. Geometric reorientation may therefore emerge from view matching rather than from an explicit representation of shape. The relation between behavioral geometry and the boundary-vector and border cells of the mammalian hippocampal formation is an active field.

### 42.6.4. Cognitive Maps, Shortcuts and Detours

Edward Tolman (1948) proposed that rats acquire a **cognitive map**, a representation of the spatial relations among places that allows novel routes ([7.16.46](/7.16.46#sec-46.1.1)). His evidence included latent learning ([7.18.41](/7.18.41#sec-41.6.1)) and a "sunburst" maze (Tolman, Ritchie and Kalish 1946) in which rats trained on a roundabout route chose, when it was blocked, a new radiating path pointing toward the goal. But a light hung over the goal box was visible from the whole maze, so the rats may simply have headed for a beacon; replications without it have generally failed. The same problem affects many later claims: a shortcut is evidence of a map only if beacons, landmarks visible from both places, path integration and familiar views can be excluded. Some studies come closer. Dogs led on a leash from a starting point to food at A and back, and then to food at B and back, ran directly from A to B in most trials, a route they had never taken (Chapuis and Varlet 1987), but the food positions may have been visible from each other. Red squirrels displaced along an L-shaped route of about 700 m failed to orient home (Bovet 1995). Honeybees and their contested map-like memory were discussed in [7.18.21](/7.18.21#sec-21.7).

The strongest evidence for maps now comes from neurophysiology combined with tracking in the wild. Rats and mice have **place cells**, grid cells and head-direction cells whose combined activity encodes position and supports planning by sequential "replay" of possible routes ([7.16.42](/7.16.42#sec-42.4), [7.16.46](/7.16.46#sec-46.6)). Egyptian fruit bats (*Rousettus aegyptiacus*) have three-dimensional place cells (Yartsev and Ulanovsky 2013), and wild bats tracked with high-resolution GPS flew straight to their foraging trees from a cave and, when translocated within their familiar range, homed along novel straight routes, whereas young bats raised in captivity and released elsewhere did not (Toledo et al. 2020). Such results satisfy the criterion of novel shortcuts in a familiar area, and they are consistent with a map-like memory, even if it is not a metric map in the cartographer's sense.

Detour tasks show that the expression of spatial knowledge depends on motivation and perception. Wolfgang Köhler (1925) found that a dog went readily around a fence to food behind it, but not when the food was close to the fence, and that hens kept pushing against the fence. Cats and dogs presented with a short detour that led them away from the goal and a longer one that kept them closer to it chose the short route when the barrier was opaque, but chose about equally when it was transparent (Poucet, Thinus-Blanc and Chapuis 1983): seeing the goal makes moving away from it hard. Two-day-old chicks detour around opaque barriers more readily than transparent ones (Regolin, Vallortigara and Zanforlin 1995). Failures on such tasks therefore do not show the absence of spatial knowledge.

## 42.7. Comparing Memory across Species

Memory exists in every animal that learns, from *Stentor* to elephants, and many of its properties are general: forgetting curves, interference, serial position effects and the dependence of long-term memory on protein synthesis are found in birds, rodents, primates and, where tested, insects. At the same time, species differ in the content, capacity and organization of memory in ways that fit their ecology: nutcrackers and chickadees in spatial memory, scrub jays in integrated memory for the content and age of caches, cowbird females and meadow vole males in spatial learning. Two lessons recur. First, differences in performance on a task are not differences in memory until perception, attention, motivation and strategy have been controlled, and closely related species often differ less than ecology would suggest, perhaps because non-storing species have memory demands of their own that we do not know. Second, specialization usually concerns what information is weighted and how long it is kept, not an all-purpose capacity. Shettleworth's (2010) summary is that cognition consists of a set of modules, for space, time, number and social relations, built from shared mechanisms and adjusted by each species' history.

## Summary

- Memory is studied with delayed matching to sample (Definition 42.1.1), radial and water mazes and natural tasks such as cache recovery. Its duration depends on the task: seconds for colors in DMTS, hours for visited arms in the radial maze, months for caches and pictures.
- Short-term memory is limited and vulnerable to proactive and retroactive interference; pigeons show directed forgetting; rats switch from retrospective to prospective coding in large radial mazes. The serial position effect (Definition 42.2.1) shifts from recency to primacy with longer delays in pigeons, monkeys, rats and humans.
- Long-term memory is large (pigeons: about a thousand picture associations; baboons: several thousand) and persistent (memories can survive metamorphosis). Monkeys monitor their own memory (metamemory); pigeons generally do not.
- Food-storing birds find caches by memory of their position relative to landmarks, with precision of centimeters. The adaptive specialization hypothesis (Definition 42.4.1) is supported by comparisons between corvid species, chickadee populations and the sexes of voles, cowbirds and pinyon jays, and criticized because many differences may reflect attention and strategy.
- Scrub jays remember what they cached, where and how long ago (episodic-like memory, Definition 42.5.1), update this knowledge flexibly, and cache for future needs; similar abilities are reported in rats, dogs and other species, without settling whether they involve conscious recollection.
- Animals locate places by **path integration**, routes, beacons and landmark vectors (gerbils store one vector per landmark; birds average landmarks), and reorient by the geometry of their surroundings, possibly through view matching. Whether animals have a **cognitive map** depends on excluding simpler mechanisms; tracking of wild bats and hippocampal recordings provide the strongest current evidence.

## Questions

1. Why does the duration of short-term memory measured in pigeons differ so much between DMTS with colors and the search for emptied food sites in a room? What does this imply for comparisons among species?

   **Answer** The two tasks differ in the material (an arbitrary color versus places, which pigeons process in an ecologically relevant way), in interference (in DMTS every trial uses the same few colors, so earlier samples interfere strongly; each foraging bout in a room is unique), and in training. Memory duration is therefore a property of a species, a task and its training, not of the species alone. Comparisons must use matched tasks, ideally several, and ask how each species performs relative to its own performance on control tasks.
2. In the eight-arm radial maze, why is the expected number of different arms in eight random choices equal to $8\bigl(1 - (7/8)^8\bigr)$, and why is this the appropriate chance level?

   **Answer** Each arm is missed by one random choice with probability $7/8$ and by all eight independent choices with probability $(7/8)^8$; by linearity of expectation, the expected number of arms entered at least once is $8\bigl(1 - (7/8)^8\bigr) \approx 5.25$. It is the appropriate chance level because it is what an animal that chose arms without any memory would achieve; trained rats enter 7–8 different arms. A stricter control is a model with a response bias (for example, preferring adjacent arms), which raises chance performance and must be excluded by showing that the rats' choices are not stereotyped.
3. What pattern of errors in Cook, Brown and Riley's 12-arm interruption experiment would indicate purely retrospective coding, and what did they find?

   **Answer** If rats remembered only the arms they had visited, the memory load during the interruption would equal the number of choices made, so errors after the delay would increase steadily with the point of interruption, being greatest after ten choices. They found instead that errors peaked when the interruption came after six choices and were lower for earlier and later interruptions, indicating that rats coded visited arms early in a trial and unvisited arms (prospective coding) later.
4. Explain the logic of Clayton and Dickinson's scrub jay experiment. Why was it essential that the food be removed before the test, and why was the "replenish" group needed?

   **Answer** Searching for worms after 4 h and for peanuts after 124 h requires remembering which food was cached where (what and where) and how long ago (when). Removing the food ensured that the birds could not detect the caches or their state by smell or sight, so the choice came from memory. The replenish group, for which worms never decayed, searched for worms after both delays; this shows that the switch to peanuts in the degrade group was not caused by forgetting the worm caches after 124 h but by combining memory of the caching episode with knowledge of the decay rate.
5. Rats disoriented in a rectangular box with distinctive corner panels make rotational errors. Give two explanations of this result and an experiment that could distinguish them.

   **Answer** (1) A geometric module: the animal reorients by an explicit representation of the shape of the space (the lengths and arrangement of the walls) and gives priority to it over features. (2) View matching: the animal moves to where the panoramic view best matches the stored view of the goal; in a rectangle, diagonally opposite corners produce similar views, especially if features are small. To distinguish them, change the visual scene without changing the geometry (for example, make the walls of different colors so that views differ strongly, or use a box whose geometry is defined only by low, non-visible borders or by tactile cues); or create shapes with the same views but different geometry. A view-matching animal should follow the views, a geometric-module animal the shape.
6. (Open question) The adaptive specialization hypothesis predicts that food-storing birds have better spatial memory than non-storers. List alternative explanations of a finding that a storing species outperforms a non-storing species on a spatial task, and how each could be tested.

   **Answer** (a) Motivation: storers may value food or the task more; test by measuring motivation (latency, persistence) and equating it with food deprivation. (b) Attention or cue preference: storers may attend to spatial cues, non-storers to features, without a difference in memory; test by tasks in which only spatial or only featural cues are available and by cue-conflict tests. (c) Perceptual differences, for example in the visual field; test with discrimination controls. (d) Familiarity with the apparatus or captivity effects; use wild-caught and hand-raised birds and several tasks. (e) Phylogeny: a single species pair confounds ecology with other differences; compare several independent pairs with phylogenetic methods. (f) Experience: storers practice spatial memory in their lives; compare hand-raised birds with controlled experience. A specialization is supported when the difference persists after these controls and is specific to spatial memory, ideally also within species across populations or sexes that differ in reliance on stored food.
