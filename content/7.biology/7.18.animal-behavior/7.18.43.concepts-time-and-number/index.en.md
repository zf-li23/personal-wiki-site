The world presents an animal with a stream of particular objects and events, never exactly repeated. To act sensibly it must treat different things as the same (all ripe figs, all hawks), notice relations between things (same or different, more or fewer), and keep track of when things happen. This chapter treats three such abstractions: concepts, time and number. They are studied mostly with the operant methods of [7.18.41](/7.18.41), and the central methodological problem is the same for all three: to show that an animal responds to the abstract property (category membership, duration, numerosity) and not to some simpler feature correlated with it. The case of Clever Hans ([7.18.1](/7.18.1)), the horse whose "arithmetic" was a response to unintended cues from his questioners, began the history of research on animal number and remains its standing warning. The sensory bases of perception are in [7.18.9](/7.18.9)–[7.18.11](/7.18.11), the circadian clock in [7.18.18](/7.18.18), physical reasoning in [7.18.44](/7.18.44) and the meaning of natural signals in [7.18.40](/7.18.40).

## 43.1. Perceptual Concepts

### 43.1.1. Categorization in Pigeons and Other Animals

Richard Herrnstein and Donald Loveland (1964) showed pigeons hundreds of color slides of outdoor scenes, about half containing one or more people, in varied sizes, positions, clothing and lighting. Pecks at a slide with a person were rewarded; pecks at a slide without one were not (a go/no-go procedure). The pigeons learned the discrimination and responded correctly to new slides they had never seen. Later experiments extended the result to trees, bodies of water, a particular person, fish, oak leaves, cats, flowers, cars and chairs (Herrnstein, Loveland and Cable 1976; Herrnstein and de Villiers 1980). Because thousands of pictures were used and the birds transferred to new ones, they could not have simply memorized each picture, although pigeons can memorize hundreds of arbitrary pictures ([7.18.42](/7.18.42#sec-42.3.1)).

**Definition 43.1.1 (Natural Concept)** A **natural concept** (perceptual or natural category) is a class of natural objects or scenes, such as trees, water or people, whose members share no single defining feature but resemble each other in many overlapping features; an animal shows that it has learned such a concept when it classifies new members correctly, and learns the category faster than an equally large set of arbitrarily grouped examples (a pseudocategory).

The pseudocategory control is essential: pigeons trained to sort pictures into two arbitrary sets learned slowly and did not transfer, whereas they learned "tree" versus "no tree" quickly. Categorization without language has since been shown in many species and with many kinds of category:

- Pigeons sorted paintings by Monet and Picasso and transferred the discrimination to new paintings by the same painters, and to Cézanne and Renoir (classified with Monet) and Braque and Matisse (with Picasso) (Watanabe, Sakamoto and Wakita 1995). Pigeons trained on histological slides and mammograms classified benign and malignant breast tissue about as accurately as trained human observers (Levenson et al. 2015).
- With a chamber in which a picture is surrounded by four keys, pigeons learned to assign pictures of people, flowers, cars and chairs each to its own key and generalized to new pictures (Bhatt et al. 1988), the multiple simultaneous categories of real life rather than one category and its complement.
- Capuchin and rhesus monkeys categorize pictures of people, monkeys and animals; blue jays learn to recognize cryptic moths on bark in pictures, the basis of experiments on **search images** ([7.18.24](/7.18.24)); honeybees learn to distinguish paintings by Monet and Picasso (Wu et al. 2013) and sort pictures of flowers, plants and landscapes (Zhang et al. 2004).

### 43.1.2. What Is Learned

Do the animals have concepts like ours, or do they respond to features that happen to be correlated with category membership in the training set? Error analysis gives part of the answer. Michael D'Amato and Paul van Sant (1988) trained capuchin monkeys to respond to slides containing people. The monkeys transferred to new slides, but rejected a close portrait of a face and shoulders, which lacked the whole-body shape of most training pictures, and accepted a picture of a jackal carrying a pink flamingo: the only red in the training set had been the clothing of some people. Pigeons trained to recognize the cartoon character Charlie Brown still recognized him when the drawings were cut into pieces and scrambled (Cerella 1980), which suggests that they used local features rather than the configuration of the figure; but when blurring (removing fine detail) and scrambling (disrupting the global configuration) were compared, pigeons relied on global shape for categories such as people and flowers and on local detail for cars and chairs (Lazareva et al. 2006).

Much of the categorization can be explained by the classical theory of stimulus generalization. After an animal is trained to respond to a stimulus, it responds also to similar stimuli, with responding falling off as the difference grows (a generalization gradient). Harley Hanson (1959) trained pigeons to peck a key lit with light of 550 nm. Pigeons trained with this stimulus alone pecked most at 550 nm and less at other wavelengths; pigeons trained with 550 nm rewarded and 555 or 560 nm unrewarded pecked most at about 540 nm, a stimulus they had never seen: the peak of the gradient shifted away from the unrewarded stimulus. Kenneth Spence (1937) explained such *peak shift* by the summation of an excitatory gradient around the rewarded stimulus (S+) and an inhibitory gradient around the unrewarded one (S−).

Peak shift has consequences beyond the laboratory. A receiver that has learned to prefer a signal and to avoid a similar one responds most strongly to an exaggerated version of the preferred signal, which may favor the evolution of exaggerated signals, of supernormal responses ([7.18.3](/7.18.3)) and of mimics that differ from their models in the direction away from the punished stimulus (Ghirlanda and Enquist 2003). Similar reasoning applies to prototypes. People classify the average of a category, even if they have never seen it, more readily than its actual members, and pigeons do the same: trained to respond to patterns containing two of three "positive" elements and one "negative" element and not to the reverse, they responded most to a pattern with all three positive elements, which they had never seen (Aydin and Pearce 1994). But the novel "prototype" contained more elements associated with reward than any training pattern, so the result follows from generalization by elements without an abstract prototype. Whether categorization requires anything beyond associative learning about many features, weighted by their reliability, is not settled; it does not require language.

### 43.1.3. Object Permanence

An animal that pursues prey behind a rock must represent the prey as continuing to exist when it is out of sight. Jean Piaget described six stages in the development of this ability in children, two of which are tested in animals.

**Definition 43.1.2 (Object Permanence)** **Object permanence** is the understanding that objects continue to exist when they cannot be perceived. It is tested by *visible displacement*, in which an object is hidden in full view of the subject, and by *invisible displacement*, in which the object is hidden inside a container that is moved behind a screen, where the object is left, and shown empty, so that the object's location must be inferred from the container's movements.

Visible displacement tasks are solved by every species tested: great apes and monkeys, dogs, cats, hamsters, chickens, doves, parrots and corvids. Invisible displacement is much harder. Great apes pass it; dogs were reported to pass it from the age of about one year (Gagnon and Doré 1994), but they had used the position of the displacement device, which was left beside the correct screen: when the device was placed at random, dogs failed (Collier-Baker, Davis and Suddendorf 2004). Cats searched correctly more often if prevented from searching for 20 s, apparently because their memory of where the object was last seen had faded, so they made fewer errors at that location (Goulet, Doré and Rousseau 1994). Such controls show again how easily simple cues produce apparently sophisticated performance. Invisible displacement may also be ecologically odd: a predator whose prey disappeared behind a rock would do well to look there first.

## 43.2. Relational Concepts

### 43.2.1. Same and Different

Some concepts are not about objects but about relations between them. Sameness is the simplest relation, and it can be applied to any objects at all.

**Definition 43.2.1 (Same–Different Concept)** An animal has a **same–different concept** if it responds to the relation of identity or non-identity between stimuli independently of the stimuli themselves, so that a discrimination trained with some stimuli transfers immediately to entirely novel ones.

Anthony Wright and colleagues (1988) trained two groups of pigeons in a matching-to-sample task. One group saw only two pictures, which served as both sample and comparison in every trial; it learned in about two weeks but performed at chance when tested with new pictures. The second group saw 152 different pictures, each once a day; it needed about 18 months to reach the same accuracy, but then matched new pictures as accurately as training pictures. Systematic variation of the training set size showed that transfer increases steadily with the number of different training stimuli (Katz and Wright 2006): abstraction is favored when memorizing particular items is impossible. Pigeons may base their judgments on the variability of a display rather than on the relation itself: shown arrays of 16 icons, all the same or all different, they also respond "different" to arrays with intermediate numbers of distinct icons in proportion to the arrays' entropy (Young and Wasserman 1997).

Same–different discrimination with transfer has been shown in baboons, rhesus monkeys, chimpanzees, California sea lions, crows and even the echidna. Three cases are especially informative:

- *Alex*, an African gray parrot trained by Irene Pepperberg to use English labels, answered "What's same?" or "What's different?" about two objects by naming the dimension ("color", "shape" or "matter") on which they were the same or differed, with new objects, and answered "none" when nothing was same or different (Pepperberg 1987).
- *Honeybees* trained in a Y-maze to choose the arm marked with the same color as a sample at the entrance transferred the rule to patterns (and others from odors to colors), and bees trained to choose the different stimulus (non-matching) learned that rule too (Giurfa et al. 2001).
- *Ducklings* that imprinted shortly after hatching on a pair of identical objects (two red spheres, say) later followed a pair of new identical objects (two blue cubes) rather than a pair of different ones, and ducklings imprinted on a pair of different objects preferred new different pairs (Martinho and Kacelnik 2016). The relation was learned in one exposure, without reinforcement, during the sensitive period of **filial imprinting** ([7.18.5](/7.18.5)).

### 43.2.2. Stimulus Equivalence

The word "apple" and an apple have nothing physical in common, yet for a person each can substitute for the other. Murray Sidman defined *stimulus equivalence* by three relations among stimuli trained in matching to sample: reflexivity (each stimulus matches itself), symmetry (if A is trained to go with B, B goes with A) and transitivity (if A goes with B and B with C, then A goes with C). Children show all three easily; animals rarely show symmetry spontaneously. Rio, a California sea lion trained on conditional discriminations among arbitrary shapes, did pass tests of symmetry and transitivity (Schusterman and Kastak 1993).

Other procedures show that animals treat stimuli as equivalent when they have the same consequences. Pigeons trained to peck one key for pictures of cars and flowers and another for people and chairs, and then retrained with new keys for flowers and chairs only, transferred the new keys to cars and people (Wasserman, DeVolder and Coppage 1992; common response). Pigeons whose correct choices in two matching tasks produced different foods matched across tasks above chance when stimuli associated with the same food were combined (Edwards, Jagielo, Zentall and Hogan 1982; common outcome). Dolphins trained with four tones, two associated with going left and two with going right, and repeatedly given reversals for one pair, came to reverse the other pair as well before receiving any feedback (von Fersen and Delius 2000). Equivalence classes are a prerequisite for symbols, and these results show that their associative foundations are present in many animals, even if the full symmetry of human symbols is rare.

## 43.3. Time

Animals time events on two scales: the time of day, by the circadian clock, and arbitrary intervals of seconds to minutes, by a separate and more flexible timing system.

### 43.3.1. Time of Day

Honeybees and garden warblers learn to visit a food source at the time of day when it is available, and they keep the schedule under constant conditions, showing that the timing depends on an endogenous **circadian rhythm** ([7.18.18](/7.18.18#sec-18.6.1)). Rats fed at three places at three times of day were confused when the time of the middle meal was changed, as expected if they used the clock rather than the order of meals (Pizzo and Crystal 2002). Daily timing is accurate to within about 1 % but cannot be set to arbitrary periods: bees cannot be trained to a 19-hour rhythm. Pigeons trained to expect food after intervals of 12 to 48 hours timed the intervals with errors proportional to their length, except at 24 hours and its simple multiples and fractions, where they were much more accurate, presumably because they used the circadian clock (Eckerman 1999).

### 43.3.2. Interval Timing

Many foraging problems require timing intervals that have nothing to do with the day. Male long-tailed hermit hummingbirds in Costa Rica, which defend leks, visit flowers along a "trapline" and must return after the flowers have refilled with nectar, but before competitors take it; Frank Gill (1988) refilled artificial flowers 10 minutes after each visit and the birds adjusted their return intervals to a little more than 10 minutes. Wild rufous hummingbirds offered eight artificial flowers, four refilled 10 minutes and four 20 minutes after being emptied, matched their visits to each flower's schedule within two or three days (Henderson et al. 2006), so they remembered both where and when.

In the laboratory, timing is studied with three procedures.

- *Fixed-interval schedules* ([7.18.41](/7.18.41#sec-41.4.2)): responding starts after about a third of the interval and accelerates toward its end.
- *The peak procedure* (Catania 1970, Roberts 1981): on most trials, food is given for the first response at time $T$ after a signal begins; on occasional probe trials no food is given and the signal lasts three or four times longer. On probe trials the response rate rises to a maximum close to $T$ and then falls; on individual trials the animal switches from low to high rate at some time before $T$ and back to low rate at some time after it.
- *Temporal bisection* (Church and Deluty 1977): rats learn to press one lever after a 2-s tone and another after an 8-s tone, and are then tested with intermediate durations. The duration judged "long" half the time, the point of subjective equality, is close to the geometric mean of the training durations (4 s), not the arithmetic mean (5 s), as expected if durations are compared on a logarithmic scale or by their ratio.

Two regularities emerge across species, from fish and pigeons to rats and humans. The peak of responding is at the trained time, so timing is accurate on average. And timing errors are proportional to the interval timed: the standard deviation of the start and stop times is a constant fraction (typically 0.2–0.4) of $T$, so that peak functions for different intervals superimpose when time is expressed as a proportion of $T$. This *scalar property* is the **Weber–Fechner law** ([7.6.35](/7.6.35#sec-35.6.3)) applied to duration: the just-discriminable difference in duration is proportional to the duration.

**Definition 43.3.1 (Scalar Expectancy Theory)** **Scalar expectancy theory** (SET; Gibbon 1977; Gibbon, Church and Meck 1984) explains interval timing by an information-processing model with three stages: a *clock*, in which a pacemaker emits pulses that a switch, closed at the start of the timed signal, passes to an accumulator; a *memory*, in which the count $m$ at reinforcement is stored, with multiplicative error, in reference memory; and a decision stage, in which the current count $n$ is compared with a sample $m^*$ from reference memory by their ratio, the animal responding when $\lvert m^* - n\rvert / m^* < b$ for a threshold $b$. Because the errors in memory and threshold are proportional to the stored value, the variability of timed behavior is proportional to the interval.

![The scalar property of interval timing (simulation in the spirit of scalar expectancy theory; illustrative parameters). A: the peak procedure. Probe trials of a rat trained with food 15, 30 or 60 s after the start of a signal; on each simulated trial the animal responds at a high rate between a start time $Ts_1$ and a stop time $Ts_2$, with $s_1 \sim \mathcal{N}(0.55, 0.12^2)$ and $s_2 \sim \mathcal{N}(1.45, 0.25^2)$ (500 trials per interval). SET accounts for the main facts of timing, and the speed of its clock can be manipulated: drugs that release dopamine, such as methamphetamine, shift peak functions to earlier times, as if the pacemaker ran faster, and dopamine antagonists shift them later (Meck 1983). But no pacemaker has been identified, and several alternative theories reproduce the scalar property without one. In the behavioral theory of timing (Killeen and Fetterman 1988), the animal passes through a sequence of behavioral states at a rate that depends on the rate of reinforcement, and learns which state is present at the time of food; interim and terminal behaviors in fixed-time schedules ([7.18.41](/7.18.41#sec-41.4.3)) are its visible counterpart. In the multiple-time-scale model (Staddon and Higa 1999), the decaying memory trace of the last reinforcer serves as the clock, which explains why larger rewards delay the start of responding. In the striatal beat-frequency model (Matell and Meck 2004), cortical neurons oscillating at different frequencies are synchronized at the start of an interval, and striatal neurons learn the pattern of oscillator phases present at the time of reinforcement; neurons in the striatum and other areas of rats and monkeys do show activity that rises, peaks or forms sequences locked to elapsed time.

Scalar timing has ecological consequences. Because a delay of 2 s or 18 s is represented with errors proportional to each, and the value of a reward declines steeply with its delay, an option with variable delays is worth more to an animal than one with a fixed delay of the same mean, which explains the risk-proneness for variable delays found in all species tested ([7.18.23](/7.18.23#sec-23.2.2)).

## 43.4. Number

Number can be judged at several levels: *relative numerosity* (which of two sets has more items), *absolute numerosity* (responding to a particular number, whatever the items), and *counting*, which requires a sequence of number tags applied according to rules. In all experiments, numerosity must be dissociated from correlated continuous variables: the total area, contour length and density of the items, the duration of a sequence of sounds or the time taken to present items one at a time. Modern experiments vary these independently, or make them uninformative.

### 43.4.1. Relative Numerosity

Otto Koehler and his students (1950s) trained pigeons, jackdaws, ravens and parrots to choose between containers with different numbers of grains or spots on their lids. The birds chose correctly more often when the numbers were far apart (seven versus four) than when they were adjacent (five versus four). Jacky Emmerton (1997) trained pigeons to classify arrays of one or two dots as "few" and of six or seven as "many", and showed that the classification survived changes in dot size, shape, brightness and spacing, and transferred to three to five dots in the expected order. Relative numerosity judgments have since been shown in monkeys, apes, dogs, wolves, coyotes, black bears, dolphins, parrots, robins, fish and insects. Many such studies use spontaneous choices rather than training: mosquitofish and guppies join the larger of two shoals (Agrillo et al. 2008), and New Zealand robins choose the cache site in which they have seen more mealworms placed, and search longer when some of the prey they saw hidden have been removed (Hunt, Low and Burns 2008).

### 43.4.2. The Approximate Number System

In all these studies, discrimination of numerosity depends on the ratio of the two numbers, not on their difference: 8 versus 16 is as easy as 4 versus 8, and 7 versus 8 is hard even though the difference equals that between 1 and 2. This is the Weber–Fechner law applied to number, and it implies a common representation of quantity across species and in human infants and adults who are not allowed to count.

**Definition 43.4.1 (Approximate Number System)** The **approximate number system** (ANS) is a nonverbal system that represents the numerosity of a set as a noisy magnitude whose variability is proportional to the numerosity (scalar variability), so that discrimination between two numerosities depends on their ratio, with a precision measured by the Weber fraction $w$.

**Formula 43.4.1 (Ratio-Dependent Discrimination)** If numerosity $n$ is represented by a Gaussian magnitude with mean $n$ and standard deviation $wn$, the probability of correctly choosing the larger of two sets $n_1 < n_2$ is

$$
\mathbb{P}(\text{correct}) = \Phi\!\left(\frac{n_2 - n_1}{w\sqrt{n_1^2 + n_2^2}}\right) = \Phi\!\left(\frac{1 - r}{w\sqrt{1 + r^2}}\right), \qquad r = \frac{n_1}{n_2},
$$
which depends only on the ratio $r$. An equivalent model represents numerosity on a logarithmic scale with constant noise.

On a logarithmic axis the curves are Gaussians of equal width (s.d. 0.12 log units); on a linear axis they would be asymmetric and broaden with the preferred number, a compressed code of the kind described by the Weber–Fechner law.](./plots/number.py)

Rhesus monkeys trained to touch two arrays of one to nine items in ascending order transferred the ordering to arrays of up to 30 items, with accuracy and reaction times that depended on the ratio of the numbers, as in humans tested without counting (Cantlon and Brannon 2006). Monkeys watching two sets of dots presented in succession chose the array whose numerosity was their sum over a distractor array; their accuracy, like that of college students, depended on the ratio between the correct sum and the distractor (Cantlon and Brannon 2007). Andreas Nieder and Earl Miller (2002, 2003) recorded neurons in the prefrontal and posterior parietal cortex of monkeys that responded most strongly to a particular numerosity of dots, whatever their size and arrangement, with tuning curves that overlap more for larger numbers, as the ANS predicts. Neurons tuned to numerosity have also been found in the nidopallium caudolaterale of crows (Ditz and Nieder 2015), a region that evolved independently of the primate prefrontal cortex, and in untrained monkeys and crows, so number sense is not a product of training.

Small numbers may be handled differently. For sets of up to three or four items, many species, including human infants, guppies and chicks, discriminate numerosities more accurately than the ratio rule predicts (one versus two may be easier than ten versus twenty), possibly because each item is tracked individually by a system of object files, as in the immediate apprehension of small numbers called subitizing in humans. Whether this is a separate system or the low end of the ANS is debated (Agrillo et al. 2012).

### 43.4.3. Absolute Number and Counting

Absolute number means responding to a specific numerosity. Koehler's raven Jakob chose, among several pots, the one whose lid bore the same number of spots as a sample card, with numbers up to seven and spots varying 50-fold in area. Rats learned to enter the third, fourth or fifth of a row of identical tunnels, even when the spacing of the tunnels was changed (Davis and Bradford 1986), and to respond only after a particular number of bursts of noise of random duration (Davis and Albert 1986). But responding to "three" is not counting. Rochel Gelman and Randy Gallistel (1978) listed the principles that define counting in children:

- *One-to-one*: each item is assigned exactly one tag.
- *Stable order*: the tags are always used in the same order.
- *Cardinality*: the tag of the last item represents the number of items in the set.
- *Abstraction*: any kind of item can be counted.
- *Order irrelevance*: items can be counted in any order with the same result.

Evidence that animals meet these criteria comes only from individuals trained with symbols. Alex the parrot answered "How many?" about sets of up to six objects with about 80 % accuracy, including subsets in heterogeneous collections ("How many purple wood?"), and later used "none" for an empty set (Pepperberg 1994; Pepperberg and Gordon 2005). The chimpanzee Ai learned Arabic numerals from 0 to 9, labeled sets with them, and touched numerals in ascending order (Matsuzawa 1985; Biro and Matsuzawa 2001). Rhesus monkeys trained to order arrays of one to four items transferred the ordinal rule to five to nine items (Brannon and Terrace 1998). The chimpanzee Sheba chose the numeral corresponding to the total number of oranges hidden in two or three places in a room (Boysen and Berntson 1989), although totals up to four can be produced in only a few ways, which could have been memorized. Honeybees can be trained to land after a specified number of landmarks along a route (Chittka and Geiger 1995; Dacke and Srinivasan 2008), and trained bees placed an empty set below sets of one or more items, treating "zero" as a numerosity at the low end of the scale (Howard et al. 2018). The present consensus is that many animals possess an approximate magnitude system that supports "more or less", ratio-dependent judgments and simple arithmetic, that some can learn to associate symbols with small numerosities, but that there is no evidence of counting with an open-ended sequence of tags outside humans.

## 43.5. Numerical Competence in Nature

What is number sense for? Several field studies show that animals use numerical information in decisions that matter for fitness.

- *Contests between groups*. Karen McComb, Craig Packer and Anne Pusey (1994) played recordings of the roars of one or three unfamiliar females to lionesses in the Serengeti. The residents approached the loudspeaker readily when they outnumbered the simulated intruders, and more cautiously, or after waiting for companions to arrive, when they did not; they approached three roaring intruders only in groups of five or more. Male chimpanzees in Kibale approached the playback of a single stranger's pant-hoot when there were at least three of them, and stayed silent and kept away when fewer (Wilson, Hauser and Wrangham 2001).
- *Mate choice by call counting*. Female frogs prefer calls with more pulses or notes, and their preference obeys Weber's law. Female túngara frogs prefer a call with more "chucks" appended to the whine, but the strength of the preference depends on the ratio of chuck numbers: a call with two chucks is strongly preferred over one with one, but six over five hardly at all; frog-eating bats, which hunt by the same calls, show the same ratio dependence, so receiver psychology limits the benefit of adding more chucks (Akre et al. 2011). In the gray treefrog, females prefer longer calls, which contain more pulses.
- **Brood parasitism**. Female brown-headed cowbirds prefer to lay in host nests that already contain three eggs rather than one, timing their laying to the host's laying period (White, Ho and Freed-Brown 2007). American coots recognize parasitic eggs laid in their nests by other coots, reject some of them and adjust the number of their own eggs, as if they counted their own eggs and ignored the parasitic ones (Lyon 2003).
- *Foraging*. Robins choose the cache with more prey (above); bees use the number of landmarks passed to judge distance.

The neural basis of counting in frogs has been found at the level of single neurons. In the auditory midbrain (torus semicircularis) of anurans, *interval-counting neurons* respond to a train of sound pulses only after a threshold number of consecutive pulses, separated by intervals within a particular range, has occurred; one interval that is too long resets the count (Alder and Rose 1998; Edwards, Alder and Rose 2002). The count is built up by excitation that accumulates from pulse to pulse and is opposed by inhibition, so that only pulses at the right rate, repeated enough times, drive the neuron to fire. Such neurons can explain why females respond to calls of the right pulse rate and prefer calls with more pulses.

These examples also show why Weber's law matters for the evolution of signals. If receivers compare signals by ratios, a male that adds one note to a long call gains little, while the same addition to a short call gains much; competition among males therefore escalates signals in steps proportional to their size, and the costs of signaling rise faster than its benefits. The perception of receivers shapes the design and evolution of signals ([7.18.39](/7.18.39)), and the law of Weber and Fechner, originally a law of human psychophysics, is part of this ecology.

## Summary

- Pigeons, monkeys and bees learn natural concepts (Definition 43.1.1) from examples and transfer them to new ones; pseudocategory controls show that true categories are easier. Much categorization can be explained by generalization over many features, with peak shift (Spence's model) and prototype effects as consequences; errors reveal that animals sometimes use features that we do not.
- Object permanence (Definition 43.1.2) for visible displacements is widespread; invisible displacements are solved convincingly only by great apes, once cues are controlled.
- The same–different concept (Definition 43.2.1) requires many training stimuli in pigeons (transfer grows with set size); it is shown by Alex the parrot, by honeybees and by ducklings through imprinting. Stimulus equivalence based on common responses or outcomes is common; full symmetry is rare.
- Animals learn the time of day by the **circadian rhythm** and time intervals by a separate system. Peak and bisection procedures show accurate mean timing, bisection at the geometric mean, and errors proportional to the interval (the scalar property, the **Weber–Fechner law** for time), explained by scalar expectancy theory (Definition 43.3.1) or by clockless alternatives.
- Numerosity is represented by the approximate number system (Definition 43.4.1): discrimination depends on the ratio (Formula 43.4.1), and number neurons in primate and corvid brains have logarithmically compressed tuning. Trained parrots, chimpanzees, monkeys and bees label or order small numbers and treat zero as a quantity, but no animal meets all criteria of counting.
- Number matters in nature: lions and chimpanzees assess the numbers of intruders, frogs count call pulses with interval-counting neurons and compare calls by ratios, coots and cowbirds use egg numbers.

## Questions

1. Why is a pseudocategory control necessary in experiments on natural concepts, and what would it mean if pigeons learned a pseudocategory as fast as a natural category?

   **Answer** Pigeons can memorize hundreds of individual pictures, so learning to respond to a set of pictures does not by itself show a concept. In a pseudocategory, the same pictures are divided into two arbitrary groups; if pigeons learn natural categories faster and transfer only them to new pictures, the advantage must come from the similarity among members of the natural category. If pseudocategories were learned as fast, the birds would be memorizing pictures, and the natural-category result would show only rote memory (transfer tests would also fail).
2. Using Spence's model, explain why peak shift occurs and predict how it changes if S− is moved farther from S+.

   **Answer** Responding is the difference between excitation, centered on S+, and inhibition, centered on S−. Near S+ on the side of S−, inhibition is strong and reduces net responding; on the opposite side it is weak, so the maximum of the difference lies on the side of S+ away from S−. If S− is moved farther away, the inhibitory gradient overlaps less with the excitatory one, so the shift becomes smaller and the peak returns toward S+; if S− is very close, the shift is larger (but discrimination is harder to learn), as Hanson found.
3. What does the scalar property of timing mean, and how could you test it with the peak procedure?

   **Answer** It means that the variability of timed behavior is proportional to the interval being timed: the coefficient of variation of start and stop times, or of the peak function, is constant. Train animals (or the same animal in different conditions) with several intervals, for example 15, 30 and 60 s, and record probe trials. Plot the mean response rate against time divided by the trained interval, and rate divided by its maximum: if the property holds, the functions superimpose. Equivalently, the width of the peak function (or the standard deviation of start times) should increase linearly with the interval.
4. A monkey discriminates 10 from 20 dots with 85 % accuracy. According to Formula 43.4.1, what accuracy is expected for 30 versus 60 dots, and for 18 versus 20? What Weber fraction is implied?

   **Answer** 30 versus 60 has the same ratio, $r = 0.5$, so the expected accuracy is again 85 %. For $r = 0.5$, $\Phi\bigl(0.5/(w\sqrt{1.25})\bigr) = 0.85$ gives $0.5/(1.118\,w) = 1.04$, so $w \approx 0.43$. For 18 versus 20, $r = 0.9$, and the argument is $0.1/(0.43 \times \sqrt{1.81}) = 0.1/0.578 \approx 0.17$, so the accuracy is $\Phi(0.17) \approx 0.57$, barely above chance.
5. Why do Gelman and Gallistel's counting principles set a higher standard than discrimination of numerosity, and which of them is hardest to test in animals?

   **Answer** Discriminating more from fewer, or responding to a specific number, can be achieved by an approximate magnitude representation without any tags. Counting requires a stable sequence of discrete tags applied one-to-one to items, and the cardinal principle, that the last tag stands for the whole set. To test the principles, an animal must be able to produce several distinct tags, which requires training with symbols; the cardinal principle, and the open-endedness of the sequence (that every number has a successor), are the hardest to demonstrate, and no animal has been shown to apply them beyond small trained sets.
6. (Open question) Female túngara frogs prefer calls with more chucks according to Weber's law. Discuss how this property of receivers could limit the evolution of the male call, and what other factors might set the number of chucks males produce.

   **Answer** Because the preference depends on the ratio of chuck numbers, each additional chuck gives a smaller relative advantage the more chucks a call already has, whereas the costs of adding chucks (energy, time, and above all attraction of frog-eating bats and blood-sucking midges, which also prefer calls with more chucks, with the same ratio rule) do not decline. Selection should therefore favor a number of chucks at which the marginal mating benefit equals the marginal cost of predation and energy, and males should adjust the number to competition (more chucks when neighbors call with more) and to predation risk (fewer when bats are near). The ratio rule of the receivers' perception thus sets diminishing returns and explains why calls do not escalate indefinitely; testing the explanation requires measuring the benefit and cost curves in the field.
