Water conducts electricity, and every living animal in water is surrounded by weak electric fields: the potentials of its muscles, the ionic leakage from its gills and skin, the rhythmic currents of breathing. Sharks, rays and many other fishes, a few amphibians and the egg-laying mammals have evolved senses that detect these fields. Two groups of freshwater fishes went further and evolved electric organs that produce weak fields of their own, which they use both to sense their surroundings in the dark and to communicate. The electric sense, entirely foreign to human experience, has been one of the most productive model systems of neuroethology. Its most famous product is the analysis of the jamming avoidance response of the South American knifefish *Eigenmannia* by Walter Heiligenberg and his colleagues: a behavior whose computational rules were deduced from behavioral experiments and whose neural implementation was then traced, neuron class by neuron class, from the receptors in the skin to the pacemaker in the medulla that sets the frequency of the electric organ. It remains the most complete account of a vertebrate behavior from sensory input to motor output.

The cellular physiology of electroreceptors and the physics of electrolocation are introduced in [7.14.43](/7.14.43#sec-43.5.3); the biophysics of electric organs in [7.6.30](/7.6.30#sec-30.8); the comparative anatomy of the receptors in [7.17.68](/7.17.68#sec-68.2). Here we emphasize behavior, the experiments that revealed it and the logic of the neural computation.

## 15.1. Electric Fields in Water

### 15.1.1. Quasi-Static Fields

Electric fields do not propagate like sound or light. When charges are separated, as across the membranes of a fish's muscles or between the inside of its gills and the surrounding water, the field is established practically instantaneously everywhere around the source, and changes of the source are felt without delay and without reflection, refraction or diffraction. For the frequencies used by animals (up to a few kilohertz) the field can be treated as quasi-static: at each moment it is the field of the momentary charge distribution, attenuated by distance and shaped by the conductivity of the medium and of the objects in it.

The decay with distance is steep. The field of a single charge (a monopole) falls as $r^{-2}$, but living sources are almost always pairs of opposite charges, dipoles, whose fields fall as $r^{-3}$; more complex sources look like dipoles from distances larger than their size. Doubling the distance from a prey therefore reduces its field eightfold, and a sense that detects fields must work at short range.

**Example 15.1.1** The bioelectric field of a small fish or crustacean is of the order of $10\,\mu\mathrm{V\,cm^{-1}}$ at $1\,\mathrm{cm}$ (the precise value depends on the animal and on whether it is breathing or injured). Taking the field to fall as $r^{-3}$, at what distance does it fall to the threshold of a shark, about $5\,\mathrm{nV\,cm^{-1}}$, and to the threshold of a catfish ampullary organ, about $1\,\mu\mathrm{V\,cm^{-1}}$?

The field ratio is $10\,000/5 = 2\,000$ for the shark, so $r = 2\,000^{1/3}\,\mathrm{cm} \approx 13\,\mathrm{cm}$; for the catfish the ratio is 10 and $r \approx 2\,\mathrm{cm}$. Even the most sensitive electroreceptors known detect small prey only within tens of centimeters; larger prey, with stronger dipoles, can be detected at somewhat greater distances, but the cube root grows slowly: a source a thousand times stronger is detected only ten times farther away.

### 15.1.2. Why Electroreception Is an Aquatic Sense

For a receptor to be stimulated, a current must flow through it, and the voltage drop across it must exceed its threshold. Air is a very poor conductor, and an animal in air is a good conductor surrounded by an insulator, so almost no current from external fields flows through its body: electroreception is practically impossible in air. In water, the conductivity of the medium relative to that of the body determines the design of the receptors (Bradbury and Vehrencamp's analysis). Fresh water conducts poorly, and freshwater fishes have high-resistance skins (for osmotic reasons), so the largest voltage drop in an external field occurs across the skin itself; their receptor organs lie in the skin, with short canals. Sea water conducts better than the body tissues, so the voltage drop across the skin is small; marine elasmobranchs place their receptors deep in the body at the end of long canals filled with a highly conductive jelly, which bring the potential of a distant skin pore to the receptor while the receptor's base sees the potential of the body interior. The ampullary canals of a skate's snout can be up to $20\,\mathrm{cm}$ long and spread over the head like an antenna array.

The few exceptions to aquatic electroreception prove the rule. The echidnas probe moist soil and termite nests with a snout whose tip is always wet with mucus, which links their electroreceptors to the prey's tissues. And bumblebees detect the weak electric fields between themselves (positively charged in flight) and flowers (negatively charged relative to the air) through the deflection of their body hairs, a mechanosensory rather than a true electrosensory mechanism (Clarke and colleagues 2013); a flower that has just been visited carries a different field, and bees can learn to use the field as a cue.

## 15.2. Passive Electroreception

### 15.2.1. Kalmijn's Sharks

That sharks detect prey by its electric field was shown by Adrianus Kalmijn (1971) in a series of experiments with small sharks (*Scyliorhinus canicula*) and rays. A shark swimming over a sandy bottom located a flatfish (a plaice) buried in the sand and attacked it accurately. The fish could be seen, smelled or electrically sensed. Kalmijn placed the plaice in a chamber of agar, which lets electric fields and odors pass but blocks vision: the sharks still attacked accurately. When the agar chamber was covered with a thin plastic film, which insulates electrically but was arranged to let odor escape, the sharks no longer found the fish. Finally he buried a pair of electrodes in the sand, passing a current that mimicked the field of a plaice: the sharks attacked the electrodes, even when a piece of fish was placed nearby as an odor source, and they preferred the active electrodes over inactive ones. The ampullae of Lorenzini, known anatomically since 1678, are electroreceptors that guide the final phase of the attack.

**Definition 15.2.1 (Ampullary Receptor)** An **ampullary receptor** is an electroreceptor organ consisting of a cavity (ampulla) lined with receptor cells and connected to the skin surface by a canal filled with conductive jelly, which responds to steady or slowly varying (below about $50\,\mathrm{Hz}$) electric fields and is used in passive electroreception.

The receptor cells are modified hair cells derived from the lateral line placodes; in skates they use an L-type calcium channel as the voltage sensor ([7.14.43](/7.14.43#sec-43.5.3)). Elasmobranch ampullae reach thresholds of about $5\,\mathrm{nV\,cm^{-1}}$, among the most sensitive sensory systems known. Their sensitivity raises the possibility that sharks also use the fields induced by their own motion through the earth's magnetic field, or by ocean currents, as navigational cues; this remains unproven.

### 15.2.2. Who Has Passive Electroreception?

**Electroreception** is an ancestral vertebrate sense ([7.14.43](/7.14.43#def-43.5.1)): lampreys, cartilaginous fishes, coelacanths, lungfishes, sturgeons and paddlefishes, bichirs and many aquatic salamanders and caecilians have ampullary organs of the same basic design, excited when the canal opening is made electrically negative (cathodal stimuli). The sense was lost in the ancestors of the neopterygian fishes, which include the teleosts, and in frogs and amniotes. It was then reinvented: in teleosts at least twice, in the catfishes together with the South American knifefishes (Gymnotiformes), and in the African osteoglossomorphs (elephantfishes, Mormyridae; the frankfish *Gymnarchus*; featherbacks). The teleost ampullary organs differ from the ancestral ones: their receptor cells bear microvilli instead of a kinocilium and are excited by anodal rather than cathodal stimuli, as expected for organs built anew without ancestral constraints.

Among mammals, the platypus carries about 40,000 electroreceptors (modified mucous glands innervated by the trigeminal nerve) on its bill, which it sweeps from side to side while foraging in murky water with its eyes, ears and nostrils closed; the echidnas have far fewer. The Guiana dolphin (*Sotalia guianensis*) detects weak fields with the vibrissal pits on its rostrum, the remnants of the whiskers of its terrestrial ancestors (Czech-Damal and colleagues 2012). Electroreception is thus a textbook case of **convergent evolution**: the same sense has evolved repeatedly from different starting structures (lateral line hair cells, mucous glands, vibrissal follicles) under the same physical opportunity.

## 15.3. Weakly Electric Fishes

### 15.3.1. Electric Organs and Discharges

The South American knifefishes (Gymnotiformes) and the African elephantfishes (Mormyriformes), unrelated groups that both live in turbid fresh water and are active at night, evolved **electric organs** ([7.6.30](/7.6.30#def-30.8.1)) that produce weak discharges, from millivolts to about a volt. The organ consists of electrocytes, modified muscle cells (in one family of knifefishes, the Apteronotidae, modified axons of the spinal electromotor neurons) arranged in series along the tail. Each discharge is commanded by a pacemaker nucleus in the medulla, whose neurons fire in synchrony and drive the electromotor neurons of the spinal cord one to one, so that the electric organ discharge (EOD) is as regular as the pacemaker; in *Eigenmannia* the coefficient of variation of the interval between discharges is of the order of $10^{-4}$, one of the most precise biological clocks known.

The discharges come in two types:

| | Wave-type | Pulse-type |
|---|---|---|
| Pattern | Continuous, nearly sinusoidal discharge at a constant frequency | Brief pulses separated by much longer, variable intervals |
| Frequency | Species-specific, about $50$–$1\,800\,\mathrm{Hz}$; each individual has its own frequency | $1$–$100$ pulses per second, rising with arousal |
| Examples | *Eigenmannia*, *Apteronotus*, *Sternopygus*; the mormyriform *Gymnarchus* | *Gymnotus*, *Brachyhypopomus*; most mormyrids, such as *Gnathonemus petersii* |
| Information | Frequency and waveform identify species, sex and individual | Pulse waveform identifies species and sex; the pattern of intervals carries social messages |

The strongly electric fishes, which stun prey and deter predators with discharges of hundreds of volts (the electric eel, *Electrophorus*, a knifefish; the electric catfish *Malapterurus*; the marine torpedo rays), evolved their organs independently, and the electric eel also produces weak discharges for electrolocation.

### 15.3.2. Tuberous Receptors and Electrolocation

To sense their own high-frequency discharges, weakly electric fishes evolved a second class of electroreceptors.

**Definition 15.3.1 (Tuberous Receptor)** A **tuberous receptor** is an electroreceptor organ of weakly electric fishes in which receptor cells lie in an intraepidermal chamber plugged by loosely packed epithelial cells, with no jelly-filled canal; it is insensitive to steady fields and tuned to the frequency range of the fish's own electric organ discharge.

Tuberous receptors are electrically coupled to the water through the capacitance of the plug, so they respond to changes of voltage rather than to steady fields. In gymnotiforms they are of two kinds, which divide the information in the discharge between them:

- *T-units* (time coders) fire one spike for each cycle of the EOD, phase-locked to its zero crossing, with a timing jitter of only some tens of microseconds; they encode the timing (phase) of the signal at their location.
- *P-units* (probability coders) fire at most one spike per cycle, with a probability that increases with the amplitude of the signal; their firing rate encodes the local amplitude.

Mormyrids have two kinds of tuberous organs: *mormyromasts*, used for active electrolocation, and *knollenorgans*, extremely sensitive organs used to detect the discharges of other fish in communication.

**Electrolocation** ([7.14.43](/7.14.43#def-43.5.2)) was discovered by Hans Lissmann in the 1950s. He trained the African frankfish *Gymnarchus niloticus* to approach one of two porous pots and rewarded the fish with food, and found that it could distinguish pots containing water of slightly different conductivity, and even a glass rod a couple of millimeters thick inside one of them, although the pots looked identical and the fish's eyes are degenerate (Lissmann and Machin 1958). An object whose conductivity differs from that of the water distorts the fish's field and casts an electric image on its skin, which the receptor array reads like a retina. The range of active electrolocation is about one body length, for the reasons shown in the figure above; within this range, the fish judges the distance of objects from the width of the image relative to its peak, and the capacitance of living things (whose membranes store charge) from the phase shift of the image, which lets mormyrids distinguish living prey from stones of the same conductivity (von der Emde).

## 15.4. The Jamming Avoidance Response: Behavior

### 15.4.1. The Problem

A wave-type fish senses objects through small modulations of the amplitude and phase of its own field. When another fish of the same species discharging at a similar frequency comes near, the two fields add, and the result is a beat: the amplitude and phase of the summed signal at each point of the skin wax and wane at the difference frequency. Such modulations resemble those produced by a moving object, and they impair electrolocation. In behavioral tests, *Eigenmannia* could detect objects much less well when exposed to a jamming signal close to its own frequency, the impairment being greatest when the two frequencies differed by only a few hertz and small when they differed by more than about $15$–$20\,\mathrm{Hz}$ (Heiligenberg 1973). The beat at a difference of $3\,\mathrm{Hz}$ is a slow modulation, precisely in the range of the modulations caused by objects passing at swimming speed; a faster beat is easily separated from them.

**Definition 15.4.1 (Jamming Avoidance Response)** The **jamming avoidance response** (JAR) is the shift of the discharge frequency of a wave-type weakly electric fish away from the frequency of a neighbor with a similar discharge frequency: the fish lowers its frequency if the neighbor's frequency is higher and raises it if the neighbor's is lower, thereby increasing the frequency difference and reducing the interference with electrolocation.

Watanabe and Takeda (1963) discovered the behavior, and Bullock, Hamstra and Scheich (1972) named it and described its properties. In *Eigenmannia* the response begins within a few hundred milliseconds of the onset of a jamming signal, develops over seconds, and shifts the frequency by up to about $10\,\mathrm{Hz}$; it is largest for frequency differences of about $3$–$5\,\mathrm{Hz}$ and declines for larger differences. When two fish meet, both perform a JAR, so their frequencies move apart until the difference is large enough to stop the interference.

### 15.4.2. How Does the Fish Know the Sign of Δf?

To shift its frequency in the right direction, the fish must know whether the neighbor's frequency is higher or lower than its own. An engineer would compare the neighbor's frequency with the fish's own pacemaker frequency, which the brain knows. Heiligenberg and his colleagues showed with a series of elegant experiments that the fish does not do this.

*Experiment 1: replacing the fish's discharge.* The electric organ of *Eigenmannia* is myogenic, so injecting curare silences it while the pacemaker continues to fire; the pacemaker frequency can be monitored from the electromotor nerve. The silenced discharge was then replaced by a sine wave of similar amplitude and frequency applied through an electrode in the mouth and one at the tip of the tail, which produces a field of nearly natural geometry, and a second sine wave, the neighbor's mimic, was applied through electrodes on either side of the fish. The curarized fish performed a normal JAR: its pacemaker frequency moved away from the neighbor's mimic. The decisive variant was to set the replacement discharge to a frequency far from the pacemaker frequency, for example $50\,\mathrm{Hz}$ below it. The fish then shifted its pacemaker in the direction appropriate for the relation between the neighbor's mimic and the *replacement* signal, not its own pacemaker. The fish uses the signals in the water, not an internal reference.

*Experiment 2: where must the signals mix?* When the two sine waves were added electronically and applied through the same pair of electrodes, so that the mixture had the same geometry everywhere on the body, the fish showed no JAR, although each receptor received the same beat as before. A JAR occurs only when the two signals mix in different proportions on different parts of the body, as they always do in nature, since the fish's own field leaves its body nearly perpendicularly everywhere while the neighbor's field comes from one side. In a two-compartment chamber with an electrically tight seal around the fish's body, jamming signals presented only to the head, where the fish's own signal was excluded, elicited no JAR; a JAR appeared when the fish's own signal and the jamming signal were allowed to mix in at least one region.

*The solution.* Write the fish's own signal at a point on the skin as $\cos(2\pi ft)$ and the neighbor's as $m\cos(2\pi(f + \Delta f)t)$, with $m < 1$ the local mixing ratio. Relative to the fish's own signal, the mixture can be written as the complex amplitude

$$
z(t) = 1 + m\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}2\pi\Delta f\,t},
\tag{15.1}
$$
whose modulus is the instantaneous amplitude and whose argument is the instantaneous phase relative to the fish's own discharge. Both oscillate at the beat frequency $\lvert\Delta f\rvert$. When the neighbor is higher ($\Delta f > 0$), the phase advances while the amplitude falls from its maximum and lags while the amplitude rises; when the neighbor is lower ($\Delta f < 0$), the relation is reversed. In a plane with phase on the horizontal axis and amplitude on the vertical axis, the point $(\arg z, \lvert z\rvert)$ therefore runs around a loop once per beat cycle, clockwise for $\Delta f > 0$ and counterclockwise for $\Delta f < 0$. The sense of rotation tells the sign of $\Delta f$. Heiligenberg summarized the rule: if a rise in amplitude coincides with a phase lag, the neighbor's frequency is higher and the fish should lower its own; if a rise coincides with a phase lead, the fish should raise its frequency.

The amplitude can be measured at a single point, but phase must be measured relative to something, and the fish has no pure copy of its own signal. It measures phase *differences between body regions*: comparing a region where the neighbor's contribution is strong with one where it is weak gives a differential phase that oscillates in the same way as the phase relative to the pure signal, with the same sense of rotation as long as the fish's own signal dominates in both regions. Since this is the case over most of the body, a "vote" among the comparisons of many pairs of regions yields the correct sign. This explains why the JAR requires different mixing ratios on different parts of the body (Experiment 2) and why no internal reference is needed (Experiment 1).

## 15.5. The Jamming Avoidance Response: Neural Implementation

### 15.5.1. From Receptors to Midbrain

The behavioral analysis predicted the neural computation: the brain must extract amplitude modulations at each point of the body, compare the timing of the signal between points, combine the two and decide on the sense of rotation. Heiligenberg, Bastian, Carr, Rose, Kawasaki, Metzner and others traced each step (Heiligenberg 1991):

1. *Receptors.* T-units encode the timing of each cycle of the local signal, and P-units its amplitude. During jamming, P-unit firing rises and falls at the beat frequency, and the timing of T-unit spikes shifts back and forth by some microseconds.
2. *Electrosensory lateral line lobe (ELL).* The receptor afferents project to the ELL of the hindbrain, where the body surface is represented in three parallel somatotopic maps. T-unit afferents converge onto spherical cells through electrical synapses; because a spherical cell fires only when many afferents arrive together, it encodes phase with less jitter than any single afferent. P-unit afferents excite basilar pyramidal cells directly (E-units, which fire when amplitude rises) and inhibit non-basilar pyramidal cells through interneurons (I-units, which fire when amplitude falls). Phase and amplitude are thus processed in separate, parallel streams.
3. *Torus semicircularis.* In the layered midbrain torus, spherical cell axons terminate in lamina 6, where neurons compare the timing of the signal between different parts of the body, computing differential phase with a resolution of about $10\,\mu\mathrm{s}$. In the laminae above and below, the amplitude information from the pyramidal cells converges with the phase information, and some neurons in deeper laminae fire more for one sign of $\Delta f$ than for the other. Their sign selectivity, however, depends on the orientation of the jamming field relative to the body, and is therefore still ambiguous.
4. *Nucleus electrosensorius.* In the diencephalic nucleus electrosensorius (nE), which receives input from many toral layers and many body regions, neurons are sign-selective independently of the geometry of the jamming field, and their sensitivity to phase modulations reaches about $1\,\mu\mathrm{s}$, close to the behavioral threshold. Behaviorally, *Eigenmannia* detects phase modulations of less than a microsecond, a temporal hyperacuity far finer than the jitter of any single receptor (Rose and Heiligenberg 1985), achieved by pooling over thousands of receptors.

### 15.5.2. From Decision to Discharge

The nucleus electrosensorius contains two small regions with opposite effects (Metzner and colleagues). Glutamate injected into the dorsal region, nE↑, produces smooth rises of the discharge frequency, and into the ventral region, nE↓, smooth falls, with time courses like those of the JAR; bilateral lesions of these regions abolish the frequency shifts of the JAR. The two pathways then diverge:

- nE↑ excites a subdivision of the central posterior/prepacemaker nucleus of the thalamus (CP/PPn-G, for gradual frequency rises), whose neurons excite the pacemaker cells of the pacemaker nucleus through AMPA-type glutamate receptors. Blocking AMPA receptors in the pacemaker nucleus abolishes the frequency rises of the JAR.
- nE↓ inhibits, through GABA, the sublemniscal prepacemaker nucleus (SPPn) of the midbrain, which provides a tonic excitation to the relay cells of the pacemaker nucleus through NMDA receptors. Lesions of the SPPn abolish JAR decreases and lower the resting frequency; injecting GABA into the SPPn lowers the frequency, as does stimulating nE↓. The frequency falls by disinhibition: nE↓ removes a tonic excitation.
- The pacemaker cells and relay cells of the pacemaker nucleus are strongly electrically coupled; the relay cells drive the spinal electromotor neurons one to one, and these drive the electric organ.

### 15.5.3. Lessons of the JAR

Three conclusions of the JAR analysis have influenced neuroscience well beyond electric fish.

*Distributed computation.* There is no "JAR neuron". The sign of $\Delta f$ is computed by a population of comparisons between many pairs of body regions, each of which may be wrong where the neighbor's signal is strong, and the decision emerges from their majority. Heiligenberg described it as a democratic decision. The precision of the behavior, sub-microsecond timing, emerges from pooling imprecise elements, a principle also found in other hyperacute senses ([7.18.11](/7.18.11)).

*Parallel streams and maps.* Time and amplitude are processed in separate channels and combined only at a later stage, an organization strikingly similar to the separate time and intensity pathways of the barn owl's auditory system ([7.16.24](/7.16.24#sec-24.5.1)), discovered in the same years.

*Evolutionary tinkering.* An engineer would have used the pacemaker frequency as a reference, which the fish's brain has; evolution built the JAR instead from components that already existed. The knifefish *Sternopygus*, which does not perform a JAR, has neurons that encode amplitude and differential phase, which it uses to sense moving objects. The JAR appears to have been constructed by connecting such pre-existing electrolocation circuits to the prepacemaker nuclei. The African wave-type frankfish *Gymnarchus*, a mormyriform, independently evolved a JAR that follows the same algorithm, using amplitude and differential phase, although the neural structures that perform the computation differ (Kawasaki 1993): convergence at the level of the computation, not of the anatomy. Not all wave fishes respond in the same way: *Apteronotus* responds to jamming mainly by raising its frequency, whatever the sign of $\Delta f$.

## 15.6. Electrocommunication

### 15.6.1. Signals in the Discharge

Electric discharges are also social signals. In wave-type knifefishes, frequency differs between species and, in many species, between the sexes: in the brown ghost knifefish *Apteronotus leptorhynchus* males discharge at higher frequencies than females, whereas in *Sternopygus* males discharge at lower frequencies, and the difference is set by steroid hormones acting on the pacemaker. Males of *Apteronotus* produce *chirps*, brief rises in frequency of up to several hundred hertz lasting tens of milliseconds, during courtship and aggressive encounters; chirps are controlled by a separate subdivision of the prepacemaker nucleus (CP/PPn-C) and are elicited by the beat of another fish's discharge. Gradual frequency rises, lasting seconds, occur during aggression.

In pulse fishes the waveform and the pattern of intervals carry the message. Mormyrid discharges differ between species in duration and shape, and in a group of mormyrids in Gabon (*Paramormyrops*), species have diverged rapidly in their discharge waveforms while changing little in morphology; since mormyrids recognize mates by discharge waveform, divergence of the signal may have driven speciation (Arnegard and colleagues 2010). Males of many species have longer discharges than females, a difference also induced by androgens.

### 15.6.2. Eavesdropping Predators

Electric signals can be intercepted. Electric eels and some catfishes, which have ampullary receptors sensitive to low frequencies, prey on weakly electric fishes and detect their discharges. Stoddard (1999) showed that the discharges of the pulse fish *Brachyhypopomus* are biphasic, with a second phase that cancels the low-frequency (and direct current) content of the first, which shifts the energy of the signal upward, out of the sensitivity range of the predators' ampullary receptors but within that of the fish's own tuberous receptors. A signal shaped by the receiver's sense organs and by the eavesdroppers' is an example of the processes treated in [7.18.38](/7.18.38) and [7.18.39](/7.18.39).

## 15.7. Telling Self from Other: Corollary Discharges in Mormyrids

An animal that produces a signal and senses it has to distinguish the consequences of its own actions from events in the world. Mormyrids solve the problem with an **efference copy** ([7.16.29](/7.16.29#sec-29.3.2)): a copy of the motor command that triggers each discharge, called a **corollary discharge**, is sent from the command nucleus to the electrosensory regions of the brain. It is used in two ways (Bell; Bell and Grant 1989):

- In the *knollenorgan* pathway, which detects the discharges of other fish, the corollary discharge inhibits the first central station for a few milliseconds after each of the fish's own discharges, exactly when the knollenorgans are stimulated by it. The fish is deaf to itself and hears only others.
- In the *mormyromast* pathway, used for active electrolocation, the fish needs to sense its own discharge, but only the changes in it caused by objects. Here the corollary discharge provides a temporal reference: the ELL contains cells that receive the corollary discharge through parallel fibers, as in the cerebellum, and learn a *negative image* of the expected sensory input after each discharge (Bell 1981). If an electrosensory stimulus is repeatedly paired with the command, the cell's response to it gradually disappears; when the stimulus is then omitted, the cell responds to the command alone with the opposite of its former response, revealing the learned image. The plasticity at the parallel-fiber synapses is anti-Hebbian: a synapse active just before a postsynaptic spike is weakened (Bell and colleagues 1997). The parallel fibers come from granule cells whose responses are spread in time after each command, providing a temporal basis from which any expected time course can be built (Kennedy and colleagues 2014).

The adaptive negative image subtracts everything that is predictable from the fish's own discharge and motion, so that the response of the ELL signals what is new, a prey or an obstacle. The same principle applies to the passive ampullary system, where corollary discharges related to the fish's own tail movements cancel the electrosensory consequences of swimming (Requarth and Sawtell 2014). The cerebellum-like structure of the ELL, and the similarity of its learning rule to that proposed for the cerebellum, link the electric sense to the general problem of predicting the sensory consequences of one's own actions ([7.16.29](/7.16.29)).

## Questions

1. Why can a shark detect a buried flatfish but not a flatfish lying a meter away, even though the flatfish's field is detectable at the shark's thresholds at some distance? Use Example 15.1.1.

   **Answer** The field of a small dipole source falls with the cube of the distance. A field of about $10\,\mu\mathrm{V\,cm^{-1}}$ at $1\,\mathrm{cm}$ falls to the shark's threshold of about $5\,\mathrm{nV\,cm^{-1}}$ at about $13\,\mathrm{cm}$; at $1\,\mathrm{m}$ it is $10^{6}$ times smaller than at $1\,\mathrm{cm}$, that is, about $10\,\mathrm{pV\,cm^{-1}}$, far below threshold. Even a much stronger source gains little range, because range grows only with the cube root of source strength. Electroreception therefore guides the final strike, after the shark has approached by smell, vision or the lateral line.

2. In Heiligenberg's replacement experiment, the curarized fish's discharge was replaced by a sine wave $50\,\mathrm{Hz}$ below its pacemaker frequency, and the neighbor's mimic was set $4\,\mathrm{Hz}$ above the replacement. In which direction did the pacemaker frequency shift, and what does this show?

   **Answer** The neighbor's mimic is $4\,\mathrm{Hz}$ above the replacement signal (but $46\,\mathrm{Hz}$ below the pacemaker). The fish lowered its pacemaker frequency, the response appropriate for a neighbor that is higher than the signal the fish senses as its own. Had the fish compared the neighbor's frequency with its pacemaker, it would have raised its frequency (or not responded, since $46\,\mathrm{Hz}$ is outside the jamming range). The result shows that the JAR is computed entirely from the electric signals at the body surface, without reference to the pacemaker.

3. Using Eq. (15.1), show that for $\Delta f > 0$ the phase leads while the amplitude falls after a beat maximum. Why does the fish need differential phase rather than phase?

   **Answer** At $t = 0$, $z = 1 + m$ is real: amplitude maximal, phase zero. For small $t > 0$, $z \approx 1 + m(1 + \mathrm{i}2\pi\Delta f t)$, whose argument is approximately $2\pi m\Delta f t/(1 + m) > 0$ for $\Delta f > 0$ (a phase lead), while the modulus decreases because the second term rotates away from the first. Phase is defined only relative to a reference, and the fish has no access to its pure signal: everywhere on its body the neighbor's signal is mixed in. Comparing the timing at two regions with different mixing ratios gives a differential phase, which behaves like the phase relative to the pure signal as long as one region is much less contaminated than the other; summing the evidence over many pairs of regions gives a reliable sign.

4. Why would the electronically mixed stimulus in Experiment 2 fail to elicit a JAR even though every receptor experiences a beat?

   **Answer** When both signals are applied through the same electrodes, the mixture has the same geometry everywhere: the ratio of neighbor to own signal is the same at all points of the skin. The beat then produces identical amplitude and phase modulations at all receptors, so the differential phase between any two regions is zero at all times. The amplitude modulation alone does not reveal the sign of $\Delta f$ (it is the same for $+\Delta f$ and $-\Delta f$), and without a differential phase the sense of rotation cannot be determined. The fish therefore receives no information about the sign and does not respond.

5. (Open question) A mormyrid's knollenorgan pathway is inhibited for a few milliseconds after each of its own discharges. What would happen if a neighbor discharged at nearly the same moment, and how might a fish that needs to communicate respond?

   **Answer** A neighbor's discharge arriving within the window of corollary-discharge inhibition would not be detected, because the pathway is blocked while the fish's own discharge stimulates the receptors. Communication is impaired when discharges coincide. Pulse mormyrids indeed show echo responses and preferred latency responses: a fish replies to a neighbor's discharge at a characteristic short latency, outside its own inhibition window, or avoids discharging at the moment it expects the neighbor's discharge. A test would be to play back pulses at controlled times relative to the fish's own discharges and record knollenorgan pathway responses and changes in the fish's discharge timing.
