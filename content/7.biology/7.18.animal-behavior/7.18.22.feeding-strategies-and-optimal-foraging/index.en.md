Every animal must eat, and almost every animal must also avoid being eaten. This chapter and the next two treat these two problems as decisions made by individuals: what to eat, where and how long to search for it, how to balance food against danger, and how to escape predators. Here we survey the ways in which animals obtain food, from grazing and hunting to farming and parasitism, and then develop the economic theory that has made foraging the best-studied problem of behavioral ecology, optimal foraging theory. The theory applies the **optimality model** introduced in [7.18.2](/7.18.2#sec-2.4.4) to the simplest of all currencies, the rate at which food is gained, and makes quantitative predictions about which prey a predator should accept and when it should leave a patch of food. The next chapter, [7.18.23](/7.18.23), adds uncertainty, the forager's internal state and the risk of predation; [7.18.24](/7.18.24) turns to the prey's side of the encounter; the competition among foragers, which makes the best decision depend on what others do, is treated with game theory in [7.18.25](/7.18.25).

The boundary with ecology should be stated at the outset. The consequences of foraging decisions for populations, the functional and numerical responses of predators and the dynamics of predator and prey, are treated in [7.17.39](/7.17.39#sec-39.1.3) (Holling's functional response) and [7.10.17](/7.10.17) (Lotka–Volterra dynamics), and will be developed in the ecology course; this chapter is about the individual forager.

## 22.1. Ways of Making a Living

### 22.1.1. Four Kinds of Consumer

Animals that feed on other living organisms can be classified by two properties of the interaction (Thompson 1982): its *lethality*, the probability that an attacked victim dies, and its *intimacy*, how long and how closely a consumer is associated with a single victim, which is roughly the inverse of the number of victims it attacks in its lifetime. Four combinations give four kinds of consumer (figure below).

| Kind | Victims in a lifetime | Effect on each victim | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| True predators | Many | Killed, usually soon after the attack, and eaten wholly or largely | Wolves, hawks, spiders, dragonflies, seed-eating rodents (seed predators) |
| Grazers and browsers | Many | Part consumed, rarely killed | Cattle and geese on grass (grazers), deer and giraffes on shrubs and trees (browsers), blood-feeding mosquitoes and vampire bats |
| Parasites | One or few | Lives on or in the host for a long time, usually without killing it | Ticks, lice, tapeworms, blood flukes, pathogens |
| Parasitoids | One per larva | Lives in or on the host and eventually kills it | Ichneumonid and braconid wasps, tachinid flies |

**Parasitoids** ([7.17.39](/7.17.39)) straddle the boundary between parasites and predators: the adult female lays eggs in or on a host, the larva feeds as a parasite, and the host dies when the larva completes its development. Herbivores are often divided further by what they eat: grazers eat grasses and other low herbaceous plants, browsers the leaves and twigs of woody plants, granivores seeds and frugivores fruit. Seed-eaters are ecologically predators, because they kill the embryo they eat; frugivores are usually mutualists, since a fruit is a plant's offer of food in exchange for the dispersal of its seeds.

![Four kinds of consumer arranged by the lethality of the interaction (the probability that an attacked victim is killed) and its intimacy (the time a consumer spends with one victim, roughly the inverse of the number of victims attacked in its lifetime). Grazers and browsers take part of many victims and rarely kill them; predators attack many victims and kill each; parasites live on or in one or a few hosts, usually without killing them; parasitoids develop in a single host and always kill it. Intermediate cases lie along the boundaries: seed predators kill the seeds they eat, and castrating parasites leave the host alive but end its reproduction.](./plots/consumer-types.py)

The four categories differ in the decisions that dominate their foraging. A predator must find, recognize, catch and subdue mobile prey that try to escape; a grazer faces abundant but poor food that must be processed in large quantities, and its main decisions concern where to feed and how to balance the intake of energy, protein, minerals and plant toxins; a parasite's foraging problem is largely the choice of a host and the defeat of its defenses; and a parasitoid female's decisions concern which host to attack and how many eggs to lay in it ([7.17.39](/7.17.39#sec-39.2.5)).

### 22.1.2. Generalists and Specialists

Diets range from extremely broad to extremely narrow. Rats, crows and raccoons eat almost anything; koalas eat the leaves of a few species of *Eucalyptus*, the giant panda bamboo, and many herbivorous insects a single genus of plant. Specialization is often the result of overcoming a defense that other consumers cannot overcome, which leaves the specialist an abundant, uncontested resource. The chisel-toothed kangaroo rat (*Dipodomys microps*) of the Great Basin desert uses its flattened lower incisors to shave off the salt-laden outer tissue of saltbush leaves (*Atriplex*) and eats the starchy inner tissue that other rodents cannot exploit. The larvae of the bruchid beetle *Caryedes brasiliensis* develop in the seeds of a tropical legume that contain large amounts of L-canavanine, a toxic analog of arginine that is incorporated into the proteins of other insects; the beetle's arginyl-tRNA synthetase discriminates against canavanine, and the larva degrades the compound and uses its nitrogen. Specialists pay for their advantage with dependence on one resource and with the risk that an **evolutionary arms race** with it ([7.10.17](/7.10.17)) will continue.

Diet breadth also varies within species. Individuals of a generalist population are often specialists: in sea otters (*Enhydra lutris*) on the California coast, each individual concentrates on a few of the many available prey types (abalone, urchins, crabs, snails), and daughters tend to share their mothers' specializations, so that the population's broad diet is a mosaic of narrow individual diets (Estes et al. 2003). Such individual specialization is common when different prey require different skills, which take time to learn ([7.18.46](/7.18.46)).

### 22.1.3. The Foraging Cycle

Whatever the diet, foraging can be divided into a sequence of phases, each with its own decisions:

1. *Search*: where to look, how fast to move and when to give up a place (patch choice and patch leaving).
2. *Encounter and recognition*: whether an item is food and whether it is worth taking (prey choice).
3. *Pursuit and capture*: whether to attack, and how.
4. *Handling*: subduing, opening, eating and digesting the item, and whether to eat it on the spot or carry it to a safe place, a nest or a store.

The time budget of the cycle is the basis of every foraging model: time spent handling one item cannot be spent searching for the next. When the cycle is repeated, the rate of food intake equals the food obtained per cycle divided by the time per cycle, the logic of Holling's disc equation ([7.17.39](/7.17.39#sec-39.1.3)), which we shall use in §22.5.

## 22.2. Foraging Skills and Hunting Strategies

### 22.2.1. Sitting and Waiting or Searching Actively

Predators can wait for prey to come to them or go out to find it. In a classic comparison of lizards in the Kalahari Desert, Huey and Pianka (1981) found that species fall into two modes. *Sit-and-wait foraging* lizards, such as the lacertid *Pedioplanis lineoocellata*, perch motionless, scan their surroundings and dash out at prey that move past; they eat mostly active prey such as beetles and grasshoppers, have low daily energy expenditure, and are cryptic and relatively safe from predators that hunt by movement. Widely foraging lizards, such as the lacertid *Heliobolus lugubris*, move almost continuously and probe the substrate; they find sedentary or hidden prey, including clumped termites that sit-and-wait foragers rarely encounter, have higher metabolic rates and endurance, and are more exposed to their own predators. The two modes form syndromes: diet, morphology, physiology, sensory systems (vision for sit-and-wait foragers, chemoreception with a forked tongue for active foragers) and predation risk change together.

Within these modes, animals adjust their search to the distribution of food. A ladybird larva or a thrush that finds a prey item slows down and turns more often, area-restricted search ([7.17.39](/7.17.39#sec-39.1.3)), which pays when prey are clumped. When prey are sparse and patchily distributed at unknown locations, mathematical models show that a search path with many short moves and rare very long ones (a Lévy walk) can find more targets than a simple random walk, and the movement paths of some marine predators, including sharks, tuna and albatrosses, have this statistical structure (Humphries et al. 2010); but similar distributions can also arise from simpler rules applied to a patchy environment, and the claim that animals have evolved Lévy search strategies remains disputed.

### 22.2.2. Traps, Lures and Aggressive Mimicry

Some predators make devices that capture prey. Orb webs, sheet webs and the silk-lined funnels of spiders, the conical sand pits of antlion larvae ([7.17.39](/7.17.39#sec-39.1.1)), the sticky threads of glowworm larvae (*Arachnocampa*) hanging from cave ceilings and the mucous nets of some marine snails and tunicates all let the predator wait while its trap does the searching. Building a trap is an investment, and orb-weaving spiders adjust it: they build larger webs, or move their web sites, after periods of low capture.

Other predators attract prey by deceit.

**Definition 22.2.1 (Aggressive Mimicry)** **Aggressive mimicry** is the resemblance of a predator (or parasite) to a signal or object that is attractive or harmless to its prey (or host), so that the victim approaches the predator or allows it to approach.

The best-known examples exploit the communication systems of the prey. Females of fireflies of the genus *Photuris* answer the flash patterns of males of other genera (*Photinus*) with the timed response flash of a *Photinus* female and eat the males that land beside them; some *Photuris* females can produce the responses of several prey species (Lloyd 1965, 1975). By eating *Photinus*, the "femmes fatales" also acquire lucibufagins, defensive steroids that *Photuris* cannot make. Bolas spiders (*Mastophora*) do not build webs; at night a female hangs from a horizontal line and swings a single thread with a sticky droplet at its end, and she emits a blend of volatile compounds that matches the sex pheromone of the moths she catches; one species changes its blend during the night as the moths whose males fly early are replaced by others that fly late (Haynes et al. 2002). Anglerfishes wave a modified dorsal fin ray (the illicium) tipped with a lure, which in deep-sea species glows with symbiotic bacteria; the alligator snapping turtle (*Macrochelys temminckii*) lies open-mouthed on the bottom and wriggles a pink, worm-like appendage of its tongue; and many predatory fishes, including the blennies that resemble the cleaner wrasse *Labroides dimidiatus*, approach their victims by mimicking a harmless species ([7.18.24](/7.18.24#sec-24.4)). Ambush predators that are camouflaged, such as the stargazers (Uranoscopidae) that lie buried in sand with only the eyes and mouth exposed, or the crab spiders that match the flowers on which they wait for pollinators, combine waiting with concealment.

### 22.2.3. Hunting Together

Hunting in groups lets predators take prey that a single hunter could not catch or subdue, and sometimes increases the rate of capture per individual. Wolves, African wild dogs, spotted hyenas and lions take ungulates much larger than themselves; Harris's hawks (*Parabuteo unicinctus*) in New Mexico hunt jackrabbits and cottontails in parties of up to six, flushing prey from cover and attacking in relays, and capture success increases with party size (Bednarz 1988). The degree of coordination varies. In the chimpanzees of the Taï Forest in Côte d'Ivoire, hunters of red colobus monkeys take distinct roles (drivers, blockers, chasers and ambushers), and individuals tend to perform the same role in different hunts (Boesch 2002); in a population of bottlenose dolphins off Cedar Key, Florida, one individual of each group consistently acts as a "driver" that herds fish toward a "barrier" of other dolphins, from which the fish leap into the air and are caught (Gazda et al. 2005). Humpback whales release curtains of bubbles in a spiral that concentrates schooling fish, and then lunge up through them.

Group hunting does not necessarily raise the intake of each hunter. In lions of the Serengeti, food intake per individual was higher for solitary females and for groups of five or more than for intermediate groups, and lions often hunted in groups when a single hunter would have done as well; females appear to live in groups for other reasons, such as the defense of cubs and territory (Packer et al. 1990). The costs and benefits of grouping, including the sharing of food and the effects of group size, are analyzed in [7.18.27](/7.18.27).

### 22.2.4. Recognizing and Attacking Prey

A predator must decide quickly whether a moving object is prey and how to intercept it. The neural mechanisms of prey recognition in toads and of interception by dragonflies, which steer using an internal model of their own movement and the prey's, are described in [7.18.13](/7.18.13). Recognition is shaped by learning: predators form search images for common prey and learn to avoid noxious ones, processes treated from the prey's point of view in [7.18.24](/7.18.24#sec-24.2). The method of attack is matched to the prey's defenses: snakes strike and release venomous prey that might injure them and follow their scent trail, mongooses attack the head of a snake, and many birds carry hard-shelled prey into the air and drop them onto rocks, as in the crows analyzed in [7.18.2](/7.18.2#sec-2.4.4).

## 22.3. Farming and Other Partnerships

A few animals produce their own food by cultivating other organisms, a form of **mutualism** in which the farmer provides habitat, food and protection and harvests part of the crop. Farming provides a supply of food that is independent of fluctuations in the environment, but it requires the farmer to defend the crop against pests and competitors.

- *Fungus-growing ants.* About 250 species of attine ants of the New World tropics cultivate fungi in their nests, a habit that arose once, about 55–65 million years ago. The leaf-cutter ants (*Atta* and *Acromyrmex*), which appeared much later, cut fresh leaves, carry them to the nest, chew them into a paste, and plant on it the hyphae of a basidiomycete fungus, *Leucoagaricus gongylophorus*, which produces swollen hyphal tips (gongylidia) rich in carbohydrates and lipids on which the ants and their larvae feed. A mature *Atta* colony with several million workers can strip as much vegetation as a large mammal. The gardens are attacked by a specialized parasitic fungus, *Escovopsis*, and many attines carry on their cuticle actinomycete bacteria (*Pseudonocardia*) that produce antifungal compounds suppressing it (Currie et al. 1999), a mutualism of three partners against a fourth.
- *Fungus-growing termites.* Termites of the subfamily Macrotermitinae in Africa and Asia grow *Termitomyces* fungi on combs built from their own feces, made of partly digested plant material; the fungus breaks down lignin and the termites eat the fungal nodules and the processed comb ([7.17.38](/7.17.38)).
- *Ambrosia beetles* bore tunnels into wood and grow fungi carried from tree to tree in special pockets of the cuticle (mycangia).
- *Ants that tend insects.* Many ants tend aphids, scale insects and leafhoppers for their **honeydew**, the sugary excreta of phloem-feeders: they stroke the aphids with their antennae to solicit droplets, protect the colonies from ladybirds and parasitoids, move them to new plants and sometimes eat a fraction of them. Ant-tended aphid colonies grow faster and survive better than untended ones, although tending can reduce the aphids' own growth and dispersal.
- *Ants and lycaenid caterpillars.* The caterpillars of many blue butterflies (Lycaenidae) secrete sugars and amino acids from a dorsal nectary organ and produce substrate-borne sounds, in return for which ants protect them. Some species have turned this relationship into social parasitism: final-instar caterpillars of the large blues (*Phengaris*, formerly *Maculinea*) are carried by *Myrmica* ants into their nests, where they either prey on ant brood or are fed by the workers like ant larvae; caterpillars of *P. rebeli* reproduce the sounds made by the ant queen and are treated preferentially by workers (Barbero et al. 2009).
- *Farming fish.* Territorial damselfishes on coral reefs weed their territories of unwanted algae and defend them against grazers; in *Stegastes nigricans* the result is a nearly pure stand of a filamentous red alga (*Polysiphonia*) that grows only inside damselfish territories (Hata and Kato 2006).

A different kind of partnership is the retention of the chloroplasts of algal food. The sacoglossan sea slug *Elysia chlorotica* sucks out the contents of the alga *Vaucheria litorea* and keeps the chloroplasts functional in the cells of its digestive gland for months, a phenomenon called kleptoplasty; the slug becomes green and leaf-shaped and can survive long periods without food. How much the stolen chloroplasts contribute to the slug's nutrition is still debated: in some sacoglossans, starved individuals kept in darkness survive about as long as those kept in light, which suggests that the plastids serve mainly as a store of food rather than as a source of photosynthate (Christa et al. 2014).

## 22.4. Parasites That Manipulate Their Hosts

### 22.4.1. Examples and Mechanisms

Parasites must reach new hosts, and many do so by altering the behavior of the host they are in, a phenomenon called **host manipulation** ([7.17.39](/7.17.39#sec-39.2.4)). The changes are often precisely those that increase transmission:

| Parasite | Host | Change in behavior | Benefit to the parasite |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fungus *Ophiocordyceps* (the *O. unilateralis* complex) | Carpenter ants (*Camponotus*) | Infected ants leave the colony's foraging trails, climb vegetation, and bite into the underside of a leaf vein about 25 cm above the ground, where they die with their mandibles locked | Microclimate suited to fungal growth; spores fall on the trail below |
| Protozoan *Toxoplasma gondii* | Rats and mice | Loss of innate aversion to the odor of cat urine, even attraction to it | Transmission to cats, the definitive host |
| Hairworm *Spinochordodes tellinii* (Nematomorpha) | Grasshoppers and crickets | Infected insects jump into water | The adult worm emerges in water, where it mates |
| Trematode *Leucochloridium paradoxum* | Land snails | Pulsating, brightly banded sporocysts in the tentacles; infected snails move to exposed, lit sites | Transmission to birds that eat the "caterpillar-like" tentacles |
| Wasp *Hymenoepimecis argyraphaga* (parasitoid) | Orb-weaving spider *Leucauge argyra* | On the night before it is killed, the spider builds a special web of a few strong lines | A durable support for the wasp's cocoon |
| Wasp *Glyptapanteles* (parasitoid) | Geometrid caterpillars | After the wasp larvae emerge, the caterpillar stands over their pupae and swings its head violently at approaching predators | Pupal mortality halved (Grosman et al. 2008) |

In carpenter ants killed by *Ophiocordyceps* in Thai rain forest, the bites were concentrated at a height of about 25 cm, where temperature and humidity were favorable for fungal growth, and infected ants bit at around solar noon in synchrony (Andersen et al. 2009; Hughes et al. 2011). Surprisingly, the fungus does not invade the ant's brain: three-dimensional reconstruction of infected ants showed fungal cells forming a network around and within the muscles of the body and the mandibles, while the brain remained free of hyphae (Fredericksen et al. 2017). The emerald jewel wasp *Ampulex compressa* manipulates its cockroach host directly through the nervous system: the female stings first into the thorax, causing brief paralysis of the front legs, and then twice into the head ganglia, injecting venom that makes the cockroach groom for half an hour and then become hypokinetic, so that the wasp can lead it by an antenna into a burrow, like a dog on a leash, where it lays an egg on it (Libersat 2003).

### 22.4.2. Is It Adaptive Manipulation?

A change in behavior after infection is not necessarily an adaptation of the parasite. It may be a side effect of disease, such as the lethargy caused by any infection, or a defense of the host, as when sick bees leave the hive to die or infected flies seek higher temperatures (behavioral fever). Poulin (1995) proposed criteria for adaptive manipulation: the change should be complex rather than a simple loss of function, should arise in parasite lineages from ancestors that did not cause it, should be consistent with the parasite's mode of transmission, and should increase the parasite's **fitness**. The best-supported cases are those in which infected hosts behave in ways that healthy hosts never do, that are precisely directed toward the next host or habitat of the parasite, and in which the parasite has been shown to transmit better as a result: rats infected with *Toxoplasma* spent more time near cat odor than uninfected rats in outdoor enclosures, a striking reversal of an innate fear (Berdoy et al. 2000).

### 22.4.3. Hosts Fight Back: Fruit Flies and Parasitoid Wasps

Hosts are selected to detect and avoid parasites, and the interaction between *Drosophila melanogaster* and its parasitoid wasps (*Leptopilina*, figitid wasps that lay eggs in fly larvae) has become a model for the behavioral defenses of hosts.

- *Escape.* When a wasp drills into it with the ovipositor, a fly larva rolls laterally, a corkscrew movement that can dislodge the ovipositor. Rolling is triggered by a class of multidendritic sensory neurons in the body wall that also respond to noxious heat and harsh touch, the larval nociceptors (Hwang et al. 2007).
- *Physiological defense.* Larvae that are parasitized produce lamellocytes, blood cells that surround the wasp egg and melanize it (encapsulation).
- *Self-medication.* Fly larvae tolerate much higher concentrations of ethanol, which occurs in rotting fruit, than wasp larvae do. Parasitized larvae prefer food containing ethanol, and ethanol consumption kills many of the wasp larvae inside them (Milan et al. 2012).
- *Protecting offspring.* Adult females that have seen female wasps lay most of their eggs on food containing ethanol, and they also reduce the number of eggs laid; the response depends on vision and persists for days after the wasps are removed (Kacsoh et al. 2013).
- *Avoidance by smell.* Flies detect the odor of *Leptopilina* through a dedicated olfactory channel: two odorant receptors, Or49a and Or85f, respond to wasp-specific compounds, and flies avoid places that smell of wasps for feeding, oviposition and resting (Ebrahim et al. 2015).

These responses show that the host's behavior is as much part of the interaction as the parasite's manipulation: each party evolves in response to the other, a case of **coevolution** ([7.10.17](/7.10.17)).

## 22.5. Optimal Foraging Theory

### 22.5.1. Currencies, Constraints and Decisions

The diversity of feeding strategies might seem to defy any general theory. Yet the foraging decisions of very different animals have the same logical structure, and in the 1960s and 1970s MacArthur and Pianka (1966), Emlen (1966) and Charnov (1976) began to analyze them as problems of economics.

**Definition 22.5.1 (Optimal Foraging Theory)** **Optimal foraging theory** is the application of optimality models to foraging decisions: it predicts which choices of diet, patch, time allocation or movement maximize a specified currency, under specified constraints, on the hypothesis that **natural selection** has favored foragers whose decisions approximately maximize that currency.

An optimal foraging model has three components (Stephens and Krebs 1986):

- *Decision*: what the forager chooses, such as whether to eat an item, how long to stay in a patch or how many prey to carry home.
- *Currency*: the quantity assumed to be maximized, the link between the decision and fitness. The commonest currency is the long-term net rate of energy intake, energy gained minus energy spent, divided by the time taken. Others are energetic efficiency (energy gained per energy spent), the probability of avoiding starvation, and the intake of a limiting nutrient.
- *Constraints*: the limits on what the forager can do and know, from the time needed to travel or handle prey and the capacity of the gut to the accuracy with which it can recognize prey.

The use of the net rate as a currency rests on a simple argument: an animal that gains energy faster has more time and energy left over for other activities that contribute to fitness, such as defending a territory, attracting mates and avoiding predators. The argument fails when time is not limiting, when the forager must meet a fixed requirement under uncertainty ([7.18.23](/7.18.23)), or when foraging itself shortens life. The theory does not assume that animals calculate. It assumes that selection has given them decision rules that produce approximately the best outcome in the environments in which they evolved, *rules of thumb* such as "leave a patch when no prey has been found for a fixed time". Asking which simple rules can approximate an optimal solution, and what they cost in performance, is part of the theory.

### 22.5.2. The Prey Model

Consider a predator that searches for prey of several types, which it encounters one at a time and at random. When it encounters an item it can eat it, spending a handling time, or reject it and continue searching. Which types should it accept?

**Definition 22.5.2 (Profitability and the Prey Model)** For a prey type $i$ with net energy content $E_i$ and handling time $h_i$, the **profitability** is $E_i/h_i$, the rate of energy gain during handling. The **prey model** (optimal diet model) is the model of prey choice in which a forager encounters prey types sequentially, as independent Poisson processes with rates $\lambda_i$ while it searches, and chooses which types to accept so as to maximize its long-term rate of energy intake.

Let the types be numbered in order of decreasing profitability, $E_1/h_1 > E_2/h_2 > \cdots$. If the forager eats all types of a set $D$ whenever it meets them, then in a long foraging period of total time $T$, spent in search for $T_s$ and in handling for the rest, it encounters $\lambda_i T_s$ items of each type $i$ in $D$, so that $T = T_s(1 + \sum_{i\in D}\lambda_i h_i)$ and the rate of intake is

$$
R(D) = \frac{\sum_{i \in D} \lambda_i E_i}{1 + \sum_{i \in D} \lambda_i h_i}, \tag{22.1}
$$

the multi-species form of Holling's disc equation.

**Theorem 22.5.1 (Optimal Diet)** The diet that maximizes Eq. (22.1) consists of the $k$ most profitable types, where $k$ is the largest number for which the profitability of type $k$ exceeds the rate obtained by eating only the better types:

$$
\frac{E_k}{h_k} > R_{k-1} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\lambda_i E_i}{1 + \sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\lambda_i h_i}. \tag{22.2}
$$

**Proof** Adding a type $j$ with rate $\lambda_j$ to a diet with intake rate $R = A/B$ (numerator $A$ and denominator $B$ as in Eq. (22.1)) gives the rate $(A + \lambda_j E_j)/(B + \lambda_j h_j)$. This exceeds $A/B$ if and only if $\lambda_j E_j B > \lambda_j h_j A$, that is, if and only if $E_j/h_j > R$: a type improves the diet exactly when its profitability exceeds the current rate. Consequently, in an optimal diet every included type has profitability above the optimal rate and every excluded type below it, so that the optimal diet contains all types above a profitability threshold. Adding types in order of profitability, the rate $R_k$ increases as long as $E_k/h_k > R_{k-1}$; once a type fails this test, $R_k$ is a weighted mean of $R_{k-1}$ and $E_k/h_k$ and is smaller than $R_{k-1}$, and all less profitable types fail too, since their profitability is even lower. $\square$

The theorem has three predictions that are not obvious:

1. *Ranking.* Prey are ranked by profitability, not by size or energy content alone; a large prey that takes a long time to handle may be worth less than a small one.
2. *Independence of its own abundance.* Whether a type is included depends on the encounter rates with the *better* types, which enter $R_{k-1}$, and not on its own encounter rate $\lambda_k$. If good prey are common enough to make a poor type not worth handling, it should be ignored however abundant it becomes.
3. *The zero–one rule.* A type is either always accepted on encounter or never accepted; there are no partial preferences, and as the better prey become more abundant the poorer types should drop out of the diet abruptly.

For two types, type 2 should be accepted when $E_2/h_2 > \lambda_1E_1/(1 + \lambda_1 h_1)$, that is, when the encounter rate with type 1 is below a threshold:

$$
\lambda_1 < \lambda_1^* = \frac{E_2}{E_1 h_2 - E_2 h_1}, \qquad \text{or equivalently} \qquad S_1 = \frac{1}{\lambda_1} > \frac{E_1h_2}{E_2} - h_1, \tag{22.3}
$$

where $S_1$ is the mean search time for an item of type 1.

The model was tested by Krebs, Erichsen, Webber and Charnov (1977) with great tits (*Parus major*) in the laboratory. To control the encounter rates exactly, they made the prey come to the predator: a bird sat beside a conveyor belt on which large and small pieces of mealworm passed a gap in a cover, each visible for half a second. A bird that picked up a worm missed the items that passed while it was eating, so handling time had a real cost. Large pieces were twice the size of small ones, and handling times were measured for each bird. When both types were scarce, the birds took both; as the encounter rate with large worms was raised past the predicted threshold, the birds became selective and took mostly large worms, and once they were selective, increasing the number of small worms did not change their choice, as predicted by the independence property. But the switch was gradual rather than a step: the birds continued to take some small worms (partial preferences).

Partial preferences are the most common discrepancy in tests of the prey model. They have several explanations that refine rather than refute the model: foragers make errors in recognizing prey types, especially when items must be identified in a fraction of a second; they need to sample the less preferred prey from time to time to track changes in the environment ([7.18.23](/7.18.23#sec-23.1)); handling times vary between items of the same type; and prey may be valuable for nutrients other than energy. Shore crabs (*Carcinus maenas*) offered equal numbers of mussels of different sizes ate mainly the sizes that gave the highest energy per second of breaking time, but also a range of sizes around them (Elner and Hughes 1978). Eurasian oystercatchers (*Haematopus ostralegus*) eating mussels first seemed to violate the model: calculated profitability increased with size, yet the birds preferred mussels of 30–45 mm rather than the largest. When Meire and Ervynck (1986) included the time wasted on large mussels that the birds attacked but could not open, and excluded large mussels covered by barnacles, which the birds never attack, the model predicted the preferred sizes. The northwestern crows of [7.18.2](/7.18.2#sec-2.4.4) took only large whelks because small ones would have cost more energy to break than they contained.

In a review of 134 studies, Sih and Christensen (2001) found that the prey model generally predicts diets well when the prey are immobile or slow (seeds, mussels, mealworms on a belt), whose encounter and handling the predator controls, but less well when the prey are mobile, because the outcome then depends heavily on the prey's behavior: on which prey are caught rather than which are chosen.

### 22.5.3. Patches and the Marginal Value Theorem

Food is usually clumped in patches: flowers on a plant, insects on a tree, seeds in a pile. As a forager exploits a patch its rate of gain falls, because the patch is depleted, because the remaining prey become wary, or because the forager takes the easiest items first. When should it leave and travel to the next patch?

Let $g(t)$ be the cumulative energy gained after time $t$ in a patch, with $g(0) = 0$ and $g$ increasing and concave (diminishing returns), and let $\tau$ be the mean travel time between patches. A forager that stays for time $t$ in each patch obtains the long-term rate $R(t) = g(t)/(\tau + t)$.

**Theorem 22.5.2 (Marginal Value Theorem)** The residence time $t^*$ that maximizes the long-term rate $R(t) = g(t)/(\tau + t)$ satisfies

$$
g'(t^*) = \frac{g(t^*)}{\tau + t^*} = R^*: \tag{22.4}
$$

the forager should leave a patch when its marginal rate of gain in the patch falls to the maximal long-term average rate $R^*$ for the habitat. With several types of patch, each should be left when its marginal rate falls to the same value $R^*$ (Charnov 1976).

**Proof** Differentiating, $R'(t) = [g'(t)(\tau + t) - g(t)]/(\tau + t)^2$, which vanishes when $g'(t) = g(t)/(\tau + t)$. Since $g$ is concave and $g(0) = 0$, the function $g'(t)(\tau + t) - g(t)$ has derivative $g''(t)(\tau + t) \le 0$ and starts at $g'(0)\tau > 0$, so it changes sign at most once, from positive to negative, and the stationary point is a maximum. With patch types $j$ visited in proportions $p_j$, the rate $\sum_j p_j g_j(t_j)/(\tau + \sum_j p_j t_j)$ is maximized when its partial derivative with respect to each $t_j$ vanishes, which gives $g_j'(t_j) = R^*$ for every $j$. $\square$

Geometrically, $R(t)$ is the slope of the line from the point $(-\tau, 0)$ to the point $(t, g(t))$ of the gain curve, and the optimum is where this line is tangent to the curve (figure below). The construction gives two qualitative predictions: a forager should stay longer in each patch when the travel time between patches is longer, and when the habitat as a whole is richer (higher $R^*$) it should leave each patch earlier, at a higher marginal rate.

Cowie (1977) tested the first prediction with great tits in an aviary containing artificial "trees", each with sawdust-filled cups in which mealworms were hidden. Travel time was increased by covering the cups with lids that took time to remove. The birds stayed longer in each patch when travel was longer, as predicted; they stayed slightly longer than the rate-maximizing time, a discrepancy that disappeared when the energy cost of travel was included in the currency.

The same mathematics applies to any decision about when to stop exploiting a resource with diminishing returns. Parent European starlings (*Sturnus vulgaris*) feeding nestlings collect leatherjackets (larvae of crane flies) in grassland; as the beak fills, each additional larva takes longer to find, so the loading curve shows diminishing returns. Kacelnik (1984) trained parents to take mealworms from a tray onto which he dropped worms at progressively longer intervals, producing a loading curve of known shape, and placed the tray at distances from 8 to 600 m from the nest on different days. The birds brought larger loads from more distant sites, and the loads closely matched those predicted by maximizing the net rate of delivery to the nest, including the energy costs of flight and of the chicks' begging; a currency based on energetic efficiency predicted the loads poorly. Male yellow dung flies (*Scathophaga stercoraria*) wait at fresh cowpats for females arriving to lay eggs. Using sterilized males, Parker (1970) showed that a male that mates second fertilizes about 80% of a female's eggs (**sperm competition**, [7.10.13](/7.10.13)) and that the proportion of eggs fertilized rises with copulation time with strongly diminishing returns. The "travel time" of a male is the time he spends guarding the female while she lays and finding another, about 156 min on average; the marginal value theorem predicts a copulation time of about 41 min, close to the observed mean of 36 min (Parker and Stuart 1976).

![The marginal value theorem applied to load size and copulation time (illustrative parameters). A: a starling's loading curve, the cumulative number of leatherjackets in its beak after $t$ s of searching, $g(t) = 9(1 - \mathrm{e}^{-0.03t})$; the load that maximizes the rate of delivery to the nest for a round-trip travel time $\tau$ is found by drawing the tangent from $(-\tau, 0)$ (Theorem 22.5.2): about 4.5 leatherjackets for $\tau = 10\,\mathrm{s}$ and 6.9 for $\tau = 60\,\mathrm{s}$. The dung fly and the honeybee illustrate the role of the currency. A foraging honeybee worker experiences diminishing returns for a different reason from the starling's: not because filling the crop slows her down, but because the weight of nectar in the crop increases the energy cost of flight, so that the net gain increases more slowly as the crop fills. Schmid-Hempel, Kacelnik and Houston (1985) trained bees to visit artificial flowers with a fixed amount of nectar and varied the flight time between flowers. The bees went home with smaller loads when flight between flowers was longer, as expected, but the loads matched the predictions of a model maximizing energetic efficiency, not net rate. A plausible reason is that a worker's lifetime is limited by its total energy expenditure, the wear of its flight muscles and wings: bees made to carry a permanent weight of more than 20 mg survived 7.5 instead of 10.8 days (Wolf and Schmid-Hempel 1989), so that a worker that spends less energy per unit of nectar may deliver more nectar to the colony over its life. Experiments in which small weights were added to or removed from foraging bees showed that the bees integrate the total weight carried since arriving at the patch, a rule of thumb that approximates the efficiency-maximizing departure decision (Schmid-Hempel 1986).

### 22.5.4. Central-Place Foraging

Starlings feeding their young, bees returning to the hive and beavers carrying branches to their lodge all return to a fixed point after each trip.

**Definition 22.5.3 (Central-Place Foraging)** **Central-place foraging** is foraging in which the forager returns to a fixed site (a nest, burrow, roost, hive or store) after each trip, carrying the food it has collected; the travel time to and from the central place enters its decisions about load size, patch residence and prey choice (Orians and Pearson 1979).

The load-size problem of starlings is one case. Orians and Pearson (1979) derived two further predictions. A forager that carries several items per trip (a multiple-prey loader) should carry larger loads from more distant patches, as in starlings and in eastern chipmunks (*Tamias striatus*), which filled their cheek pouches more fully at feeding stations farther from their burrows (Giraldeau and Kramer 1982). A forager that carries one item per trip (a single-prey loader) should become more selective with distance: since every trip costs the travel time, it pays to accept only larger items from far away. Beavers (*Castor canadensis*), which cut trees and drag the branches to their pond, cut trees of all sizes near the water but fewer and larger trees at greater distances (Jenkins 1980).

## 22.6. Testing and Criticizing the Theory

### 22.6.1. When Is Defense Worth It?

Economic reasoning applies to any decision whose costs and benefits can be measured in the same units. A classic example concerns the territoriality of golden-winged sunbirds (*Drepanorhynchus reichenowi*) in the highlands of Kenya, which in the nonbreeding season feed on the nectar of mint flowers (*Leonotis nepetifolia*) and sometimes defend patches of flowers against other nectar-feeders (Gill and Wolf 1975).

**Example 22.6.1 (The Economics of a Sunbird Territory)** Gill and Wolf measured nectar production per flower and estimated that a sunbird needs about 8 h of foraging per day to meet its energy needs when each flower contains 1 µL of nectar, 4 h at 2 µL, 2.7 h at 3 µL and 2 h at 4 µL. Excluding other nectar-feeders from a patch raises the nectar available per flower. Suppose defense raises it from 2 to 3 µL. The bird then needs 2.7 h of foraging instead of 4 h and can rest for the 1.3 h saved. Resting costs about $1.7\,\mathrm{kJ\,h^{-1}}$ and foraging about $4.2\,\mathrm{kJ\,h^{-1}}$ (Gill and Wolf's values, 400 and 1000 cal per hour), so the saving is $1.3 \times (4.2 - 1.7) \approx 3.3\,\mathrm{kJ}$ per day. Chasing intruders costs about $12.6\,\mathrm{kJ\,h^{-1}}$ (3000 cal per hour), so the territory pays if the bird spends less than $3.3/12.6 \approx 0.26\,\mathrm{h}$, about 16 min a day, in defense. If unguarded flowers already provide 4 µL, defense would raise the nectar to at most a little more and save almost no foraging time, and the bird should not defend; and if intruders are so numerous that defense takes most of the day, it should not defend either.

:::

Sunbirds defended territories at intermediate levels of nectar production and gave them up when nectar was very abundant or very scarce, consistent with the calculation. The general theory of when resources are economically defensible, and of how competitors distribute themselves over patches of different quality, is developed in [7.18.25](/7.18.25) and [7.18.26](/7.18.26).

### 22.6.2. Diets as Solutions to Several Constraints

The prey model treats energy as the only currency. Many herbivores, however, are limited by several nutrients and by the capacity of the gut, and their diets can be analyzed as problems of linear programming. Belovsky (1978) applied this approach to moose (*Alces alces*) on Isle Royale in Lake Superior, which eat terrestrial plants (leaves of deciduous trees and herbs) and aquatic plants in summer. Aquatic plants are rich in sodium, which is scarce in the terrestrial vegetation, but they are watery and bulky and fill the rumen quickly. Belovsky estimated the rumen capacity, the time available for feeding, the sodium requirement and the energy content of the two kinds of food, and found that the diet maximizing energy intake within these constraints predicted the observed proportion of aquatic plants closely; a diet that minimized feeding time predicted it poorly.

![Diet choice as linear programming, in the spirit of Belovsky's (1978) analysis of moose (illustrative numbers). The feasible diets form the shaded polygon. Dashed lines are diets of equal energy, $12T + 8A$ (MJ per day); the energy-maximizing diet lies at a vertex of the polygon, here where the rumen and sodium constraints bind ($T = 10$, $A = 4$, 29% aquatic plants); the time constraint is slack. Changing the constraints moves the optimum to another vertex, which is how the model makes testable predictions.](./plots/diet-lp.py)

Linear programming shows that a diet can be optimal although it does not maximize the intake of any single nutrient: the optimum lies at the corner where the binding constraints meet. A more general framework, nutritional geometry, represents foods and requirements as vectors in a space whose axes are nutrients (Simpson and Raubenheimer 2012). Locusts, cockroaches, predatory beetles, spiders and primates regulate their intake of protein and carbohydrate (or lipid) toward an intake target: given foods that are imbalanced in opposite directions, they mix them to reach the target, and when they cannot, they follow characteristic rules of compromise. Predators, too, select prey for nutrients: wolf spiders that had been fed a lipid-rich diet captured and ate more of the prey that were rich in protein, and selectively extracted protein from them (Mayntz et al. 2005).

### 22.6.3. Criticisms and Their Answers

Optimal foraging theory has been criticized on several grounds (Pierce and Ollason 1987; Gould and Lewontin 1979 for adaptationism in general):

- *It cannot be falsified.* When a model fails, the investigator changes the currency or the constraints until it fits, as in the oystercatcher example. The answer is that each model tests a specific hypothesis about currency and constraints, which is rejected when the predictions fail; what is not tested in a single study is the general assumption that animals are well designed, which is a working hypothesis justified by its success across many studies (Stephens and Krebs 1986). The revised oystercatcher model was accepted only because the additional factors, the failed attacks and the barnacles, were measured independently.
- *Animals are not optimal.* Animals face many demands at once, their environments change faster than they can evolve, and genetic and developmental constraints limit what selection can produce. Optimality models do not claim perfection; a discrepancy between prediction and behavior is information about the constraints, often more interesting than a fit.
- *Some diets seem to make no sense.* The jumping spider *Bagheera kiplingi* of Central America feeds mostly on the protein- and lipid-rich tips (Beltian bodies) of ant-acacia leaves, which the acacias produce for their ant mutualists, rather than on insects like other jumping spiders (Meehan et al. 2009). Such cases are puzzles that call for an analysis of the alternative's costs (here, perhaps, the scarcity of insect prey on ant-defended acacias and the spider's skill at evading the ants), not refutations of the approach.
- *Energy is not everything.* Foraging takes place in the presence of predators, competitors and parasites, and the forager's internal state changes the value of food. This criticism is the most productive, and the next chapter is devoted to it.

The pike cichlid (*Crenicichla saxatilis*), a predator of guppies in Trinidad, illustrates how the choice of model matters. Johansson and coauthors (2004) measured the time taken to detect, approach, stalk and attack guppies of four sizes, the capture success for each size, and the handling time after capture. From these data they built two models of prey value, one based on attack time and capture success alone and one that also included handling time. The two models ranked the size classes differently, and the cichlids' strong preference for large guppies could be judged optimal or not depending on which costs were counted, a reminder that a test of optimality is always a test of a particular currency and set of constraints.

## Summary

- Consumers can be classified by the lethality and intimacy of their interaction with victims: predators, grazers and browsers, parasites and **parasitoids**. Diets range from generalist to specialist; specialists often exploit food whose defenses other consumers cannot overcome.
- The foraging cycle consists of search, recognition, pursuit and handling; time spent handling cannot be spent searching, which is the basis of all foraging models.
- Predators sit and wait or search actively, two syndromes of diet, physiology, senses and risk; they build traps, use lures and **aggressive mimicry** (Definition 22.2.1), and hunt cooperatively, although group hunting does not always increase intake per individual.
- Ants, termites, beetles and fish farm fungi, insects or algae; ants tend aphids for **honeydew**; sea slugs keep stolen chloroplasts. Parasites manipulate host behavior to increase transmission (**host manipulation**); hosts such as *Drosophila* evolve escape, avoidance and self-medication.
- **Optimal foraging theory** (Definition 22.5.1) specifies a decision, a currency and constraints. The prey model (Definition 22.5.2, Theorem 22.5.1) predicts that prey are ranked by profitability $E/h$ and that a type is included if its profitability exceeds the rate obtained without it (Eq. 22.2), independently of its own abundance, with a zero–one rule (Eq. 22.3); partial preferences are the commonest deviation.
- The **marginal value theorem** (Theorem 22.5.2): leave a patch when the marginal rate of gain falls to the long-term average rate; stay longer when travel is longer. It predicts load size in starlings and copulation time in dung flies; honeybees maximize efficiency rather than rate.
- **Central-place foraging** (Definition 22.5.3): loads increase and single-prey loaders become more selective with distance from the central place.
- Economic analyses predict territorial defense (Example 22.6.1) and diets under several constraints (linear programming, nutritional geometry). Failures of a model reveal currencies and constraints; the theory works best for immobile prey.

## Questions

1. (Calculation) In the great tit experiment, large worms had twice the energy of small ones and both took about 5 s to handle. Using Eq. (22.3), find the encounter rate with large worms above which the birds should ignore small worms. If handling of small worms took 2.5 s instead, how would the threshold change, and why?

   **Answer** With $E_1 = 2E_2$ and $h_1 = h_2 = 5\,\mathrm{s}$, $\lambda_1^* = E_2/(E_1h_2 - E_2h_1) = 1/(2 \times 5 - 5) = 0.2\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$, a mean search time of 5 s per large worm. If $h_2 = 2.5\,\mathrm{s}$, the two types have equal profitability ($E_2/h_2 = E_1/h_1$), the denominator $E_1h_2 - E_2h_1$ becomes zero and $\lambda_1^*$ is infinite: small worms are worth taking at any abundance of large ones, because they are no longer less profitable. In general, the closer the profitabilities of the two types, the more abundant the better type must be before the poorer one is dropped.

2. A bee forages in a meadow where all flower patches are of the same kind. What does the marginal value theorem predict about the time spent in each patch if (a) the patches become farther apart, (b) a second, richer kind of patch is added to the meadow, and (c) all patches are made richer by the same factor?

   **Answer** (a) Longer travel lowers the long-term rate $R^*$; the tangent from $(-\tau, 0)$ touches the gain curve later, so the bee should stay longer in each patch. (b) Adding richer patches raises $R^*$ for the habitat; the bee should leave the original patches earlier, when their marginal rate falls to the new, higher $R^*$, and stay in the richer patches until their marginal rate falls to the same value. (c) If every gain function is multiplied by a constant $c$, both $g'(t)$ and $g(t)/(\tau + t)$ scale by $c$ and Eq. (22.4) is unchanged, so the residence time stays the same; only if richness changes the shape of the gain curve (for example, faster depletion) does $t^*$ change.

3. The prey model predicts that the inclusion of a prey type does not depend on its own abundance. Describe an experiment that tests this prediction, and give two reasons why real foragers might nevertheless take more of a poor prey type when it becomes very common.

   **Answer** Keep the encounter rate with the profitable type above the threshold of Eq. (22.3) and vary the encounter rate with the poor type over a wide range, as Krebs and coauthors did on the conveyor belt; measure the proportion of poor items accepted. The prediction is that acceptance stays near zero at all densities of the poor type. Real foragers may take more of a common poor type because (i) recognition errors increase when poor items are frequent, so more are taken by mistake; (ii) frequent encounters with the poor type may form a search image or reduce the cost of recognizing it; (iii) the poor type may supply a nutrient missing from the better one; and (iv) sampling to track changes in prey quality is cheaper when the poor type is common.

4. (Calculation) In Example 22.6.1, suppose intruders become twice as frequent, so that a sunbird holding a territory at 3 µL per flower must spend 25 min a day chasing them. Should it defend? What if, in addition, unguarded flowers contain only 1 µL while defended flowers contain 2 µL?

   **Answer** At 2 versus 3 µL, the saving is $3.3\,\mathrm{kJ}$ per day and 25 min of chasing costs $12.6 \times 25/60 \approx 5.3\,\mathrm{kJ}$, so defense does not pay. At 1 versus 2 µL, defense reduces foraging from 8 h to 4 h; resting for the 4 h saved instead of foraging saves $4 \times (4.2 - 1.7) = 10\,\mathrm{kJ}$, which exceeds the cost of 5.3 kJ, so defense pays. (The 25 min spent chasing also reduce resting time, which changes the numbers only slightly.) The value of a territory depends on how much defense increases the resource relative to its undefended level.

5. (Open question) How would you distinguish experimentally between three hypotheses for the behavior of carpenter ants killed by *Ophiocordyceps*: adaptive manipulation by the fungus, a side effect of infection, and a defensive response of the ant colony (sick workers leaving the nest)?

   **Answer** A side effect predicts unspecific changes (lethargy, loss of coordination) and no relation between the ants' final position and fungal success. A defense of the colony predicts that infected ants leave the nest and trails, but does not predict the precise height, leaf side, timing and death grip; healthy colony members might also remove infected individuals. Adaptive manipulation predicts that the final positions are those that maximize fungal growth and spore dispersal. Tests: transplant ants killed by the fungus to other heights and microhabitats and measure fungal development and spore production (Andersen and coauthors found that cadavers moved away from the biting zone developed poorly); compare the behavior of ants infected by related fungi that do not manipulate; examine whether the fungus secretes compounds that act on muscles or neurons, and whether these compounds alone reproduce parts of the behavior.

6. (Open question) Parent birds feeding young are central-place foragers. Predict how the size of prey brought to the nest should change with the distance of the feeding site for a species that carries one prey per trip (such as many terns) and for one that carries a beakful (such as starlings), and propose an observational test.

   **Answer** A single-prey loader should become more selective with distance: since each trip costs the full travel time, small prey that are worth taking near the nest are not worth carrying from far away, so the mean size of prey delivered should increase with distance. A multiple-prey loader should bring larger loads (more items) from farther away, while the size of individual items may change little. Test: mark the feeding sites of individually identified parents with GPS loggers or by direct observation, photograph the prey at the nest, and regress prey size (terns) or load size (starlings) on the distance of the trip, controlling for time of day, chick age and prey availability at each site.
