Vision delivers more information per second than any other sense, and for many animals it is the main source of information about the location and identity of objects at a distance: prey, predators, mates, landmarks. Yet eyes differ more than any other sense organ. Some animals see only light and dark, others form images sharper than ours; some see ultraviolet light or the polarization of the sky, some see fewer colors than we do; some see behind their heads, others in a narrow cone in front; some follow flicker ten times faster than we can. These differences make sense when we ask what each animal needs to see.

This chapter treats vision from the viewpoint of the behaving animal. It begins with the evolution of eyes and the two image-forming designs, summarizes how visual information is processed in parallel streams, and then examines the trade-offs that shape eyes: resolution against sensitivity, field of view against binocular overlap, depth of field against light gathering, and speed against noise. It ends with color, ultraviolet and polarization vision and with the visual world of one well-studied animal, the pigeon. The optics, photoreceptors and retinal circuits of the vertebrate eye are described in animal physiology ([7.14.41](/7.14.41)), the physics of photon detection in biophysics ([7.6.18](/7.6.18#sec-18.6)), the computations of the retina and visual cortex in computational neuroscience ([7.16.17](/7.16.17), [7.16.18](/7.16.18)), and insect eyes in zoology ([7.17.27](/7.17.27#sec-27.5)). The general principles of sensation and the idea of the Umwelt were introduced in [7.18.9](/7.18.9); how particular visual features release behavior (the prey-catching of toads, the escape of locusts) follows in [7.18.13](/7.18.13), and the design of visual signals in [7.18.38](/7.18.38).

## 10.1. Eyes

### 10.1.1. The Evolution of Eyes

Light-sensitive molecules are older than eyes. All animal photoreceptors use **opsins**, G protein–coupled receptors bound to a retinal chromophore, which arose early in animal evolution and diversified into the c-opsins of ciliary photoreceptors and the r-opsins of rhabdomeric photoreceptors ([7.16.53](/7.16.53#sec-53.4.1)). From such cells, eyes were assembled many times. Nilsson (2009) ordered the diversity of eyes by the tasks they can perform: measuring ambient light (day length, depth in water), detecting the direction of light (phototaxis), low-resolution vision (orientation to large structures), and high-resolution vision (finding prey and mates, recognizing objects), each stage requiring the previous one ([7.17.44](/7.17.44#sec-44.9.2)). Living animals contain every stage, from the eyespot of a larva through the pigment cup of a flatworm and the pinhole eye of *Nautilus* to the lens eyes of cephalopods and vertebrates.

Darwin found the eye the hardest organ to explain by natural selection, and then answered his own objection: if gradations from a simple to a perfect eye exist, each useful to its owner, the difficulty disappears. Nilsson and Pelger (1994) quantified the answer: a patch of photoreceptors can become a focused camera eye in a few hundred thousand generations under weak selection ([7.10.32](/7.10.32#sec-32.3.2)). At the molecular level, the development of very different eyes is controlled by the same transcription factor, Pax6, an example of **deep homology** ([7.14.41](/7.14.41#sec-41.6.1)).

### 10.1.2. Two Ways to Form an Image

Only two optical designs give high-resolution vision in animals, and both evolved more than once.

- The **compound eye** of arthropods consists of many **ommatidia**, each looking in a slightly different direction. In an **apposition eye** each ommatidium is optically isolated and its photoreceptors receive light only through its own facet; this design suits day-active insects. In a **superposition eye** light entering many facets is focused onto one rhabdom, which makes the image much brighter; it is found in nocturnal moths and many beetles and crustaceans ([7.17.27](/7.17.27#sec-27.5.3)).
- The **camera eye** of vertebrates and cephalopods forms one image with a lens on a retina. The two camera eyes are a classic case of **convergent evolution**: they look alike but develop differently, and the cephalopod retina is upright, with the photoreceptors facing the light, whereas the vertebrate retina is inverted, with the light passing through the neural layers before it reaches the photoreceptors and the nerve fibers leaving through a blind spot ([7.14.41](/7.14.41#sec-41.6.3)). Jumping spiders have small camera eyes with a long focal length and a movable, tiered retina that gives them the sharpest vision of any animal of their size.

Each design has its strengths. Compound eyes cover a wide field of view, are compact and very fast, but their resolution is limited by diffraction at the small facets: a compound eye with the resolution of the human eye would need a radius of several meters ([7.6.18](/7.6.18#sec-18.6.6)). Camera eyes give high resolution in a small package but need a large head for a wide field of view, which is why most vertebrates have two laterally placed eyes.

### 10.1.3. From Retina to Brain

Vision is the most thoroughly studied sense at the level of circuits, and a few principles matter for behavior.

- The image is decomposed early. Retinal ganglion cells have **center–surround receptive fields** that respond to local contrast rather than absolute light intensity, and more than thirty types of ganglion cells send separate representations of the same image (ON and OFF contrast, motion direction, color, overall brightness) to the brain in parallel ([7.16.17](/7.16.17#sec-17.7)).
- In mammals, neurons of the primary visual cortex respond to oriented edges and bars, as Hubel and Wiesel discovered by trying stimuli until they found the "best" one for a neuron; neurons with similar preferences are grouped in columns ([7.16.18](/7.16.18#sec-18.2)). Beyond the primary cortex, processing splits into a ventral stream that identifies objects and a dorsal stream that guides action ([7.14.41](/7.14.41#sec-41.4.3)).
- Processing is serial (each stage builds more complex features from the previous ones) and parallel (different features are processed in separate channels). In nonmammalian vertebrates the optic tectum, homologous to the superior colliculus of mammals, carries out much of the analysis that releases behavior, and in toads and frogs it contains detectors for prey-like and predator-like moving objects ([7.18.13](/7.18.13)).

For a behavioral biologist, the consequence is that "what an animal sees" is not a picture but a set of features extracted for particular tasks. The rest of this chapter concerns the properties of the eye itself, which set the limits within which those features can be extracted.

## 10.2. Resolution and Sensitivity

### 10.2.1. Acuity

Spatial acuity, the finest pattern an eye can resolve, is usually given as the highest spatial frequency of a grating, in cycles per degree, that the animal can discriminate from a uniform field. It differs among animals by more than four orders of magnitude (Caves and colleagues 2018): about $140$ cycles per degree in large eagles, $60$ in humans, about $10$ in cats, $1$ in rats, and less than $1$ in most insects. Two physical limits set it.

- The photoreceptor mosaic samples the image; a grating can be resolved only if each cycle covers at least two receptors (the sampling limit). The angle between neighboring receptors is their spacing divided by the focal length, so a long eye with thin, densely packed receptors resolves more.
- Diffraction blurs the image; an aperture of diameter $A$ cannot transmit spatial frequencies above $A/\lambda$ cycles per radian. A larger pupil allows a finer image.

Both limits favor large eyes, and indeed acuity rises with eye size across vertebrates. Eagles have eyes nearly as large as ours in a much smaller head, densely packed cones, and a deep fovea that may act as a magnifying lens.

**Example 10.2.1** Estimate the two limits for the human fovea. With a pupil of $A = 3\,\mathrm{mm}$ and light of $\lambda = 550\,\mathrm{nm}$, the diffraction cutoff is $A/\lambda = 3\times10^{-3}/5.5\times10^{-7} \approx 5500$ cycles per radian, or $5500\times\pi/180 \approx 95$ cycles per degree. Foveal cones are about $2.5\,\mathrm{\mu m}$ apart and the focal length of the eye is about $17\,\mathrm{mm}$, so the sampling angle is $\Delta\phi = 2.5\times10^{-3}/17 \approx 1.5\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{rad}$, and the highest frequency that can be sampled is $1/(2\Delta\phi) \approx 3400$ cycles per radian, about $60$ cycles per degree. The measured acuity of about $60$ cycles per degree thus matches the sampling limit, which in turn lies slightly below the diffraction limit: the mosaic is as fine as the optics justify, but no finer.

:::

Acuity is not uniform across the retina. Most vertebrates have a region of high receptor density, either a round area or **fovea** for looking at objects, or a horizontal band, the visual streak, that images the horizon. A streak is typical of animals of open plains (rabbits, many ungulates, cheetahs), whose predators and prey appear along the horizon; a fovea is typical of animals that fixate objects. Many birds of prey and some other birds have two foveae in each eye, one for lateral and one for frontal vision. The eyes of the four-eyed fish *Anableps* have two retinal regions and two pupils, one for seeing above and one for seeing below the water surface.

### 10.2.2. Rods and Cones

The duplex retina of vertebrates separates two tasks that conflict. Rods are very sensitive: they respond to single photons, and the signals of many rods converge onto each ganglion cell, which increases sensitivity further but reduces resolution. Cones are less sensitive but faster, come in several spectral classes, and in the fovea each connects to its own ganglion cells, which preserves resolution and allows color vision ([7.14.41](/7.14.41#sec-41.2.2)). In dim light we see with rods: sensitively, but without color and with poor acuity. The proportion of rods and cones follows the activity pattern of a species. Nocturnal mammals have rod-dominated retinas; some diurnal lizards, squirrels and birds have retinas dominated by cones; deep-sea fish have pure rod retinas, some with many rod opsin genes.

### 10.2.3. Photon Noise and Night Vision

Why should resolution and sensitivity conflict? Because light consists of photons, and in dim light their number is small. If a receptor channel catches on average $N$ photons in one integration time, the actual number fluctuates with a Poisson standard deviation of $\sqrt N$. A target that differs from its background by a contrast $C$ changes the catch by $CN$, and it can be detected reliably only if this difference exceeds a few standard deviations:

$$
CN \ge k\sqrt{N}, \qquad C_{\min} = \frac{k}{\sqrt{N}}, \tag{10.1}
$$
with $k \approx 2$–$3$ (the Rose–de Vries law). Every hundredfold decrease of light level raises the smallest detectable contrast tenfold. To see in dim light, an eye must increase $N$, and every way of doing so costs something:

- a wider pupil and a shorter focal length relative to it (a low f-number) collect more light per receptor; the tawny owl has an f-number of about $1.3$, the human dark-adapted eye about $2.1$; this requires a large eye relative to the head;
- wider and longer receptors absorb more photons but sample the image more coarsely;
- pooling the signals of many receptors (spatial summation) increases the catch of a channel but reduces the resolution in proportion to the area pooled;
- a longer integration time increases the catch but blurs motion;
- a reflecting **tapetum lucidum** behind the retina gives photons a second chance of absorption but scatters light.

The figure shows why night vision is achieved more by neural summation and slow integration than by optics alone: an owl's large pupil gains a factor of a few over ours, but pooling a hundred receptors gains a factor of a hundred in photon catch and of ten in contrast sensitivity. Nocturnal animals are, as a result, not just sensitive but coarse and slow, and they often move slowly as well. Remarkable exceptions show how far the limits can be pushed. The elephant hawkmoth *Deilephila elpenor* discriminates colors in dim starlight with superposition eyes (Kelber and colleagues 2002), and nocturnal geckos, whose ancestors were diurnal lizards that had lost their rods, see colors at night with cones that have become large and very sensitive (Roth and Kelber 2004). The nocturnal sweat bee *Megalopta* finds its nest in the rain forest at twilight with apposition eyes whose signals are summed in space and time ([7.17.27](/7.17.27#sec-27.5.4)).

## 10.3. Where the Eyes Point

### 10.3.1. Visual Fields

With two eyes, an animal can trade the width of its view against the region seen by both eyes.

**Definition 10.3.1 (Visual Field, Binocular Vision)** The **visual field** of an eye or of an animal is the region of space, measured as angles around the head, from which light can reach the retina while the eyes are at rest. The part of the field seen by both eyes at the same time is the binocular field, and seeing with both eyes in this region is **binocular vision**; the rest of the field is seen by one eye only (monocular field), and the region seen by neither eye is the blind area.

Binocular vision allows *stereopsis*, the perception of depth from the small differences (disparities) between the images of the two eyes, but it is not the only source of depth information: motion parallax (the relative motion of near and far objects when the head moves), the known size of objects, occlusion, shading and accommodation also give distance. The visual fields of animals reflect the tasks for which they need to see.

![Horizontal visual fields seen from above (approximate values with the eyes at rest). The binocular field (yellow), the monocular fields of the two eyes together (blue) and the blind area (gray). Humans: total field about 200°, binocular field about 120°. Tawny owl (Strix aluco): 201° and 48°. Pigeon: about 340° and 35°. Rabbit: a complete panorama of 360°, with a binocular field of about 24° in front and about 9° behind the head. Data from Hughes (1977), Martin (1984) and Wynne and Udell (2020).](./plots/visual-fields.py)

A common generalization holds that prey species have lateral eyes and panoramic vision to watch for predators, and predators have frontal eyes and wide binocular fields for judging the distance of prey. It contains a large truth: grazing mammals such as rabbits, deer and horses have nearly complete panoramic vision, and many predators (cats, primates, owls) have frontal eyes. But it is incomplete in two ways. First, frontal eyes are also found in animals that are not predators (fruit bats, many primates that eat fruit and leaves), and the cat's frontal eyes may serve as much to improve vision in dim light, because the two eyes' images are combined and much of the field is seen twice, as to judge depth. Second, among birds, comparative studies by Graham Martin show that the width of the binocular field depends mainly on the need to see the bill and what it holds: birds that peck or probe with precise control of the bill have a narrow binocular field around it, whatever their diet, and species that find food by touch rather than by sight, such as woodcocks and ducks that filter food, have a binocular field behind as well as in front of the head and see the whole sky without moving their heads (Martin 2017). Owls, the predators with the most frontal eyes among birds, have a binocular field of only about $50^\circ$, and their eyes are almost immobile tubes; they turn the head instead.

Stereopsis has been demonstrated behaviorally in mammals, in owls and falcons, in toads, and even in an insect: praying mantises fitted with tiny colored glasses that showed each eye a different image struck at virtual prey whose disparity indicated that it was within reach (Nityananda and colleagues 2016). The mantis computes disparity from changes in the image rather than from matching the detailed patterns of the two eyes as we do, an independent solution to the same problem.

### 10.3.2. Stabilizing the Image

An image that moves on the retina is blurred, and motion caused by the animal's own movement masks the motion of other objects. Animals therefore stabilize their gaze. Primates move their eyes; birds, whose eyes are large and nearly fixed in the skull, move their heads. A walking pigeon thrusts its head forward and then holds it still in space while the body catches up, so that for most of each step the image is stationary and moving objects stand out (Frost 1978); when pigeons walk on a treadmill that keeps the surroundings stationary, they stop bobbing. Hovering kestrels hold the head still in the wind while the body moves below it, and herons and egrets hold the head still while stalking.

### 10.3.3. Pupil Shape

Pupils are round in humans and many other animals, vertical slits in domestic cats, foxes, geckos and many snakes, and horizontal slits or rectangles in goats, sheep, horses and deer. The shape affects two properties of the image.

- A slit can close much further than a round pupil. The area of a cat's pupil changes about 135-fold between dark and bright light, that of the human pupil about 15-fold. Animals that are active both at night and in daylight need this range.
- The blur of an object outside the plane of focus is proportional to the extent of the pupil in each direction. A vertical slit, narrow from side to side and tall from top to bottom, blurs horizontal edges strongly and vertical edges hardly at all; it gives a large depth of field for vertical contours and a small one for horizontal contours.

Banks and colleagues (2015) compared the pupils of 214 species of land vertebrates and found a striking regularity: vertical slits occur mostly in ambush predators with frontal eyes that are active by day and night, and among these mostly in species with eyes close to the ground (shoulder height below about $40\,\mathrm{cm}$); horizontal pupils occur almost only in prey animals with lateral eyes, mostly grazers; round pupils occur in active foragers and in larger predators. Their optical analysis explains the pattern. For an ambush predator judging the distance to prey on the ground, stereopsis works best with sharp vertical contours (the disparities are horizontal), while the strong blur of horizontal contours provides a second depth cue whose gradient on the ground plane is steepest for eyes close to the ground. For a grazing prey, a horizontal pupil aligned with the ground widens the field of view along the horizon, improves the image of the ground ahead, and reduces glare from the sky above; when a goat or a sheep lowers its head to graze, its eyes rotate in their orbits (cyclovergence) by up to $50^\circ$ so that the pupils stay horizontal.

## 10.4. Color, Ultraviolet and Polarization

### 10.4.1. Color Vision Is Comparison

A single class of photoreceptors cannot distinguish color from brightness: a dim light at its peak wavelength and a bright light far from it can produce the same response. Color vision requires at least two receptor classes with different spectral sensitivities and neurons that compare their outputs. Animals with two classes (**dichromacy**) can distinguish colors along one dimension, those with three (**trichromacy**) along two, those with four (**tetrachromacy**) along three. Lights that produce the same ratios of responses in all receptor classes look identical even if their spectra differ: for humans a mixture of red and green light matches a yellow light, but for a pigeon, with more receptor classes, no mixture of blue and yellow light matched green (Palacios and Varela 1992).

Karl von Frisch (1914) proved that honey bees see color with a simple and rigorous design. He trained bees to collect sugar water from a dish on a blue card, then offered the blue card without food among cards of every shade of gray. If bees saw only brightness, some gray of the same brightness should have been confused with blue; the bees chose the blue card. Similar tests showed that bees confuse red with dark gray (they lack a red receptor) and that they see ultraviolet. The receptor classes behind these abilities are now known for many species (figure below).

![Spectral sensitivities of the photoreceptors used in color vision, computed with the visual-pigment template of Govardovskii and colleagues (2000) from the wavelengths of peak absorption. Human cones (420, 534 and 564 nm); dog (429 and 555 nm), a dichromat like most mammals; honey bee (344, 436 and 544 nm), a trichromat shifted toward the ultraviolet; blue tit single cones (371, 449, 502 and 563 nm), a tetrachromat with an ultraviolet-sensitive cone (Hart and colleagues 2000). The gray band marks ultraviolet light, invisible to humans because our lens absorbs it. Filtering by the colored oil droplets of bird cones, which narrows their sensitivity, is not included.](./plots/cone-spectra.py)

The repertoire of cones has a history ([7.14.41](/7.14.41#sec-41.6.5)). Early vertebrates had four cone opsins; birds, reptiles and many fish kept all four and added colored oil droplets that sharpen the spectral tuning; mammals lost two during a long nocturnal phase of their history and are mostly dichromats; Old World primates regained trichromacy by duplicating an opsin gene on the X chromosome. A dog thus sees a red ball on green grass much as a red–green color-blind person does: by its brightness rather than its hue. Trichromacy in primates may have been favored for detecting ripe fruit and young, reddish leaves against mature foliage; in New World monkeys, where only some females are trichromats, trichromatic females find ripe fruit more efficiently in some studies, but dichromats are better at detecting camouflaged insects, which may help to maintain both types in the population.

### 10.4.2. Ultraviolet Signals

Many birds, reptiles, fish and insects see near-ultraviolet light (about $300$–$400\,\mathrm{nm}$), and they use it in foraging and in signaling. Flowers that look uniformly yellow to us often have ultraviolet-absorbing centers that guide bees to the nectar. Male sulphur butterflies (*Colias*) have ultraviolet-reflecting wing patches that females, but not humans, see; males with the patches experimentally removed are less successful in courtship. In blue tits, males have an ultraviolet-reflecting crown; females prefer males with high ultraviolet reflectance, and males whose crowns were treated with an ultraviolet-blocking cream lost their attractiveness (Hunt and colleagues 1998; Andersson and colleagues 1998). Lizards display ultraviolet throat patches in contests, and male three-spined sticklebacks are chosen partly by ultraviolet cues.

Such findings have a methodological lesson: a signal that looks dull or identical to us may be conspicuous to the receiver, so color signals must be measured by spectrophotometry and interpreted through models of the receiver's photoreceptors ([7.18.38](/7.18.38)). The lesson cuts both ways. Kestrels were reported to find voles by the ultraviolet reflectance of their urine trails (Viitala and colleagues 1995), a widely cited example of ultraviolet foraging; later measurements of the ultraviolet sensitivity of raptor eyes, whose ocular media absorb much near-ultraviolet light, and of the reflectance of vole urine suggest that the trails would be barely visible at the distances at which kestrels hunt, so the claim is now doubtful (Lind and colleagues 2013).

### 10.4.3. Polarization

Sunlight scattered in the sky is partly polarized in a pattern that depends on the position of the sun, and light reflected from water surfaces is horizontally polarized. Many insects, crustaceans, cephalopods and some fish detect the plane of polarization with rhabdomeric photoreceptors whose microvilli are aligned (**polarization** vision; [7.14.41](/7.14.41#sec-41.6.6)). They use it in three ways.

- As a compass: bees, desert ants and monarch butterflies read the pattern of the sky to determine the direction of the sun when it is hidden, and rotating the plane of polarization with a filter above a tethered, flying monarch rotates the direction of its flight by the same angle (Reppert and colleagues 2004; [7.18.21](/7.18.21)).
- To find water: aquatic insects such as backswimmers and water beetles recognize water from the air by its horizontally polarized reflection. The same cue has become a trap: asphalt roads, dark cars and glass facades reflect strongly polarized light, and mayflies and other aquatic insects lay their eggs on them by the thousands (polarized light pollution; Horváth and colleagues 2009).
- As a private channel of communication: cuttlefish and some mantis shrimps display polarization patterns on the body that predators unable to see polarization would not detect; mantis shrimps also detect circularly polarized light, one of very few abilities of its kind known in animals.

## 10.5. Seeing Fast

The temporal resolution of an eye is usually measured as the *critical flicker fusion frequency*, the highest rate at which a flickering light is still seen as flickering. It is about $60\,\mathrm{Hz}$ for human cones in bright light and lower in dim light, about $15$–$20\,\mathrm{Hz}$ for rods; it exceeds $100\,\mathrm{Hz}$ in pigeons and many other birds, and $200$–$300\,\mathrm{Hz}$ in fast-flying flies, whose photoreceptors are both rhabdomeric and metabolically expensive ([7.17.27](/7.17.27#sec-27.5.6)). Across vertebrates, temporal resolution is higher in small animals with high metabolic rates and in species that pursue fast prey or fly fast through cluttered habitats (Healy and colleagues 2013). In effect, small, fast animals live in a world that runs in slow motion compared with ours: a fly can react to a swatter before we have finished moving it.

Fast vision costs energy and photons: a fast receptor integrates over a short time and therefore catches fewer photons per integration time (Eq. (10.1)), so high temporal resolution is affordable only in bright light. The mismatch between human and animal flicker perception has practical consequences: fluorescent lights driven at $100$ or $120\,\mathrm{Hz}$, steady for us, may flicker for poultry and caged birds, which is a concern for animal welfare ([7.18.47](/7.18.47)).

## 10.6. The Pigeon's Visual World

The pigeon is the vertebrate whose vision has been studied most thoroughly by behavioral methods, and it shows how the properties discussed above combine into one Umwelt (Wynne and Udell 2020).

- Field of view. The pigeon sees about $340^\circ$ around its head and almost nothing directly behind it, but only about $35^\circ$ binocularly, in front and below the bill. It uses the binocular field at close range, when pecking at grain; it is very hard to approach a pigeon from behind unseen.
- Two kinds of focus. The lower frontal field, which looks at the ground, is nearsighted, and the lateral and upper fields are normal or slightly farsighted, so that a pigeon sees the grain at its feet and the horizon and sky sharply at the same time without accommodating. Its acuity is best at about $10\,\mathrm{cm}$, where it resolves about $0.06\,\mathrm{mm}$; at a distance it is lower than ours and about a tenth of that of a hawk.
- Color and ultraviolet. Pigeons have four classes of single cones, with sensitivity extending into the near ultraviolet, and colored oil droplets, which give their color space more dimensions than ours (Section 10.4.1). The lateral fields, used in flight, are the ones most sensitive to ultraviolet light.
- Objects and pictures. Pigeons trained to discriminate photographs of two objects transfer the discrimination to the objects themselves, and vice versa (Spetch and Friedman 2006); they use shading and perspective to recognize three-dimensional shapes in pictures, and they confuse the same letters of the alphabet that humans confuse (Blough and Blough 1997). Yet the time they take to recognize a rotated shape depends less on the angle of rotation than in humans, a hint that a flying animal, which sees objects from all angles, may represent them differently ([7.18.43](/7.18.43)).

Pigeons are also the classic subjects of experiments on concepts and categorization, and their abilities there build on the visual system described here.

## Summary

- Eyes evolved many times from opsin-based photoreceptors, through a sequence of functional stages; high-resolution vision is achieved by two designs, compound eyes (apposition for day, superposition for night) and camera eyes (convergent in vertebrates and cephalopods). Visual information is processed in parallel channels and serial stages, and what an animal "sees" is a set of extracted features.
- Acuity is limited by receptor sampling and diffraction and rises with eye size (Example 10.2.1: the human fovea is sampling-limited at about 60 cycles per degree). Foveae look at objects, visual streaks at the horizon. The duplex retina separates sensitive rods from fast, color-coding cones.
- Photon noise sets the smallest detectable contrast, $C_{\min} = k/\sqrt N$ (Eq. (10.1)). Night vision gains photons by large apertures, wide receptors, spatial pooling, long integration and tapeta, at the cost of resolution and speed.
- The visual field and binocular vision (Definition 10.3.1) trade panoramic surveillance against frontal overlap; in birds the binocular field depends mostly on control of the bill. Stereopsis is one of several depth cues and has evolved independently in mantises. Birds stabilize the image by head bobbing.
- Vertical-slit pupils occur in ambush predators with frontal eyes near the ground, horizontal pupils in grazing prey (Banks and colleagues 2015): slit pupils shape depth of field differently along the two axes and allow a large range of pupil area.
- Color vision compares receptor classes (dichromacy, trichromacy, tetrachromacy); bees and many birds see ultraviolet, which carries signals in flowers, butterflies and blue tits; claims of ultraviolet foraging must be checked against the receiver's sensitivity. Polarization serves as a compass, a water detector (and a trap on asphalt), and a private signal.
- Temporal resolution is high in small, fast animals and requires bright light. The pigeon combines a panoramic field, a nearsighted lower field, tetrachromatic ultraviolet vision and picture recognition.

## Questions

1. (Calculation) A wedge-tailed eagle has foveal cones about $2\,\mathrm{\mu m}$ apart and a posterior nodal distance (effective focal length) of about $21\,\mathrm{mm}$. Estimate its sampling-limited acuity and compare it with the human value of Example 10.2.1.

   **Answer** The sampling angle is $\Delta\phi = 2\times10^{-3}/21 \approx 9.5\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{rad}$, and the highest resolvable frequency is $1/(2\Delta\phi) \approx 5250$ cycles per radian, or $5250\times\pi/180 \approx 92$ cycles per degree, about 1.5 times the human value. Behavioral measurements give about $140$ cycles per degree for this eagle (Reymond 1985); the difference suggests a longer effective focal length than assumed, for example by the magnifying effect of the deep, steep-walled fovea, which acts as a negative lens. To reach this sampling acuity, the pupil must be at least about $140\times180/\pi \times 5.5\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm{m} \approx 4.4\,\mathrm{mm}$ wide so that diffraction does not limit the image, which eagles satisfy in daylight.

2. (Calculation) By Eq. (10.1), how much larger must the photon catch of a channel be to detect a target of contrast 0.1 instead of 0.3? List three ways in which a nocturnal animal could achieve this, and the cost of each.

   **Answer** $C_{\min} \propto N^{-1/2}$, so lowering $C_{\min}$ by a factor of 3 requires $N$ to be $9$ times larger. Ways: (1) widen the pupil relative to focal length (a lower f-number), which needs a larger eye and reduces depth of field; (2) pool the signals of about nine times as many receptors, which reduces spatial resolution threefold in each dimension; (3) integrate nine times longer, which blurs moving objects and slows reactions. A tapetum, wider receptors or more pigment per receptor are further options, each with its own cost (scatter, coarser sampling, larger eye).

3. A common textbook statement holds that herbivores have lateral eyes "for monocular vision" to watch for predators, and carnivores frontal eyes "for depth perception". What is right and what is incomplete in this statement?

   **Answer** It is right that many prey species, especially grazing mammals, have laterally placed eyes with nearly panoramic fields and that many mammalian predators have frontal eyes with large binocular fields. It is incomplete because (a) lateral-eyed animals also have binocular fields, small but used (the pigeon pecks within its binocular field); (b) frontal eyes also occur in non-predators such as fruit bats and many primates, and in nocturnal animals frontal eyes may serve sensitivity (summation of both images) as much as stereopsis; (c) in birds, binocular field width depends mostly on the need to see and control the bill, not on diet, and tactile foragers such as woodcocks have panoramic vision whatever they eat; (d) depth can be judged monocularly by motion parallax and other cues, so frontal eyes are not necessary for judging distance, and stereopsis evolved independently in insects.

4. Design an experiment to test whether female blue tits use the ultraviolet reflectance of the male crown in mate choice, with controls for the side effects of the treatment.

   **Answer** Capture males and treat the crown of one group with a cream or a mixture that absorbs ultraviolet light but does not change the reflectance at longer wavelengths (checked by spectrophotometry), and treat a control group with the same base without the ultraviolet absorber, so that smell, handling and texture are the same. In a choice apparatus, present each female with one treated and one control male behind ultraviolet-transmitting partitions, alternating sides, and record the time she spends near each; repeat with partitions that block ultraviolet light for both males as a further control. Field tests can compare pairing success or extra-pair paternity of treated and control males. A preference for control males when ultraviolet light is visible, and no preference when it is blocked, shows that the ultraviolet component is used.

5. Why do small, fast-flying insects have higher flicker fusion frequencies than large mammals, and why is this possible only in bright light?

   **Answer** A small animal flying fast through clutter sees images move across its retina at very high angular speeds and must react within milliseconds; it needs receptors that follow rapid changes, and its high metabolic rate can pay for fast, energetically costly transduction. A fast receptor integrates over a short time, so it catches few photons per integration time; by the Rose–de Vries law its contrast sensitivity is then low unless the light is bright. In dim light the receptor must integrate longer, lowering temporal resolution; nocturnal insects indeed have slower receptors than diurnal ones.
