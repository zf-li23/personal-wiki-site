Animal behavior is the study of what animals do: how they find food and avoid being eaten, choose mates and care for young, defend territories, cooperate and cheat, signal to one another, find their way over thousands of kilometers, and learn from experience and from each other. Its distinctive feature is that every behavior can be explained in two complementary ways. **Proximate causes** describe how the behavior is produced within the individual, by its genes, development, senses, neurons and hormones; **ultimate causes** describe why it exists, by the effect it has on survival and reproduction and by the evolutionary history through which it arose. Niko Tinbergen divided these explanations further into four logically independent questions, mechanism, development, function and evolution, and **Tinbergen's four questions** are the framework of the whole course. They correspond to the two research programs that the field grew into: **neuroethology**, which asks how nervous systems generate natural behavior, and **behavioral ecology**, which asks what the behavior is for and predicts it with quantitative models of costs and benefits. The most informative studies, from the moth that hears a bat to the songbird that learns its song, answer several of the questions at once and show how the answers constrain one another.

The course follows this framework, with the proximate questions first and the ultimate ones after them. A foundational part introduces the questions and the history of the field, the methods of describing behavior and testing hypotheses about it, the sign stimuli and fixed action patterns of classical ethology, and the contributions of genes and of experience to the development of behavior. The neuroethology part, the longest, begins with the principles and model systems of the field, the transmitters and modulators that set behavioral states, and the brain systems for feeding, fear and working memory; it then reads the sensory worlds of animals, chemical, visual, mechanical and auditory, and treats the classic systems one by one: echolocation and the bat–moth arms race, feature detection in toads, locusts and dragonflies, escape circuits and central pattern generators, electroreception and the jamming avoidance response, acoustic communication in insects and frogs, and birdsong. Chapters on rhythms, hormones, migration and navigation close it. Behavioral ecology follows in three parts. The first concerns survival: **optimal foraging theory** and foraging under risk, defense against predators, game theory and the **evolutionarily stable strategy**, territories and contests, and life in groups. The second concerns reproduction: sexual selection by competition and by choice, mating systems, parental care, and the conflicts within families and with brood parasites. The third concerns social evolution, from **kin selection** and cooperation among unrelated individuals to cooperative breeders and social insects. Four chapters on communication then treat signals as information, their production and transmission, the evolution of their honesty and their use in social networks. A part on learning and cognition returns to all four questions, from conditioning and memory through concepts, number, tool use and theory of mind to social learning and culture, language and the applications of behavioral biology. A course summary reads the whole course once more through the four questions.

Animal behavior sits where the courses that explain how organisms work meet those that explain how they evolved, and it links to them rather than repeating them. The theory of selection on which the functional models rest, from sexual selection and life history to kin selection, the hawk–dove game and coevolution, comes from [evolutionary biology](/7.10); neurons, synapses, hormones and the physiology of the senses from [animal physiology](/7.14); and the circuits behind rhythms, social behavior, reward, place cells and decisions, with the learning rules of conditioning and reinforcement, from [computational neuroscience](/7.16). The animals themselves, their senses, migrations, colonies and defenses, are described in [zoology](/7.17), heritability and plasticity in [genetics](/7.4) and [developmental biology](/7.5), and the physics of magnetoreception and photon detection in [biophysics](/7.6). The mathematical tools come from the stability of equilibria in [ordinary differential equations](/2.4) for replicator dynamics, [dynamic programming](/4.1.13) for state-dependent foraging, [network science](/1.4.7) for social networks, experimental design from the [design and analysis of experiments](/3.8), and tests for angular data from [nonparametric statistics](/3.6.7). In turn, the decisions of individuals treated here, where to forage, when to flee, whether to defend a territory or join a group, are the starting point of population and community processes in [ecology](/7.19).

When I studied animal behavior during olympiad training, I was always tempted to see parallels in human society. On reflection I came to think that these parallels cannot be carried over directly. The theories of behavioral ecology predict behavior from the costs and benefits it has for the fitness of the actor, and they work because they can be tested on animals whose ecology is known; much of human behavior depends on language, culture and institutions in ways too complex for these models to explain, let alone to guide. The analogy is a source of questions, not of answers. The university course gave me a perspective different from that of olympiad training. It is weighted heavily toward neuroethology, toward how nervous systems produce behavior, while the quantitative view of behavior as a strategy, analyzed with optimality models and game theory, was comparatively undeveloped, although it is what turns many of the questions of the field into testable predictions.

These pages are arranged to keep both perspectives. The English textbooks form the main line. Neuroethology, the emphasis of the university course, takes its full weight in sixteen chapters, from the principles of the field to the classic model systems; the quantitative models of behavioral ecology, optimal foraging, games and evolutionarily stable strategies, the honesty of signals and kin selection, are added systematically, each with its assumptions and predictions; and Tinbergen's four questions connect the two, so that a mechanism is always read together with the function it serves. Human behavior appears only briefly, in the evolution of language and in the applications of behavioral biology, which is consistent with my view that the theories of animal behavior cannot simply be extended to it.
