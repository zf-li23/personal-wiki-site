The world of most animals changes in a regular way. Light, temperature, the risk of predation, the availability of food and the presence of mates vary with the rotation of the Earth, the tides, the phases of the Moon and the seasons, and an animal that does the right thing at the right time gains. A male field cricket calls at dusk, when females move about and the danger of being eaten by birds is past; a hamster begins to run in the evening, a fiddler crab emerges at low tide, a garden warbler becomes restless at night in autumn even in a cage, and a ground squirrel enters hibernation in autumn even if kept in constant conditions for years. Some of these changes are direct responses to the environment, but most are driven by internal clocks that anticipate the environment and are set by it.

This chapter treats the clocks from the point of view of behavior: how their existence is demonstrated and measured (18.2), where they reside in different animals (18.3), what they are good for (18.4), how annual cycles of breeding, moult, migration and hibernation are timed by day length and by circannual clocks (18.5), and how animals use their clocks to learn the time of day and to read the sun as a compass (18.6). The molecular feedback loops of the circadian clock, the light input to the **suprachiasmatic nucleus**, the theory of the **phase response curve** and sleep are treated in [7.16.35](/7.16.35); the physiology of the SCN in [7.14.46](/7.14.46#sec-46.4.5); photoperiodism and circadian clocks of plants in [7.13.16](/7.13.16) and [7.13.21](/7.13.21); insect diapause in [7.17.29](/7.17.29#sec-29.7); and oscillator models in [7.6.33](/7.6.33). Hormonal mechanisms that translate seasonal information into behavior follow in [7.18.19](/7.18.19), migration in [7.18.20](/7.18.20), and the time-compensated sun compass in [7.18.21](/7.18.21).

## 18.1. The Temporal Structure of Behavior

Behavior has rhythms with periods from milliseconds (wingbeats, song syllables, generated by **central pattern generators**, [7.18.14](/7.18.14)) to years. Rhythms with periods of hours or longer that match a geophysical cycle are called by the cycle they match:

| Rhythm | Period | Environmental cycle | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ultradian | Shorter than about 20 h | None in particular | Feeding bouts of voles (2–3 h); sleep cycles |
| Circadian | About 24 h | Rotation of the Earth | Locomotion, cricket calling, hormone secretion, sleep |
| Circatidal | About 12.4 h | Lunar tides (two per lunar day of 24.8 h) | Emergence of fiddler crabs and shore crabs at low tide |
| Circasemilunar | About 14.8 d | Spring and neap tides | Emergence of the marine midge *Clunio*; spawning of grunion |
| Circalunar | About 29.5 d | Phases of the Moon | Spawning of corals and palolo worms; moonlight avoidance |
| Circannual | About 365 d | Seasons | Reproduction, moult, migration, hibernation |

The prefix *circa-* ("about") marks rhythms that persist under constant conditions with a period that only approximately matches the environmental cycle. For any rhythmic behavior, two non-exclusive hypotheses must be distinguished. Under the *environmental stimulus hypothesis*, the animal responds directly to cues that change with the cycle: a cricket might begin to call when the light falls below a threshold. Under the *endogenous clock hypothesis*, an internal timer with a built-in schedule drives the behavior and is only adjusted by environmental cues. Most daily and annual rhythms turn out to be endogenous rhythms entrained by the environment.

## 18.2. Endogenous Rhythms

### 18.2.1. Free-Running Rhythms and Zeitgebers

The first evidence of an endogenous rhythm came from plants: in 1729 the astronomer Jean-Jacques d'Ortous de Mairan kept a *Mimosa* plant in a dark cupboard and saw that its leaves still opened and closed every day. The decisive test is to remove all environmental time cues and see whether the rhythm persists, and with what period.

**Definition 18.2.1 (Free-Running Rhythm)** A **free-running rhythm** is a biological rhythm that persists in constant conditions (constant darkness DD or constant light LL, constant temperature, no other time cues), with its own **free-running period** $\tau$. A free-running period that differs from the period of any environmental cycle is evidence that the rhythm is generated endogenously.

Males of the Australian field cricket *Teleogryllus commodus* kept in constant light continued to call for several hours each day, but each bout began about 25–26 hours after the previous one, so the calling drifted later against clock time day after day (Loher 1972). A rhythm driven by an uncontrolled environmental variable would keep exactly 24 hours (or the period of that variable), so the drift itself is evidence of an internal oscillator. Free-running periods are characteristic of species and individuals: about $24.2\,\mathrm{h}$ in humans living in temporal isolation (Czeisler and colleagues 1999), $23.5$–$23.8\,\mathrm{h}$ in laboratory mice, slightly over $24\,\mathrm{h}$ in hamsters. They are heritable: single-gene mutations change them (the *period* mutants of *Drosophila*, [7.18.4](/7.18.4)), and they vary between populations in ways that match the environment.

In nature the clock runs at exactly 24 hours because it is reset every day.

**Definition 18.2.2 (Zeitgeber)** A **zeitgeber** ("time giver") is an environmental cycle that entrains an endogenous rhythm, that is, synchronizes it to its own period by daily phase adjustments. The light–dark cycle is the dominant zeitgeber for most animals; temperature cycles, the time of feeding, social cues and, for intertidal animals, the mechanical agitation of the tides can also act as zeitgebers.

When the crickets were transferred from constant light to 12 hours of light and 12 hours of darkness (LD 12:12), their calling was **entrained**: after a few transient days it began about 2 hours before lights-off every day and ended about 2.5 hours before lights-on (Loher 1972). The calling now anticipated dusk, which a direct response to falling light intensity could not do. The stable time relation between the rhythm and the zeitgeber is the *phase angle of entrainment*: an animal with a long $\tau$ must be advanced every day and settles at a different phase from one with a short $\tau$ ([7.16.35](/7.16.35#sec-35.2.2)). Entrainment works only within a limited *range of entrainment*, typically zeitgeber periods within a few hours of $\tau$; outside it the rhythm free-runs while being pulled back and forth by the zeitgeber (relative coordination).

Two phenomena can mimic entrainment and must be excluded. Masking is a direct effect of the environment on the behavior without any effect on the clock: bright light suppresses the activity of nocturnal rodents and darkness suppresses the activity of birds. Masking and entrainment are distinguished by releasing the animal into constant conditions: an entrained rhythm then starts free-running from the phase set by the zeitgeber, whereas a masked behavior reappears at the phase of the underlying clock. Second, the behavior may be timed by several clocks: rats with lesions of the SCN, which lose their light-entrained rhythms, still anticipate a daily meal by an increase in activity a few hours beforehand, controlled by a separate food-entrainable oscillator (Stephan, Swann and Sisk 1979).

A third defining property of circadian clocks, temperature compensation, distinguishes them from ordinary biochemical processes: $\tau$ changes little with temperature ($Q_{10} \approx 0.8$–$1.2$; [7.16.35](/7.16.35#sec-35.1.1)), even in ectotherms whose body temperature follows the environment. A clock whose rate doubled for every $10\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ would be useless as a clock for a lizard or a bee. Temperature *cycles*, however, can entrain the clocks of ectotherms.

### 18.2.2. Actograms

**Definition 18.2.3 (Actogram)** An **actogram** is a graphical record of an activity rhythm in which each day is plotted as one horizontal row, with time of day along the row and activity (for example wheel revolutions or infrared-beam crossings per bin) as marks or bars, and successive days below each other. In a *double-plotted* actogram each row shows two consecutive days (day $k$ and day $k + 1$), so that rhythms that drift across midnight remain continuous.

The actogram makes the properties of a rhythm visible at a glance. In an entrained animal, onsets of activity line up vertically at a fixed phase relative to the zeitgeber. In a free-running animal they form a slanted line; a line drifting to the left means that activity starts earlier each day ($\tau < 24\,\mathrm{h}$), one drifting to the right means $\tau > 24\,\mathrm{h}$, and the slope gives $\tau - 24\,\mathrm{h}$ per day. A phase shift appears as a step in the line of onsets, followed by the same slope as before. Rodent wheel running, the standard assay, gives very precise onsets: the free-running period of a mouse can be estimated to within a few minutes from two weeks of data, by fitting a line to the onsets or by periodogram analysis of the counts.

![Model: double-plotted actograms of simulated wheel running by a nocturnal rodent. A: entrained to LD 12:12 (yellow: lights on from 06:00 to 18:00; top bar), activity starts at lights-off; from day 11 the animal is in constant darkness (gray) and free-runs with $\tau = 23.6\,\mathrm{h}$, so the onsets (dashed line) drift 24 minutes earlier every day. B: an animal free-running in constant darkness receives a 1-h light pulse (star) in its early subjective night on day 8, which delays its rhythm by 2.5 h, and one in its late subjective night on day 17, which advances it by 1.5 h. After each shift the onsets continue with the same slope, so the clock was reset without a change in its period.](./plots/actogram.py)

Phase shifts by light pulses given at different circadian times yield the **phase response curve** (PRC). Its shape is remarkably similar in nocturnal and diurnal animals and in insects and vertebrates: pulses in the subjective day have little effect, pulses in the early subjective night cause delays, and pulses in the late subjective night cause advances (DeCoursey 1960; Pittendrigh and Daan 1976). The PRC explains entrainment: dusk light falls on the delay part and dawn light on the advance part, so a clock that runs fast is held back by dusk and one that runs slow is pushed forward by dawn. A nocturnal rodent in its burrow may see daylight for only a few minutes at dusk when it looks out, and that is enough. Experiments with *skeleton photoperiods*, two short light pulses per day at the times of dawn and dusk, entrain many animals almost as well as a full day, as the PRC predicts. The theory is developed in [7.16.35](/7.16.35#sec-35.2.2).

### 18.2.3. Aschoff's Rule

Constant light changes the free-running period, and does so in opposite directions in nocturnal and diurnal animals.

**Definition 18.2.4 (Aschoff's Rule)** **Aschoff's rule** (named by Pittendrigh in 1960 after the regularities described by Jürgen Aschoff) states that in constant light the free-running period of nocturnal animals lengthens, and that of diurnal animals shortens, with increasing light intensity; correspondingly, the ratio of activity time to rest time decreases with intensity in nocturnal animals and increases in diurnal animals.

![Aschoff's rule (schematic, after Aschoff 1960). A: free-running period $\tau$ against the logarithm of constant light intensity: $\tau$ lengthens in nocturnal animals (blue) and shortens in diurnal ones (orange), typically by a few tenths of an hour per tenfold change in intensity. B: the ratio of activity time to rest time falls with intensity in nocturnal animals and rises in diurnal ones.](./plots/aschoff-rule.py)

Aschoff's rule can be understood from the PRC if constant light acts like a continuous series of pulses weighted by the shape of the curve (a "velocity response"): if the delay region dominates, light lengthens $\tau$, as in nocturnal rodents. It is a rule, not a law: it holds well for many rodents and passerine birds, but many diurnal mammals and nonpasserine birds are exceptions, and very bright constant light makes the rhythms of many animals arrhythmic. Its practical lesson is that $\tau$ must be measured in constant darkness, or at a stated light intensity, to be comparable among individuals.

## 18.3. Where Are the Clocks?

The search for the anatomical location of clocks followed the same logic in every animal: a clock should be necessary for the rhythm (its removal abolishes the rhythm in constant conditions), should keep time in isolation, and should transfer its properties (period or phase) when transplanted into an arrhythmic host.

- *Cockroaches and crickets.* In the cockroach *Leucophaea maderae*, cutting the optic tracts or removing the optic lobes abolishes the free-running locomotor rhythm, whereas cutting the compound eyes' nerves only prevents entrainment. Transplanting optic lobes from donors with a known $\tau$ into lobeless hosts restored rhythms with the period of the donor (Page 1982). Field crickets show the same: cutting the eye nerves makes calling free-run, but disconnecting the optic lobes from the rest of the brain abolishes the calling rhythm, which then occurs at all hours (Page 1985).
- *Sea slugs.* The isolated eye of *Aplysia californica*, maintained in culture in darkness, generates a circadian rhythm of compound action potentials in its optic nerve for several days, and the eyes of *Bulla* do the same; each eye is a self-contained clock that also entrains to light (Jacklet 1969). In intact animals, the eye clocks drive the locomotor rhythm.
- *Fruit flies.* The clock neurons of *Drosophila*, a network of about 150 neurons including the ventral lateral neurons that release the neuropeptide PDF, are the locus of the molecular feedback loop of *period* and *timeless* ([7.16.35](/7.16.35#sec-35.1.2)); light reaches them through **cryptochrome** within the neurons as well as through the eyes.
- *Birds.* Pinealectomized house sparrows (*Passer domesticus*) became arrhythmic in constant darkness, although they still entrained to a light–dark cycle (Gaston and Menaker 1968). Transplanting a pineal gland into the anterior chamber of the eye of an arrhythmic pinealectomized sparrow restored a rhythm whose phase was that of the donor's clock (Zimmerman and Menaker 1979): the transplanted gland carried the time with it. Isolated chick pineal cells in culture secrete melatonin rhythmically and can be entrained by light, because they contain photopigments of their own (Takahashi, Hamm and Menaker 1980). The avian circadian system is a set of coupled clocks in the pineal, the retina and hypothalamic nuclei comparable to the SCN; their relative importance differs among species, and the pineal hormone melatonin, secreted at night, couples them.
- *Mammals.* Lesions of the **suprachiasmatic nucleus** of the hypothalamus abolish the rhythms of locomotion, drinking and hormone secretion of rats (Moore and Eichler 1972; Stephan and Zucker 1972). Golden hamsters carrying the *tau* mutation, discovered by chance in a shipment of animals, have free-running periods of about 22 h (heterozygotes) or 20 h (homozygotes) (Ralph and Menaker 1988); the mutation alters the enzyme casein kinase 1ε, which phosphorylates PER proteins (Lowrey and colleagues 2000). SCN tissue transplanted from fetal *tau* hamsters into SCN-lesioned wild-type hosts restored rhythmicity with the short period of the donor genotype (Ralph and colleagues 1990), the decisive proof that the SCN contains the master clock. The SCN signals time to the rest of the body through neural projections and secreted factors such as prokineticin 2, whose expression in the SCN is rhythmic and whose infusion at night suppresses the wheel running of rats (Cheng and colleagues 2002), and indirectly through the rhythm of melatonin from the pineal gland, whose secretion the SCN drives.

The comparison shows a general design: in every animal there are one or a few master clocks, located where light information enters the brain (optic lobes, eyes, pineal, the hypothalamus above the optic chiasm), which are entrained by light and synchronize *peripheral clocks* in other tissues. Most cells of the body contain the molecular clock, and peripheral clocks can be reset by other zeitgebers, such as the time of feeding for the liver. In vertebrates the clocks are molecularly homologous, and the core feedback loop is shared with insects, so the circadian clock is at least as old as the common ancestor of bilaterians.

## 18.4. The Adaptive Value of Clocks

### 18.4.1. Anticipation and Fitness

A clock has two advantages over a direct response to the environment. It allows an animal to prepare in advance: to raise its body temperature and blood glucose before waking, to start foraging at the time when flowers open, to return to the nest before dark. And it allows the animal to keep time when the environmental cues are unreliable or invisible, as for a mammal in a burrow or a fish in deep water. Several tests support the hypothesis that clocks increase fitness.

- Eastern chipmunks (*Tamias striatus*) whose SCN had been lesioned were released into their natural habitat with intact controls. The lesioned animals were more active at night inside their burrows, and in the first months more of them were killed by weasels, which hunt at night (DeCoursey, Walker and Smith 2000).
- Mice carrying the *tau* allele of casein kinase 1ε, whose free-running period is about 22 h and whose phase of entrainment is abnormal, survived less well and produced fewer offspring than wild-type mice in outdoor enclosures (Spoelstra and colleagues 2016).
- In cyanobacteria, strains with a free-running period matching the light–dark cycle outcompete strains with mismatched periods in mixed culture, but not in constant light (Ouyang and colleagues 1998); the advantage lies in the resonance between clock and environment.

### 18.4.2. Clocks Lost and Modified

If clocks are adaptations to a cyclic world, they should be weak or absent in animals that live without cycles. Young honeybee workers that care for brood inside the dark hive work around the clock, show no circadian rhythm of activity and express little PER protein in the brain; when they become foragers they develop robust rhythms and rhythmic clock gene expression (Toma and colleagues 2000). The switch is reversible and depends on social context: nurses taken away from the brood become rhythmic, foragers returned to tend brood lose their rhythm (Shemesh and colleagues 2007). Naked mole rats (*Heterocephalus glaber*), which live in permanently dark burrows, show irregular bouts of activity without a clear circadian pattern in most individuals (Davis-Walton and Sherman 1994), although some individuals do entrain to light. The Somalian cavefish *Phreatichthys andruzzii*, isolated in caves for millions of years, has a clock that no longer responds to light, because its photoreceptor opsins are mutated, and that can be entrained by food, with an infradian free-running period of about 47 hours (Cavallari and colleagues 2011). In the Arctic, reindeer in the constant light of summer and the darkness of winter show ultradian rather than circadian activity patterns (van Oort and colleagues 2005).

### 18.4.3. Tides and the Moon

Coastal animals face a second cycle, the tides, which advance by about 50 minutes per day. Fiddler crabs (*Uca*) plug their burrows at high tide and come out to feed and court at low tide; in the laboratory in constant conditions they continue to be active at the times of low tide on their home beach for more than a week, with a circatidal period of about 12.4 h (Bennett, Shriner and Brown 1957). In the field, tidal and daily rhythms interact. Galápagos marine iguanas (*Amblyrhynchus cristatus*) feed on algae exposed at low tide; only when low tide falls far from midday, about once every two weeks, do the largest iguanas switch to diving for subtidal algae around noon, the time of day when they can rewarm quickly on the hot rocks afterward (Rubenstein and Wikelski 2003). The marine midge *Clunio marinus* lives in the lowest intertidal zone and its adults, which live only a few hours, must emerge, mate and lay eggs during the few days around spring tides when the lowest low tides expose their habitat at the right time of day; a circasemilunar clock entrained by moonlight or tidal turbulence determines the day and a circadian clock the hour, and local populations differ genetically in both (Kaiser and colleagues 2016).

The Moon also changes the risk of being seen. Banner-tailed kangaroo rats (*Dipodomys spectabilis*) in Arizona, recorded by a feeder that marked each visit on a rotating paper disc, foraged in autumn and winter only when the Moon was down (Lockard 1978); in late spring, when food was short, they also foraged by moonlight and later in the day. Their predators respond in turn: African lions hunt most successfully on dark nights around the new moon, and attacks of lions on people in Tanzania are most frequent in the evenings of the days after full moon, when the Moon rises late (Packer and colleagues 2011). Many marine invertebrates synchronize spawning to the lunar cycle: mass spawning of many species of corals on the Great Barrier Reef takes place on a few nights after the full moon in late spring, which maximizes the chance that gametes of the same species meet.

## 18.5. Annual Rhythms

### 18.5.1. Photoperiodism in Birds

Outside the tropics, food for raising young is abundant only for a few months, and most birds must prepare for breeding weeks in advance: the gonads of a male white-crowned sparrow shrink in autumn to about 1 % of their breeding mass and must regrow before the birds return from their wintering grounds. Day length is the most reliable predictor of season. William Rowan (1925) caught dark-eyed juncos (*Junco hyemalis*) in autumn in Alberta and exposed them, in outdoor aviaries in the Canadian winter, to artificially lengthened days; their testes grew in midwinter despite temperatures far below freezing. Rowan's experiment founded the study of avian **photoperiodism**, the control of seasonal processes by day length (first established in plants, [7.13.21](/7.13.21#def-21.1.1)).

How does a bird measure day length? Erwin Bünning (1936) proposed for plants that the circadian clock divides the day into a photophil phase and a photophobe phase and that long days are recognized when light falls into the latter, the **external coincidence model**. Birds follow it. White-crowned sparrows have a circadian rhythm of photosensitivity, entrained by dawn: light in the first 12 hours or so after dawn has little effect, whereas light about 16–20 hours after dawn stimulates the release of luteinizing hormone (LH) and gonadal growth (Follett, Mattocks and Farner 1974). Donald Farner (1964) tested a striking prediction. Sparrows kept on a short-day schedule of 8 hours of light and 16 hours of darkness (8L:16D) were transferred to 8L:28D. The ratio of light to dark was even lower than before, yet their testes grew: in a 36-hour cycle, every second light period falls at the time of the circadian cycle when the birds are photosensitive. Similar results were obtained in house finches (Hamner 1964), and a single hour of light in the middle of the night (a night interruption) mimics a long day.

![Model: external coincidence in avian photoperiodism. A: four light schedules (yellow light, black darkness) and the circadian rhythm of photosensitivity (blue), set by the first dawn and peaking 16–20 h after it. Gonads grow only when light coincides with the sensitive phase: under long days (16L:8D), under the 36-h schedule 8L:28D, whose second light period falls at circadian hours 12–20 (Farner 1964), and with a 1-h night interruption; short days (8L:16D) do not stimulate. B: day length over the year at 48° N, the critical day length of about 13 h, and the resulting gonadal cycle (schematic): the gonads grow when days exceed the critical length in spring and regress in summer although days are still long, because the birds have become photorefractory; short days in autumn restore photosensitivity.](./plots/photoperiod-coincidence.py)

Birds do not perceive photoperiod with their eyes alone. Ducks and sparrows that were blinded remained photoperiodic, and light delivered to the hypothalamus through a thin quartz rod stimulated their testes, whereas covering the skull with black ink or feathers blocked the response (Benoit 1935; Menaker and Keatts 1968): the photoreceptors lie in the brain. Light penetrates the skull of a small bird well, particularly at long wavelengths. Deep-brain photoreceptors in the hypothalamus contain opsins such as OPN5 (Nakane and colleagues 2010). The molecular pathway has been worked out in the Japanese quail, which responds to a single long day: long days induce the synthesis of thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) in the pars tuberalis of the pituitary, which acts on ependymal cells of the hypothalamus to induce type 2 deiodinase (Dio2), the enzyme that converts the thyroid hormone $\mathrm{T_4}$ into the active $\mathrm{T_3}$; local $\mathrm{T_3}$ then triggers morphological changes at the median eminence that allow the release of gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) and hence of LH and FSH (Yoshimura and colleagues 2003; Nakao and colleagues 2008). The same TSH–Dio2 switch, driven in mammals by the duration of nightly melatonin secretion acting on the pars tuberalis, controls seasonal breeding in sheep and hamsters ([7.14.37](/7.14.37#sec-37.9.3)).

Gonadal growth in spring is followed in many species by *photorefractoriness*: after weeks of long days the gonads regress in summer, although the days are still long, and the birds do not respond to long days again until they have experienced the short days of autumn. Photorefractoriness ends the breeding season in time for moult and migration and prevents breeding in the long days of late summer, when food for young is declining.

Even in the tropics, where day length varies little, some birds measure it. Spotted antbirds (*Hylophylax naevioides*) in Panama, where the longest and shortest days differ by less than an hour, began gonadal growth and sang six times more when day length was increased from 12 to 13 hours; an increase of only 17 minutes was sufficient (Hau and colleagues 1998).

### 18.5.2. Circannual Rhythms

Photoperiodic responses explain the timing of breeding, but some annual rhythms persist without any seasonal cue.

**Definition 18.5.1 (Circannual Rhythm)** A **circannual rhythm** is an endogenous rhythm with a period of about a year that persists in constant conditions (constant photoperiod and temperature) with a free-running period that usually differs from 12 months, and that is entrained to the year by seasonal zeitgebers, chiefly the annual cycle of photoperiod.

Testing for a circannual rhythm takes years. Golden-mantled ground squirrels (*Callospermophilus lateralis*) born in captivity, blinded and kept in constant darkness at constant temperature with unlimited food, entered **hibernation** each year at about the time of wild squirrels, for four years (Pengelley and Asmundson 1974). European stonechats (*Saxicola rubicola*) from Kenya, raised in Germany at a constant 12L:12D, went through repeated cycles of testicular growth and regression and of moult; one male completed nine cycles in 7.5 years, a free-running period of about 10 months (Gwinner and Dittami 1990). Eberhard Gwinner showed in garden warblers and blackcaps that the timing and amount of nocturnal migratory restlessness, as well as moult and body mass, follow circannual rhythms in constant photoperiod; the rhythms of birds from different populations differ in ways that match their migratory schedules, and remain different when the birds are raised under identical conditions (Helm and colleagues 2009). The circannual program is therefore heritable and adapted to the local annual cycle. Its cellular mechanism is unknown, though the pars tuberalis, whose cells can generate circannual changes in hormone output in sheep with the hypothalamus disconnected (Lincoln and colleagues 2006), is a candidate.

### 18.5.3. Predictable and Unpredictable Cues

Photoperiod tells a bird the season but not whether this year's season will be good. John Wingfield distinguished *initial predictive information*, chiefly photoperiod, which starts the long preparation for breeding; *supplementary information* from local conditions (temperature, rainfall, food), which adjusts the timing to the year and the place; *synchronizing and integrating information* from mates and social partners; and *modifying information*, such as storms, which can interrupt breeding ([7.18.19](/7.18.19)). The weight of these cues differs with the predictability of the environment.

| Environment | Cue that times breeding | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Temperate, seasonal | Photoperiod, fine-tuned by temperature and food | White-crowned sparrow; great tit (laying date follows spring temperature) |
| Tropical with small changes of day length | Small photoperiod changes, rainfall | Spotted antbird (17 min suffices) |
| Arid, with unpredictable rains | Rainfall and its effects | Rufous-winged sparrow (*Peucaea carpalis*), Sonoran Desert: HVC, RA, LH and singing increase after the first summer monsoon storms (Strand and colleagues 2007); Galápagos small ground finch |
| Unpredictable food (conifer seeds) | Food abundance, superimposed on a residual photoperiodic cycle | Red and white-winged crossbills breed in almost any month when cone crops are large (Benkman 1990), but their testes still regress in October–December even with ample food (Hahn 1998) |

Opportunistic breeders keep their reproductive system in partial readiness. Zebra finches from the arid interior of Australia, where breeding follows rain at any time of year, maintain gonads of intermediate size and breeding-season LH levels outside the breeding season, whereas zebra finches from a seasonal habitat in southern Australia regress their gonads completely (Perfito and colleagues 2007): readiness is costly but allows breeding within days of rain.

Social stimuli adjust reproductive timing as well. Male red crossbills housed with their mates regained reproductive condition after the winter break faster than males kept without females within sight and sound of the pairs (Hahn 1995), and free-living male song sparrows whose mates had been given estradiol implants, prolonging their sexual receptivity, remained in breeding condition for months longer than controls (Runfeldt and Wingfield 1985). The partners' behavior is part of the environment that a reproductive clock reads.

## 18.6. The Clock as a Tool

### 18.6.1. Time Memory

A clock also allows an animal to learn *when* something happens. In 1910 the Swiss psychiatrist Auguste Forel noticed that honeybees came to his breakfast table on the terrace at breakfast time even on days when no jam was served. Ingeborg Beling (1929), a student of Karl von Frisch, trained marked bees to collect sugar water at a feeder for two hours each day and then offered an empty feeder. The bees arrived at the training time and not at other times, even when the experiment was done in a room with constant light, temperature and humidity, so they did not depend on the position of the sun. They could be trained to two or more times a day (up to nine in later experiments, Koltermann 1971), but not to intervals other than 24 hours, such as every 19 hours: their time sense is based on a circadian clock, not on an interval timer. Max Renner (1955) trained bees in a closed room in Paris to feed at a certain hour of French time, flew them overnight to New York and tested them in an identical room: they came at the trained Paris time, five hours earlier by local clocks, proving that the clock was internal and not set by an unknown geophysical cue. This time memory (*Zeitgedächtnis*) allows a forager to visit flowers when they offer nectar, and flowers of different species open and secrete nectar at characteristic times of day, as Linnaeus noted when he designed a flower clock.

Birds and mammals learn both the time and the place of food. Garden warblers (*Sylvia borin*) in an aviary with four rooms, in each of which food was available for three hours of the day in a fixed sequence, learned to visit the right room at the right time and continued to do so when food was offered in all rooms, and they kept the schedule in constant light, as expected if it was timed by a free-running circadian clock (Biebach, Gordijn and Krebs 1989). Such time–place learning matches the distribution of resources that are renewed on a daily schedule. Interval timing, the estimation of arbitrary durations from seconds to minutes, which does not depend on the circadian clock, is treated in [7.18.43](/7.18.43).

### 18.6.2. The Sun as a Compass

The most important use of the clock in orientation is to compensate for the movement of the sun. The sun's azimuth (its compass bearing) changes on average by $15^\circ$ per hour, so an animal that steers by the sun must change its angle to the sun over the day to keep a constant heading. The rate is not uniform: it is slow in the morning and evening and fast around noon, and it depends on latitude and season. At low latitudes in summer, when the sun passes nearly overhead, the azimuth swings by more than $100^\circ$ in an hour around noon.

![The sun's azimuth over the day on the June solstice at three latitudes, computed from solar geometry. A: azimuth (clockwise from north) against local solar time; the sun is due south at noon. B: rate of change of the azimuth. The mean is 15° per hour, but the rate is small in the morning and evening and peaks at noon, strongly so at 25° N, where the sun passes within 2° of the zenith. A time-compensated sun compass must be calibrated to the local course of the sun.](./plots/sun-azimuth.py)

Animals solve the problem with a time-compensated sun compass, discovered by Gustav Kramer in starlings and by von Frisch in bees, and treated with its experimental analysis in [7.18.21](/7.18.21). Its signature is a clock-shift experiment: shifting an animal's clock by holding it under an artificial light–dark cycle displaced by six hours makes it misread the sun's position and deviate from its intended direction by roughly the angle the sun moves in six hours. Migrating monarch butterflies held on a light schedule shifted by six hours flew southeast instead of southwest in a flight simulator outdoors (Froy and colleagues 2003); their sun compass is integrated in the central complex of the brain, and the clock that compensates for the sun's movement lies partly in the antennae (Merlin, Gegear and Reppert 2009). Young pigeons and bees learn the course of the sun at their home site during their first weeks of outdoor experience; pigeons raised seeing the sun only in the afternoon could not use it correctly in the morning (Wiltschko and colleagues 1981).

## Summary

- Behavior has ultradian, circadian, circatidal, circasemilunar, circalunar and circannual rhythms. Most daily and annual rhythms are endogenous, anticipate the environment and are adjusted by it.
- An endogenous rhythm free-runs in constant conditions with its own period $\tau$ (Definition 18.2.1); it is **entrained** to the environmental period by zeitgebers (Definition 18.2.2), chiefly light; entrainment must be distinguished from masking. Circadian clocks are temperature compensated.
- Actograms (Definition 18.2.3) show entrainment, free-running and phase shifts; the **phase response curve** (delays in the early subjective night, advances in the late subjective night) explains entrainment. Aschoff's rule (Definition 18.2.4): constant light lengthens $\tau$ in nocturnal and shortens it in diurnal animals.
- Master clocks lie where light enters the brain: the optic lobes of cockroaches and crickets, the eyes of *Aplysia*, the clock neurons of *Drosophila*, the pineal (with retina and hypothalamus) of birds, the **suprachiasmatic nucleus** of mammals (transplants carry the donor's period, as with *tau* hamsters); they synchronize peripheral clocks.
- Clocks increase fitness (SCN-lesioned chipmunks, *tau* mice, cyanobacteria with mismatched periods) and are reduced in arrhythmic environments (nurse bees, naked mole rats, cavefish, Arctic reindeer). Tidal and lunar rhythms time foraging, emergence (*Clunio*), predator avoidance (kangaroo rats, lions) and spawning.
- Birds measure day length by the coincidence of light (**external coincidence model**) with a circadian photosensitive phase (8L:28D stimulates, 8L:16D does not); deep-brain photoreceptors trigger the TSH–Dio2–$\mathrm{T_3}$–GnRH pathway; photorefractoriness ends breeding. Circannual rhythms (Definition 18.5.1) persist in constant conditions (ground squirrels, stonechats with $\tau \approx 10$ months, warblers' migratory restlessness) and are entrained by photoperiod.
- Breeding is timed by initial predictive cues (photoperiod) and adjusted by supplementary cues (rain, food, temperature) and social cues, with opportunistic breeders in unpredictable environments keeping reproductive readiness.
- Clocks allow time memory (bees trained to 24-h times, Renner's Paris–New York experiment; time–place learning in warblers) and the time-compensated sun compass, whose rate of compensation must match the non-uniform course of the sun.

## Questions

1. A mouse in constant darkness starts running 20 minutes earlier each day. When placed in LD 12:12, should its activity onset come before or after lights-off, compared with a mouse whose $\tau$ is exactly 24 h? Use the phase response curve.

   **Answer** The mouse has $\tau = 24\,\mathrm{h} - 20\,\mathrm{min} = 23.67\,\mathrm{h}$: its clock runs fast and must be delayed by 20 minutes every day to stay entrained. Delays are produced by light in the early subjective night, so the mouse entrains at a phase at which the light at the end of the light period (dusk) falls slightly into its early subjective night. Its subjective night, and therefore its activity onset, begins somewhat before lights-off, earlier than in a mouse with $\tau = 24\,\mathrm{h}$, for which no net shift is needed and the light can fall in the dead zone. In general, animals with shorter $\tau$ entrain with an earlier phase angle (they are "early chronotypes").

2. In an experiment, a nocturnal rodent stops running when the lights are turned on in the middle of its active phase and resumes when they are turned off. Is this evidence that light entrains its clock? How would you find out?

   **Answer** Not necessarily: the immediate suppression of activity by light is masking, a direct effect on behavior that may leave the clock unaffected. To test for an effect on the clock, give the light pulse to an animal free-running in constant darkness and keep it in darkness afterward; extrapolate the line of activity onsets from the days before the pulse and compare it with the onsets on the following days. A step in the onset line (a delay after an early-night pulse, an advance after a late-night pulse) that persists for many days shows that the clock was reset. If the onsets continue on the extrapolated line, light only masked the activity.

3. (Calculation) A bird uses a time-compensated sun compass with a fixed rate of $15^\circ$ per hour, and its clock has been shifted six hours fast. It is released at noon at $40^\circ$ N in June and wants to fly south. Using the figure of the sun's azimuth, estimate its deviation, and explain why it differs from $90^\circ$.

   **Answer** At true noon the sun is due south (azimuth $180^\circ$), but the bird's clock reads 18:00. With the fixed $15^\circ$-per-hour rule it expects the sun six hours past noon at $180^\circ + 90^\circ = 270^\circ$ (west); to fly south it keeps the sun $90^\circ$ to its right. Since the real sun is in the south, keeping it $90^\circ$ to the right means heading $180^\circ - 90^\circ = 90^\circ$, east: a deviation of $90^\circ$ counterclockwise. If the bird has learned the true course of the sun, it expects the sun at about $290^\circ$ at 18:00 in June at $40^\circ$ N and keeps it about $110^\circ$ to its right, heading about $70^\circ$ (east-northeast), a deviation of about $110^\circ$. The deviation thus depends on the time of day, the season and the latitude, not only on the size of the shift. Measured deflections of clock-shifted pigeons are often somewhat smaller than predicted, partly because the birds also use their magnetic compass.

4. Why is the experiment of Farner with 8L:28D a more convincing test of the external coincidence model than the observation that long days stimulate gonadal growth?

   **Answer** The observation that long days stimulate is consistent with many models, including the simplest one, an "hourglass" that measures the total amount of light per day. The 8L:28D schedule gives less light per hour than the non-stimulatory 8L:16D, so an amount-of-light model predicts no growth. The external coincidence model predicts growth because the 36-h cycle places every second light period at the circadian phase 12–20 h after the previous dawn, when the birds are photosensitive. The testes grew. A test is convincing when its result is predicted by one hypothesis and contradicts the alternatives; the resonance schedule is such a test.

5. (Open question) Clocks are thought to be adaptive because they allow anticipation. Design a study that compares the fitness benefit of anticipation with that of a purely reactive response, in an animal of your choice.

   **Answer** One design uses the *Drosophila* clock. Generate flies of the same genetic background that are arrhythmic (*per*$^{01}$ or *tim*$^{01}$) or have short or long periods, and keep populations in outdoor cages or in chambers simulating natural light and temperature cycles, with food that is available only at a fixed time (for example a daily window of fresh yeast in the morning). Measure feeding (by dye), fecundity and survival, and in competition experiments the change in frequency of the genotypes over generations. The anticipation hypothesis predicts that rhythmic flies whose period matches 24 h arrive at the food when it appears and outcompete arrhythmic flies, which respond only after detecting food, and that the advantage disappears when food is constantly available or appears at random times. Mismatched periods should do worse than matched ones under a 24-h cycle but better under a matching cycle (a resonance test as in cyanobacteria). Controls must check for pleiotropic effects of the mutations, for example by rescuing the clock with a transgene.
