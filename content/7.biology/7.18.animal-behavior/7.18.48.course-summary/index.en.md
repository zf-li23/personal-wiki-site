Forty-seven chapters have treated animal behavior one problem at a time: how a moth hears a bat, when a bird should leave a patch, why a worker ant does not reproduce, whether a chimpanzee knows what another has seen. This final chapter does not repeat them. It reads the course again along the framework set out at its start, **Tinbergen's four questions** (Definition 1.3.1), and asks what each part of the course contributed to each question, which ideas and models recur across chapters that seem to have nothing in common, and where the answers to the different questions meet or fail to meet.

Section 48.1 shows how the chapters are distributed over the four questions and follows a few behaviors through all four. Sections 48.2 and 48.3 collect the recurring principles of mechanism and development, Section 48.4 the models of behavioral ecology, arranged in five families, and Section 48.5 the comparative evidence on the evolution of behavior. Section 48.6 asks how far the assumptions behind the functional models can be trusted, Section 48.7 where the study of mechanism and the study of function have informed each other, and Section 48.8 lists open questions. Section 48.9 collects the links to other courses. Every statement refers to the chapter where it is developed; the summary adds no new results, only a new arrangement.

## 48.1. Four Questions, Forty-Seven Chapters

### 48.1.1. How the Course Divides the Questions

The four questions cross two distinctions ([7.18.1](/7.18.1#sec-1.3)): the **proximate cause** of a behavior, its mechanism and development within the individual, against its **ultimate cause**, its function and evolutionary history; and the behavior as it is now (mechanism, function) against how it came to be (development, evolution). The questions are logically independent, but the most informative studies combine them, and the course was arranged so that the proximate questions come first ([7.18.6](/7.18.6)–[7.18.21](/7.18.21)) and the ultimate ones follow ([7.18.22](/7.18.22)–[7.18.40](/7.18.40)), with the chapters on learning and cognition ([7.18.41](/7.18.41)–[7.18.47](/7.18.47)) returning to all four.

![Which of Tinbergen's four questions each chapter of the course mainly answers. Columns: chapters 7.18.1 to 7.18.47, grouped into the seven parts of the course. Filled discs: questions that carry the main argument of the chapter; open rings: secondary themes. The neuroethology chapters answer the mechanism question and treat function as background; the chapters of behavioral ecology answer the function question, and many of them also the evolutionary one, through the comparative method; development is concentrated in the foundations, in birdsong, hormones and navigation, and in the chapters on learning and culture, the forms of development driven by experience. The assignment is a reading aid, not data](./plots/four-questions-matrix.py)

The figure shows an asymmetry that the course shares with the field. Mechanism and function are each treated in about twenty chapters; development and evolution are each the main subject of fewer than ten. The reason is partly historical: **neuroethology** and **behavioral ecology** grew as separate programs, the one asking how nervous systems produce natural behavior, the other what the behavior is for ([7.18.1](/7.18.1#sec-1.2.5)), and each built its own toolkit. Development was long treated under the heading of "instinct versus learning" ([7.18.3](/7.18.3#sec-3.5)), and evolution, in the strict sense of history, requires phylogenies that were not available for most behaviors until recently ([7.18.2](/7.18.2#sec-2.3)).

### 48.1.2. One Behavior, Four Answers

A few behaviors in the course have been studied at all four levels, and they show best what each answer contributes.

| Behavior | Mechanism | Development | Function | Evolution |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mobbing in gulls ([7.18.1](/7.18.1#sec-1.5)) | Sight of a predator near the nest releases alarm calls and attacks | Learned recognition of dangerous species | Distracts egg predators: eggs inside the colony survive better | Lost in the cliff-nesting kittiwake; convergent in swallows and ground squirrels |
| Birdsong ([7.18.17](/7.18.17)) | Song system: HVC → RA motor pathway, anterior forebrain loop, the **syrinx** | Sensory and sensorimotor phases of **song learning** in a **sensitive period** | Territory defense, neighbor recognition, female choice | **Vocal learning** evolved two or three times in birds, convergent with humans |
| Bat–moth interaction ([7.18.12](/7.18.12)) | Two receptor cells (A1, A2) in the moth ear; delay-tuned neurons and Doppler-shift compensation in bats | Largely innate in moths | Turning away and erratic flight raise survival | Arms race: tiger moth clicks and jamming, stealthy bats, convergent ultrasonic hearing in mantises and lacewings |
| Bird migration ([7.18.20](/7.18.20), [7.18.21](/7.18.21)) | Sun, star and magnetic compasses; clock-and-compass program | Inherited program in juveniles; star compass learned before the first migration | Seasonal resources weighed against energy, time and mortality | Evolutionarily labile; a new route of blackcaps evolved within decades |
| Honey bee division of labor ([7.18.5](/7.18.5#sec-5.6.1), [7.18.19](/7.18.19#sec-19.7), [7.18.36](/7.18.36#sec-36.9.2)) | Juvenile hormone–vitellogenin switch; brain gene expression | Age polyethism, adjusted by inhibition from foragers | Colony efficiency; response thresholds | **Eusociality** arose under lifetime monogamy |

The examples illustrate the claim of [7.18.1](/7.18.1#sec-1.3.2) that the answers constrain one another. The mechanism of song learning, a **basal ganglia** loop that injects variability and is reinforced by **dopamine**, explains why song learning has a sensitive period and why dialects drift by **cultural evolution**; the function of song in neighbor recognition explains why learning from local tutors pays ([7.18.17](/7.18.17#sec-17.5.6)). The sensitivity of the moth ear, matched to the distance at which bats can detect moths, is a mechanism that cannot be understood without its function ([7.18.12](/7.18.12#sec-12.5)).

## 48.2. Mechanism: How Nervous Systems Produce Behavior

The chapters on mechanism did not derive the cellular physiology of neurons, which belongs to animal physiology and computational neuroscience; they asked what properties of nervous systems matter for natural behavior. Six principles recur.

1. Choose the animal for the problem. **Krogh's principle** (Definition 6.2.1) made a few specialists the classic systems: the barn owl for sound localization ([7.18.11](/7.18.11#sec-11.4.2)), *Eigenmannia* for the **jamming avoidance response** ([7.18.15](/7.18.15)), the toad for prey recognition ([7.18.13](/7.18.13#sec-13.2)), crickets and frogs for acoustic communication ([7.18.16](/7.18.16)), the zebra finch for vocal learning. Identified neurons allow results from many individuals to be combined (Definition 6.2.2).
2. Senses are filters. Each animal lives in its own **Umwelt** (Definition 9.1.1), and its receptors and early circuits pass only the stimuli that matter: the A1 cell of the moth ear, the ears of parasitoid flies tuned to their hosts, matched filters in the frog inner ear, the seasonally retuned saccule of the midshipman ([7.18.13](/7.18.13#sec-13.1)). Physics sets the limits: photon noise for night vision ([7.18.10](/7.18.10#sec-10.2.3)), head size for interaural time differences ([7.18.11](/7.18.11#sec-11.4.1)), the steep fall of dipole fields for electroreception ([7.18.15](/7.18.15#sec-15.1)), the $(Q/K)^{1/3}$ growth of a pheromone's active space ([7.18.38](/7.18.38#sec-38.4.2)).
3. Recognition is computation in populations, not in single cells. A **feature detector** (Definition 13.1.2) such as the toad's T5(2) neuron or the locust LGMD responds to a configuration of stimuli, but the decision to strike or flee emerges from circuits shaped by inhibition, modulation and learning ([7.18.13](/7.18.13#sec-13.2.4)). Likewise the **command neuron** (Definition 14.4.1) proved rare; most decisions are made by distributed populations ([7.18.14](/7.18.14#sec-14.4.2)). The JAR is the clearest case: a democratic computation over the whole body surface, carried out in parallel time and amplitude streams ([7.18.15](/7.18.15#sec-15.5.3)).
4. Speed and rhythm have dedicated designs. Escape uses giant fibers and electrical synapses ([7.18.14](/7.18.14#sec-14.1)); rhythmic behavior uses a **central pattern generator** built around a half-center oscillator, fitted to the body by sensory feedback ([7.18.14](/7.18.14#sec-14.5)). Fixed action patterns ([7.18.3](/7.18.3)) are the behavioral face of such motor programs.
5. States are set chemically. The same stimulus produces different behavior in different states. A **neuromodulator** such as serotonin, octopamine or dopamine reconfigures polymorphic networks over seconds to hours ([7.18.7](/7.18.7#sec-7.5)); hormones act over hours to a lifetime, through organizational and activational effects ([7.18.19](/7.18.19#sec-19.2)), and couple behavior to social status and to stressors in both directions ([7.18.19](/7.18.19#sec-19.5)). This is the mechanistic content of what classical ethology called **motivation** ([7.18.3](/7.18.3#sec-3.4)).
6. Internal models anticipate the world. Circadian and circannual clocks anticipate the environment and are entrained by it ([7.18.18](/7.18.18)); corollary discharges cancel self-generated signals in electric fish and singing crickets ([7.18.15](/7.18.15#sec-15.7), [7.18.16](/7.18.16#sec-16.1.3)); dragonflies steer by predicting the motion of prey ([7.18.13](/7.18.13#sec-13.4.2)); ants and bees keep a running estimate of home by **path integration** ([7.18.21](/7.18.21#sec-21.6.1)).

A recurrent finding is that the same molecular and cellular parts are reused for very different behaviors. Opsins, G protein–coupled receptors and mechanically gated channels serve all senses ([7.18.9](/7.18.9#sec-9.2)); the PKG gene *foraging*, vasopressin and oxytocin, and the vitellogenin–juvenile hormone network recur across distant lineages ([7.18.5](/7.18.5#sec-5.7)); and neurotoxins of plants and animals converge on a handful of channels ([7.18.6](/7.18.6#sec-6.4)). Diversity of behavior arises less from new parts than from new combinations and new tuning of old ones.

## 48.3. Development: Genes, Experience and Sensitive Periods

The course replaced the opposition between instinct and learning with an interactionist view ([7.18.3](/7.18.3#sec-3.5), [7.18.4](/7.18.4#sec-4.1)): every behavior develops through genes and environment, only differences in behavior can be attributed to differences in genes or environment, and "innate" conflates several properties (present at birth, developing without practice, species-typical, heritable). Four themes run through the chapters.

- **Sensitive periods** (Definition 5.2.1) recur wherever an early experience must calibrate a lasting representation: **filial imprinting** in precocial birds ([7.18.5](/7.18.5#sec-5.3)), the tutor memory of songbirds ([7.18.17](/7.18.17#sec-17.2)), the alignment of the barn owl's auditory map with vision ([7.18.11](/7.18.11#sec-11.4.3)), olfactory imprinting of salmon on the home stream ([7.18.21](/7.18.21#sec-21.5.1)), the learned star compass of night migrants ([7.18.21](/7.18.21#sec-21.3.1)) and perceptual narrowing in human speech ([7.18.47](/7.18.47#sec-47.3.1)). In each case learning is guided by predispositions (the head and neck of a fowl, conspecific syllables), and the period often ends because experience itself commits the representation.
- Genes of large effect and supergenes show that single loci can switch whole behavioral programs: *foraging*, *npr-1*, the vasopressin receptor of voles ([7.18.4](/7.18.4#sec-4.3)); the **supergene** morphs of fire ants, ruffs and white-throated sparrows ([7.18.4](/7.18.4#sec-4.4)), which reappear as genetic strategies in [7.18.28](/7.18.28#sec-28.3.5) and as a green beard in [7.18.33](/7.18.33#sec-33.4.4). Most behavioral variation is nonetheless polygenic, pleiotropic and environment-dependent ([7.18.4](/7.18.4#sec-4.6)).
- Switches and conditional strategies. A **developmental switch** (Definition 5.6.1) turns environmental cues into discrete phenotypes: locust phases, cannibal salamanders, horned and hornless beetles, territorial and nonterritorial cichlids ([7.18.5](/7.18.5#sec-5.6)). In behavioral ecology the same structure is a **conditional strategy**, whose switch point is set where the marginal individual does equally well with both tactics (Proposition 28.3.1); partial migration ([7.18.20](/7.18.20#sec-20.5.3)) and alternative male tactics ([7.18.28](/7.18.28#sec-28.3)) are examples.
- Experience across generations. Maternal licking in rats changes the methylation of the glucocorticoid receptor gene and is transmitted nongenetically ([7.18.4](/7.18.4#sec-4.5)); early conditions act through **maternal effects** and predictive adaptive responses ([7.18.5](/7.18.5#sec-5.4.3)); and social learning extends transmission beyond the family into traditions and **animal culture** ([7.18.46](/7.18.46)).

Learning itself (Definition 41.1.1) is the most flexible form of development, and the course treated its value in the same way as any other trait: learning pays when the environment is predictable within a lifetime but not between generations (Formula 41.1.1), and what an animal learns easily is shaped by selection, as **preparedness** (Definition 41.5.2) and **conditioned taste aversion** (Definition 41.5.1) show.

## 48.4. Function: The Models of Behavioral Ecology

The function chapters all start from an **optimality model** (Definition 2.4.1): a decision, a currency and a set of constraints, from which quantitative predictions follow ([7.18.2](/7.18.2#sec-2.4.4)). The models of the course fall into five families, distinguished by how the payoff of a behavior is determined.

![The core models of the course in five families: optimality with fixed payoffs, games with frequency-dependent payoffs, inclusive fitness (payoffs weighted by relatedness), information and signaling (receivers as decision makers) and learning rules. Numbers are the sections of 7.18 where each model is derived. Each column reads downward from the general result to its applications; horizontal arrows mark where one family builds on another: an optimality problem becomes a game when the payoff depends on what others do, inclusive fitness weights the payoffs of a game by relatedness, and detection thresholds and prediction errors join the information family to learning. Notes give further links: Grafen's honest signal is an ESS, the Sir Philip Sidney game uses relatedness, the matching law is the individual counterpart of the ideal free distribution, and sampling a bandit resembles the decision to leave a patch](./plots/model-families.py)

### 48.4.1. Optimality with Fixed Payoffs

When the payoff of an option is fixed by the environment, the best behavior is found by maximizing. **Optimal foraging theory** (Definition 22.5.1) supplies the canonical results.

- The **prey model** (Theorem 22.5.1): rank prey by profitability $E/h$ and include a type if its profitability exceeds the rate obtained without it, independently of its own abundance.
- The **marginal value theorem** (Theorem 22.5.2): leave a patch when the marginal rate of gain falls to the long-term average rate. The same logic sets the load size of starlings, the copulation time of dung flies ([7.18.22](/7.18.22#sec-22.5.3)) and the departure fuel load of a migrant at a stopover (Example 20.4.1); **central-place foraging** (Definition 22.5.3) is its extension to loads carried home.
- When fitness is a nonlinear function of food, the variance of rewards matters through **Jensen's inequality**, and **risk-sensitive foraging** follows the **energy budget rule** (Definition 23.2.2).
- When the value of food depends on the animal's reserves and the time left, decisions are solved by **dynamic programming** (Formula 23.3.1), which explains why small birds fatten in the afternoon ([7.18.23](/7.18.23#sec-23.3.2)).
- When food and danger trade off, **Gilliam's rule** (Formula 23.6.1) minimizes mortality per unit growth; the economic model of escape (Formula 24.6.1) sets the flight initiation distance where the costs of staying and fleeing are equal; giving-up densities measure the **landscape of fear** (Definition 23.6.2).
- Parental decisions are optimality problems too: the parent's optimum equates marginal benefit and marginal cost (Proposition 31.3.1), and costs to future reproduction make the best clutch smaller than Lack's (Example 31.3.1). Territorial defense pays when a resource is economically defensible (Example 22.6.1, [7.18.26](/7.18.26#sec-26.3.1)).

### 48.4.2. Games: When Payoffs Depend on Others

As soon as individuals compete for the same resources, the best behavior depends on what others do, and maximization gives way to stability ([7.18.25](/7.18.25#sec-25.1.1)). An **evolutionarily stable strategy** is a **Nash equilibrium** that also resists invasion by mutants that do equally well against residents (Proposition 25.1.1), and every ESS is an asymptotically stable rest point of the **replicator dynamics** (Theorem 25.1.1). The course used this machinery more than any other.

| Problem | Model | Key result |
|---|---|---|
| Fighting over a resource | **Hawk–dove game** (Example 25.1.1) | Mixed ESS $p^* = V/C$; escalation when $V \geq C$ |
| Respect for ownership | **Bourgeois strategy** (Example 25.2.1) | Arbitrary asymmetries can settle contests, though the classic butterfly example fails |
| Contests without weapons | **War of attrition** (Theorem 25.2.1); assessment models (Algorithm 26.4.1) | Exponential persistence with mean $V/k$; durations distinguish mutual from self-assessment |
| Distribution over habitats | **Ideal free distribution** (Definition 25.3.1); **despotic distribution** | Input matching, often undermatching; removal experiments reveal despotism |
| Finding versus joining | **Producer–scrounger game** (Proposition 25.4.1) | Frequency-dependent equilibrium of scroungers |
| Alternative mating tactics | Switch point (Proposition 28.3.1); rock–paper–scissors (Theorem 28.3.1) | Conditional tactics need not have equal payoffs; genetic strategies must, or cycle |
| Sperm competition | ESS ejaculate expenditure (Proposition 28.4.1) | Investment rises with the risk of competition |
| Vigilance and group size | Example 27.2.1; Proposition 27.5.1 | Groups grow beyond the optimal to the stable size |
| Parental care | Care and desertion (Theorem 32.2.1); partial compensation (Theorem 32.3.1); **negotiation model** | Biparental care is stabilized by partial compensation |
| Mate choice | Optimal acceptance threshold (Proposition 29.5.1) | Choosiness falls with sampling costs |

The table shows why game theory became the common language of behavioral ecology: territories, mating tactics, parental care and group life, which look like separate subjects, are all problems in which the payoff of a behavior depends on its frequency.

### 48.4.3. Inclusive Fitness: Payoffs Weighted by Relatedness

When actor and recipient share genes by descent, the payoff to a behavior includes its effects on relatives. **Kin selection** and **Hamilton's rule** ([7.18.33](/7.18.33#sec-33.2)) depend as much on ecology, through $B$ and $C$, as on genetics, through $r$: helping parents rear full siblings requires $B/C > 1$ (Formula 33.2.1). The rule explains helping, restraint and harm in one framework: alarm calls directed at kin ([7.18.33](/7.18.33#sec-33.3)), selfish restraint by cannibalistic salamanders ([7.18.33](/7.18.33#sec-33.6)), and spite by soldier larvae when relatedness to rivals is negative ([7.18.33](/7.18.33#sec-33.7)). The same logic yields conflict as well as cooperation:

- Kin competition can cancel the benefits of helping relatives in viscous populations ([7.18.33](/7.18.33#sec-33.5)) and favors dispersal of at least half the offspring (Theorem 26.6.1).
- **Parent–offspring conflict** arises because an offspring values itself more than its siblings, so its optimal investment exceeds its parent's whenever $r < 1$ (Proposition 32.4.1); costly begging can resolve it ([7.18.32](/7.18.32#sec-32.6.2)).
- In social insects, queens and workers disagree over the sex ratio (Formula 36.7.1) and over who produces males, and **worker policing** is predicted where queens mate multiply ([7.18.36](/7.18.36#sec-36.8)); these predictions, made from relatedness alone, are the most precise quantitative successes of the theory ([7.18.36](/7.18.36#sec-36.11)).
- Within cooperative groups, the sharing of reproduction is described by models of **reproductive skew** (Formula 35.5.1), with mixed empirical support ([7.18.35](/7.18.35#sec-35.5.2)).

Cooperation among nonrelatives requires direct benefits: by-products, reciprocity or enforcement ([7.18.34](/7.18.34#sec-34.2)). Here the theory and the evidence diverge most clearly: **tit for tat** is a celebrated solution of the iterated **prisoner's dilemma**, yet convincing cases of reciprocity in animals are few, whereas by-product benefits, punishment and sanctions are well documented ([7.18.34](/7.18.34#sec-34.4.4)).

### 48.4.4. Information: Receivers as Decision Makers

The communication chapters treated a receiver as a decision maker that must act without knowing the true state of the world ([7.18.37](/7.18.37#sec-37.3)). Bayes' theorem (Formula 37.3.1) updates its estimate; the threshold probability $p^* = L_2/(L_1 + L_2)$ (Proposition 37.3.1) tells it when to act; and the **value of information** (Definition 37.3.1) is never negative and is zero unless a signal can change the decision (Proposition 37.3.2). The same structure, two states of the world, a noisy cue and a criterion set by prior probabilities and by the costs of the two errors, appears throughout the course under different names:

| Context | Decision | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Fear ([7.18.8](/7.18.8#sec-8.3.2)) | Is this a predator? | The smoke-detector principle: false alarms are cheap, so the optimal alarm is biased toward them (Example 8.3.1) |
| Mimicry ([7.18.24](/7.18.24#sec-24.4.2)) | Attack this prey? | Batesian mimics are protected when rare, close to the model and when the model is very noxious (Formula 24.4.1) |
| Kin and nestmate recognition ([7.18.33](/7.18.33#sec-33.4.5), [7.18.40](/7.18.40#sec-40.3.1)) | Accept this individual? | Stricter thresholds where intruders are common or costly to accept |
| Brood parasitism ([7.18.32](/7.18.32#sec-32.7.2)) | Reject this egg? | Once mimicry is good, acceptance may be optimal |
| Mate choice ([7.18.29](/7.18.29#sec-29.5.1)) | Accept this male? | The threshold equals the expected payoff of further search |

This is **signal detection theory** applied to natural decisions, and it explains why receivers are sometimes gullible and sometimes fastidious without assuming that they are mistaken.

The reliability of signals under conflict of interest is the central problem of communication ([7.18.39](/7.18.39#sec-39.2)). The course distinguished three guarantees. An **index signal** (Definition 39.3.1) cannot be faked because it is physically tied to the quality it signals (formant dispersion and body size). A **costly signal** (Definition 39.4.1), Zahavi's **handicap principle** made rigorous by Grafen, is honest if better senders pay lower marginal costs, which makes an honest, increasing signal level an ESS (Proposition 39.4.1); the costs need not be visible at equilibrium. And **cheap talk** (Definition 39.5.1) is stable when interests coincide or when social costs punish cheaters. Among relatives, the Sir Philip Sidney game (Example 39.4.1) shows that honest signals of need can be stable for a range of costs set by relatedness, joining the information family to inclusive fitness. Deception persists at frequencies that receivers tolerate ([7.18.39](/7.18.39#sec-39.6.2)), limited by frequency dependence, as in Batesian mimicry.

### 48.4.5. Learning Rules: Decisions from Experience

The last family describes how individuals arrive at good decisions without knowing the payoffs in advance. The **Rescorla–Wagner rule** explains blocking, overshadowing and conditioned inhibition by a prediction error, and attention-based rules such as Pearce–Hall (Formula 41.3.1) explain latent inhibition ([7.18.41](/7.18.41#sec-41.3.4)). Choice between reinforcement schedules follows the matching law (Formula 41.4.1), the individual counterpart of the **ideal free distribution**: an individual allocates its responses among options as a population of foragers allocates itself among patches. Songbirds learn by a perturbation rule whose rate is proportional to the variance of exploration (Eq. (17.1)), an instance of **reinforcement learning** in a basal ganglia circuit. Foragers facing a two-armed bandit sample, then exploit the better option, the **exploration–exploitation trade-off** ([7.18.23](/7.18.23#sec-23.1.2)). And at the level of populations, Rogers' paradox shows that indiscriminate copying does not raise mean fitness, whereas selective social learning and **conformist bias** (Definition 46.6.1) do ([7.18.46](/7.18.46#sec-46.2.2), [7.18.46](/7.18.46#sec-46.6.1)).

Learning rules connect the functional models to mechanism. An optimality model says what an animal should do; a learning rule says how it could come to do it, and its departures from optimality, such as undermatching or risk-proneness for variable delays, are predictions about mechanism (Section 48.7).

## 48.5. Evolution: History and Comparison

The evolutionary question asks how a behavior arose from that of ancestors. Its main tool is the **comparative method**: cross-species correlations between behavior and ecology, such as those of Cullen's kittiwakes, Crook's weaverbirds and Jarman's antelopes, corrected for shared ancestry by **phylogenetically independent contrasts** ([7.18.2](/7.18.2#sec-2.3)). Four patterns recur.

- Convergence is common. Echolocation evolved in bats and toothed whales ([7.18.12](/7.18.12#sec-12.4.5)); bat-detecting ears in moths, mantises, lacewings and crickets ([7.18.12](/7.18.12#sec-12.5.3)); electric organs and electrolocation in gymnotiform and mormyriform fishes ([7.18.15](/7.18.15#sec-15.3)); vocal learning two or three times in birds and several times in mammals ([7.18.17](/7.18.17#sec-17.4.2)); stereopsis in mantises ([7.18.10](/7.18.10#sec-10.3.1)); complex cognition in corvids, parrots, primates and octopuses with very different brains ([7.18.8](/7.18.8#sec-8.1.3)); and tool use many times over ([7.18.44](/7.18.44#sec-44.1.4)). **Convergent evolution** is the strongest evidence for adaptation in the comparative data, because each origin is an independent test.
- Losses are as informative as gains. Kittiwakes have lost mobbing ([7.18.1](/7.18.1#sec-1.5.3)); cats, pandas and sea lions have lost taste receptor genes that their diets made useless ([7.18.9](/7.18.9#sec-9.6.2)); clocks are reduced in caves and in the Arctic summer ([7.18.18](/7.18.18#sec-18.4.2)); passive electroreception was lost and reinvented ([7.18.15](/7.18.15#sec-15.2.2)).
- Signals are built from preexisting material. Displays arise by **ritualization** (Definition 39.1.1) of intention movements and other behavior, and new preferences arise from **sensory bias** in receivers ([7.18.39](/7.18.39#sec-39.1)); the túngara chuck and the water mite's courtship exploit receptive systems that evolved for other purposes ([7.18.16](/7.18.16#sec-16.6.4), [7.18.29](/7.18.29#sec-29.4)). **Sensory drive** (Definition 38.6.1) adds the habitat, coupling signals, sense organs and the light or sound environment.
- Behavior can evolve fast. Blackcaps evolved a new northwestern migration route within decades ([7.18.20](/7.18.20#sec-20.5.2)); silent flatwing crickets spread on Kauai within about 20 generations under parasitoid attack ([7.18.16](/7.18.16#sec-16.5)); guppies changed their shoaling within about 100 generations and their coloration within about ten when predation changed ([7.18.27](/7.18.27#sec-27.4), [7.18.39](/7.18.39#sec-39.8)). An **evolutionary arms race**, between bats and moths, cuckoos and hosts, toxic prey and resistant predators, can be followed in real time and across populations ([7.18.12](/7.18.12#sec-12.6), [7.18.32](/7.18.32#sec-32.7), [7.18.6](/7.18.6#sec-6.4.3)); the **life–dinner principle** (Definition 24.1.1) explains why such races are asymmetric.

The deepest evolutionary question of the course, the origin of eusociality, was answered by combining comparison with theory: obligate eusociality arose only where queens were lifetime monogamous (Definition 36.5.1), as **Hamilton's rule** predicts, whereas the haplodiploidy hypothesis (Definition 36.4.1) fails once relatedness is weighted by **reproductive value** (Example 36.4.1).

## 48.6. How Far Can the Assumptions of Behavioral Ecology Be Trusted?

The models of Section 48.4 rest on assumptions that are not themselves tested in any single study. The course returned to them repeatedly, and it is useful to collect the answers.

### 48.6.1. Optimization

Behavioral ecology assumes that selection has produced behavior that is good at maximizing fitness in the environment in which it evolved. This is a working hypothesis, not a claim of perfection ([7.18.22](/7.18.22#sec-22.6.3)). Each model tests a particular currency and set of constraints, and a model whose predictions fail is rejected; what is not tested in one study is the general assumption that animals are well designed, which is justified by the success of the approach across many studies. A failure is information: the oystercatchers of [7.18.22](/7.18.22#sec-22.6.3) did not select mussels as the simple model predicted until the costs of failed attacks and barnacle-covered shells were measured, and the pike cichlid could be judged optimal or not depending on which costs were counted. Optimization is most convincing where the currency can be measured independently and the predictions are quantitative, as for immobile prey ([7.18.22](/7.18.22#sec-22.5.2)), and least convincing where the currency is chosen after the fact.

### 48.6.2. The Phenotypic Gambit and Genetic Constraints

Game-theoretic models treat a strategy as an inherited decision rule and ignore the details of inheritance, the *phenotypic gambit* ([7.18.25](/7.18.25#sec-25.1.1), [7.18.37](/7.18.37#sec-37.5.1)). The justification is that genetic details usually affect how fast and how closely a population approaches an equilibrium rather than which equilibria are possible. The course also met the exceptions. Supergenes lock several traits together, so that behavioral morphs are inherited as units and cannot be optimized separately ([7.18.4](/7.18.4#sec-4.4)); genetic strategies must have equal payoffs at equilibrium, whereas the tactics of a conditional strategy need not ([7.18.25](/7.18.25#sec-25.4.3), [7.18.28](/7.18.28#sec-28.3.1)); **animal personality** and **behavioral syndromes** show that behaviors in different contexts are correlated, which can prevent each from reaching its own optimum ([7.18.25](/7.18.25#sec-25.5)); and intralocus sexual conflict makes the best sire for sons a poor sire for daughters ([7.18.29](/7.18.29#sec-29.6)).

### 48.6.3. Current Function and Past History

Function concerns the present effect of a behavior on fitness, evolution its history ([7.18.1](/7.18.1#sec-1.3.2)), and the two need not coincide. A preference that females now express for a male trait may have originated as a sensory bias that evolved for foraging, and the trait may then be maintained by any of the mechanisms of mate choice ([7.18.29](/7.18.29#sec-29.4)); a structure may be co-opted for a new signaling function, as in the panda principle ([7.18.39](/7.18.39#sec-39.1.4)); and a host behavior that appears to serve a parasite may be a by-product of infection rather than an adaptive manipulation ([7.18.22](/7.18.22#sec-22.4.2)). Showing that a behavior is adaptive now does not show why it arose; the comparative method, by mapping the order of origin of trait and preference, is needed for the second claim.

### 48.6.4. Mechanisms as Constraints

Animals do not compute optima; they follow mechanisms that produce good behavior in the environments where those mechanisms evolved. When the environment changes, the mechanisms show through. Supernormal stimuli are preferred to natural ones ([7.18.3](/7.18.3#sec-3.2.2)); hosts of brood parasites feed chicks that look nothing like their own because their rules are "feed what is in the nest" ([7.18.32](/7.18.32#sec-32.7.3)); insects lay eggs on asphalt that reflects polarized light like water, an **ecological trap** ([7.18.26](/7.18.26#sec-26.1.2)); and migrants keep their timing when their food peak advances, producing **phenological mismatch** (Definition 20.7.1). These cases are not failures of the functional approach but evidence that function and mechanism must be studied together: a rule of thumb is adaptive only relative to the range of conditions in which it was selected.

### 48.6.5. Measuring Fitness

The weakest link in many studies is not the theory but the measurement of fitness consequences. The costs and benefits in **Hamilton's rule** are hard to estimate, because they require knowing how much actor and recipient would have reproduced without the behavior; experiments that remove helpers or manipulate their opportunities are far more informative than correlations between individuals that differ in quality ([7.18.33](/7.18.33#sec-33.8)). The same applies to the costs of signals ([7.18.39](/7.18.39#sec-39.4.4)) and to the strength of the landscape of fear in large mammals ([7.18.23](/7.18.23#sec-23.6.3)). Where predictions can be made without measuring $B$ and $C$, as in the sex-ratio conflicts of social insects, the theory has had its clearest successes.

## 48.7. Where Mechanism Meets Function

The two programs of the course, neuroethology and behavioral ecology, are increasingly one. The study of decisions that combines the economic models of behavioral ecology with recordings of neural activity is sometimes called neuroeconomics; the comparative study of cognition asks the same question of learning and memory. The course contains several places where the two programs informed each other.

- The survival value of a neuron. Roeder's moth ear linked the thresholds of two identified sensory cells to the distances at which bats detect moths and to the moth's two defenses ([7.18.12](/7.18.12#sec-12.5)), one of the first studies to connect identified neurons with the function of a natural behavior.
- Learning rules as mechanisms of optimization. The song system learns by reinforcement, with dopamine signaling performance errors ([7.18.17](/7.18.17#sec-17.3.3)); the matching law produces approximately ideal free behavior in individuals ([7.18.41](/7.18.41#sec-41.4.2)); and the risk-proneness of animals of all species tested for variable delays, whatever their energy budget, follows from the way they discount and time delays (roughly hyperbolic discounting and errors proportional to the interval, the **Weber–Fechner law** for time) rather than from their energetic state ([7.18.23](/7.18.23#sec-23.2.2), [7.18.43](/7.18.43#sec-43.3.2)).
- Perception limits signal evolution. Receivers compare signals by ratios, so each added chuck of the túngara frog brings a smaller increase in attractiveness ([7.18.37](/7.18.37#sec-37.4.3)); the sensitivities of receivers determine which colors and frequencies are worth producing ([7.18.38](/7.18.38#sec-38.3.2)).
- Ecology predicts brains. Food-storing birds have a larger hippocampus that grows with experience and encodes each cache separately ([7.18.23](/7.18.23#sec-23.5)), the strongest support for the **adaptive specialization hypothesis** (Definition 42.4.1); artificial selection on brain size in guppies showed both the cognitive benefit and the energetic cost that comparative hypotheses invoke ([7.18.8](/7.18.8#sec-8.1.4)).
- Optimal thresholds in circuits. The smoke-detector principle predicts the bias of the fast route to the amygdala ([7.18.8](/7.18.8#sec-8.3.2)), and the threshold models of recognition and mimicry predict the decisions of guard bees and predators.
- Collective decisions resemble neural ones. Honey bee swarms choose a nest site by recruitment, cross-inhibiting stop signals and a quorum, the same architecture as competing neural populations in a decision circuit ([7.18.36](/7.18.36#sec-36.9.3), [7.18.27](/7.18.27#sec-27.7.2)).

The common lesson is that a mechanism tells us which optima are reachable, and a functional model tells us which mechanisms are worth looking for.

## 48.8. Open Questions

The course has repeatedly ended its arguments with unresolved questions. The main ones are listed here, each with the chapter where the evidence is reviewed.

1. Mechanisms of the magnetic sense and of the navigational map. The inclination compass of birds probably uses radical pairs in cryptochrome, and map cues include odors and magnetic intensity and inclination, but the receptors and the integration of map information are unresolved ([7.18.21](/7.18.21#sec-21.3.2), [7.18.21](/7.18.21#sec-21.4.3)).
2. Cognitive maps in insects and in the wild. Novel shortcuts can often be explained by path integration and view matching; whether insects have a **cognitive map** remains debated, and the best evidence in vertebrates comes from tracking wild bats and hippocampal recordings ([7.18.21](/7.18.21#sec-21.7), [7.18.42](/7.18.42#sec-42.6.4)).
3. Distributed decisions. How populations of neurons choose between behaviors when no single command neuron exists, from the leech's swim–crawl decision to the toad's prey–predator decision ([7.18.14](/7.18.14#sec-14.4.2), [7.18.13](/7.18.13#sec-13.2.4)).
4. How general is the challenge hypothesis? Testosterone responses to social challenges proved less general than first supposed ([7.18.19](/7.18.19#sec-19.3.2)).
5. Good genes and the peacock. Indirect benefits of mate choice are difficult to measure; the peacock's train remains debated ([7.18.29](/7.18.29#sec-29.3.2), [7.18.39](/7.18.39#sec-39.4.4)).
6. Why is reciprocity rare? The iterated prisoner's dilemma promises much, but the evidence in animals is thin, perhaps because of its cognitive demands ([7.18.34](/7.18.34#sec-34.4.4)).
7. Reproductive skew. Concession, tug-of-war and restraint models make different predictions, and the empirical support is mixed ([7.18.35](/7.18.35#sec-35.5.2)).
8. Is host manipulation adaptive? Many cases may be by-products of pathology ([7.18.22](/7.18.22#sec-22.4.2)).
9. The strength of the landscape of fear in large mammals, where predator effects are hard to separate from other variables ([7.18.23](/7.18.23#sec-23.6.3)).
10. Minds of animals. Whether episodic-like memory involves conscious recollection ([7.18.42](/7.18.42#sec-42.5)), whether apes track false beliefs (**theory of mind**, [7.18.45](/7.18.45#sec-45.4.4)), what the mirror test shows ([7.18.45](/7.18.45#sec-45.1.2)), and what animal reasoning is beyond associative learning ([7.18.44](/7.18.44#sec-44.6)); each requires methods that exclude simpler explanations, under **Morgan's canon**.
11. Cumulative culture. Why it is weak or absent outside humans despite widespread **social learning** and traditions ([7.18.46](/7.18.46#sec-46.6.2)), and what this implies for the evolution of language ([7.18.47](/7.18.47#sec-47.3.3)).
12. The role of play in learning remains uncertain ([7.18.41](/7.18.41#sec-41.6.2)).
13. Behavior in a changing world. Whether migrants and other animals can adjust their timing, routes and habitat choices to climate change, noise, light and habitat loss fast enough ([7.18.20](/7.18.20#sec-20.7), [7.18.38](/7.18.38#sec-38.2.4), [7.18.47](/7.18.47#sec-47.4.2)).

## Summary

- **Tinbergen's four questions** organize the course (Section 48.1): mechanism and development are proximate, function and evolution ultimate; the neuroethology chapters answer the first, the behavioral ecology chapters the third and, through comparison, the fourth. Birdsong, gull mobbing, the bat–moth interaction, migration and honey bee division of labor have been studied at all four levels.
- Mechanism (Section 48.2): choose the right animal (**Krogh's principle**); senses filter the world into an **Umwelt**; recognition and decisions are computed by populations rather than single **command neurons**; escape and rhythm use dedicated designs; neuromodulators and hormones set behavioral states; clocks, corollary discharges and path integration anticipate the world.
- Development (Section 48.3): genes and environment interact in every behavior; **sensitive periods** calibrate imprinting, song, auditory maps, homing odors and star compasses; supergenes and switches produce discrete morphs and **conditional strategies**; learning pays when the environment is predictable within but not between generations.
- Function (Section 48.4): five families of models.

  | Family | Representative results |
  |---|---|
  | Optimality | Prey model, **marginal value theorem**, energy budget rule, dynamic programming, Gilliam's rule, escape distance, parent's optimum |
  | Games | ESS and replicator dynamics, hawk–dove and bourgeois, war of attrition, **ideal free distribution**, producer–scrounger, switch points, care and negotiation |
  | Inclusive fitness | **Hamilton's rule**, kin competition, parent–offspring conflict, sex-ratio conflict and policing, spite, skew |
  | Information | Bayes, the threshold probability, value of information, signal detection; index, costly and cheap signals; the Sir Philip Sidney game |
  | Learning rules | Rescorla–Wagner, Pearce–Hall, the matching law, the song perturbation rule, exploration–exploitation, Rogers' paradox, conformist bias |

- Evolution (Section 48.5): convergence is common (echolocation, electric fishes, vocal learning, tool use); losses are informative; signals arise from preexisting traits and sensory biases; behavior can evolve within decades; eusociality arose under lifetime monogamy.
- Assumptions (Section 48.6): optimization is a tested working hypothesis, not a claim of perfection; the phenotypic gambit usually holds but fails for supergenes, correlated behaviors and sexual conflict; current function differs from past history; mechanisms constrain behavior in novel environments (supernormal stimuli, ecological traps, mismatch); measuring fitness costs and benefits is the weakest link.
- Mechanism and function meet (Section 48.7) in the moth ear, in learning rules that approximate optima, in perceptual limits on signals, in the hippocampus of food-storing birds and in decision architectures shared by brains and swarms.

## Questions

1. Answer Tinbergen's four questions for the honey bee waggle dance, using only material from the course.

   **Answer** Mechanism: the dancer measures distance by the optic flow experienced on the outbound flight ([7.18.13](/7.18.13#sec-13.5.2)) and direction with a sun compass based on polarized light, compensated for the sun's movement by the circadian clock ([7.18.21](/7.18.21#sec-21.6.3), [7.18.18](/7.18.18#sec-18.6.2)); experiments with a robotic bee and harmonic radar show that recruits use the information ([7.18.40](/7.18.40#sec-40.4.6)). Development: foraging and dancing belong to the late phase of age polyethism, set by the juvenile hormone–vitellogenin switch and adjusted by inhibition from foragers ([7.18.5](/7.18.5#sec-5.6.1), [7.18.19](/7.18.19#sec-19.7)). Function: the dance directs nestmates to rich patches, and its value is greatest when food is patchy; its larger scatter for near sources has been interpreted as spreading recruits over a patch ([7.18.40](/7.18.40#sec-40.4.6)); in swarms the same signal is used to choose a nest site by a quorum ([7.18.36](/7.18.36#sec-36.9.3)). Evolution: comparison of stingless bees, dwarf honey bees and cavity-nesting honey bees suggests that the dance arose as a ritualized flight toward the food, which pointed directly at the target on an open comb before it was transposed to the vertical comb in the dark (Example 39.1.1). Each answer constrains the others: the odometer explains systematic errors in distance, and the function explains why the information is worth the cost of dancing.

2. A critic says: "Optimal foraging theory cannot be falsified, because whenever a prediction fails the investigator adds a new cost." How would you reply?

   **Answer** Each optimality model tests a specific hypothesis about a currency and constraints, and that hypothesis is rejected when its predictions fail ([7.18.22](/7.18.22#sec-22.6.3)). A revised model is acceptable only if the added factor is measured independently, as the failed attacks and barnacle-covered mussels of oystercatchers were; a cost invented to fit the data is not a test. What a single study does not test is the general assumption that animals are well designed, but that is a research strategy justified by its success in many independent studies with quantitative predictions, such as the marginal value theorem (Theorem 22.5.2) and the energy budget rule (Definition 23.2.2). Failures are informative about constraints and mechanisms (Section 48.6.4), and the pike cichlid example shows the right conclusion: a test of optimality is always a test of a particular model.

3. In what sense is the matching law of operant conditioning the individual counterpart of the ideal free distribution?

   **Answer** In the ideal free distribution with continuous input, competitors distribute themselves so that the payoff per individual is equal in all patches, which gives input matching: the ratio of the numbers of foragers equals the ratio of the input rates (Definition 25.3.1, Eq. (25.3)). In the matching law (Formula 41.4.1), a single animal distributes its responses or time between concurrent schedules in proportion to the reinforcements obtained from them, which makes the return per response approximately equal across options. Both are equilibria in which no further gain is possible by shifting effort, one across individuals and one within an individual over time. Both also show the same characteristic deviation, undermatching ([7.18.25](/7.18.25#sec-25.3.2)), which suggests that similar limits on discrimination and sampling act in both.

4. Why does the best quantitative support for kin selection come from conflicts in social insect colonies rather than from the explanation of worker altruism?

   **Answer** To explain altruism with Hamilton's rule one must measure $B$ and $C$, the change in the reproduction of recipient and actor caused by the behavior, which is very hard in the field ([7.18.33](/7.18.33#sec-33.8)). Conflict predictions follow from relatedness alone: with a singly mated queen, workers favor a 3 : 1 investment ratio in females and males, the queen 1 : 1 (Formula 36.7.1); where queens mate with more than two males effectively, workers are more related to the queen's sons than to other workers' sons and should police worker-laid eggs ([7.18.36](/7.18.36#sec-36.8.1)). These predictions were made in advance and confirmed across species, split sex ratios and colonies ([7.18.36](/7.18.36#sec-36.11)). The explanation of altruism itself rests on lifetime monogamy as the ancestral state and on ecological benefits that made $B/C > 1$, not on the haplodiploidy hypothesis, which fails (Example 36.4.1).

5. The smoke-detector principle, the protection of Batesian mimics, the acceptance threshold of guard bees and the rejection of cuckoo eggs are said to be the same problem. State the common structure and one prediction it makes in each case.

   **Answer** In each case a receiver must decide between two actions on the basis of a noisy cue that is distributed differently in two states of the world (predator or not, model or mimic, nestmate or intruder, own egg or parasite egg). It should act when the posterior probability of one state exceeds a threshold $p^* = L_2/(L_1 + L_2)$ set by the costs of the two errors (Proposition 37.3.1), and the posterior depends on prior frequencies (Formula 37.3.1). Predictions: fear responses are biased toward false alarms because missing a predator is far costlier (Example 8.3.1); predators attack mimics less when the model is common and very noxious (Formula 24.4.1); guards become stricter when intruders are common or costly ([7.18.33](/7.18.33#sec-33.4.5)); and when parasite eggs are rare or mimicry is good, hosts should accept, because rejection errors destroy their own eggs ([7.18.32](/7.18.32#sec-32.7.2)).

6. (Open question) Give two cases from different parts of the course in which a mechanism explains a departure from what a functional model predicts, and say what each case implies for the study of adaptation.

   **Answer** One case is risk-proneness for variable delays: the energy budget rule predicts risk aversion when the budget is positive, yet animals almost always prefer variable to fixed delays with the same mean. Animals discount delayed rewards roughly hyperbolically, a convex function of the delay, so by **Jensen's inequality** a mixture of short and long delays is worth more than a fixed delay of the same mean, and their estimates of intervals have errors proportional to the interval ([7.18.23](/7.18.23#sec-23.2.2), [7.18.43](/7.18.43#sec-43.3.2)); variable delays are therefore preferred whatever the energetic state. A second is the feeding of cuckoo chicks by hosts that reject odd eggs: chick rejection would pay, but a rule learned from the first brood risks misimprinting on a parasite, so most hosts feed whatever is in the nest ([7.18.32](/7.18.32#sec-32.7.3)). Other answers include supernormal stimuli ([7.18.3](/7.18.3#sec-3.2.2)), ecological traps ([7.18.26](/7.18.26#sec-26.1.2)) and phenological mismatch ([7.18.20](/7.18.20#sec-20.7.1)). The implication is that natural selection shapes decision mechanisms, not decisions; a functional model should be tested in the range of conditions in which the mechanism evolved, and its failures outside that range point to the mechanism.
