Hormones connect the information that animals receive with the behaviors they choose, over time scales that neurons alone cannot span. A neural signal lasts milliseconds; a surge of testosterone can change the readiness of a bird to fight for hours, a seasonal rise can change it for months, and a hormone acting on the developing brain can change it for life. Hormones are also changed by behavior: a fight raises testosterone, the sight of a courting male stimulates a female's ovary, the presence of a predator raises glucocorticoids. The relation is two-way, and behavioral endocrinology studies both directions.

This chapter begins with the methods of the field (19.1) and the distinction between organizational and activational effects (19.2). It then follows the main hormones of vertebrate social life: testosterone and the challenge hypothesis (19.3), the hormones of parental care (19.4) and the hormonal consequences of social status (19.5). Section 19.6 treats the hormonal response to stressors, and 19.7 the hormones that organize the lives of insects. The physiology of the endocrine glands and axes is in [7.14.32](/7.14.32)–[7.14.38](/7.14.38); the sexual differentiation of the brain, the neurons of parental behavior and the neuropeptides of social bonds in [7.16.36](/7.16.36); neurotransmitters and neuromodulators, including the catecholamines of the stress response, in [7.18.7](/7.18.7). The seasonal timing of the reproductive axis by photoperiod was treated in [7.18.18](/7.18.18#sec-18.5), and the hormonal basis of insect metamorphosis and polyphenism in [7.17.29](/7.17.29#sec-29.4).

## 19.1. Hormones as Links Between Environment and Behavior

### 19.1.1. Berthold's Experiment and the Replacement Design

In 1849 Arnold Adolph Berthold castrated young roosters. They did not develop the comb and wattles of adult males, did not crow, did not fight other males and showed no interest in hens. In two castrated birds he transplanted a testis into the abdominal cavity: they developed normally into crowing, fighting, courting cocks. Since the transplanted testis had no nerve connections, Berthold concluded that the testis acts on the body and behavior through the blood. This was the first experiment of endocrinology, and its design is still the basic test of whether a **hormone** activates a behavior: remove the gland, observe the loss of the behavior, replace the hormone (now by an implant of the purified hormone) and observe its restoration.

Castrated red deer stags (*Cervus elaphus*) do not fight or attempt to mate during the autumn rut, and testosterone implants restore both (Lincoln, Guinness and Short 1972). Castrated male green anoles (*Anolis carolinensis*) stop courting females, and testosterone restores courtship (Crews 1974). Such experiments show that a hormone is necessary and sufficient for a behavior, but not how it acts. Hormones change the probability that a stimulus elicits a response rather than causing the behavior directly. They can act on sensory systems (estradiol increases the sensitivity of the auditory system of female fish and birds to the songs of males), on central circuits that integrate information and choose actions (the preoptic area and hypothalamus, [7.16.36](/7.16.36)), and on the effectors (the muscles of the syrinx or the larynx, the comb, the dewlap).

### 19.1.2. Measuring Hormones in Free-Living Animals

The integration of endocrinology with field studies became possible when hormones could be measured in small samples.

- *Immunoassays.* In a radioimmunoassay (RIA, developed by Rosalyn Yalow and Solomon Berson in the late 1950s), the hormone in a sample competes with a radioactively labeled standard for binding to a specific antibody; the less label is bound, the more hormone the sample contained. Enzyme immunoassays (ELISA, [7.15](/7.15)) replace the radioactive label with an enzyme that produces a colored product and need no radioactivity. The concentration is read from a standard curve of known concentrations, usually fitted by a four-parameter logistic function, and is reliable only within the range of the standards. Antibodies can cross-react with related steroids, so mass spectrometry (liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry) is increasingly used for specificity.
- *Sample types.* Plasma from small blood samples gives the circulating concentration at the moment of sampling. Hormones or their metabolites in feces, urine, saliva and in water that has held fish integrate over hours; those in hair and feathers over weeks. Noninvasive samples allow repeated measurements of the same individuals, as in long-term studies of primates and meerkats.
- *Capture effects.* Glucocorticoids rise within minutes of capture. To measure the baseline, blood must be taken within about three minutes of capture (Romero and Reed 2005), and the *stress response* is measured by sampling again at fixed times (for example 15 and 30 minutes) while the animal is held: the standardized capture-and-restraint protocol.

### 19.1.3. Associated and Dissociated Reproductive Patterns

The castration–replacement design presumes that the hormone is present when the behavior occurs. David Crews pointed out that this is not always so. In the green anole the testes grow and testosterone rises in spring as males begin to defend territories and court: an *associated* reproductive pattern, in which gonadal activity and mating coincide. In the red-sided garter snake (*Thamnophis sirtalis parietalis*) of Manitoba, males emerge from winter dens and court and mate intensely for a few weeks in spring, when their testosterone is very low and their testes are small; sperm for these matings were produced in the previous summer, and testosterone peaks in late summer, when the snakes do not mate: a *dissociated* pattern (Crews 1984). Castration just before the mating season did not stop courtship in that season, but castrated males courted progressively less in the following years (Crews 1991), so testosterone in the previous year is needed to prepare the mating behavior of the next spring, partly through aromatization to estradiol in the brain. Mating in the garter snake is triggered by an increase in temperature after hibernation. Dissociated patterns are adaptations to short breeding seasons in extreme environments, and they warn against the assumption that a behavior is controlled by whichever hormone is high at the time.

## 19.2. Organizational and Activational Effects

### 19.2.1. Two Modes of Action

**Definition 19.2.1 (Organizational and Activational Effects)** An **organizational effect** of a hormone is a permanent effect on the structure and function of the nervous system (and other tissues), exerted during a sensitive period of development, that determines how the individual will respond to hormones and stimuli later in life. An **activational effect** is a transient, reversible effect of a hormone on behavior in the adult (or in a later life stage), which lasts only while the hormone is present.

The distinction was formulated in the **organizational–activational hypothesis** of Phoenix, Goy, Gerall and Young (1959) after their experiments on prenatally androgenized guinea pigs ([7.16.36](/7.16.36#def-36.2.1)). In rodents, the masculinizing effects of testosterone on the brain during the perinatal period are mostly mediated by its conversion to estradiol by aromatase within neurons. The two kinds of effects interact: an adult female rat given testosterone shows less male-typical mounting than a male given the same hormone, because her brain was not organized to respond to it.

The distinction is clear in principle but often blurred in practice. Hormones have organizational effects not only before birth but also at puberty, when gonadal steroids remodel the adolescent brain, and the "permanent" effects of early hormones depend on later experience. Activational effects, conversely, can change neural structure: seasonal testosterone enlarges the song nuclei of canaries by recruiting new neurons ([7.18.17](/7.18.17#sec-17.3.5)).

### 19.2.2. Organizational Effects in Natural Variation

Organizational effects are not only laboratory phenomena; they contribute to individual differences in natural populations.

- *Intrauterine position.* Mouse fetuses develop in a row in each uterine horn, and steroids diffuse between neighbors. A female that developed between two male fetuses (a 2M female) is exposed to more testosterone than one between two females (0M): as an adult she has a longer anogenital distance, is more aggressive toward other females, is less attractive to males and has longer estrous cycles (vom Saal and Bronson 1980). Similar effects occur in gerbils, pigs and other litter-bearing mammals.
- *Spotted hyenas.* Female spotted hyenas (*Crocuta crocuta*) are larger than males, dominant to them and have a masculinized external genital anatomy. During late pregnancy the placenta converts androstenedione to testosterone, exposing fetuses of both sexes to high androgen levels (Licht and colleagues 1992). Within a population, the offspring of mothers with higher androgen levels late in pregnancy, who tend to be high-ranking females, are more aggressive and mount more as cubs (Dloniak, French and Holekamp 2006). A mother can thus adjust the behavior of her offspring to her social position, at the cost of difficult births through the elongated clitoris.
- *Birds.* In birds the female is the heterogametic sex (ZW), and the default brain is masculine; estradiol given to female zebra finch nestlings masculinizes the song system ([7.18.17](/7.18.17#sec-17.3.5)) and makes them prefer females as partners when adult (Mansukhani and colleagues 1996). Mothers deposit steroids in their eggs: in canaries, eggs laid later in a clutch receive more testosterone, and chicks from these eggs beg more intensely and grow faster, which partly compensates for their later hatching (Schwabl 1993).

### 19.2.3. Hormones as Timers

Hormones can also time a sequence of behaviors. Male house mice that have never mated commonly kill pups they encounter; after mating, a male becomes paternal toward pups about three weeks later, the gestation length of the mouse, and becomes infanticidal again about seven weeks after mating, when his own young have been weaned (Perrigo, Belvin and vom Saal 1990). The adaptive logic is clear: pups encountered within three weeks of mating cannot be the male's own, those born at three weeks probably are. Perrigo and colleagues showed how the male times the switch by keeping mice under light–dark cycles of 22 or 27 hours: the transition depended on the number of light–dark cycles since mating, not on real time, so the male counts days with his circadian system. The hormonal mechanism involves progesterone: male mice lacking the progesterone receptor never attacked pups and were unusually paternal, although their testosterone and progesterone levels were normal (Schneider and colleagues 2003).

A hormone can also come from another individual. In *Drosophila*, a female that has mated rejects courting males for about a week and lays eggs. The switch is caused by *sex peptide*, a 36-amino-acid peptide in the male's seminal fluid: females mated with males whose sex peptide gene had been silenced remained as receptive as virgins (Chapman and colleagues 2003), and females lacking the sex peptide receptor, a G-protein-coupled receptor expressed in neurons of the reproductive tract, also remated readily (Yapici and colleagues 2008). Sex peptide is a male manipulation of female behavior, an example of sexual conflict at the endocrine level ([7.18.28](/7.18.28)).

## 19.3. Testosterone and Reproductive Behavior

### 19.3.1. Sexual Behavior or Aggression?

Testosterone is secreted by the Leydig cells of the testes under the control of the **hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal axis**: GnRH from the hypothalamus releases LH and FSH from the pituitary, LH stimulates testosterone production, and testosterone and estradiol feed back negatively on GnRH and LH ([7.14.37](/7.14.37#sec-37.2)). In the brain, testosterone acts directly through androgen receptors and, after aromatization, through estrogen receptors. In male Japanese quail, sexual motivation, measured as the time a male spends looking at a female through a window, depends on testosterone, but castrated males given testosterone together with an aromatase inhibitor lose the response: the active signal is estradiol made from testosterone in the preoptic area (Balthazart and colleagues 2003).

In many songbirds, however, high testosterone is not needed for mating. Castrated male white-crowned sparrows on long days still mounted females that solicited copulation (Moore and Kranz 1983), and in populations that raise two broods a season, males copulate for the second brood when their testosterone has fallen to low levels (Wingfield and Moore 1987). In these species testosterone is more important for territorial aggression. It peaks early in the breeding season, when males establish territories, and rises again during periods of social instability.

### 19.3.2. The Challenge Hypothesis

**Definition 19.3.1 (Challenge Hypothesis)** The **challenge hypothesis** (Wingfield, Hegner, Dufty and Ball 1990) proposes that in seasonally breeding vertebrates, testosterone rises above the breeding baseline needed for spermatogenesis and sexual behavior only in response to social challenges, such as territorial intrusions, the establishment of territories and the guarding of fertile mates, and that the pattern of testosterone secretion reflects a trade-off between aggression and other activities, particularly paternal care.

Three levels of testosterone are distinguished: the nonbreeding baseline (A), the breeding baseline (B) and the physiological maximum (C). The hypothesis makes comparative predictions. In monogamous species in which males feed the young, testosterone should be kept near level B and rise to C only briefly during challenges, because sustained high testosterone would interfere with paternal care; their response to a challenge, $(C - B)$, is large. In polygynous species without paternal care, males should keep testosterone near C throughout the breeding season and respond little to additional challenges. A comparison of 20 bird species supported these predictions (Wingfield and colleagues 1990).

![The challenge hypothesis (schematic). Plasma testosterone through the year in a male bird. A: nonbreeding baseline; B: breeding baseline; C: physiological maximum. In a monogamous species with paternal care (A panel), testosterone rises to C only during challenges: territory establishment, the female's fertile period and territorial intrusions, and returns to B while the male feeds young. In a polygynous species without paternal care (B panel), testosterone stays near C throughout the breeding season, and a challenge adds little.](./plots/challenge-hypothesis.py)

The challenge hypothesis prompted hundreds of studies and was extended to fish, lizards, mammals (including humans) and to females. Experimental territorial intrusions in the breeding season raise testosterone in male song sparrows, and male spotted antbirds, tropical birds that defend territories all year with low testosterone, respond to simulated intrusions in the breeding season with a rapid rise of testosterone (Hau and colleagues 2000). Outside the breeding season, antbirds maintain aggression with the steroid precursor dehydroepiandrosterone (DHEA), which can be converted to active steroids in the brain without the costs of high circulating testosterone (Hau and colleagues 2004). The hypothesis has also needed revision. Later meta-analyses found that a rise in testosterone in response to a simulated intrusion is much less general than originally thought: many species, including many in which males show strong territorial aggression, do not raise circulating testosterone after a challenge (Goymann, Moore and Oliveira 2019). The current view is that testosterone is one of several regulators of aggression, that local synthesis in the brain can decouple neural from circulating levels, and that responsiveness to challenges is itself evolutionarily labile.

Females have testosterone too, and the terms "male hormone" and "female hormone" are misleading. In dunnocks (*Prunella modularis*), several females can share one or more males and compete for their help ([7.18.30](/7.18.30)); females in polygynous groups, which fight other females, had higher testosterone than monogamous females (Langmore, Cockrem and Candy 2002), although fighting could raise testosterone rather than the reverse.

### 19.3.3. The Costs of Testosterone

If testosterone promotes success in competition for mates, why do males not keep it high all year? The challenge hypothesis assumes costs, and they have been documented.

- *Paternal care.* Male dark-eyed juncos (*Junco hyemalis*) given testosterone implants sang more, expanded their home ranges, obtained more extra-pair fertilizations and were more attractive to females, but fed their nestlings less, and their offspring fared worse; estimated over a lifetime, implanted males did not have higher fitness than controls (Ketterson and Nolan 1999; Reed and colleagues 2006). Natural variation shows the same trade-off: unmanipulated juncos given an injection of GnRH, which causes a brief rise of testosterone whose size varies among individuals, were more aggressive toward an intruder and fed their nestlings less the more testosterone they produced (McGlothlin and colleagues 2007). In human fathers testosterone falls after the birth of a child, more in fathers who spend more time in child care (Gettler and colleagues 2011).
- *Survival.* In side-blotched lizards (*Uta stansburiana*), orange-throated males, which have the highest testosterone and defend large territories with many females, have higher mortality than the other morphs (Sinervo and colleagues 2000; [7.18.28](/7.18.28)).
- *Immunity and energy.* Testosterone suppresses some immune functions in many vertebrates, and males of many species carry more parasites than females; Folstad and Karter (1992) proposed that this makes testosterone-dependent ornaments honest signals of the male's ability to resist parasites (the immunocompetence handicap hypothesis, [7.18.39](/7.18.39)). Testosterone also increases energy expenditure, by raising activity and metabolic rate.

Not all paternal behavior is suppressed by testosterone. In the monogamous California mouse (*Peromyscus californicus*), whose fathers huddle, groom and retrieve pups, castration reduces paternal care and testosterone restores it; the effect requires aromatization of testosterone to estradiol in the brain (Trainor and Marler 2002). The effects of a hormone depend on where it is converted and which receptors the target cells express, and these evolve.

## 19.4. Hormones of Parental Care

### 19.4.1. The Ring Dove's Reciprocal Cycle

Daniel Lehrman's studies of the ring dove (*Streptopelia risoria*) in the 1950s and 1960s showed how hormones and behavior drive each other through a reproductive cycle. When a male and a female are placed together, the male courts the female with bow-cooing for one or two days. The sight and sound of the courting male stimulate the female's pituitary to release gonadotropins, and her ovary begins to secrete estradiol; a female separated from a male by a glass plate still develops follicles, so the visual stimulus is sufficient. Estradiol (with the male's courtship) leads to nest building by both partners; progesterone, rising at ovulation, leads to egg laying and to incubation, shared by both sexes; and the sight of the partner sitting on the eggs and the stimuli of incubation raise prolactin, which stimulates the crop sac of both parents to produce crop milk, with which they feed the squabs. Rae Silver and Mei-Fang Cheng extended the work: a female's own nest-coos, given in response to the male's courtship, stimulate her ovary more than the male's coos do (Cheng 1992), so the female's behavior is part of the stimulus for her own endocrine change.

![Reciprocal interactions between hormones and behavior in the reproductive cycle of the ring dove (after Lehrman 1965 and Silver 1978). Upward arrows: hormones change behavior; downward arrows: stimuli from the partner and from the nest act through the brain and pituitary on hormone secretion. Male courtship → gonadotropins and estradiol in the female → nest building → progesterone → egg laying and incubation → prolactin → crop milk and feeding of the squabs; prolactin also maintains incubation.](./plots/ring-dove.py)

The ring dove cycle illustrates a general principle: the endocrine system of each partner is regulated by the behavior of the other, so the behavior of a pair is synchronized through reciprocal stimulation. The same holds for pairs of many monogamous birds, in which the courtship and incubation of one partner advance the reproductive state of the other.

### 19.4.2. Prolactin, Oxytocin and Vasopressin

**Prolactin**, the pituitary hormone of lactation in mammals, is associated with parental behavior across vertebrates. It rises in incubating birds of both sexes, maintains incubation and brooding, and stimulates crop milk in pigeons and doves; in some fishes it promotes the fanning of eggs and the secretion of mucus on which the young feed (in discus fish), and in mammalian fathers of biparental species it rises around the birth of the young. Prolactin did not evolve for parental care: it is an ancient hormone of osmoregulation in fishes that was recruited repeatedly for parental functions.

The neuropeptides **oxytocin** and vasopressin act as hormones in the periphery and as neuromodulators in the brain ([7.16.36](/7.16.36#sec-36.5)). In ewes, vaginocervical stimulation during birth releases oxytocin in the brain, which opens a sensitive period of a few hours in which the mother learns the odor of her lamb and accepts it while rejecting other lambs (Kendrick and colleagues 1997); oxytocin infused into the brain of a nonpregnant, estrogen-primed ewe induces maternal behavior toward a strange lamb. In prairie voles, oxytocin receptors in the nucleus accumbens of females and vasopressin V1a receptors in the ventral pallidum of males mediate the formation of pair bonds, and species differences in the distribution of these receptors account for differences in mating systems ([7.16.36](/7.16.36#sec-36.5); [7.18.30](/7.18.30)). The galanin neurons of the medial preoptic area that control parental behavior in mice are described in [7.16.36](/7.16.36#sec-36.4).

## 19.5. Social Status and Hormones

Hormones are not only causes of social behavior but also consequences of social position. The African cichlid fish *Astatotilapia burtoni* of Lake Tanganyika is the best-studied example (Fernald 2012). Males exist in two phenotypes. Territorial males (about a tenth to a third of males in natural populations) are brightly colored, with a black eye bar, defend a pit-shaped territory, court females and spawn. Nonterritorial males are cryptically colored like females, school with females and do not reproduce. The difference extends to the brain: the GnRH1 neurons of the preoptic area, which control the pituitary–gonadal axis, are several times larger in volume in territorial males than in nonterritorial males, and the testes are correspondingly larger (Davis and Fernald 1990).

Status changes frequently in the field, because territories are lost to larger males and gained when owners disappear, and the physiology follows status. When a territorial male is removed, a nonterritorial male that can take over the territory shows the eye bar and begins to display within minutes; within 20–30 minutes the immediate early gene *egr-1* is induced in his GnRH1 neurons (Burmeister, Jarvis and Fernald 2005) and plasma LH rises (Maruska and Fernald 2010); over the following days his GnRH1 neurons enlarge and his testes grow. Conversely, a territorial male that loses his territory loses his colors at once and his GnRH1 neurons shrink over weeks. The perception of a social opportunity thus controls the reproductive axis directly, in the order behavior, gene expression in the brain, hormones, gonads: the reverse of the order assumed in the classical view in which hormones cause behavior.

Status also affects glucocorticoids, but the direction depends on how status is maintained (Goymann and Wingfield 2004). In species where dominants hold their rank without constant fighting and subordinates are harassed, subordinates have higher glucocorticoids; where dominants must repeatedly fight to keep their rank, or must do most of the work of breeding, dominants have higher levels, as in cooperatively breeding African wild dogs and dwarf mongooses (Creel 2001). In meerkats (*Suricata suricatta*), dominant females evict subordinate females late in their own pregnancy; evicted subordinates have elevated glucocorticoids, reduced reproductive hormones and higher rates of abortion, a mechanism of reproductive suppression (Young and colleagues 2006; [7.18.35](/7.18.35)). Social bonds buffer stress: male Barbary macaques with more affiliative partners had lower glucocorticoid metabolites in their feces over periods of social and ecological challenge (Young and colleagues 2014).

## 19.6. Stress

### 19.6.1. The Stress Response

An animal sighting a predator, caught in a storm or attacked by a rival must divert energy and attention to the immediate threat. The responses that do this are evolutionarily ancient and shared by all vertebrates.

**Definition 19.6.1 (Stress Response)** The **stress response** is the coordinated set of physiological and behavioral changes elicited by a stressor, a stimulus that threatens or is perceived to threaten homeostasis. In vertebrates it has two hormonal arms: the *sympathetic–adrenal medullary* response, which releases **norepinephrine** from sympathetic nerves and **epinephrine** from the adrenal medulla within seconds; and the activation of the **hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis**, in which corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH) from the hypothalamus releases adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) from the pituitary, which stimulates the adrenal cortex (interrenal tissue in fishes and amphibians) to secrete **glucocorticoids** (**cortisol** in fishes and most mammals, corticosterone in birds, reptiles, amphibians and rodents) within minutes.

The sympathetic response is the "fight or flight" reaction described by Walter Cannon in 1915: the heart beats faster and harder, blood is redistributed from the gut and skin to the muscles, the airways dilate, glycogen is broken down and glucose and fatty acids are released into the blood. Its neural control, including the "low road" from the thalamus to the **amygdala** that triggers rapid defensive responses before a stimulus is consciously identified, is treated in [7.18.8](/7.18.8) and [7.16.37](/7.16.37). Glucocorticoids act more slowly and longer. Within minutes to hours, they mobilize energy (gluconeogenesis, lipolysis), increase food searching or suppress feeding depending on conditions, modulate immune responses, inhibit the reproductive axis and reproductive behavior, and enhance the consolidation of memories of the threatening event. They also terminate the response: glucocorticoids feed back on the hypothalamus, pituitary and hippocampus to inhibit CRH and ACTH. Glucocorticoids should thus be thought of as hormones that help the animal cope with the stressor and return to normal, not as damaging "stress hormones".

### 19.6.2. Acute and Chronic Stress

The stress response is adaptive when it is brief. When stressors persist (chronic social subordination, prolonged food shortage, repeated predator attacks), prolonged elevation of glucocorticoids suppresses growth, reproduction and immune function, causes muscle wasting and can damage the hippocampus (Sapolsky, Romero and Munck 2000). The *reactive scope* model (Romero, Dickens and Cyr 2009) formalizes this: hormone levels within a normal range track predictable demands, such as the daily and seasonal cycles; levels in the "reactive homeostasis" range allow responses to unpredictable stressors; and only when mediators remain beyond this range, or when repeated activation wears down the capacity to respond, do the costs exceed the benefits ("homeostatic overload").

Predators can impose chronic stress on prey populations. Snowshoe hares (*Lepus americanus*) in the declining phase of their population cycle, when predators are abundant, have high cortisol, poor body condition and reduced reproduction (Boonstra and colleagues 1998), and stressed mothers produce stressed offspring (Sheriff, Krebs and Boonstra 2009), which may prolong the low phase of the cycle. In a field experiment, Zanette and colleagues (2011) protected song sparrow nests from predators with electric fences and nets and broadcast either the calls of predators or of nonpredators throughout the breeding season: females exposed to predator calls fed their nestlings less and produced about 40 % fewer offspring, although no predator ever reached the nests. Fear alone reduced reproduction. The behavioral side of this "landscape of fear" is treated in [7.18.23](/7.18.23).

### 19.6.3. Emergency Life-History Stages

Wingfield and colleagues (1998) proposed that unpredictable perturbations of the environment trigger an *emergency life-history stage*: a temporary state, mediated by glucocorticoids, in which the animal abandons its current life-history stage (breeding, moult, migration) and redirects behavior to survival, either by sheltering in place and reducing activity, or by leaving the area in search of a better place. In spring snowstorms in the Arctic, white-crowned sparrows and Lapland longspurs abandon their territories and gather in flocks in snow-free areas, with high corticosterone, and return when the storm passes; their breeding resumes within days. If a storm comes after the eggs are laid, the cost of abandoning the clutch is high, and the birds of many Arctic species dampen their adrenocortical response during the short period of parental care, the "modulation of the stress response" (Wingfield and Sapolsky 2003). The stress response is thus itself regulated according to life-history trade-offs, in the same way as the testosterone response to challenges.

## 19.7. Hormones and Behavior in Insects

Insect behavior is regulated by hormones as well, chiefly **juvenile hormone** (JH), a sesquiterpenoid secreted by the corpora allata, and the ecdysteroids that control molting ([7.17.29](/7.17.29#sec-29.4)). JH has been recruited for many functions in adults, often regulating the allocation of resources between reproduction and other activities: in wing-polymorphic crickets of the genus *Gryllus*, the long-winged, flight-capable morph shows a daily surge of JH in the evening, the time of its nocturnal flights, which the short-winged morph lacks (Zera and colleagues 2007); in locusts and aphids, JH and biogenic amines participate in density-dependent changes in form and behavior ([7.18.5](/7.18.5)).

The best-studied case is the division of labor of honeybee workers ([7.18.36](/7.18.36), [7.17.38](/7.17.38)). Workers perform tasks in an age sequence (age polyethism): cleaning cells and nursing larvae in the first two to three weeks of adult life, then building comb, guarding and finally foraging outside the hive. The transition to foraging is accompanied by a rise of JH, a fall of the yolk protein vitellogenin, which nurses use to make royal jelly, and large changes in brain gene expression. JH and vitellogenin inhibit each other: JH suppresses vitellogenin synthesis in the fat body, and vitellogenin suppresses JH (Amdam and Omholt 2003). Knocking down vitellogenin by RNA interference caused workers to start foraging earlier (Nelson and colleagues 2007); treatment with JH or its analog methoprene causes precocious foraging, and removal of the corpora allata delays it, although allatectomized bees eventually forage (Sullivan and colleagues 2000), so JH paces the transition rather than being required for it.

The timing is socially regulated. In a colony composed of a single cohort of young bees, some bees begin to forage at the age of 5–7 days instead of 2–3 weeks; conversely, in colonies with an excess of foragers, some bees continue nursing for weeks, and foragers can even revert to nursing if the nurses are removed (Robinson 1992). Contact with foragers delays the maturation of young bees through ethyl oleate, a substance produced by foragers and transmitted by food exchange (Leoncini and colleagues 2004). Huang and Robinson (1992) proposed an activator–inhibitor model of this regulation: each worker's JH rises with age, and an inhibitor transferred from older bees slows the rise, so the proportion of foragers in a colony adjusts to its needs without central control.

The double-repressor system is a bistable switch (compare the toggle switches of gene regulation, [7.9.16](/7.9.16)): small differences in the inputs determine when a worker flips from the nurse state to the forager state, and the flip is rapid and stable. Social inputs, nutrition and genotype all act on it, which is how a colony's allocation of labor tracks its needs.

## Summary

- Hormones link environment, physiology and behavior in both directions: they change the probability of behaviors by acting on sensory systems, central circuits and effectors, and behavior and social stimuli change hormone secretion. Berthold's experiment founded the castration–replacement design. Hormones are measured by immunoassays and mass spectrometry in blood, excreta, hair, feathers and water; baseline glucocorticoids require sampling within minutes of capture.
- Associated reproductive patterns (anole) and dissociated patterns (red-sided garter snake, which mates when testosterone is low) differ in the timing of gonadal hormones and mating.
- Organizational effects (Definition 19.2.1) are permanent effects during sensitive periods; activational effects are transient. Natural examples: intrauterine position in mice, maternal androgens in spotted hyenas, egg testosterone in canaries. Hormones also time behaviors (infanticide and paternal care in male mice, counted in light–dark cycles and dependent on progesterone receptors); the male *Drosophila* sex peptide switches off female receptivity.
- Testosterone, acting directly or after aromatization to estradiol, promotes sexual motivation (quail) and territorial aggression; many birds mate with low testosterone. The challenge hypothesis (Definition 19.3.1) relates testosterone to social challenges and to the trade-off with paternal care (levels A, B, C; monogamous versus polygynous species); later work found that testosterone responses to challenges are less general than first supposed. Costs of testosterone include reduced paternal care (juncos), survival (side-blotched lizards) and immunity; in California mice testosterone promotes paternal care via estradiol.
- In the ring dove, courtship, nest building, incubation and feeding of the young are linked to estradiol, progesterone and **prolactin** by reciprocal stimulation between partners. **Oxytocin** mediates maternal bonding in sheep and pair bonding in voles.
- Social status controls hormones: in *Astatotilapia burtoni* a rise in status induces *egr-1* in GnRH1 neurons within half an hour, followed by LH and gonadal growth. Glucocorticoids are higher in subordinates or in dominants depending on how rank is maintained.
- The stress response (Definition 19.6.1) has a fast sympathetic–adrenal medullary arm and a slower **hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis** arm with negative feedback; acute responses are adaptive, chronic elevation is costly (reactive scope). Predation risk alone reduced the reproduction of song sparrows by 40 %. Emergency life-history stages redirect behavior to survival, and the stress response is modulated by life-history state.
- In honeybees, **juvenile hormone** and vitellogenin form a double-repressor switch that sets the age of the nurse-to-forager transition; inhibition by foragers (ethyl oleate) adjusts it to colony needs.

## Questions

1. Male white-crowned sparrows copulate when their testosterone is low, and castrated males still mount females. Does this show that testosterone has no role in their reproductive behavior? What experiment would you do next?

   **Answer** No. It shows only that high circulating testosterone at the time of mating is not necessary for copulation. Testosterone may (a) be needed earlier, to organize or prime the neural circuits of sexual behavior (as in garter snakes), (b) act at low concentrations or after local conversion to estradiol in the brain, where neural levels need not follow plasma levels, or (c) control other components of reproductive behavior, especially territorial aggression and song. Next experiments: castrate males before the breeding season and implant some with testosterone and others with empty capsules, then measure courtship, copulation, song rate and responses to simulated intrusions separately; add a group given testosterone plus an aromatase inhibitor, or an androgen receptor antagonist, to separate androgenic and estrogenic actions; and measure steroid levels in brain regions (preoptic area, song nuclei) as well as in plasma.

2. Using the challenge hypothesis, predict the testosterone profiles of (a) a male of a polygynous lekking species, (b) a male of a monogamous species that guards his mate only during her fertile period, (c) a female of a sex-role-reversed species in which females compete for males, and explain the predictions.

   **Answer** (a) A lekking male gives no paternal care and competes for matings throughout the display season, so testosterone should remain high (near level C) through that season with little additional response to challenges. (b) The monogamous male should keep testosterone near level B, needed for spermatogenesis and song, rising to near C during territory establishment and his mate's fertile period, when mate guarding and aggression pay, and falling again when he feeds the young, since testosterone reduces paternal care. (c) In sex-role-reversed species (phalaropes, jacanas) females compete for males, so the challenge hypothesis predicts that female androgens should rise in competitive contexts; the evidence is mixed: in phalaropes and other sex-role-reversed birds female testosterone remains lower than that of males, and competition may be mediated by other steroids or by sensitivity of the brain rather than by circulating levels, illustrating why the hypothesis is now viewed as one regulator among several.

3. (Calculation) In the HPA model of Section 19.6.1, suppose glucocorticoid clearance becomes faster ($\tau_g$ halved). Without solving the equations, predict qualitatively the effect on the baseline level of glucocorticoid and on the duration of the response to an acute stressor.

   **Answer** At steady state $g = \tau_g a$, so halving $\tau_g$ at a given ACTH level halves $g$. Lower $g$ weakens the negative feedback, so CRH and ACTH rise and partly compensate: the new baseline glucocorticoid is lower than before but by less than a factor of two, and baseline ACTH is higher. After an acute stressor, $g$ follows ACTH more closely and decays faster, with a time constant of about $\tau_g/2$ instead of $\tau_g$, so the glucocorticoid peak is earlier, smaller and shorter; the termination of the response is faster. The example illustrates that a hormone level depends on both secretion and clearance, and that feedback systems buffer changes in either.

4. (Discussion) Describe from your own experience the difference between the response to acute danger and to chronic stress, and propose a hypothesis for why the stress response evolved in a form that becomes harmful when prolonged.

   **Answer** Acute danger (a near accident, a sudden noise at night) is felt within a second as a pounding heart, fast breathing and heightened attention, the sympathetic–adrenal medullary response, and subsides within minutes; the glucocorticoid response follows and fades over hours. Chronic stress (weeks of overwork, conflict, worry) is felt as disturbed sleep, changes in appetite, fatigue and susceptibility to illness. A hypothesis: the stress response evolved to deal with brief threats, such as predator attacks, fights and storms, that were either survived within hours or ended the animal's life; it reallocates resources from growth, reproduction, digestion and some immune functions to immediate survival, which is cheap if brief. Selection on its long-term costs was weak because sustained, inescapable stressors were rare or because individuals facing them had low reproductive prospects anyway. In addition, some costs are inevitable consequences of the benefits (energy taken from reproduction cannot be used for it). Modern humans and captive or urban animals face prolonged psychosocial stressors, which keep the response active far beyond the time scale it was designed for, a mismatch between an evolved mechanism and a new environment.

5. (Open question) In *Astatotilapia burtoni*, social ascent induces *egr-1* in GnRH1 neurons within minutes. Design experiments to determine which sensory information the fish uses to recognize the opportunity to rise in status and through which neural pathway it reaches the GnRH1 neurons.

   **Answer** First, identify the cue: remove the dominant male of a community tank, or present a subordinate with a vacant territory, under different sensory conditions: in darkness or with an opaque partition (vision blocked), with the lateral line blocked (cobalt or aminoglycosides), with the nares plugged (olfaction blocked), or in an arena where the dominant male is still visible behind a transparent partition but cannot interact. Measure the eye bar, behavior and *egr-1* in GnRH1 neurons 30 minutes later. If ascent requires the visual absence of the dominant male, vision is the key channel. Second, trace the pathway: map *egr-1* or *c-fos* expression in brain regions after ascent (the social behavior network: preoptic area, ventral telencephalon homologous to the lateral septum and amygdala, the ventral tuberal nucleus), use tract tracing from GnRH1 neurons to identify their inputs, and test candidate regions and transmitters by local pharmacological blockade (for example of dopaminergic or noradrenergic inputs, or kisspeptin) during the ascent opportunity. A pathway is supported if its activation precedes the GnRH1 response and its blockade prevents it without preventing other behaviors.
