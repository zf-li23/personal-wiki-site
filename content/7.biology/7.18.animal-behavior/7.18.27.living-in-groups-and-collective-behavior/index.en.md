A starling flock wheeling over a roost at dusk, a shoal of sardines contracting into a ball as a dolphin approaches, a herd of wildebeest crossing a river: groups are among the most conspicuous sights in nature. They raise two kinds of question. The first is evolutionary: why do individuals join groups at all, when group life brings competition for food, the spread of parasites and conspicuousness to predators? The second concerns mechanism: how do hundreds or thousands of individuals, each responding only to its neighbors, move as one, and how does a group decide where to go?

This chapter treats both. Most groups considered here are aggregations of individuals that are not necessarily related and do not necessarily cooperate; each member is assumed to act in its own interest, and the analysis uses the cost–benefit and game-theoretic tools of the previous chapters (**ideal free distribution**, **evolutionarily stable strategy**, [7.18.25](/7.18.25); economic defense of resources, [7.18.26](/7.18.26)). The evolution of cooperation and helping in groups of relatives and nonrelatives is the subject of [7.18.33](/7.18.33)–[7.18.35](/7.18.35), and the societies of social insects of [7.18.36](/7.18.36); alarm signals are treated with communication ([7.18.39](/7.18.39)), and social learning with cognition ([7.18.46](/7.18.46)). The second half of the chapter, on collective motion and group decisions, connects behavior with the mathematics of **self-organization** ([7.5.12](/7.5.12)) and with network science ([1.4.7](/1.4.7)). The consequences of grouping for predator–prey dynamics belong to ecology (7.19).

## 27.1. The Costs and Benefits of Group Living

There are no automatic benefits of living in a group, but there are some automatic costs (Alexander 1974). Groups attract predators by their conspicuousness, intensify competition for food and mates, increase the risk that a female's offspring are sired by another male or that her eggs are parasitized by another female, and transmit parasites and pathogens. Cliff swallows (*Petrochelidon pyrrhonota*) nest in colonies of a few to thousands of nests; the number of blood-sucking swallow bugs (*Oeciacus vicarius*) per nestling increased with colony size, and nestlings in naturally infested nests were much lighter than those of the same age in nests fumigated with insecticide (Brown and Brown 1986). For grouping to evolve, benefits must outweigh such costs for the individuals that join.

|---|---|---|

| Better foraging | Information about food; cooperative hunting; defense of food against competitors | 27.3, [7.18.26](/7.18.26) |
| Physical advantages | Huddling for warmth; aerodynamic or hydrodynamic savings in locomotion | 27.3.3 |
| Reproduction | Access to mates (leks); help in rearing young | [7.18.29](/7.18.29), [7.18.35](/7.18.35) |

## 27.2. Grouping and Predation

### 27.2.1. Dilution and Predator Swamping

George Williams (1966) and William Hamilton (1971) rejected the idea that gregariousness evolves for the good of the population and sought benefits for the individual. The simplest is that an individual in a group shares the risk of being the one taken.

**Definition 27.2.1 (Dilution Effect)** The **dilution effect** is the reduction in an individual's risk of being attacked that results from the presence of other potential victims: if a predator that finds a group of $N$ takes one prey, each member's risk per attack is $1/N$.

Suppose a group of $N$ is attacked at rate $a(N)$ and each attack kills one member. The risk per individual is $a(N)/N$, which falls with $N$ as long as the attack rate increases less than proportionally with group size, that is, as long as a group of $N$ is less than $N$ times as conspicuous as a single animal. This combination of reduced encounter and dilution is called *attack abatement* (Turner and Pitcher 1986). Marine water striders (*Halobates robustus*) sitting on the sea surface are attacked from below by small fish (*Sardinops sagax*); the attack rate on a flotilla did not depend on its size, so the attack rate per individual fell almost exactly as $1/N$, a hundredfold in a group of a hundred (Foster and Treherne 1981). More often larger groups are attacked more, but not enough to cancel dilution: wild horses in the Camargue in southern France attracted more biting tabanid flies in a group of 36 than in a group of three, but each horse in the larger group received about 3 bites rather than 10 (Duncan and Vigne 1979).

Dilution can also be achieved in time, by *predator swamping*: if prey appear synchronously in numbers larger than the predators can eat, each individual's risk falls. Adults of the mayfly *Dolania americana* emerge from the water at dawn within a two-week period, mate, lay eggs and die within about an hour; on days when more females emerged, a smaller proportion of them were eaten before laying (Sweeney and Vannote 1982), and parthenogenetic mayflies, which need not synchronize to mate, are synchronized too. The 13- and 17-year periodical cicadas are an extreme case ([7.17.39](/7.17.39#sec-39.4.5)).

### 27.2.2. The Selfish Herd

Hamilton (1971) asked how a predator that attacks the nearest prey would shape the spacing of prey. Imagine frogs sitting on the edge of a circular pond, with a snake that surfaces at a random point and seizes the nearest frog. Each frog's *domain of danger* is the stretch of bank closer to it than to any other frog, from halfway to one neighbor to halfway to the other. A frog can reduce its domain by jumping into the gap between two close neighbors, and if every frog does so, the frogs aggregate. In two dimensions, the domain of danger of each prey is its cell in the **Voronoi diagram** of the prey positions ([4.6.4](/4.6.4#def-4.2.1)), and its risk is proportional to the area of the cell.

**Definition 27.2.2 (Selfish Herd)** A **selfish herd** is a group that forms because each individual reduces its own risk of predation by moving close to others, so that they lie between it and potential predators (Hamilton 1971).

![Hamilton's selfish herd, simulated. A predator appears at a random point and takes the nearest prey, so each prey's risk is proportional to the area of its Voronoi cell, its domain of danger (shading: larger cells darker). A: 40 prey scattered at random. B: after 25 steps in which each prey moves a short distance toward the center of its three nearest neighbors, the prey have formed clumps; prey in the middle of clumps have small domains, those on the edge large ones. C: mean domain of danger of the prey that end up in the interior and on the edge of the group, averaged over 30 runs: aggregation does not reduce the average risk (the total area is fixed) but shifts risk from the center to the periphery](./plots/selfish-herd.py)

The model makes three predictions: individuals should approach others when threatened, there should be competition for central positions, and peripheral individuals should be attacked more. Jens Krause (1993) added single minnows (*Phoxinus phoxinus*) to shoals of dace (*Leuciscus leuciscus*) that had been habituated to alarm substance from injured skin; when the alarm substance was added, the naive minnows moved into positions surrounded by neighbors on all sides. In bluegill sunfish (*Lepomis macrochirus*) nesting colonies, large males occupy the central nests and small males the periphery, where egg predation is higher (Gross and MacMillan 1981). Redshanks (*Tringa totanus*) attacked by sparrowhawks were, on average, about five body lengths farther from their nearest neighbor than that neighbor was from its own nearest neighbor (Quinn and Cresswell 2006). The most direct test of the domain-of-danger assumption used decoys: Alta De Vos and Justin O'Riain (2010) towed rafts carrying groups of styrofoam seal silhouettes near a Cape fur seal colony in False Bay, South Africa, where great white sharks attack seals from below, and found that a decoy's probability of being struck increased with the area of its domain of danger.

The selfish herd also illustrates a theme of [7.18.25](/7.18.25): an evolutionarily stable outcome need not be good for the group. If solitary prey each obtain fitness $P$, and a "social" mutant that hides behind another gains $B$ at its victim's expense and pays a cost $C$ (the time spent seeking a shield), then the payoff matrix to the row player is

| | against Solitary | against Social |
|---|---|---|
| Solitary | $P$ | $P - B$ |
| Social | $P + B - C$ | $P - C$ |

Social invades solitary whenever $B > C$, and Social is then the ESS, even though a population of social prey ($P - C$) does worse than a population of solitary prey ($P$): a selfish herd is a **prisoner's dilemma**. Position within a group also involves a **trade-off**: hungry fish swim at the front of a shoal, where they find food first but are attacked more, and satiated fish move to the center (Krause 1993).

### 27.2.3. Confusion

**Definition 27.2.3 (Confusion Effect)** The **confusion effect** is the reduced success of a predator attacking a group, caused by the difficulty of singling out and tracking one target among many similar, moving prey.

Squid, cuttlefish, pike and perch attacking small fish in aquaria all made fewer captures per attack on shoals of 6 or 20 than on single prey (Neill and Cullen 1974). A predator can defeat confusion by targeting an odd individual. In shoals of silvery minnows (*Hybognathus nuchalis*) attacked by largemouth bass, prey of a conspicuous color were captured disproportionately (Landeau and Terborgh 1986), which may explain why mixed-species shoals often sort themselves by appearance. With computer-generated prey projected into a tank, Christos Ioannou and colleagues (2012) showed that bluegill sunfish preferentially attacked virtual prey that were isolated or moved independently of their neighbors, so that predation selects for coordinated movement. In a three-dimensional simulation of starling flocks presented to human "predators", targeting errors increased with flock size and density, separating the confusion effect from dilution (Hogan and colleagues 2017).

### 27.2.4. Many Eyes and Vigilance

Many predators depend on surprise. Goshawks (*Accipiter gentilis*) attacking flocks of wood pigeons (*Columba palumbus*) succeeded less often against larger flocks, mainly because larger flocks took flight when the hawk was farther away (Kenward 1978): the more pigeons, the more likely that one is looking up.

**Definition 27.2.4 (Many-Eyes Effect)** The **many-eyes effect** is the increase with group size in the probability that a predator is detected before it can attack, because more individuals are scanning (Pulliam 1973). If each of $N$ group members independently scans a fraction $p$ of the time, the probability that at least one is scanning at a given moment is

$$
1 - (1 - p)^N. \tag{27.1}
$$

Ostriches (*Struthio camelus*) feeding on the plains of Tsavo National Park in Kenya each spent less time with the head up as group size increased, yet the proportion of time at least one bird was scanning increased, closely following Eq. (27.1) for birds raising their heads independently (Bertram 1980). The ostriches also raised their heads at random intervals, so that a stalking lion could not predict when it was safe to move. Grouped animals thus gain twice: each can feed more, and the group detects predators sooner. For detection to benefit those that did not see the predator, the information must spread. It does: minnows that could not see a model pike behind a one-way mirror hid when they saw their shoal mates react (Magurran and Higham 1988), and the escape reaction of water striders spreads across a flotilla faster than the predator moves, as each insect bumps into its neighbors (the "Trafalgar effect", after the signals passed between Nelson's ships; Treherne and Foster 1981).

Vigilance poses a game. In a large group, an individual that scans less gains feeding time while still benefiting from the vigilance of others; if everyone cheats, the group is exposed. The ESS level of vigilance depends on what others do (McNamara and Houston 1992). A simple model shows the logic.

**Example 27.2.1 (An ESS of Vigilance)** An individual scanning a fraction $v_i$ of the time feeds at rate $1 - v_i$. Predators arrive with a hazard $k$; an attack fails if any group member is scanning, and otherwise kills one of the $N$ members at random, so the individual's survival is $\exp[-k(1 - v_i)(1 - v)^{N-1}/N]$ if the others scan a fraction $v$. With fitness $W = (1 - v_i)\exp[-k(1 - v_i)(1 - v)^{N-1}/N]$,

$$
\frac{\partial \ln W}{\partial v_i} = -\frac{1}{1 - v_i} + \frac{k(1 - v)^{N-1}}{N},
$$
which is zero at $v_i = v$ when $(1 - v)^N = N/k$. The ESS is $v^* = 1 - (N/k)^{1/N}$ for $N < k$ and $v^* = 0$ for larger groups. Individual vigilance falls steeply with group size, and so does the probability that the group detects an attack, $1 - (1 - v^*)^N = 1 - N/k$: dilution makes each individual less dependent on detection.

B: the ESS of Example 27.2.1 with predation hazard k = 10: the evolutionarily stable fraction of time an individual spends scanning (red) and the probability that the group detects an attack (blue) both decline with group size. C: optimal and stable group sizes (Section 27.5.1): with individual fitness W(n) peaking at a group of 7, solitary individuals gain by joining until groups reach 22, when a newcomer would do as well alone](./plots/vigilance.py)

:::

Two observations reduce the temptation to cheat. Cheetahs (*Acinonyx jubatus*) stalking Thomson's gazelles chose the least vigilant gazelle in 14 of 16 hunts, and gazelles that were caught had been less vigilant during the stalk than those that escaped (FitzGibbon 1989). And vigilant individuals react first when a companion flees: Steven Lima (1994) rolled a ball silently down a ramp toward a single bird in a flock of sparrows and juncos, out of sight of the others; the target fled first, then birds that had been vigilant when it left, and last those that had not.

In some species, vigilance is taken in turns by sentinels that watch from a high perch while the others forage. Sentinel behavior looks altruistic, but in meerkats (*Suricata suricatta*) and Arabian babblers (*Turdoides squamiceps*) individuals go on guard when satiated (meerkats given extra food went on guard three times as often as controls), sentinels detect predators first and are first to reach cover, and solitary individuals also act as sentinels when satiated (Clutton-Brock and colleagues 1999; Wright and colleagues 2001). A model shows that guarding can be the best selfish option for a well-fed individual (Bednekoff 1997). Sentinels of meerkats and pied babblers (*Turdoides bicolor*) give quiet calls while on guard, a "watchman's song"; playback of these calls made foragers reduce their own vigilance and increase their feeding success (Manser 1999; Hollén, Bell and Radford 2008).

### 27.2.5. Mobbing and Communal Defense

Prey need not be passive.

**Definition 27.2.5 (Mobbing)** **Mobbing** is the approach and harassment of a predator by one or more prey individuals, often with loud calls and sometimes with attacks or by defecating on it, which drives the predator away or deters it from hunting in the area.

Hans Kruuk (1964), working with Tinbergen, showed that black-headed gulls (*Chroicocephalus ridibundus*) mobbing crows and other egg predators near their colony reduced the predators' success: hen eggs placed near the colony were taken less often where mobbing was intense, and nests in dense parts of the colony suffered less predation. Breeding success of common guillemots (*Uria aalge*) was higher in densely packed parts of colonies, where neighbors jointly repel gulls (Birkhead 1977), and artificial nests placed near colonies of fieldfares (*Turdus pilaris*), which mob crows and defecate on them, survived better than those placed near solitary fieldfare nests (Andersson and Wiklund 1978). Humpback whales mob killer whales attacking other species, and Africanized honey bees and vespid wasps attack intruders to their nests en masse.

Mobbing has costs: mobbers can be injured or killed, and mobbing calls can attract more predators. Nests of pied flycatchers (*Ficedula hypoleuca*) at which recorded mobbing calls were played suffered more predation by pine martens than control nests (Krama and Krama 2005). Mobbing also transmits information: naive European blackbirds (*Turdus merula*) that watched conspecifics mobbing a stuffed bird, which the observers saw as a novel object, subsequently mobbed that object themselves, a form of social learning of enemy recognition (Curio, Ernst and Vieth 1978; [7.18.46](/7.18.46)).

## 27.3. Grouping and Foraging

### 27.3.1. Information Centers

Comparative studies of birds show that species feeding on large, unpredictable clumps of food, such as fruiting trees, seed patches or insect swarms, tend to live in groups ([7.18.2](/7.18.2)). For them, the problem is finding a patch; once found, a patch usually holds enough for many. Peter Ward and Amotz Zahavi (1973) proposed that communal roosts and colonies serve as information centers.

**Definition 27.3.1 (Information Center Hypothesis)** The **information center hypothesis** states that communal roosts and breeding colonies allow individuals that have failed to find food to locate good feeding sites by following successful individuals when they depart (Ward and Zahavi 1973).

The hypothesis raises the question of what successful birds gain by being followed. Following may be pure eavesdropping, tolerated because food patches are large, or successful birds may actively recruit others because larger groups at a patch are safer or better able to overcome defenders (the *recruitment center* hypothesis; Richner and Heeb 1996). The best evidence comes from ravens (*Corvus corax*). In Maine, territorial pairs could defend a large carcass against one or two juveniles but not against a gang of six or more; naive juveniles released at a communal roost followed knowledgeable birds to carcasses the next morning, whereas naive birds released away from roosts were rarely seen at carcasses, and knowledgeable birds led the pre-roost soaring displays and the dawn departures (Marzluff, Heinrich and Marzluff 1996). In a roost of up to 1500 ravens in Anglesey, Wales, John Wright and colleagues (2003) baited carcasses with colored plastic beads that the ravens later regurgitated in pellets at the roost; birds that fed at the same carcass slept together, the cluster grew from night to night as naive birds joined, and groups left the roost led by birds roosting at the center of the cluster and flew directly to the carcass. In cliff swallows, birds returning to the colony from good insect swarms were followed by unsuccessful birds (Brown 1986), and food sharing can be adjusted to its cost: house sparrows that found food gave "chirrup" recruitment calls when the food was divisible (crumbs) but not when it was a single piece (Elgar 1986).

### 27.3.2. Cooperative Hunting

Predators hunting in groups can take prey that a single hunter could not. Spotted hyenas (*Crocuta crocuta*) in the Serengeti take Thomson's gazelle fawns alone, but zebras defend themselves by kicking and biting against a single hyena; for zebra hunts hyenas assemble in packs of 10–25, follow the zebra group in a crescent, and converge on one animal that falls behind (Kruuk 1972). Lionesses (*Panthera leo*) in Etosha National Park, Namibia, took consistent positions in hunts, some acting as "wings" that circled the prey and others as "centers" that waited in ambush, and hunts succeeded more often when most lionesses were in their preferred positions (Stander 1992). Chimpanzees in the Taï Forest of Côte d'Ivoire hunt red colobus monkeys in the canopy with differentiated roles (drivers, blockers, ambushers), and hunting success increases with the number of hunters (Boesch 1994); Harris's hawks (*Parabuteo unicinctus*) hunt rabbits in groups with surprise attacks from several directions (Bednarz 1988). Often the benefit is simpler: a simultaneous attack by many predators scatters a shoal of prey fish, and each predator then captures prey more easily, as in black-headed gulls and jacks (*Caranx*) attacking anchovy shoals.

The benefit of group hunting does not by itself explain group size. In Serengeti lions, food intake per lion per wildebeest hunt was greatest in pairs, yet hunting groups were larger (Caraco and Wolf 1975); the main advantage of large prides is probably the defense of territories and cubs against other prides and infanticidal males (Packer, Scheel and Pusey 1990; Mosser and Packer 2009), and group size is analyzed in Section 27.5.

### 27.3.3. Physical Advantages

Groups also save energy. Emperor penguins (*Aptenodytes forsteri*) incubating eggs through the Antarctic winter huddle in dense groups in which individuals continually move between the cold periphery and the warm center; huddling reduces each bird's heat loss substantially (Gilbert and colleagues 2006). Northern bald ibises (*Geronticus eremita*) flying in V formation, tracked with GPS and accelerometers, positioned themselves where the upwash from the wingtip vortex of the bird ahead is strongest and timed their wingbeats to exploit it (Portugal and colleagues 2014), and fish swimming in schools can save energy in the wakes of their neighbors.

## 27.4. The Evolution of Shoaling in Guppies

Most studies of group living measure short-term costs and benefits, such as feeding rates and predation risk. Guppies (*Poecilia reticulata*) in the streams of Trinidad show how these translate into evolution. Populations in downstream sites with many predatory fishes shoal more strongly than populations in headwaters where predators are absent (Seghers 1974). In 1957, Caryl Haskins moved 200 guppies from a predator-rich site in the Caroni drainage to a predator-free headwater of the Oropuche drainage; their descendants later colonized downstream sites with predators. Anne Magurran and colleagues (1992) tested laboratory-reared fish from these populations by giving single guppies a choice between an empty compartment and one with a shoal: the descendants of the introduced fish had reduced their shoaling tendency in the predator-free site and had increased it again where they encountered predators, within about 100 generations. Shoaling has a cost: guppies that shoal more are less aggressive in competition for food (Magurran and Seghers 1991), so the evolution of shoaling reflects a **trade-off** between safety and competitive ability.

## 27.5. Group Size and Skew

### 27.5.1. Optimal and Stable Group Sizes

If the benefits of grouping increase with group size at a decreasing rate and the costs at an increasing rate, individual fitness is greatest at an intermediate, optimal group size. But groups need not have this size. Richard Sibly (1983) pointed out that if individuals are free to join, a solitary animal gains by joining a group of optimal size, because its fitness in a group slightly larger than the optimum still exceeds its fitness alone. Joining continues until the fitness of a group member has fallen to that of a solitary individual.

**Proposition 27.5.1 (Stable Group Size)** Let $W(n)$ be the fitness of a member of a group of $n$, with a maximum at $n_{\mathrm{opt}}$, and suppose that solitary individuals join groups freely. Then groups grow beyond $n_{\mathrm{opt}}$ to a stable size $n_{\mathrm{s}}$, the largest $n$ with $W(n) \geq W(1)$, at which a newcomer does no better by joining than by staying alone.

**Proof** A solitary individual joins a group of $n$ if $W(n + 1) > W(1)$. For $n_{\mathrm{opt}} \leq n < n_{\mathrm{s}}$ this holds, so groups keep growing; once $n = n_{\mathrm{s}}$, $W(n_{\mathrm{s}} + 1) < W(1)$, and newcomers do better alone. $\square$

The argument is the ideal free distribution applied to groups ([Definition 25.3.1](/7.18.25#def-25.3.1)): payoffs are equalized between the "habitats" of group membership and solitude, at a level much lower than the optimum. Groups larger than the predicted optimum, as in the lions above, are therefore not evidence against an optimality analysis. The conclusion changes if group members can exclude newcomers, which they should do once the group has reached its optimum, so that the outcome depends on who controls membership (Giraldeau and Gillis 1985), or if groups consist of relatives, whose losses from an extra member are partly the joiner's own losses in inclusive fitness (Higashi and Yamamura 1993).

### 27.5.2. Individual Differences and Skew

Members of a group do not benefit equally: dominants take the safest positions and the best food, and often most of the reproduction. Subordinates should accept a smaller share only as long as they cannot do better elsewhere, and dominants should concede just enough to keep useful subordinates in the group; *skew theory* analyzes this bargaining ([7.18.35](/7.18.35)). In the coral-dwelling goby *Paragobiodon xanthosomus*, groups form a size hierarchy in which only the largest pair breeds, and each subordinate is about 90–95% of the length of the fish above it. Subordinates whose size ratio exceeded about 0.95 were likely to be evicted, and when offered extra food some subordinates refused it and stayed small, while those that ate and grew were evicted (Wong and colleagues 2007, 2008): subordinates restrain their growth to remain in the group and queue for a breeding position. In banded mongooses (*Mungos mungo*), in contrast, subordinate females did not restrain their breeding, and dominant females evicted pregnant subordinates when the group contained more than about five breeding females (Cant and colleagues 2010).

## 27.6. Collective Motion and Self-Organization

### 27.6.1. Local Rules

How do flocks and schools move in such coordination? Early naturalists imagined a leader or even "thought transference". The modern answer is that coordinated group movements emerge from simple local rules followed by each individual in response to its nearest neighbors (Couzin and Krause 2003), an example of **self-organization**.

**Definition 27.6.1 (Collective Motion)** **Collective motion** is the coordinated movement of a group of animals that arises from interactions among neighboring individuals, without a leader or an external template specifying the pattern of the group.

A simple example is the formation of traffic lanes. In columns of New World army ants (*Eciton burchellii*), which can include 200,000 foragers, outbound ants use both edges of the trail and inbound ants carrying prey use the center. Iain Couzin and Nigel Franks (2003) showed that three individual rules generate the lanes in simulations with parameters measured from real ants: ants follow the pheromone gradient detected by their two antennae, they have a preferred direction (away from or toward the nest), and they turn to avoid oncoming ants, with outbound ants turning more readily than laden inbound ants. The lanes reduce collisions and increase the flow of ants and prey. Pedestrians in a crowded corridor form lanes by similar rules.

### 27.6.2. The Zone Model

Couzin and colleagues (2002) modeled schools and flocks with three behavioral zones around each individual.

**Algorithm 27.6.1 (Zone Model of Collective Motion)** Each of $N$ individuals moves at constant speed with heading $\boldsymbol{u}_i$ (a unit vector) and position $\boldsymbol{c}_i$. At each time step:

1. If any neighbors lie within the *zone of repulsion* (distance $< r_{\mathrm{r}}$), the desired direction is away from them, $\boldsymbol{d}_i = -\sum_j (\boldsymbol{c}_j - \boldsymbol{c}_i)/\lVert \boldsymbol{c}_j - \boldsymbol{c}_i \rVert$, and the other zones are ignored.
2. Otherwise, the individual aligns with the headings of neighbors in the *zone of orientation* ($r_{\mathrm{r}} \leq$ distance $< r_{\mathrm{o}}$), $\boldsymbol{d}_{\mathrm{o}} = \sum_j \boldsymbol{u}_j$, and moves toward neighbors in the *zone of attraction* ($r_{\mathrm{o}} \leq$ distance $< r_{\mathrm{a}}$), $\boldsymbol{d}_{\mathrm{a}} = \sum_j (\boldsymbol{c}_j - \boldsymbol{c}_i)/\lVert \boldsymbol{c}_j - \boldsymbol{c}_i \rVert$; if both zones contain neighbors, $\boldsymbol{d}_i$ is the average of the two unit vectors.
3. Neighbors in a blind sector behind the individual are not perceived. The individual turns toward $\boldsymbol{d}_i$, with a little random error, by at most a maximum turning angle per time step.

The state of the group is described by two order parameters: the polarization $\lVert \frac{1}{N}\sum_i \boldsymbol{u}_i \rVert$, which is 1 when all individuals head the same way, and the normalized angular momentum about the group's centroid, which is 1 for a perfect rotating mill.

:::

Changing only the width of the orientation zone, with repulsion and attraction fixed, produces four collective states: a *swarm* with cohesion but little alignment (as in insect swarms), a *torus* rotating around an empty center (as in milling schools of barracuda and jacks), a *dynamic parallel group* and a *highly parallel group*, moving in one direction (as in migrating schools and flocks). The transitions between states are abrupt, and the model shows **hysteresis**: a group that has become polarized stays polarized when the orientation zone is narrowed again, over a range of values at which a group starting as a swarm would form a torus, a form of collective memory (Couzin and colleagues 2002). The behavior of a whole group can thus change dramatically with a small change in the response of individuals to their neighbors, for example a change in motivation or in light level.

![The zone model of collective motion (Algorithm 27.6.1) in two dimensions. A: the zones of repulsion, orientation and attraction around an individual, with the blind sector behind it. B–D: groups of 40 individuals after 500 time steps, shown with their recent tracks, for three widths of the orientation zone: a swarm (little alignment), a rotating torus and a polarized group; P is the polarization and M the normalized angular momentum. E: polarization and milling, averaged over the end of several runs, against the radius of the orientation zone: the group switches from swarm to mill to polarized motion as a single individual-level parameter changes](./plots/collective-motion.py)

All three states are seen in real fish schools. Golden shiners (*Notemigonus crysoleucas*) tracked for hours in a large tank spent their time in swarm, milling and polarized states, with abrupt switches between them (Tunstrøm and colleagues 2013). Detailed tracking of individuals has also shown where the model is too simple: in mosquitofish and golden shiners, explicit alignment appears weak or absent, and alignment emerges from attraction and repulsion combined with speed changes (Herbert-Read and colleagues 2011; Katz and colleagues 2011). In flocks of European starlings (*Sturnus vulgaris*) reconstructed in three dimensions from stereo photographs over Rome, each bird interacted with a fixed number of about six or seven nearest neighbors regardless of their distance, a *topological* rather than metric rule, which makes flocks robust to changes in density (Ballerini and colleagues 2008), and the correlation in velocity fluctuations extended across the whole flock whatever its size, so that a turn initiated anywhere could spread through all of it (Cavagna and colleagues 2010).

Collective motion can also bring collective abilities. Groups of golden shiners, which prefer shade, followed a moving patch of darkness more effectively the larger the group, although single fish could not detect the light gradient: each fish slowed down in darker regions, and the group as a whole was steered by these local speed changes, a form of emergent sensing (Berdahl and colleagues 2013). In desert locusts ([7.18.5](/7.18.5#sec-5.6.2)), marching juveniles in an arena switched from disordered movement to coordinated marching above a critical density, as models of self-propelled particles predict (Buhl and colleagues 2006); the motion is driven partly by cannibalism, as locusts move away from others approaching from behind and toward those ahead (Bazazi and colleagues 2008). Predators exploit or provoke the dynamics of groups: attacks on shoals produce waves of escape, sudden "flash expansions" and the "fountain effect", in which the shoal splits around the predator and reforms behind it.

## 27.7. Group Decisions

### 27.7.1. Leadership by Informed Individuals

Groups must decide when to move and where to go, and their members may differ in information and interests. Couzin and colleagues (2005) extended the zone model by giving a few individuals a preferred direction, toward a food source or a migration target. Without any signal, and without the others knowing which individuals are informed, a small informed minority could guide the whole group; the larger the group, the smaller the proportion of informed individuals needed for a given accuracy. When informed individuals disagree, the group follows the majority, or, if the two factions are about equal, takes their average direction (when the preferred directions are close) or follows one faction (when they are far apart). Uninformed individuals can even promote decisions by the majority, by diluting the influence of a strongly opinionated minority (Couzin and colleagues 2011). Experiments with people walking in a hall, instructed only to stay with the group or to go to a target without communicating, confirmed that a small informed minority guided the group (Dyer and colleagues 2009).

B: leadership without signals, in a simple model of self-propelled individuals that avoid, align with and are attracted to their neighbors; a proportion of them also prefer a direction g. Accuracy is the mean cosine between the direction of travel of the group and g: in a group of 50, a few percent of informed individuals suffice, whereas a group of 10 needs at least one informed member, a higher proportion](./plots/group-decisions.py)

In real groups, the individuals with the most pressing needs often lead: hungrier or bolder fish lead shoals, and lactating zebras initiate movements to water. GPS collars recording the positions of all 25 members of a troop of olive baboons (*Papio anubis*) in Kenya every second showed that troop members did not simply follow dominant individuals; they followed individuals that moved in a highly directed way, and the probability of following increased with the number of initiators moving in the same direction; when initiators disagreed, followers compromised when the directions differed little and chose one direction when they differed widely, as the models predict (Strandburg-Peshkin and colleagues 2015).

### 27.7.2. Voting and Quorum Responses

Some groups reach consensus by something like voting. Herds of African buffalo (*Syncerus caffer*) in Lake Manyara, Tanzania, rest during the day and move to graze at dusk; before departure, females stand up, gaze in one direction with the head held high and lie down again, and the direction in which the herd eventually moves was predicted by the average direction of these gazes (Prins 1996). Red deer resting in groups stood up and moved off when about 60% of adults had stood, rather than when the dominant female did (Conradt and Roper 2003). Decisions shared in this way are favored when group members have similar interests, because pooling independent estimates reduces errors, as Francis Galton (1907) found when the median of nearly 800 guesses of the weight of an ox at a country fair came within 1% of the truth; the advantage requires that the estimates be independent, and it is lost if individuals copy each other.

A widespread mechanism of collective decision is the quorum response.

**Definition 27.7.1 (Quorum Response)** A **quorum response** is a steep, threshold-like increase in the probability that an individual performs a behavior as a function of the number of other individuals already performing it, so that the group commits to an option once the number of individuals that have chosen it exceeds a threshold, the quorum (Pratt and colleagues 2002; Sumpter and Pratt 2009).

The ant *Temnothorax albipennis* lives in colonies of a few hundred workers in rock crevices and must find a new nest when its crevice breaks. A scout that finds a suitable cavity returns and leads nestmates to it one at a time by tandem running, the follower tapping the leader with its antennae; recruits that accept the site lead others in turn. When the number of ants at a new site reaches a quorum, about 20 in one laboratory study, recruiters switch to carrying nestmates, which is about three times faster (Pratt and colleagues 2002). The quorum combines speed and accuracy: slow recruitment lets the colony inspect several sites before one accumulates enough ants, and good sites reach the quorum first because scouts accept them sooner. When the old nest has been destroyed and speed matters, colonies use a lower quorum and make more mistakes (Franks and colleagues 2003). The same mechanism appears in fish: three-spined sticklebacks deciding which way to swim past a replica predator followed a replica leader much more readily when two or more replicas went the same way, a steep quorum-like response (Ward and colleagues 2008).

Swarms of honey bees (*Apis mellifera*) choose new nest sites by a process studied by Thomas Seeley and colleagues. A swarm of about 10,000 workers and the queen hangs from a branch while a few hundred scouts inspect cavities up to several kilometers away; each scout advertises a site by a **waggle dance** on the swarm surface ([7.17.38](/7.17.38)), dancing longer for better sites, and recruits inspect the site and dance for it in turn. Support for poor sites decays, because scouts dance less and stop dancing; scouts dancing for one site also deliver "stop signals" (head butts with a brief vibration) to scouts dancing for competing sites, a cross-inhibition that breaks deadlocks between equal sites (Seeley and colleagues 2012). The decision is made at the site: when about 15 scouts are present there at once, they return and produce piping signals that prepare the swarm for flight, and the swarm flies to the site guided by the scouts, about 5% of the swarm, which fly rapidly through it in the right direction (Seeley and Visscher 2004). The process resembles the competition between neural populations in the brain during a perceptual decision, a parallel analyzed in [7.18.36](/7.18.36).

## 27.8. Social Network Analysis

Within groups, individuals do not interact equally with all others, and the pattern of interactions affects the flow of information, the transmission of disease and the outcome of social competition.

**Definition 27.8.1 (Social Network Analysis)** **Social network analysis** represents the individuals of a population as the vertices of a graph and their social relationships (associations, grooming, aggression, cooperation) as edges, weighted by the frequency or strength of the relationship, and describes the structure of the population and the position of each individual by graph measures ([1.4.7](/1.4.7)).

Associations are often inferred from co-occurrence in groups (the *gambit of the group*: individuals seen in the same group are assumed to associate) and quantified by association indices, such as the *simple ratio index*, the number of sampling periods in which two individuals were seen together divided by the number in which either was seen. Interactions such as grooming give directed and weighted edges. The position of an individual is measured by its degree (number of partners), strength (sum of edge weights), **eigenvector centrality** (connections to well-connected individuals) and **betweenness centrality** (the share of shortest paths between other pairs that pass through it, which identifies individuals that bridge subgroups); the cohesion of local neighborhoods by the **clustering coefficient**; and the division of a population into subgroups by **community structure** detection. Because animal networks are built from samples, observed measures must be compared with null models, networks obtained by randomly permuting the observed group memberships, to decide whether a structure is more than the result of chance co-occurrence (Bejder, Fletcher and Bräger 1998; Croft, James and Krause 2008).

B: network-based diffusion of an innovation, in which a naive individual's daily probability of learning increases with its association strength to individuals that already know it: the innovation spreads through the whole population faster when it starts with the individual of highest betweenness than when it starts with the individual of lowest degree](./plots/social-network.py)

Social networks have helped to answer questions that could not be asked with group averages. In bottlenose dolphins (*Tursiops* sp.) of Doubtful Sound, New Zealand, the network divided into two communities linked by a few individuals of high betweenness, "brokers" whose presence may hold the population together (Lusseau and Newman 2004). In long-tailed manakins (*Chiroxiphia linearis*), in which pairs of unrelated males perform cooperative courtship displays, a young male's network centrality predicted whether he would later rise to alpha status (McDonald 2007), and males formed partnerships mostly with males that shared partners with them, "friends of friends" (Edelman and McDonald 2014). In wild great tits near Oxford, all birds carried passive transponders read at feeders; Lucy Aplin and colleagues (2015) trained two birds in each of several subpopulations to open a puzzle box by sliding its door either to the left or to the right. The new behavior spread along the network of associations to about 75% of the birds within 20 days, each subpopulation adopted the variant its demonstrators had introduced, and the local traditions persisted over two years, a case of animal culture ([7.18.46](/7.18.46)). In Tasmanian devils (*Sarcophilus harrisii*), contact networks recorded with proximity collars are used to study the transmission of devil facial tumor disease, a transmissible cancer that passes between individuals by bites (Hamede and colleagues 2009).

## 27.9. A Survey of Animal Social Organization

Animal groups range from temporary aggregations at a resource to permanent societies with division of labor. A rough classification, with the chapters where the more complex forms are treated:

| Organization | Features | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Solitary | Individuals meet mainly to mate; territories or home ranges | Many cats, bears, most spiders |
| Aggregations | Individuals gather at a resource or shelter, without coordinated behavior | Flies at carrion, overwintering ladybirds, monarch roosts |
| Mobile groups without individual relationships | Coordinated movement, often anonymous members | Fish schools, starling flocks, locust bands, wildebeest herds |
| Fission–fusion societies | Stable community whose members meet in changing subgroups | Chimpanzees, spider monkeys, spotted hyenas, dolphins, elephants |
| Stable groups with individual relationships and hierarchies | Kin-based or mixed groups, cooperative defense | Lion prides, baboon troops, wolf packs |
| Cooperative breeders | Nonbreeding helpers assist breeders ([7.18.35](/7.18.35)) | Meerkats, many birds, naked mole-rats |
| Eusocial colonies | Reproductive division of labor, overlapping generations ([7.18.36](/7.18.36)) | Ants, termites, social bees and wasps |

Aggregations and mobile groups are explained mainly by the selfish benefits analyzed in this chapter. Stable societies with individual relationships add the possibility of long-term cooperation, reciprocity and kin-based altruism, which are the subjects of the next part of the course.

## Summary

- Group living has automatic costs (competition, parasites such as swallow bugs, conspicuousness) and benefits that must exceed them: protection from predators, better foraging, physical savings and reproductive benefits.
- Protection from predators:

  | Mechanism | Key evidence |
  |---|---|
  | Dilution (Definition 27.2.1), predator swamping | Water striders, Camargue horses, mayflies |
  | Selfish herd (Definition 27.2.2), domains of danger as Voronoi cells | Minnows, bluegills, redshanks, decoy seals; a prisoner's dilemma |
  | Confusion (Definition 27.2.3), oddity | Neill and Cullen's predators; virtual prey |
  | Many eyes (Definition 27.2.4, Eq. (27.1)), vigilance ESS (Example 27.2.1), sentinels | Pigeons and goshawks, ostriches, cheetahs and gazelles, meerkats and babblers |
  | Mobbing (Definition 27.2.5), communal defense | Gulls, guillemots, fieldfares; costs in flycatchers |

- Foraging benefits include information centers (Definition 27.3.1; ravens, cliff swallows) and cooperative hunting (hyenas, lions, chimpanzees, Harris's hawks); huddling and formation flight save energy.
- Guppies evolved weaker and then stronger shoaling as predation changed, within about 100 generations, at the cost of competitiveness.
- Groups grow beyond the optimal size to the stable size at which joiners do as well as solitary individuals (Proposition 27.5.1), unless members control entry or are related; benefits within groups are skewed (gobies, banded mongooses).
- Collective motion (Definition 27.6.1) emerges from local rules: army ant lanes, the zone model (Algorithm 27.6.1) with swarm, torus and polarized states and hysteresis, observed in golden shiners; starlings interact with six or seven topological neighbors; groups can sense collectively.
- Groups decide by leadership of informed or needy individuals (an informed minority suffices in large groups; baboons) and by voting and quorum responses (Definition 27.7.1): buffalo, red deer, *Temnothorax* ants (quorum of about 20, lowered when speed matters), sticklebacks and honey bee swarms (waggle dances, stop signals, quorum of about 15 scouts).
- Social network analysis (Definition 27.8.1) describes relationships with graph measures and null models; it identifies brokers (dolphins), predicts status (manakins), and traces the spread of innovations (great tits) and disease (devils).
- Social organization ranges from solitary life through aggregations, mobile groups and fission–fusion societies to cooperative breeding and eusociality.

## Questions

1. In the Camargue, a group of 3 horses attracted 30 biting flies and a group of 36 attracted 108. Compute the attack rate per horse in each group. By what factor would the attack rate on the large group have to increase, relative to the small one, for grouping to give no dilution advantage?

   **Answer** Per horse: $30/3 = 10$ in the small group and $108/36 = 3$ in the large group. The large group is 12 times larger; dilution would be canceled only if it attracted 12 times as many flies as the small group, 360 instead of the observed 108. The observed increase, by a factor of 3.6, is far below 12, so each horse gains from being in the larger group despite its greater conspicuousness.
2. Explain why, in Hamilton's model, aggregation in a selfish herd does not reduce the average risk of predation in the population, and why it nevertheless evolves. What would change if larger groups were attacked less often?

   **Answer** If a predator appears at a random point and always takes the nearest prey, exactly one prey dies per attack regardless of how prey are arranged; the domains of danger partition the whole area, so their mean area is fixed (total area divided by the number of prey), and aggregation only redistributes risk, from individuals in the center to those on the edge. Each individual that moves closer to others reduces its own domain at the expense of its neighbors, so the behavior spreads even though it brings no average benefit, like a prisoner's dilemma. If aggregated prey were attacked less often in total (because predators encounter clumped prey less often, or are confused or detected sooner), aggregation would also reduce the average risk, and grouping would be favored for the population as well as for the individual.
3. In Example 27.2.1, compute the ESS vigilance for groups of 1, 2 and 5 when $k = 10$, and the probability that the group detects an attack. Why does the group's probability of detection decline even though more individuals are present?

   **Answer** $v^* = 1 - (N/k)^{1/N}$: for $N = 1$, $1 - 0.1 = 0.9$; for $N = 2$, $1 - 0.2^{1/2} = 0.55$; for $N = 5$, $1 - 0.5^{1/5} = 0.13$. The group detection probability is $1 - N/k$: 0.9, 0.8 and 0.5. Detection declines because each individual's marginal benefit from scanning falls with group size for two reasons: an undetected attack kills only one of $N$ members (dilution), and the individual's scanning matters only when all others fail to scan, which becomes rarer as $N$ grows; each member therefore invests less in vigilance, and at the ESS the decrease in individual vigilance more than offsets the increase in the number of watchers. The model ignores costs of vigilance beyond lost feeding and assumes that detection by one member warns all.
4. Using Proposition 27.5.1, explain why observed groups may be larger than the size that maximizes individual fitness. How would you test whether groups in a population are of stable rather than optimal size?

   **Answer** If joining is free, solitary individuals join any group in which they would do better than alone; since fitness at the optimum is much higher than solitary fitness, groups keep growing past the optimum until a member's fitness falls to the solitary level. Tests: (1) measure the fitness (or a proxy, such as intake rate or survival) of individuals in groups of different sizes and of solitary individuals; stable-size theory predicts that the commonest group sizes give members about the same fitness as solitary individuals, not the maximum. (2) Look for resistance to joiners: if members exclude newcomers, groups should be closer to the optimum. (3) Manipulate the cost of joining (for example, the distance between groups) or the relatedness of members, and test whether group size shifts in the predicted direction.
5. A colony of *Temnothorax* ants uses a quorum of 20 workers at a new site before switching from tandem running to carrying. Explain the advantage of a quorum rule compared with (a) moving to the first site found and (b) waiting until every scout agrees, and predict how the quorum should change when the old nest has been destroyed.

   **Answer** (a) Moving to the first site found is fast but often chooses a poor site, because the first site found is not necessarily the best. (b) Waiting for unanimity would be accurate but slow, since scouts that have found different sites would rarely all agree, and the colony would remain exposed. A quorum lets the colony accumulate independent assessments: good sites are accepted sooner by more scouts and so reach the quorum first, while slow tandem running gives time to compare sites, and the switch to rapid carrying then commits the colony quickly. The quorum thus sets the balance between speed and accuracy. When the old nest is destroyed and the colony is exposed, the cost of delay increases, so the quorum should be lower: colonies then decide faster but choose poor sites more often, as Franks and colleagues found.
6. Explain how betweenness centrality differs from degree, and why an individual of high betweenness may matter more for the spread of an innovation or a disease through a population than one with many partners.

   **Answer** Degree counts an individual's direct partners; betweenness measures the share of shortest paths between all other pairs of individuals that pass through it. An individual of high degree within a dense community adds little to spread across the population, since its partners are also connected to each other; an individual linking otherwise separate communities, even with few partners, is the route by which something passing along edges reaches the other communities. An innovation or pathogen starting with, or passing through, such a broker spreads to the whole population sooner (as in the figure for Section 27.8), and removing or vaccinating brokers is an efficient way to slow disease transmission; in dolphins, a few brokers link the two communities of the population.
7. (Open question) Design an experiment to test whether the confusion effect, rather than dilution, explains the lower capture success of predators attacking large groups.

   **Answer** Dilution reduces the risk per individual even if the predator's success per attack is unchanged; confusion reduces the success per attack. A test must therefore measure the success of individual attacks, not the risk to individuals. Present a predator (for example, a fish such as a perch or a bluegill) with groups of prey of different sizes and record, for each attack, whether it ends in a capture, and the predator's behavior during the approach (switching between targets, hesitations). Confusion predicts that the capture probability per attack falls with group size and with the density and similarity of prey, and that it increases if one prey is made distinctive (oddity), because the predator can then track it. Virtual prey projected on a screen allow group size, density and coordination to be varied independently of real prey behavior (as in Ioannou and colleagues' experiments) and remove the possibility that larger real groups detect the predator sooner (the many-eyes effect); human or computer "predators" tracking targets in simulated flocks provide a further test.
