A female Belding's ground squirrel that sees a coyote stands up and whistles, and every squirrel within earshot runs for a burrow; the caller is the one most likely to be caught. A tiger salamander larva that has developed into a broad-headed cannibal eats other larvae, but it eats its own siblings less often than strangers. A soldier larva of a parasitoid wasp, which will never reproduce, spends its short life killing other larvae in the same caterpillar, most of them its brothers. These three behaviors, helping at a cost, refraining from harm, and harming at a cost, are explained by the same theory: an individual's behavior affects the reproduction of others who may carry copies of its genes, and selection on genes takes those effects into account.

The theory itself, **kin selection**, **inclusive fitness** and **Hamilton's rule**, was developed in evolutionary biology and is derived from the **Price equation** in [7.10.16](/7.10.16#sec-16.3), together with the pedigree measures of **identity by descent** of [7.4.24](/7.4.24). This chapter does not repeat the derivation. It treats the theory as a tool of behavioral research: how relatedness, benefits and costs are measured in wild animals, how animals direct help to relatives, why relatedness alone does not decide the outcome, and what the rare cases of spite tell us. The next chapter turns to cooperation among nonrelatives ([7.18.34](/7.18.34)); helpers in families of birds and mammals ([7.18.35](/7.18.35)) and the colonies of social insects ([7.18.36](/7.18.36)) are the main fields in which these ideas are tested. Conflicts among relatives over parental care are the subject of [7.18.32](/7.18.32).

## 33.1. Social Behaviors and Their Fitness Consequences

A behavior is *social* when it has fitness consequences both for the individual that performs it, the actor, and for another individual, the recipient. Hamilton (1964) classified social behaviors by the signs of these two effects, measured over the lifetime of each party:

| Effect on actor | Recipient gains | Recipient loses |
|---|---|---|
| Actor gains | mutual benefit (+/+) | selfishness (+/−) |
| Actor loses | **altruism** (−/+) | **spite** (−/−) |

The definitions are those of [Definition 16.1.1](/7.10.16#def-16.1.1) in 7.10.16. They describe consequences, not intentions, and they refer to lifetime fitness. A bird that defends a territory pays a short-term cost and harms its neighbors, but the behavior is selfish, not spiteful, because the territory pays off later. A vampire bat that regurgitates blood to a roost-mate today and is fed back next week is not altruistic over its lifetime either: such reciprocal exchanges are mutually beneficial ([7.18.34](/7.18.34)).

Mutually beneficial and selfish behaviors can be understood by asking how they increase the actor's own reproduction. Altruism and spite cannot: they lower the actor's reproduction and can spread only through their effects on others. Hamilton showed that a gene can increase its transmission either through the reproduction of the individual that carries it, its **direct fitness**, or through the reproduction of other individuals that carry copies of it, its **indirect fitness**. Parental care is the familiar case of the second route: a parent that feeds its young lowers its own survival and raises theirs, and the young carry its genes. There is nothing special about offspring. Siblings, nephews and cousins also carry copies of an individual's genes by descent, with lower probabilities, and helping them can spread a gene in the same way.

## 33.2. Relatedness and Hamilton's Rule in the Field

### 33.2.1. Measuring Relatedness

In an outbred population, the **relatedness** $r$ between two individuals can be read from a **pedigree**: at each generation link there is a meiosis, and a given gene is passed on with probability $\tfrac12$, so for a path of $L$ links connecting the two individuals through a common ancestor the probability is $(\tfrac12)^L$, and

$$
r = \sum_{\text{paths}} \left(\tfrac12\right)^{L}. \tag{33.1}
$$

Full siblings are connected by two paths of two links each (through the mother and through the father), $r = 2 \cdot (\tfrac12)^2 = \tfrac12$; half siblings by one, $r = \tfrac14$; first cousins by two paths of four links, $r = \tfrac18$. In haplodiploid species the links through haploid males do not involve meiosis, which gives the asymmetric values discussed in [7.18.36](/7.18.36).

Relatedness in Hamilton's rule is more general than this genealogical value. It is a regression coefficient of the genotype of the recipient on that of the actor ([Theorem 16.3.1](/7.10.16#thm-16.3.1) in 7.10.16): a measure of how much more similar two individuals are genetically than two individuals drawn at random from the population (Grafen 1985). Defined in this way, $r$ can be positive, zero or negative, and its average over the population is zero. Negative relatedness, harming individuals that share fewer of one's genes than average, is the key to spite (Section 33.7).

Pedigrees of wild animals are rarely known, because paternity cannot be assigned by observation and the history of the population is unknown. Since the 1990s relatedness has been estimated directly from genotypes at many polymorphic loci, first **microsatellite** markers and now genome-wide single-nucleotide polymorphisms. The estimator of Queller and Goodnight (1989) compares the allele frequencies shared by a pair of individuals with those of the population, which is exactly the regression definition. Molecular estimates have changed the picture of many societies. In meerkats (*Suricata suricatta*), natal subordinates are typically half siblings of the pups they help raise, but immigrant subordinate males can also be related to them, through the dominant male or because they father some pups of subordinate females (Griffin and colleagues 2003). In the paper wasp *Polistes dominula*, about a third of the helpers on a nest are unrelated to the queen whose brood they rear (Queller and colleagues 2000). In the social amoeba *Dictyostelium discoideum*, the fruiting bodies that form in the wild have a mean relatedness of about 0.98, so that the cells that die to form the stalk are almost always helping clone-mates (Gilbert and colleagues 2007).

### 33.2.2. Benefits and Costs

Hamilton's rule, $rB > C$, has three terms, and biologists have often been tempted to measure the easiest one, relatedness, and neglect the other two. Yet $B$ and $C$, which depend on ecology, are equally important. When benefit and cost are measured in offspring, the rule can be written in a form that makes this clear.

**Formula 33.2.1 (Offspring-Equivalent Form of Hamilton's Rule)** An individual that forgoes $C$ offspring of its own in order to help a relative produce $B$ extra offspring is favored if

$$
\frac{B}{C} > \frac{r_{\text{actor, own offspring}}}{r_{\text{actor, recipient's offspring}}}.
$$

:::

An individual choosing between breeding and helping its mother rear further full siblings is equally related to its offspring and to its siblings ($\tfrac12$), so helping pays if each own offspring forgone is replaced by more than one extra sibling, $B/C > 1$. Helping a sister rear her young (nieces and nephews, $r = \tfrac14$) requires $B/C > 2$, and helping a cousin's brood $B/C > 8$. The ratio $B/C$ is large when the helper has little chance of breeding itself, so that $C$ is small, or when its help is very effective; this is why habitat saturation and harsh environments, rather than unusually high relatedness, are the usual explanations of helping in birds and mammals ([7.18.35](/7.18.35)).

![Hamilton's rule in practice. A: helping is favored above the curve B/C = 1/r. The points are field estimates discussed in this chapter and in 7.18.35: coalitions of wild turkeys (r = 0.42, B = 6.1 offspring, C = 0.9 offspring; the dashed lines mark the minimum relatedness r = 0.15 for these values) and primary helpers of pied kingfishers (r = 0.32, B = 1.8 siblings, C = 0.43 offspring). B: the offspring-equivalent form of the rule (Formula 33.2.1): the number of extra offspring a recipient must raise for each own offspring forgone, for some options open to an outbred diploid, and for a haplodiploid female rearing full sisters instead of daughters (which ignores her brothers; see 7.18.36)](./plots/hamiltons-rule.py)

The first complete field test of the rule estimated all three terms in wild turkeys (*Meleagris gallopavo*), whose males court females either alone or in coalitions in which one male does all the mating ([Example 16.3.2](/7.10.16#ex-16.3.2) in 7.10.16): with $r = 0.42$, $B = 6.1$ and $C = 0.9$ offspring, $rB - C = 1.7 > 0$, and the rule would be satisfied for any $r$ above $C/B = 0.15$, even for half brothers. The calculation also shows the limits of such tests. The data are observational: it is assumed that a subordinate deprived of his partner would do as well as an average solitary male, and that the dominant without his partner would do as well as a solitary male. If subordinates are males of low quality that could not breed alone anyway, the true cost is lower still; removal experiments would be needed to measure it. The alternatives that rely on direct benefits could be excluded in turkeys: subordinates fathered no offspring (unlike subordinate ruffs, *Calidris pugnax*, which share matings on the lek), and a subordinate does not inherit the display role when his dominant partner dies, as subordinate manakins do ([7.10.16](/7.10.16#sec-16.2.1)), since coalitions form before adulthood and change only by death.

## 33.3. Alarm Calls and Nepotism

Alarm calls are a classic case of a cheap but real altruistic act. Paul Sherman studied Belding's ground squirrels (*Urocitellus beldingi*) in the subalpine meadows of California for many years. Females mate in spring soon after they emerge from hibernation, and each rears a single litter in a nest burrow on a small territory; juvenile males disperse, while juvenile females settle near their mothers, so that females live their whole lives among female kin and males among nonrelatives. When a terrestrial predator such as a coyote, badger or weasel approached, the first squirrel to see it often gave a staccato alarm call, and callers were stalked and killed at a higher rate than noncallers (Sherman 1977). Three observations pointed to kin selection:

- Adult females called much more often than adult males.
- Females with close relatives living nearby called more often than females without them, and this held for females whose relatives were not their own offspring: young females that had not yet bred called to warn their mothers and sisters.
- Close female relatives seldom fought over burrows and jointly chased away intruders. About 8% of the young born in the study population were killed by other ground squirrels, either young males in search of a meal or immigrant females trying to take over a burrow, and the killers were not close relatives of the victims (Sherman 1981).

In the round-tailed ground squirrel (*Xerospermophilus tereticaudus*), young males called before they left the area where they were born, and stayed silent after they had dispersed away from their relatives (Dunford 1977). The comparison isolates the effect of kin within the same individuals.

Since warning one's offspring is a form of parental care, the more interesting question is whether calling can be maintained by benefits to non-descendant kin. John Hoogland (1983) answered it in black-tailed prairie dogs (*Cynomys ludovicianus*), which live in family groups called coteries, typically one adult male with three or four adult females and their young; females stay in the natal coterie all their lives, so that the females and yearling males of a coterie are usually close relatives. Instead of waiting for natural attacks, Hoogland pulled a stuffed badger (*Taxidea taxus*) across the colony, which allowed him to standardize the distance of the "predator" in more than 700 trials. Individuals with no close kin in their coterie called least often; those with offspring and those with only non-descendant kin called equally often, and more often than the first group. Immigrants without kin did call sometimes, so other benefits must also be involved: a call may tell the predator that it has been seen and that a surprise attack will fail, a function of alarm calls analyzed with communication in [7.18.40](/7.18.40), and a predator that is unsuccessful in an area may be less likely to return.

These studies are consistent with kin selection but do not test Hamilton's rule quantitatively, because the costs and benefits of a single call cannot be counted in offspring. A rarer form of nepotism shows the rule at work in a species that is otherwise solitary. North American red squirrels (*Tamiasciurus hudsonicus*) defend individual territories year round and do not live in groups, yet in 19 years of study at Kluane, Yukon, females adopted orphaned pups on five occasions, always pups of relatives; with relatedness, litter size and the effect of an extra pup on the survival of the female's own litter measured, the estimated $rB$ exceeded $C$ in every case of adoption (Gorrell and colleagues 2010).

## 33.4. How Animals Recognize Kin

### 33.4.1. Recognition Systems

Kin selection requires that help go preferentially to relatives. One way to achieve this is to treat relatives differently from nonrelatives.

**Definition 33.4.1 (Kin Recognition and Kin Discrimination)** **Kin recognition** is the internal process by which an individual assesses the genetic relatedness of others from cues associated with kinship; **kin discrimination** is the observable differential treatment of conspecifics according to their relatedness.

:::

Animals may discriminate without any process deserving the name recognition (by location, for instance), and recognition may exist without being expressed in behavior; the distinction matters when we interpret experiments. Any recognition system has three components (Reeve 1989; Bradbury and Vehrencamp 2011): a *production* component, the cue or label carried by the individual to be recognized; a *perception* component, in which the recognizer compares the cue with a template, innate or learned, and assesses their similarity; and an *action* component, the decision to accept (help, tolerate, mate) or to reject. Mechanisms differ in which cue and template they use.

### 33.4.2. Location and Familiarity

The simplest rule is "treat anyone in my home as kin". It works wherever the only individuals likely to be found at a place are relatives. Many parent birds ignore their own chick placed just outside the rim of the nest, yet feed a strange chick placed inside it. Female Belding's ground squirrels accept any pup placed in their burrow before the pups first emerge, at about 25 days of age, but treat pups introduced after emergence as strangers (Holmes and Sherman 1982). Location is easily exploited: a reed warbler (*Acrocephalus scirpaceus*) may mob an adult common cuckoo (*Cuculus canorus*) at its nest and a minute later resume feeding the cuckoo chick inside it, a consequence of **brood parasitism** treated in [7.18.32](/7.18.32).

A second rule is to learn the characteristics of individuals encountered in a reliable context during development, typically nestmates or littermates, and later to treat them as kin. Recognition by *familiarity* can discriminate any classes the animal has learned, but it cannot identify relatives it has never met. Holmes and Sherman (1982) separated familiarity from kinship in Belding's ground squirrels by cross-fostering pups shortly after birth, creating four kinds of rearing groups: siblings reared together, siblings reared apart, nonsiblings reared together and nonsiblings reared apart. When the yearlings were paired in an arena, animals reared together rarely fought, whether or not they were related, and nonsiblings reared together were no more aggressive than true siblings reared together. Rearing association is therefore the main cue. In long-tailed tits (*Aegithalos caudatus*), whose failed breeders help at the nests of relatives ([7.18.35](/7.18.35)), the cue is the contact call, the "churr": in a cross-fostering experiment, foster siblings developed calls as similar as those of true siblings reared together, and helpers later chose whom to help according to whom they had been reared with, not according to genetic relatedness (Sharp and colleagues 2005).

### 33.4.3. Phenotype Matching

**Definition 33.4.2 (Phenotype Matching)** **Phenotype matching** is kin recognition in which an individual compares a genetically influenced phenotypic cue of another individual (an odor, a call, an appearance) with a template learned from itself or from familiar relatives, and treats it according to the degree of similarity. It allows the recognition of relatives that have never been met, and with a self-referent template the discrimination of degrees of relatedness.

:::

The same ground squirrel study detected this second mechanism. Among animals reared apart, genetic sisters were less aggressive to each other than unrelated females, although they had never met after birth. Field data added a finer distinction. Female Belding's ground squirrels mate with up to eight males (3.3 on average) on the single afternoon on which they are receptive, and genetic analysis showed that 78% of litters had more than one father (Hanken and Sherman 1981). Full sisters within a litter fought less and helped each other more than half sisters, although both had shared the same uterus and burrow, so that some cue correlated with paternity must be involved. Mateo (2002) identified it: squirrels investigated cubes rubbed on the oral and dorsal glands of other individuals for less time the more closely they were related to the donor, as if the odors of close relatives were more familiar. The effect occurred only in females, the sex that behaves nepotistically in the field. In golden hamsters (*Mesocricetus auratus*) reared from birth only with unrelated foster siblings, females still discriminated the odors of unfamiliar relatives from those of nonrelatives, evidence that the template can be the animal's own odor (Mateo and Johnston 2000).

Phenotype matching depends on cues that vary genetically among individuals. In vertebrates, odors associated with the highly polymorphic **major histocompatibility complex** and the major urinary proteins of mice are good candidates, detected in part through the **vomeronasal organ**. The social amoebae provide a cleaner case. When cells of two unrelated lineages of *Dictyostelium purpureum*, one labeled with a fluorescent dye, were mixed on agar, the cells preferentially formed slugs and fruiting bodies with their own clone-mates: the average relatedness in the fruiting bodies was about 0.8, instead of the 0.5 expected if cells aggregated at random (Mehdiabadi and colleagues 2006). In *D. discoideum* the cue has been traced to a pair of highly polymorphic genes, *tgrB1* and *tgrC1*, which encode cell-adhesion proteins that bind each other; cells whose alleles at these loci differ segregate during aggregation (Benabentos and colleagues 2009).

### 33.4.4. Recognition Alleles: Green Beards

A gene could also recognize copies of itself directly, if it produced a recognizable label, detected the label in others and caused its bearer to help them: Hamilton's (1964) idea, named the **green-beard effect** by Dawkins (1976) ([7.10.16](/7.10.16#sec-16.3.1)). The three functions need not be encoded by a single gene; they must only be tightly linked. Such systems face two difficulties: a mutant that displays the beard without helping (a "falsebeard") gains the benefits without paying the costs, and the rest of the genome, which does not share the green-beard allele, may evolve to suppress it.

Green beards are nevertheless known. In the fire ant *Solenopsis invicta*, colonies of the polygyne social form accept several queens. Workers carrying the *b* allele at the locus then called *Gp-9* kill, by dismembering them, newly mated queens that lack it (genotype *BB*) and accept those that carry it, apparently recognizing them by odor (Keller and Ross 1998). It later emerged that *Gp-9* lies within a supergene of more than 600 genes on a "social chromosome" that does not recombine, which explains how label, recognition and behavior stay together (Wang and colleagues 2013). Most other examples are cell-adhesion genes in microorganisms, such as *csA* in *D. discoideum* ([7.10.16](/7.10.16#sec-16.3.1)) and *FLO1* in baker's yeast, whose product makes cells stick to each other in flocs that protect the inner cells from stress, while nonexpressing cells are excluded (Smukalla and colleagues 2008). In such genes the adhesion molecule is both the beard and the means of preferential help, which leaves no room for a falsebeard. A green-beard-like system also occurs in side-blotched lizards (*Uta stansburiana*), whose genetically similar but unrelated blue-throated males settle next to each other and cooperate in territory defense against orange-throated males (Sinervo and colleagues 2006).

### 33.4.5. Where to Set the Threshold

The cues of kin and nonkin overlap, and every recognition system makes errors. Reeve (1989) treated nestmate recognition as a problem of **signal detection theory** ([7.16.47](/7.16.47)): the recognizer perceives the dissimilarity between an individual's cue and its template and accepts individuals below an acceptance threshold. A permissive threshold accepts almost all relatives but also many nonrelatives; a strict threshold rejects nonrelatives but also some relatives. Natural selection should set the threshold where the expected payoff is maximal, which depends on the relative frequencies of the two kinds of individuals and on the costs of the two kinds of error.

![Kin recognition as signal detection: the acceptance threshold model (Reeve 1989). A: the perceived dissimilarity between an individual's cue and the recognizer's template has overlapping distributions for kin (green) and non-kin (red); with threshold t, kin above t are wrongly rejected and non-kin below t wrongly accepted (shaded areas). B: the threshold that maximizes the recognizer's expected payoff, when accepting kin gains 1, accepting non-kin costs c and rejecting gives nothing, against the proportion of non-kin among the individuals met (normal distributions with means 1 and 3, s.d. 0.8): the optimal threshold becomes stricter as non-kin become more common and as their acceptance becomes more costly](./plots/acceptance-threshold.py)

The model predicts that recognizers become less permissive when intruders are common or dangerous, even at the price of rejecting more of their own kin. Guards at the entrance of honey bee (*Apis mellifera*) colonies, which inspect incoming bees by their cuticular odors, behaved as predicted: when robbing by bees of other colonies was intense, guards rejected more nonnestmates and also more nestmates than when robbing was rare (Downs and Ratnieks 2000). The same logic explains why discrimination is not universal. Where almost all the individuals a helper meets are relatives, as in many groups formed by **philopatry**, indiscriminate help is almost as good and costs nothing in errors. Across cooperatively breeding birds and mammals, kin discrimination is strongest where the benefit of helping is greatest and where groups contain a mixture of relatives and nonrelatives (Griffin and West 2003; Cornwallis and colleagues 2009).

Kin recognition serves other functions besides nepotism. Avoiding mating with close relatives, which would produce **inbreeding depression**, requires the recognition of kin as well, and the same cues may be used for both purposes; Japanese quail (*Coturnix japonica*) reared with their siblings, for instance, prefer to court first cousins over both siblings and unrelated birds (Bateson 1982), a balance between inbreeding and outbreeding. Mate choice is treated in [7.18.29](/7.18.29).

## 33.5. Kin Selection without Kin Recognition

Kin discrimination is a conditional strategy: the individual adjusts its behavior to the relatedness of each partner. Where discrimination is impossible, selection can still adjust a fixed behavior to the average relatedness among interacting individuals. Hamilton (1964) pointed out that limited **dispersal**, which he called population viscosity, keeps relatives together, so that indiscriminate help to neighbors is help to relatives on average. Bacteria are the clearest case: clonal growth and limited movement mean that a cell is usually surrounded by clone-mates. The production of **siderophores** by *Pseudomonas aeruginosa* is a cooperative "public good" exploited by nonproducing mutants; in experimental evolution, the producers increased when each subpopulation was founded by a single clone and lost ground to nonproducers when subpopulations were founded by two clones (Griffin, West and Buckling 2004; [7.10.16](/7.10.16#sec-16.3.1)).

Limited dispersal has a second consequence, which can cancel the first. If relatives stay together, they also compete with each other for food, breeding sites and mates, and the extra offspring produced by helping a neighbor compete with the helper's own relatives.

**Definition 33.5.1 (Kin Competition)** **Kin competition** is competition for resources among related individuals, which reduces the indirect fitness benefit of helping relatives (and the indirect cost of harming them), because the extra reproduction of the recipient comes partly at the expense of other relatives of the actor.

:::

Suppose that an actor pays $c$, a neighbor gains $b$, and a fraction $a$ of the competition faced by the extra offspring is local, among individuals related to the actor by $r$ (the scale of competition; $a = 0$ when competition is global). For large groups the inclusive fitness effect of the behavior is approximately

$$
-c + rb - a\,r\,(b - c), \tag{33.2}
$$

so that helping is favored if $b\,r(1 - a) > c\,(1 - ar)$ (West, Pen and Griffin 2002). With $a = 0$ this is Hamilton's rule. With $a = 1$ it reduces to $-c(1 - r) < 0$: however high the relatedness, the benefit to the recipient is exactly offset by the cost to other relatives with whom it competes. Taylor (1992) found this exact cancellation in a simple model of a viscous population with nonoverlapping generations, in which the increase in relatedness due to limited dispersal is exactly balanced by the increase in kin competition. The cancellation is broken by anything that separates the scale of cooperation from the scale of competition: overlapping generations, dispersal of groups rather than individuals ("budding"), empty sites that allow successful groups to expand, or dispersal of one sex. Griffin and colleagues (2004) varied both factors in their *Pseudomonas* experiment: siderophore producers did best when relatedness was high and competition global, and worst when relatedness was low and competition local.

![Kin competition can cancel the benefit of helping relatives (Eq. 33.2). A: the critical benefit-to-cost ratio for altruism against relatedness for four values of the scale of competition a, the fraction of the competition among the extra offspring that is local among relatives of the actor; a = 0 gives Hamilton's rule b/c > 1/r, and as a approaches 1 altruism becomes impossible at any relatedness short of clones. B: the inclusive fitness effect of helping (b = 3, c = 1) against a, for r = 1/2 and r = 3/4; helping is disfavored beyond a* = (rb − c)/(r(b − c))](./plots/kin-competition.py)

Kin competition is the reason why the evidence for limited dispersal as a promoter of cooperation must be judged with care, and why the ecology of competition, not only the genetic structure of a population, determines whether helping evolves. It also has consequences far beyond helping: competition among related males for mates on a patch is the basis of **local mate competition** and its female-biased sex ratios ([7.10.13](/7.10.13#sec-13.3.2)), and competition among related offspring for a parent's resources is part of the family conflict of [7.18.32](/7.18.32).

## 33.6. Selfish Restraint

Hamilton's rule applies to any behavior with consequences for others, not only to help. A selfish act benefits the actor and harms the recipient; if the recipient is a relative, the harm has an indirect cost, and individuals should be less selfish toward kin. Cannibalism is the most extreme selfish behavior, and it is common in species with a wide range of body sizes, such as fishes, amphibians and many insects.

Larvae of the Arizona tiger salamander (*Ambystoma mavortium nebulosum*) develop into one of two morphs: a typical morph that eats invertebrates, and a larger cannibal morph with a broad head and enlarged teeth that eats other larvae. Cannibals develop more often at high density and when food is scarce. Pfennig and Collins (1993) reared larvae in groups of 16, either all siblings or a mixture of siblings and nonsiblings. Cannibals developed in a smaller proportion of the sibling groups, and when housed with siblings and nonsiblings, cannibals ate nonsiblings preferentially; they used chemical cues to discriminate. The alternative explanation that cannibals avoid kin because pathogens adapted to a genotype are more easily acquired from relatives was not supported by subsequent experiments. Pfennig, Sherman and Collins (1999) then estimated the terms of Hamilton's rule. Individual cannibals differ in how strongly they discriminate; when a discriminating or a nondiscriminating cannibal was placed with 24 typical larvae, six of them its siblings, about four siblings survived with the discriminator and two with the nondiscriminator, an indirect benefit $B \approx 2$, while discriminators grew and matured as fast as nondiscriminators, so that $C \approx 0$. With $r = \tfrac12$, the rule $B/C > 2$ is satisfied. Spadefoot toad tadpoles (*Spea*), which also develop a carnivorous morph, show the same restraint toward siblings.

The same principle explains variation in the harm that parasites do to their hosts. If the parasites in a host are clone-mates, they share an interest in exploiting the host prudently, since its early death ends their common resource; if a host is infected by unrelated strains, each gains by taking as much as it can before the others do, and **virulence** should be higher (Frank 1996). Nematodes that live in figs with their pollinating wasps are more virulent in fig wasp species in which several foundress wasps, carrying unrelated nematodes, enter the same fig (Herre 1993).

## 33.7. Spite

Hamilton (1970) noticed that his rule also predicts spite. A spiteful act has $C > 0$ and $B < 0$, so that $rB - C > 0$ requires $r < 0$: the recipient must be less related to the actor than an average member of the population with whom the actor competes. This is less paradoxical than it sounds. Harming a nonrelative can be favored if it frees resources for others who are more closely related to the actor than the victim is. Spite can therefore be seen as altruism toward a *secondary recipient*: what matters is that the actor is more closely related to the individuals that benefit from reduced competition than to those it harms (Gardner and West 2004). The conditions are demanding. The behavior must be costly to the actor and must not bring it a delayed direct benefit; it must be directed at individuals of relatively low relatedness; and competition must be local and intense enough that harming them benefits closer relatives.

Many behaviors once proposed as spiteful turn out to be selfish. Territorial aggression reduces competition for the aggressor's own resources; herring and western gulls that kill chicks at neighboring nests, mammals that kill infants sired by other males, macaques that harass the daughters of other females, and sticklebacks that eat eggs in neighbors' nests all reduce competition for their own offspring (West and Gardner 2010). Two convincing cases of spite involve close relatives that are clones.

The polyembryonic parasitoid wasp *Copidosoma floridanum* lays an egg, often one male and one female egg, into the egg of a moth such as *Trichoplusia ni*. Each egg divides asexually (**polyembryony**) into hundreds or thousands of embryos as the caterpillar grows, so that a host contains a clone of sisters and a clone of brothers. Most embryos develop into larvae that will eventually consume the caterpillar and emerge as adults, but some develop early into a sterile soldier caste, mostly from the female embryos. Soldiers have long mandibles, move through the host's body and attack other larvae, and die without ever developing further (Strand and colleagues). The resources of a caterpillar allow only a fraction of the larvae to complete development. A female soldier is related by $r = 1$ to her clonal sisters and by $r = \tfrac14$ to her brothers (haplodiploid sister–brother relatedness), and by about 0 to the larvae of other females that have parasitized the same host. When the experimenters introduced labeled sisters, brothers or unrelated larvae into hosts containing a developing female brood, soldiers attacked unrelated larvae and brothers far more often than sisters (Giron and colleagues 2004). The cue proved to be the extraembryonic membrane that surrounds each larva: when the membrane was removed, attack rates no longer depended on kinship, and when membranes were transplanted between larvae, soldiers attacked according to their relatedness to the donor of the membrane rather than to the larva inside (Giron and Strand 2004). All three conditions for spite are met: soldiers are sterile, they preferentially kill larvae to which they are less related than average, and the freed resources go to their clonal sisters (Gardner and colleagues 2007).

Many bacteria produce bacteriocins, protein toxins lethal to cells of the same or closely related species that lack the matching immunity protein. The colicins of *Escherichia coli* are encoded on plasmids together with an immunity gene and often a lysis gene, and producing cells release the toxin by bursting, so that production is costly and in some cases suicidal. Because the toxin and immunity genes are linked, clone-mates of a producer are immune and only nonrelatives are killed; and because bacteria grow clonally, a producer's neighbors include both clone-mates and competing strains. A model of this situation (Gardner, West and Buckling 2004) predicts that spite pays most when producers are at intermediate frequencies in a patch: when they are rare, the toxin kills too few competitors to repay the cost, and when they are common, few susceptible cells remain to be killed. Inglis and colleagues (2009) confirmed the prediction with pyocin-producing strains of *P. aeruginosa*: the relative fitness of producers was highest at intermediate starting frequencies.

In both cases the actors are clones, and harming nonrelatives is the most effective way of helping clone-mates. Spite should be rare elsewhere, because the variation in relatedness within a local group is rarely large enough. Whether humans and other primates show spite in Hamilton's sense is debated. Captive chimpanzees punish a partner that steals their food by collapsing the table that holds it, but not a partner that merely receives more food than they do (Jensen, Call and Tomasello 2007): retaliation of this kind can be selfish, because it changes the partner's future behavior ([7.18.34](/7.18.34)).

## 33.8. Kin Selection and Its Critics

Kin selection is not a special mechanism alongside "group selection" or "individual selection"; it is a way of accounting for all the consequences of a gene's effects. Any model of social evolution can be analyzed either by partitioning selection within and between groups (**multilevel selection**) or by inclusive fitness, and the two give the same predictions when applied correctly ([7.10.16](/7.10.16#sec-16.5)). In 2010 Nowak, Tarnita and Wilson argued that inclusive fitness theory was limited to special cases and had not explained the evolution of eusociality, and proposed instead a population-genetic analysis in which relatedness plays no causal role. The reply, signed by more than 130 researchers (Abbot and colleagues 2011), pointed out that inclusive fitness theory is general when relatedness is defined as a regression coefficient, that the models of Nowak and colleagues were themselves special cases of it, and that the theory had generated successful and specific predictions, above all about conflicts in insect colonies over sex ratios and male production, which are discussed in [7.18.36](/7.18.36).

The fair criticisms concern practice rather than principle. Measuring $B$ and $C$ is hard, because it requires knowing how much the actor and the recipient would have reproduced without the behavior; the parameters are often estimated from observational differences between individuals that may also differ in quality, and experiments that remove helpers or manipulate their opportunities are much more informative. The theory also says little about mechanisms. It predicts what an animal should do given the costs, benefits and relatedness it faces, and leaves to the study of recognition, development and physiology the question of how the animal does it.

## Summary

- Social behaviors are classified by their lifetime fitness effects on actor and recipient: mutual benefit, selfishness, **altruism** and **spite**. Only the last two require indirect fitness to explain them.
- **Relatedness** can be calculated from pedigrees (Eq. 33.1) but is defined statistically, relative to the population, and is now estimated from molecular markers; such estimates have shown unexpected kin structure in meerkats, paper wasps and social amoebae.
- **Hamilton's rule** depends as much on ecology ($B$, $C$) as on genetics ($r$). In offspring units (Formula 33.2.1), helping parents rear full siblings needs $B/C > 1$, helping a sister $B/C > 2$. Wild turkey coalitions satisfy the rule with $rB - C = 1.7$.
- Alarm calls of ground squirrels and prairie dogs, and adoption of orphaned kin by red squirrels, are directed toward relatives as predicted.

| Recognition mechanism | Cue and template | Recognizes unfamiliar kin? | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Location | Place | No | Birds feeding any chick in the nest; cuckoos exploit it |
| Familiarity | Characteristics learned during development | No | Ground squirrels reared together; long-tailed tit churr calls |
| Phenotype matching (Definition 33.4.2) | Genetic cue compared with self or kin | Yes, and degrees of kinship | Full vs half sisters in ground squirrels; hamsters; *Dictyostelium* *tgr* genes |
| Recognition alleles (**green-beard effect**) | Label, recognition and action linked to one locus | Only bearers of the allele | Fire ant *Gp-9* supergene, *csA*, *FLO1* |

- Recognition is a signal-detection problem: the acceptance threshold should be stricter when nonkin are common or costly to accept (honey bee guards).
- Kin competition (Definition 33.5.1) can cancel the benefit of helping relatives in viscous populations (Eq. 33.2); cooperation is favored when relatedness is high and competition global (*Pseudomonas* siderophores).
- Kin selection also favors selfish restraint toward relatives: cannibalistic tiger salamanders avoid eating siblings ($B \approx 2$, $C \approx 0$); parasites in clonal infections are less virulent.
- Spite requires negative relatedness and local competition; it occurs in soldier larvae of polyembryonic wasps and in bacteriocin-producing bacteria, and is most advantageous at intermediate frequencies of producers.
- Inclusive fitness and multilevel selection are equivalent accountings; the weak point of kin selection studies is the measurement of $B$ and $C$, not the theory.

## Questions

1. A female bird can either breed on her own, raising on average 1.5 offspring, or help her parents, raising their output from 2.0 to 3.2 offspring. Which option is favored if her parents are both alive? If her father has been replaced by an unrelated male?

   **Answer** Helping costs $C = 1.5$ own offspring ($r = \tfrac12$) and produces $B = 1.2$ extra siblings. If both parents are hers, the siblings are full siblings ($r = \tfrac12$), and Formula 33.2.1 requires $B/C > 1$; since $1.2/1.5 = 0.8$, breeding is favored. With a stepfather the siblings are half siblings ($r = \tfrac14$), and helping would require $B/C > 2$, which is even further from being met. Helping would become favored only if her own expected success fell below 1.2 offspring, for example because no territory is available; this is the ecological constraint discussed in 7.18.35. Indeed, Seychelles warbler and white-fronted bee-eater helpers are more likely to leave or stop helping when a parent has been replaced.

2. How would you distinguish experimentally between recognition by familiarity and by phenotype matching in a rodent?

   **Answer** Cross-foster newborn pups to create unfamiliar siblings (reared apart) and familiar nonsiblings (reared together), and later test the behavior of each class toward the other in neutral arenas, removing spatial cues. If animals treat familiar nonsiblings like siblings and unfamiliar siblings like strangers, familiarity is the mechanism; if unfamiliar siblings are treated better than unfamiliar nonsiblings, phenotype matching is involved. Within litters with multiple paternity, a difference in treatment of full and half siblings that share the same rearing environment also indicates phenotype matching; rearing an animal entirely without kin, as in the hamster study, tests whether its own phenotype can serve as the template.

3. Why are green-beard genes expected to be rare, and why are most known examples cell-adhesion genes in microorganisms?

   **Answer** A green beard must produce a label, recognize it in others and direct help to bearers; if these functions are separable, a mutant that keeps the label but drops the help gains the benefits without the costs and spreads, destroying the system. Genes elsewhere in the genome, which do not benefit from help directed to beard bearers, are also selected to suppress the beard. In a homophilic adhesion protein the label and the cooperative act are the same molecule: cells expressing it stick to one another and are thereby included in the protected group or the fruiting body, while nonexpressing cells are excluded, so that a falsebeard cannot arise. In fire ants the separate functions are kept together by the absence of recombination in a supergene.

4. Why does Eq. (33.2) predict that helping is never favored when all competition is local ($a = 1$), and which features of real populations break this cancellation?

   **Answer** With $a = 1$ the expression becomes $-c + rb - r(b - c) = -c(1 - r)$, negative for any $r < 1$. The extra $b - c$ offspring produced in the group replace, one for one, offspring of other group members, who are related to the actor by the same $r$ as the recipient; the gain through the recipient ($rb$) is exactly matched by the loss through the displaced relatives ($rb - rc$), leaving only the actor's own cost. The cancellation is broken whenever the extra offspring do not simply displace relatives: when generations overlap, so that offspring compete partly with unrelated adults; when groups disperse as units (budding) or successful groups can expand into empty sites; when one sex disperses, so that the cooperating sex and the competing sex differ; and when competition is partly global, as in the *Pseudomonas* experiment with global competition, where cooperators were favored at high relatedness.

5. Explain why spite is most advantageous at intermediate frequencies of producers in a patch, and why spite is expected mainly in clonal organisms.

   **Answer** The benefit of producing a toxin comes from killing competitors, which is proportional to the frequency of producers (the amount of toxin) times the frequency of susceptible cells, while the cost is paid by each producer regardless. When producers are rare, the toxin kills few cells and the cost exceeds the benefit; when producers are common, few susceptible cells remain, so selection on production vanishes. The change in frequency, proportional to $f(1 - f)(kf - c)$, is therefore greatest at intermediate $f$. Spite needs large differences in relatedness within the group of competitors: the victims must be much less related to the actor than the beneficiaries. In clonal organisms the beneficiaries are clone-mates ($r = 1$) and the victims other strains ($r \approx 0$), which maximizes this difference; in sexual populations with ordinary family structure, the differences are smaller and harming is rarely the most efficient way to help kin.
