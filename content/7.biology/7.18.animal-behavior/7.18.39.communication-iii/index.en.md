The previous chapters described what signals are for ([7.18.37](/7.18.37)) and how physics shapes them ([7.18.38](/7.18.38)). This chapter turns to their evolution, and to two questions in particular. The first concerns origins: a signal requires both a sender that produces it and a receiver that responds, so how can a new signal arise when neither the display nor the response yet exists? The answer is that new signals are built from preexisting material, either from traits of senders that already provided cues to receivers or from preexisting biases of receivers that senders could exploit. The second question concerns reliability: when the interests of sender and receiver differ, senders gain by exaggeration and deceit, so why do receivers continue to believe signals? The theory of honest signaling, from Zahavi's **handicap principle** ([7.10.13](/7.10.13)) to the game-theoretic models of Grafen and Maynard Smith, is one of the central achievements of behavioral ecology, and it applies to signals of quality in courtship and contests, of need in families, and of unprofitability to predators.

The chapter builds on the classical ethology of releasers and conflict behavior ([7.18.3](/7.18.3)), on game theory ([7.18.25](/7.18.25)) and on the theory of sexual selection ([7.10.13](/7.10.13)); the sexual selection of signals is developed further in [7.18.29](/7.18.29), and the social functions of signals in [7.18.40](/7.18.40).

## 39.1. The Origins of Signals

### 39.1.1. Reconstructing the History of Signals

Signals, like other traits, can be traced through phylogenies. Konrad Lorenz's comparison of the courtship displays of ducks (1941) was one of the first uses of behavior as a taxonomic character: homologous display elements could be recognized across species by their form and their position in display sequences, just as homologous bones are recognized by their position in the skeleton. With modern molecular phylogenies, signals can be mapped onto trees with the **comparative method** ([7.10.3](/7.10.3#def-3.5.1)), to ask which states are ancestral, how often a display has been gained or lost, and whether a female preference arose before or after the male trait it favors. These questions require three kinds of evidence: the phylogeny, the distribution of the signal among species, and the distribution of the receiver's response, which may exist even in species that do not produce the signal.

### 39.1.2. Sender Precursors and Ritualization

Most displays have recognizable origins in behavior or physiology that once served other functions. Any action that is reliably correlated with what an animal will do next is a cue to others: a bird that crouches and raises its tail is about to fly; a dog that bares its teeth may bite. If receivers respond to such cues in ways that benefit the sender, selection on the sender will modify the cue to make it more effective, and the cue becomes a signal.

**Definition 39.1.1 (Ritualization)** **Ritualization** is the evolutionary modification of a behavior pattern, physiological response or morphological trait into a signal, typically by exaggeration, repetition, stereotypy and simplification of form, and often by the addition of conspicuous structures or colors that enhance it, until the display becomes partly or wholly independent of its original causes.

Tinbergen and Lorenz identified several sources from which displays have been ritualized.

**Definition 39.1.2 (Intention Movement)** An **intention movement** is the incomplete initial phase of a behavior, such as the crouch before take-off or the raising of a foot before walking, which reveals what the animal is about to do.

- *Intention movements*: the threat postures of many birds and mammals contain the initial movements of attack (raised bill, lowered head, bared teeth), and the take-off intention movements of birds have been ritualized into flight displays and the "head-up" postures of courtship.
- *Conflict behavior*: ambivalent postures combining attack and escape, and **displacement activities** ([7.18.3](/7.18.3#sec-3.4.3)) such as preening, drinking and feeding performed out of context. In the courtship of many ducks, a male turns his head and briefly preens behind the wing, a movement derived from displacement preening; in the mandarin duck (*Aix galericulata*) the bill touches an enlarged, brightly colored orange "sail" feather, so that the movement now displays a structure that has evolved to be displayed (Lorenz 1941).
- *Autonomic responses*: raising of hair and feathers, which in a frightened or aggressive animal changes its apparent size; dilation of blood vessels, which produces flushing of the skin; urination and defecation, which in many mammals have become scent marking.
- *Feeding and care*: courtship feeding in birds derives from the feeding of young, and the "begging" postures of females in many birds and the regurgitation displays of canids derive from the interactions of parent and offspring.

Ritualized displays typically have a *typical intensity* (Morris 1957): they are performed in nearly the same form over a wide range of motivational levels, whereas the behavior from which they derive varied continuously. Stereotypy reduces the information that the display carries about the sender's motivation, but it makes the display easier to recognize at a distance and against noise, an increase of $d'$ in the language of **signal detection theory** ([7.18.37](/7.18.37#sec-37.3.4)).

**Example 39.1.1 (The Origin of the Honey Bee Dance)** The waggle dance of honey bees ([7.17.38](/7.17.38)) is a ritualized flight toward a food source. Comparison of living species suggests how it arose (Dyer 2002). Stingless bees (Meliponini), the sister group of honey bees in many analyses, recruit nestmates by running excitedly through the nest, by producing pulsed sounds whose duration in some species of *Melipona* is correlated with the distance to food, by guiding recruits to food in short flights or by laying scent trails, but they do not indicate direction by a dance. In the dwarf honey bees, such as *Apis florea*, which nest in the open on a single comb, the dance is performed on the horizontal upper surface of the comb, and the waggle run points directly toward the food, like an abbreviated take-off flight. In the cavity-nesting *A. mellifera* and *A. cerana*, the dance is performed in darkness on the vertical comb, and the direction of the food relative to the sun is transposed into an angle relative to gravity ([7.18.21](/7.18.21#sec-21.6.3)). The sequence from a movement that points toward the goal, to a symbolic transposition of direction, shows how a cue (the take-off direction) became a conventional code.

:::

Some signals derive from structures rather than movements. Female spotted hyenas (*Crocuta crocuta*) have an enlarged, erectile clitoris, a "pseudopenis", which is presented and sniffed during greeting ceremonies between clan members, mostly initiated by subordinates (East, Hofer and Wickler 1993). Matthews (1939) proposed that the structure arose as a by-product of exposure of female embryos to high levels of androgens, and was only later co-opted for signaling. Stephen Jay Gould called this logic the *panda principle*, after the giant panda's "thumb", an enlarged wrist bone co-opted for grasping bamboo: evolution builds new functions from available parts. The androgen hypothesis has been only partly supported, because treating pregnant females with antiandrogens reduced but did not abolish the masculinization of their daughters' genitalia (Drea and colleagues 1998), but it illustrates that a trait need not have evolved as a signal to become one.

### 39.1.3. Receiver Precursors: Sensory Exploitation

New signals can also arise from the receiver's side. Receivers have sensory systems and response rules that evolved for detecting food, predators or mates, and a sender that happens to produce a stimulus matching such a **sensory bias** ([7.10.13](/7.10.13#def-13.2.3)) will elicit a response without any prior history of signaling. The hypothesis that signals evolve by sensory exploitation predicts that the receiver's preference evolved before the signal, which can be tested by comparing species on a phylogeny.

- *Water mites.* Males of the water mite *Neumania papillator* court females by vibrating their forelegs near them; the vibrations resemble those of copepods, the mite's prey. Females adopt a predatory posture and clutch at vibrating males, and hungry females respond more than satiated ones, as expected if the response is a feeding response; the phylogeny of related mites indicates that the predatory response to vibration preceded male courtship trembling (Proctor 1991, 1992).
- *Swordtails and platyfish.* Males of most swordtails (*Xiphophorus*) have a long extension of the tail fin, the sword, which females prefer. Alexandra Basolo (1990) attached artificial swords to males of the platyfish *X. maculatus*, a swordless species, and found that females preferred males with swords, which suggested that the preference predated the sword. Later phylogenies, however, indicated that swords may have been present in the ancestors of platyfish and lost (Meyer and colleagues 1994), so the preference could be a relic of a lost trait rather than a preexisting bias. The dispute illustrates the dependence of such tests on the reliability of the phylogeny.
- *Túngara frogs.* The preference of females of *Physalaemus coloradorum*, which produce no chucks, for calls with chucks added indicates that the preference for chucks arose before chucks ([7.18.16](/7.18.16#sec-16.6.4)).
- *Novel ornaments.* Female zebra finches preferred males fitted with an artificial white crest over unadorned males and over males with red or green crests, although no estrildid finch has such a crest (Burley and Symanski 1998), showing that preferences for novel traits can exist before the traits evolve.

When the sender's signal mimics a stimulus to which the receiver has evolved to respond in another context, the signal is said to exploit a sensory trap.

**Definition 39.1.3 (Sensory Trap)** A **sensory trap** is a signal that mimics a stimulus to which the receiver responds adaptively in another context (food, shelter, escape from predators), and so elicits that response for the sender's benefit (Christy 1995).

Males of the fiddler crab *Uca beebei* build mud pillars at the entrance of their burrows; females searching for mates are more likely to approach burrows with pillars, and when threatened by predators they also run to pillars, which resemble the objects that crabs use as landmarks for refuges (Christy 1988). The ultrasonic courtship songs of some moths offer a striking case. Female moths of many species have ears that detect the echolocation calls of bats ([7.18.12](/7.18.12)). In the tobacco cutworm *Spodoptera litura*, recorded male songs and simulated bat calls were equally effective in making females accept males, so females apparently do not distinguish the two, consistent with an origin of the songs from a bat-detection system (Nakano and colleagues 2010). In the Asian corn borer *Ostrinia furnacalis*, the male's quiet ultrasonic song, produced by rubbing specialized scales on the wings against the thorax, resembles the calls of distant bats, to which females respond by freezing; the song suppresses the female's escape and increases the male's chance of copulating, a deceptive exploitation (Nakano and colleagues 2008). In the Japanese lichen moth *Eilema japonica*, by contrast, females distinguish the ultrasound of males from that of bats and answer only bats with defensive clicks (Nakano and colleagues 2003). A sensory trap that harms the receiver should select for discrimination, and the lichen moth may show the outcome.

### 39.1.4. Preexisting Traits or Preexisting Biases?

The two routes are not mutually exclusive and are often difficult to distinguish. A preference observed for an artificial trait may be a genuine preexisting bias, or the relic of an ancestral signal that was lost while the preference remained, as the swordtail example shows; male ornaments appear to be gained and lost more readily than female preferences (Wiens 2001). Once a signal has originated by either route, coevolution of sender and receiver typically modifies both: the receiver's response is tuned to the signal (adaptive fastidiousness) and the signal to the receiver's sense organs and the environment (sensory drive, [7.18.38](/7.18.38#sec-38.6)).

## 39.2. The Problem of Reliability

A signal is *honest*, or reliable, if it is correlated with the condition it advertises strongly enough that receivers gain by responding to it, and *deceptive* if it gives the receiver false information to the sender's benefit. Reliability is not a problem when sender and receiver have the same interests ([7.18.37](/7.18.37#sec-37.5.2)). It becomes a problem whenever the sender would prefer the receiver to respond in the same way regardless of the true condition: every male would like to be chosen, every contestant would like its opponent to retreat, every chick would like to be fed. Three kinds of mechanism keep signals reliable despite such conflicts (Maynard Smith and Harper 2003; Bradbury and Vehrencamp 2011).

| Guarantee | Why the signal is reliable | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Index | The signal cannot be produced unless the sender has the quality signaled: its form is physically linked to the quality | Toad croak frequency, red deer formants, the vibration amplitude of a spider's web |
| Handicap | Faking is possible but does not pay: the signal costs more, or benefits less, for senders of low quality | Long tails, carotenoid colors, vigorous displays, chick begging |
| Conventional | The signal is arbitrary and cheap; it is reliable because interests coincide, or because dishonest senders are detected and punished by receivers (social costs) | Honey bee dances, alarm calls, status badges of paper wasps |

Two other categories complete the scheme. *Proximity signals* are threats whose reliability is guaranteed by the risk of approaching a rival closely, where it can attack. Modifiers carry no information themselves but make other features easier (*amplifiers*) or harder (*attenuators*) to assess: a contrasting border around a color patch makes small differences in the patch easier to see (Hasson 1997).

## 39.3. Index Signals

**Definition 39.3.1 (Index Signal)** An **index signal** is a signal whose intensity or form is causally and inescapably linked to the quality it indicates, so that it cannot be faked whatever the costs and benefits (Maynard Smith and Harper 1995).

Body size is the commonest quality advertised by indices, because the size of the structures that produce a signal limits its properties. Male common toads (*Bufo bufo*) compete for females in the breeding pond; a male clasping a female is often attacked by rivals that try to displace him, and larger males are better at holding on and at displacing others. The dominant frequency of the croak depends on the size of the larynx and therefore of the body: larger males croak at lower frequencies. Davies and Halliday (1978) silenced clasping males of different sizes and played back croaks of high or low pitch when a rival touched them; rivals attacked less persistently when they heard a deep croak, whatever the size of the clasping male, which shows that the deep croak itself deters rivals. Toads also assess rivals by touch, and the croak is only one cue.

Red deer stags (*Cervus elaphus*) roar during the rut, and roaring contests are the first phase of their assessment ([7.18.26](/7.18.26#sec-26.4.2)). During a roar, a stag retracts its larynx toward the sternum, extending the vocal tract to the limit set by its anatomy. Because the spacing of the formants is inversely proportional to the length of the vocal tract ([7.18.38](/7.18.38#sec-38.2.1)), the minimum formant dispersion in a roar is an index of the length of the tract and, through it, of body size: across stags, formant dispersion decreased with body weight (Reby and McComb 2003). Stags responded more strongly to playbacks of roars whose formants had been lowered by computer resynthesis, simulating a larger rival, than to roars with raised formants (Reby and colleagues 2005). The rate of roaring, in contrast, is a measure of stamina and condition, which can be increased only by paying an energetic cost, so a contest involves an index and a handicap together.

Indices are not completely incorruptible: selection can loosen the link between the signal and the quality. The descended larynx of red deer and of koalas, and the elongated tracheae coiled in the body of some birds such as the trumpet manucode (*Phonygammus keraudrenii*), which lower the formants beyond what the body size would produce, have been interpreted as exaggerations of apparent size (Fitch 1999). Such exaggeration is limited by anatomy, and receivers can recalibrate; an index remains informative as long as signal and quality remain correlated across senders.

Performance signals that require a particular physiological capacity are also indices. In the lizard *Anolis cristatellus*, the number of push-up displays performed toward a predator was correlated with the endurance of the lizard ([7.18.24](/7.18.24#sec-24.6.3)); a displaying lizard demonstrates a capacity it either has or lacks. Bradbury and Vehrencamp also recognize *informational indices*, signals that require particular knowledge: a territorial song sparrow can match the song type of a neighbor only if it shares that type in its repertoire, which indicates a long-term neighbor ([7.18.40](/7.18.40)).

## 39.4. Handicaps and Costly Signals

### 39.4.1. Zahavi's Idea

Amotz Zahavi (1975) proposed that the extravagant ornaments of males, such as the peacock's train, are reliable signals of quality precisely because they are costly: a male who survives despite carrying a handicap demonstrates that he is of high quality. The idea was initially rejected by theoreticians, because in early genetic models a gene for a handicap did not spread: the cost fell on the male and his sons, and the indirect benefit through better-quality offspring was not enough (Maynard Smith 1976). The idea was rescued in a modified form, in which what matters is not the handicap itself but the differential cost of the signal for senders of different quality.

**Definition 39.4.1 (Costly Signal)** A **costly signal** is a signal whose reliability is maintained by its cost: senders of different quality (or need) differ in the marginal cost or the marginal benefit of signaling at a given level, so that at equilibrium each type gives the signal level that is best for it, and the signal level reveals the type.

### 39.4.2. Grafen's Model

Alan Grafen (1990) showed with a general game-theoretic model that the handicap principle works. A sender of quality $q$ chooses a signal level $s$; the receiver observes $s$, infers a quality $\hat q(s)$ and responds accordingly; the sender's fitness $w(s, q, \hat q)$ increases with the quality attributed to it and decreases with the level of signaling.

**Proposition 39.4.1 (Honest Signaling Equilibrium)** Suppose that $w$ increases with $\hat q$, decreases with $s$, and that the marginal rate of substitution between perceived quality and signal level favors high-quality senders:

$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial q}\left(-\frac{\partial w/\partial s}{\partial w/\partial \hat q}\right) < 0,
$$
that is, the extra perceived quality a sender requires to compensate for a unit increase in signaling cost is smaller for senders of higher quality. Then there is an evolutionarily stable equilibrium in which the signal level $s^*(q)$ increases strictly with quality and the receiver infers quality correctly, $\hat q(s^*(q)) = q$ (Grafen 1990).

The intuition is visible in a simple special case. Let the receiver's response be worth $b\hat q$ to the sender and let the cost of signaling at level $s$ be $s^2/(2q)$, so that the same signal is cheaper for better senders. If the receiver believes that the signal reveals quality, the best signal for a sender of quality $q$ maximizes $bs/k - s^2/(2q)$ for a receiver who reads $\hat q = s/k$; the optimum is $s^* = bq/k$, which increases with $q$, and the receiver's belief is self-fulfilling. A low-quality sender could produce the signal of a high-quality one, but it would pay more for it than the benefit is worth.

Two consequences are important. First, at the honest equilibrium, high-quality senders may pay a small cost for their signals; what keeps the system honest is the cost that a low-quality sender *would* pay if it signaled at a higher level. The costs that maintain honesty may therefore not be visible in measurements of signals at equilibrium, and the observed cost of a signal is not a measure of its reliability (Számadó 2011). Second, the costs that matter are *strategic costs*, the costs needed for reliability, which add to the *efficacy costs* needed to make the signal detectable ([7.18.38](/7.18.38); Maynard Smith and Harper 2003). Later analyses showed that honest signaling does not require that signals be costly at equilibrium at all, only that dishonest signals would be costly; signals can then be cheap for honest senders (Lachmann, Számadó and Bergstrom 2001).

### 39.4.3. Signals of Need: the Sir Philip Sidney Game

Handicaps can signal need as well as quality. A nestling begging for food signals hunger; parent and offspring disagree about how much food the offspring should receive (**parent–offspring conflict**, [7.10.16](/7.10.16)), so why should the parent believe the begging? John Maynard Smith (1991) analyzed the problem with a game named after the Elizabethan soldier who, mortally wounded, gave his water to a dying soldier with the words "thy necessity is yet greater than mine".

**Example 39.4.1 (The Sir Philip Sidney Game)** A beneficiary is needy with probability $m$; without a resource, its survival is $1 - a$ if it is needy and $1 - b$ if not ($a > b$), and with the resource it survives with certainty. A donor holds the resource; if it gives the resource away, its own survival drops from 1 to $1 - d$. Before the donor decides, the beneficiary may signal at a cost $c$ to its survival. The two are related by $r$, and each maximizes its **inclusive fitness** (own survival plus $r$ times the other's).

Consider the strategy pair in which needy beneficiaries signal, non-needy ones do not, and the donor gives the resource only after a signal. It is an equilibrium when four conditions hold. The donor gives after a signal if $ra \ge d$ and withholds otherwise if $d \ge rb$. A needy beneficiary signals if $(1 - c) + r(1 - d) \ge (1 - a) + r$, that is, $c \le a - rd$; a non-needy one refrains if $(1 - b) + r \ge (1 - c) + r(1 - d)$, that is, $c \ge b - rd$. Honest signaling is therefore stable if

$$
rb \le d \le ra \quad\text{and}\quad b - rd \le c \le a - rd.
$$

:::

The signal must be costly enough that non-needy beneficiaries do not give it, but not so costly that needy ones do not; and the donor must have a reason to respond. The cost needed for honesty falls as relatedness increases, because a related beneficiary values the donor's survival too. The game has a sobering corollary (Bergstrom and Lachmann 1997): the costs of honest signaling can be so high that both players would be better off without communication, at a *pooling equilibrium* in which the donor ignores signals and either always or never gives. The evidence on begging in birds, where nestlings beg more when hungrier and parents feed more vigorous beggars, and its interpretation as costly signaling or as scramble competition, are treated in [7.18.32](/7.18.32).

The region is bounded on the left and right by the donor's conditions rb ≤ d ≤ ra and below and above by b − rd ≤ c ≤ a − rd. ### 39.4.4. Evidence for Condition-Dependent Signals

The handicap principle predicts that signals should be *condition dependent*: their expression should increase with the sender's quality, and more steeply than other traits. Stalk-eyed flies (*Teleopsis dalmanni*) offer a clear test. Males have eyes on long stalks, and females prefer males with larger eye spans. When larvae were raised on poor food, males' eye span decreased much more than their wing length or than the eye span of females, and the sensitivity of eye span to stress differed among genetic lines, so that eye span revealed both condition and genetic variation for condition (David and colleagues 2000). Carotenoid colors are condition dependent in many birds and fishes ([7.18.38](/7.18.38#sec-38.3.1)), and the *immunocompetence handicap hypothesis* (Folstad and Karter 1992; [7.18.19](/7.18.19)) proposes that ornaments that depend on testosterone are honest because testosterone suppresses immunity, which only males in good condition can afford.

In peacocks (*Pavo cristatus*), offspring of males with more elaborate trains grew faster and survived better in a breeding experiment (Petrie 1994), which supports a link between ornament and genetic quality. Later field work in Japan, however, found that peahens did not prefer males with more eyespots or longer trains (Takahashi and colleagues 2008), and the relative importance of the train, the shivering display and vocalizations in peahen choice remains debated, a reminder that even the classic example is not settled. The evidence for good genes and for the costs of ornaments is reviewed in [7.18.29](/7.18.29).

## 39.5. Conventional Signals and Social Costs

Many signals are arbitrary in form and cheap to produce: alarm calls, the distance code of the honey bee dance, the black facial marks of paper wasps.

**Definition 39.5.1 (Cheap Talk)** **Cheap talk** is signaling by signals whose production cost is negligible and unrelated to the sender's condition, so that their reliability cannot depend on production costs.

Cheap talk is stable when sender and receiver have the same preferences over the receiver's responses, or when their conflict is small enough that the sender loses more by being misunderstood than it gains by misleading (Crawford and Sobel 1982, in economics). It can also be stable under conflict if dishonest senders pay *social costs*, imposed by receivers that test the signal and punish senders whose signal does not match their behavior.

Paper wasps (*Polistes dominulus*) provide the clearest demonstration. Foundresses that start colonies together form dominance hierarchies, and their faces carry variable black spots on the clypeus; wasps with more broken patterns are larger and more dominant. Elizabeth Tibbetts and James Dale (2004) painted the faces of subordinate wasps to give them patterns of dominants. When such wasps were paired with a rival, they received much more aggression than controls painted without changing their pattern: a wasp whose badge claimed more than its behavior supported was tested and attacked. The badge is cheap to produce, but cheating is punished. Whether such badges of status are general is discussed in [7.18.40](/7.18.40), where some classic cases have failed later tests.

## 39.6. Deception

### 39.6.1. Deceptive Signals

**Definition 39.6.1 (Deceptive Signal)** A **deceptive signal** is a signal that provides receivers with false information about a condition, benefiting the sender at the receiver's expense.

Deception occurs between species and within them.

- **Aggressive mimicry**: predators imitate signals that attract their prey. Females of fireflies of the genus *Photuris* answer the flash patterns of males of *Photinus* with the response flash of *Photinus* females, lure the males and eat them (Lloyd 1965); they also acquire from their prey defensive steroids (lucibufagins) that they cannot synthesize (Eisner and colleagues 1997). Bolas spiders (*Mastophora*) emit chemicals that mimic the sex pheromones of particular moth species and catch the approaching males with a sticky ball on a thread; anglerfishes wave a lure.
- *False alarms*: fork-tailed drongos (*Dicrurus adsimilis*) in the Kalahari follow foraging meerkats and pied babblers and give genuine alarm calls when predators approach; but they also give false alarm calls when a meerkat has caught a large prey item, and steal the item when the meerkat flees. Drongos mimic the alarm calls of other species, including meerkats, and when meerkats ceased to respond to a repeated false call, drongos switched to a different call type, which restored the response (Flower 2011; Flower, Gribble and Ridley 2014).
- *Bluffing*: stomatopods (mantis shrimps) defend cavities with a powerful raptorial appendage, and threaten intruders by spreading it (the meral spread). After molting, the exoskeleton is soft for several days and a fight would be fatal; newly molted *Gonodactylus bredini* nevertheless threatened intruders more often than intermolt individuals, and the threat often deterred them (Steger and Caldwell 1983). The bluff works because intruders cannot tell soft from hard individuals by the threat alone, and most threatening residents are hard.
- **Batesian mimicry** of warning signals ([7.18.24](/7.18.24#sec-24.4.1)) is deception of predators by palatable prey, and the moth songs of Section 39.1.3 are deception of females by males.

### 39.6.2. Why Deception Does Not Destroy Communication

If signals can be faked, why do receivers continue to respond? The answer follows from the value of information ([7.18.37](/7.18.37#sec-37.3.5)): a receiver should respond to a signal as long as responding is better, on average, than ignoring it. A meerkat that ignores alarm calls risks death; one that flees from every call loses an occasional meal. If a fraction $f$ of calls are false and the losses are $L_1$ for staying when a predator is present and $L_2$ for fleeing unnecessarily, the meerkat should keep fleeing as long as the probability $1 - f$ that a call is genuine exceeds the red line $L_2/(L_1 + L_2)$, which with a high cost of being caught can tolerate a large proportion of false alarms.

Deception is therefore expected to persist at a frequency that receivers tolerate, and its frequency is limited by frequency dependence: the more common the deceptive signal, the less receivers respond, and the less deception pays. Batesian mimics are most successful when rare relative to their models ([7.10.17](/7.10.17)), drongos' false alarms work only intermittently, and bluffing stomatopods are effective because molting individuals are a minority at any time. Receivers can also improve discrimination, by learning which individuals or call types are unreliable, as meerkats do with drongo calls, and senders can counter by varying their signals, as drongos do: deception sustains an arms race between senders and receivers rather than destroying communication.

Bradbury and Vehrencamp list several sources of unreliable signals other than tactical deception: systems out of equilibrium (as when a new mimic has just appeared), perceptual errors by receivers, senders of different classes that receivers cannot distinguish (molting stomatopods), and receivers of different classes for which the same signal has different values.

## 39.7. Eavesdropping

**Definition 39.7.1 (Eavesdropping)** **Eavesdropping** is the detection and use of a signal by an individual other than the receiver for which the signal evolved, to the eavesdropper's benefit and often to the sender's cost.

Two kinds of eavesdropping are distinguished. In *interceptive eavesdropping*, predators, parasites and parasitoids use signals to find their hosts: the fringe-lipped bat hunting túngara frogs by their chucks and the parasitoid fly *Ormia ochracea* locating crickets by their song ([7.18.16](/7.18.16#sec-16.5)), *Photuris* fireflies locating *Photinus* males, the checkered beetles (Cleridae) that locate bark beetles by their aggregation pheromones, and electric eels detecting the discharges of weakly electric fishes ([7.18.15](/7.18.15#sec-15.6.2)). In *social eavesdropping*, conspecifics extract information from signaling interactions between others: female chickadees and great tits listen to song contests between their mates and neighbors, and males adjust their fighting behavior after watching others fight. Social eavesdropping turns pairwise exchanges into communication networks ([7.18.40](/7.18.40)).

Interceptive eavesdropping is a major cost of signaling and has shaped signals in three ways. Senders signal less, or less conspicuously, when eavesdroppers are present: túngara males stop chucking when bats fly over, and crickets on Kauai lost their song altogether under parasitism by *Ormia*. Signals move into channels or frequencies that eavesdroppers cannot detect well: the pulse discharges of *Brachyhypopomus* shift energy away from the frequencies to which predatory electroreceptors are sensitive, and the northern swordtail *Xiphophorus nigrensis* has ultraviolet ornaments that females see but that its main fish predator, which lacks ultraviolet sensitivity, cannot (Cummings, Rosenthal and Ryan 2003). And signals may be designed to be hard to localize: Peter Marler (1955) noted that the "seet" alarm calls given by many small passerines when a hawk flies over are high, thin tones that begin and end gradually, features that make them difficult to locate. Later measurements of hawk hearing showed that such calls are in a frequency range where hawks are less sensitive than their prey (Klump, Kretzschmar and Curio 1986), a mechanism of privacy based on the eavesdropper's audiogram rather than on localization.

## 39.8. Multiple Signals and Signals under Predation

Why do many males advertise with several ornaments at once, such as the red throat, blue eyes and zigzag dance of a stickleback, or the colors, calls and displays of many birds? Three hypotheses have been proposed (Møller and Pomiankowski 1993; Candolin 2003). *Multiple messages*: each signal conveys a different aspect of quality, as when one ornament reflects current condition and another past condition or age; a female that assesses several gains more information than from one. *Backup signals* (redundant signals): each is an imperfect indicator of the same quality, and combining them gives a more reliable estimate, as in **cue integration** ([7.16.27](/7.16.27#sec-27.2)); errors in the assessment of one are partly corrected by the others. *Unreliable signals*: some signals are remnants of past selection or products of Fisherian processes ([7.10.13](/7.10.13)) that carry little information, and receivers largely ignore them. The hypotheses differ in their predictions: multiple messages predict that the signals are only weakly correlated with each other and with different aspects of quality; backup signals predict positive correlations among them and with one aspect of quality. In sticklebacks, the red coloration and the courtship activity of males provide different information: courtship rate reflects the male's current condition and, together with color, predicts his ability to care for eggs (Candolin 2003).

Signals also evolve under the opposing pressures of sexual selection and predation. John Endler's (1980) studies of guppies in Trinidad are the classic demonstration. In streams with dangerous predatory fishes, males have fewer and smaller color spots than in streams where only a small predator that rarely eats adult guppies is present. When guppies from a high-predation site were transferred to a stream without dangerous predators, or raised in artificial ponds with different predators, the number and size of the spots increased within a few years in the absence of dangerous predators and decreased in their presence, a change of heritable color patterns in about ten generations. Female preference for bright males pulls in one direction, predation in the other, and the balance shifts with the local predator community.

## 39.9. Honesty and Human Language

Human language is the most powerful system of conventional signals known: arbitrary, cheap to produce and, because it is combinatorial and can refer to things absent in space and time, capable of conveying unlimited information. Cheap signals should be unreliable whenever interests conflict, so why is language so informative? Its reliability appears to rest less on production costs than on the features that stabilize cheap talk in other animals: much of human communication occurs among kin and allies who share interests; listeners check what they are told against other evidence and against the speaker's past reliability; and liars risk their reputation, a social cost that is large in societies where individuals interact repeatedly. Language also has index components that are hard to fake, such as the vocal correlates of body size, sex and emotional state. The evolution of language is treated in [7.18.47](/7.18.47).

## Summary

- New signals arise from preexisting material:

  | Route | Mechanism | Examples |
  |---|---|---|
  | Sender precursors | **Ritualization** (Definition 39.1.1) of **intention movements** (Definition 39.1.2), conflict behavior, autonomic responses, feeding and care | Threat postures, mock preening of ducks, the honey bee dance (Example 39.1.1) |
  | Preexisting traits | Co-option of structures (panda principle) | Hyena pseudopenis greeting |
  | Receiver precursors | Exploitation of **sensory bias**; **sensory traps** (Definition 39.1.3) | Water mites, swordtails, túngara frogs, fiddler crab pillars, moth ultrasound |

- Reliability is a problem under conflict of interest; it is guaranteed by indices, handicaps, or conventional signals stabilized by common interest or social costs; modifiers amplify or attenuate.
- **Index signals** (Definition 39.3.1) cannot be faked: toad croaks, red deer formant dispersion, push-up endurance, song type matching; anatomical exaggeration (descended larynx, elongated trachea) loosens but does not break them.
- **Costly signals** (Definition 39.4.1): Grafen's model (Proposition 39.4.1) shows that if marginal costs are lower for better senders, an honest, increasing signal level is an ESS; honesty depends on the costs cheaters would pay, which need not be visible at equilibrium.
- The Sir Philip Sidney game (Example 39.4.1) gives honest signals of need when $rb \le d \le ra$ and $b - rd \le c \le a - rd$; signaling costs may leave both parties worse off than a pooling equilibrium.
- Condition dependence: stalk-eyed fly eye span, carotenoid colors, immunocompetence handicaps; peacocks are still debated.
- **Cheap talk** (Definition 39.5.1) is stable under common interest or social costs (paper wasp badges).
- **Deceptive signals** (Definition 39.6.1): *Photuris* fireflies, bolas spiders, drongo false alarms with call switching, bluffing stomatopods, Batesian mimics. Deception persists at frequencies receivers tolerate, limited by frequency dependence and receiver learning.
- **Eavesdropping** (Definition 39.7.1) by predators and parasites shapes signals: suppression, private channels (ultraviolet in swordtails), hard-to-localize or inaudible alarm calls; social eavesdropping creates networks.
- Multiple signals may carry multiple messages or back each other up; predation opposes sexual selection (guppies evolve within about ten generations).

## Questions

1. Explain the difference between ritualization and sensory exploitation as origins of a signal, and describe the phylogenetic evidence needed to show that a female preference arose by sensory exploitation.

   **Answer** In ritualization, the signal derives from a trait of the sender that already provided a cue to receivers (an intention movement, an autonomic response), and selection on both sides refines it; the receiver's response existed as a response to the cue. In sensory exploitation, the signal arises when a sender trait happens to match a preexisting bias of the receiver that evolved in another context (food, predators), so the response predates the signal. To show sensory exploitation, one needs a reliable phylogeny, the distribution of the male trait among species, and the distribution of the female preference, including in species without the trait; the preference should be present in outgroups that lack the trait and be inferred as ancestral to the trait's origin (as in *Physalaemus coloradorum* for the chuck). One must also exclude the possibility that the outgroups once had the trait and lost it (the swordtail problem).
2. Female toads cannot see their rivals well in the dark breeding pond. Why is croak frequency a reliable signal of size, and what experiment showed that rivals use it?

   **Answer** The frequency of a croak is set by the size of the vocal apparatus, which scales with body size; a small male cannot produce a deep croak, so the signal is an index that cannot be faked regardless of costs. Davies and Halliday silenced clasping males and played back deep or high-pitched croaks when rivals touched them; rivals attacked less when they heard deep croaks, independently of the actual size of the clasping male, which showed that the croak itself influences the rival's decision.
3. In the special case of Grafen's model in Section 39.4.2, with $b = 1$, $k = 1$ and costs $s^2/(2q)$, compute the optimal signal and net payoff of senders with $q = 1$ and $q = 2$, and the payoff of a $q = 1$ sender that imitates the $q = 2$ signal. Why is the system honest?

   **Answer** The net payoff is $s - s^2/(2q)$, maximized at $s^* = q$. For $q = 1$: $s^* = 1$, payoff $1 - 1/2 = 0.5$. For $q = 2$: $s^* = 2$, payoff $2 - 4/4 = 1$. A $q = 1$ sender signaling $s = 2$ gets $2 - 4/2 = 0$, less than 0.5. The signal is honest because the cost of a given signal level is higher for the low-quality sender, so imitating the high-quality signal costs it more than the extra benefit of being perceived as high quality.
4. In the Sir Philip Sidney game with $a = 0.6$, $b = 0.2$, $r = 0.5$ and $d = 0.2$, find the range of signaling costs for which honest signaling is stable. Is it stable at $d = 0.35$?

   **Answer** The donor's conditions: $rb = 0.1 \le d = 0.2 \le ra = 0.3$, satisfied. The cost must satisfy $b - rd = 0.2 - 0.1 = 0.1 \le c \le a - rd = 0.6 - 0.1 = 0.5$. At $d = 0.35$, $d > ra = 0.3$: the donor's inclusive fitness loss from giving exceeds the gain to a needy relative, so the donor would not give even after a signal, and the signaling equilibrium does not exist.
5. Fork-tailed drongos give false alarm calls. Explain, using the value of information, why meerkats continue to respond to alarm calls, and why drongos switch call types.

   **Answer** Fleeing from a false alarm costs a meerkat a food item; ignoring a genuine alarm may cost its life. As long as the probability that an alarm is genuine exceeds the red line $L_2/(L_1 + L_2)$, which is low when $L_1 \gg L_2$, responding is better on average than ignoring, so a substantial proportion of false alarms is tolerated. Meerkats do learn: they respond less to a call type that has repeatedly been false. By switching to a different call type, drongos present a signal to which the meerkats' learned distrust does not apply, restoring the response; deception is thus maintained by an arms race between receiver learning and sender variation, and its success is frequency dependent.
6. Why might a bird that gives an alarm call when a hawk flies over use a high-frequency, gradually beginning tone? Give two hypotheses and the evidence for each.

   **Answer** (1) The localization hypothesis (Marler 1955): tones that begin and end gradually and lack broadband transients provide poor cues to direction (few onset and phase cues), so the hawk cannot easily find the caller. (2) The audibility hypothesis: hawks are less sensitive than small passerines at high frequencies, so calls in this range are audible to nearby conspecifics but less detectable to the predator (Klump and colleagues measured hawk audiograms showing reduced sensitivity in the range of seet calls). The two are not exclusive; experiments on the localization ability of hawks for such calls have given mixed results, supporting the role of reduced audibility.
7. (Open question) Design a study to test whether the number of eyespots in peacock trains is a handicap signal of quality.

   **Answer** Predictions of the handicap hypothesis: (1) eyespot number and train size are condition dependent, more than other traits (manipulate condition by diet or parasite load and measure train growth at the next molt, compared with tarsus length); (2) producing or carrying a larger train is costly, more for males in poor condition (measure survival or flight performance with experimentally shortened or lengthened trains); (3) females prefer males with more eyespots in free-living populations (observe matings and manipulate eyespots by removing some); (4) offspring of preferred males have higher viability (cross-fostering or artificial insemination to separate genetic from rearing effects, as in Petrie 1994). Conflicting results between populations (Petrie versus Takahashi and colleagues) suggest also measuring other components of display (shivering rate, calls) as alternative signals.
