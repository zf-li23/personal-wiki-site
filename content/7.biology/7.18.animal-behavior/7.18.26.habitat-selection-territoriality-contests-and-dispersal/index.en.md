Where an animal lives determines what it eats, which predators it meets and whom it mates with. Many animals choose: a young bird settles in one patch of woodland rather than another, a male defends a stretch of shore against all comers, a juvenile ground squirrel leaves its mother's burrow and travels hundreds of meters before settling. This chapter is about these decisions, which together determine how individuals are spread over the landscape: where to settle (habitat selection), whether to defend the place (territoriality), how to resolve the contests that defense entails, and whether to stay at the place of birth or leave it (dispersal).

The analysis builds directly on the previous chapter ([7.18.25](/7.18.25)): the **ideal free distribution** and the despotic distribution are the two reference models of habitat selection, the bourgeois strategy and the war of attrition are the starting points for understanding contests, and the cost–benefit reasoning of **optimality models** decides when defense pays. The genetic consequences of dispersal, gene flow and population structure, were treated in [7.10.10](/7.10.10); the signals used in contests and territorial advertisement belong to the chapters on communication ([7.18.39](/7.18.39), [7.18.40](/7.18.40)), and bird song as a territorial signal to [7.18.17](/7.18.17). Migration, the regular return movement between areas, has its own chapter ([7.18.20](/7.18.20)). As in the previous chapters, we analyze the decisions of individuals; their consequences for population regulation and the dynamics of metapopulations belong to ecology (7.19).

## 26.1. Habitat Selection

### 26.1.1. Choosing Where to Breed

Species live in characteristic habitats, and individuals usually prefer them when given a choice. We might expect the preferred habitat to be the one where reproduction is most successful. Yet often it is not. In the blackcap (*Sylvia atricapilla*), a European warbler, males first settle along streams in deciduous forest and only later in mixed coniferous woodlots away from water, a clear order of preference; but the average number of young raised in the two habitats was the same (Weidinger 2000). The ideal free model explains why: as the preferred habitat fills, the payoff there falls, until the second-ranked habitat is as good, and from then on both are filled so that payoffs are equal. In line with this, nesting blackcaps were four times denser in the preferred streamside habitat. Habitat selection that depends on the number of individuals already present is called *density-dependent habitat selection*; it equalizes average success between habitats under ideal free conditions and leaves differences under despotic conditions ([7.18.25](/7.18.25#sec-25.3.4)).

Gordon Orians (1969) applied the same reasoning to female red-winged blackbirds (*Agelaius phoeniceus*), which choose among territories of polygynous males that differ in the quality of their nest sites. A female settling on an already crowded good territory shares its resources and the male's help with other females; she may do as well or better as the first female on a poorer territory. Females did settle on poorer territories when the better ones held many females, the basis of the polygyny threshold model of mating systems ([7.18.30](/7.18.30)). Whether females are free to settle or are excluded by the aggression of resident females differs among species.

The ideal free model treats habitat quality as known. To measure it empirically, ecologists often estimate a *resource selection function*, a function proportional to the probability that a unit of habitat (a pixel of a map, a patch of vegetation) is used, fitted from the locations of tracked animals and maps of resources and vegetation (Manly and colleagues 2002); it is a tool of wildlife management, for example in predicting the effect of a road or a clearcut.

### 26.1.2. Habitat Cues and Public Information

Animals cannot measure the future reproductive value of a habitat directly; they use cues that are correlated with it. Some cues are structural features of the habitat: the type of vegetation, the presence of water, the shape of a cavity. Some are learned early in life: many animals prefer, as adults, the type of habitat in which they were raised, a phenomenon called *natal habitat preference induction* (Davis and Stamps 2004), which may be adaptive because surviving to adulthood in a habitat shows that one's phenotype suits it. And many are social. The presence of conspecifics shows that a site is suitable, and juveniles of territorial lizards (*Anolis aeneus*) settle preferentially near established residents, even though residents compete with them (Stamps 1988). More informative still is the reproductive success of others. Collared flycatchers (*Ficedula albicollis*) on Gotland prospect breeding patches late in the breeding season, when the young of other pairs are visible; experimentally increasing or reducing the number of young in nests of a patch, by moving nestlings between patches, changed the number of birds that settled there the next year (Doligez, Danchin and Clobert 2002). This is *public information*: information on the quality of an option obtained by observing the performance of others ([7.18.46](/7.18.46)).

Because cues are only correlated with quality, they can mislead when the environment changes faster than the cue preferences can evolve.

**Definition 26.1.1 (Ecological Trap)** An **ecological trap** is a situation in which animals prefer, or settle in, a habitat in which their fitness is lower than in available alternatives, because a cue they use in choosing habitat no longer predicts the quality of the habitat (Schlaepfer, Runge and Sherman 2002).

Many aquatic insects find water by the horizontally polarized light reflected from its surface; they are attracted even more strongly to dry asphalt roads, dark car bodies and glass, which reflect light that is more strongly polarized than water, and mayflies lay their eggs on them by the thousands (Kriska and colleagues 1998; Horváth and colleagues 2009). Birds settle in agricultural fields with the vegetation structure of the grasslands they evolved in and lose their nests to mowing; turtle hatchlings crawl toward artificial lights rather than the sea. Ecological traps are a concern for conservation because they can drive a population down even where good habitat remains: the animals choose the bad habitat.

## 26.2. Home Range, Core Area and Territory

Animals use space in three overlapping ways.

**Definition 26.2.1 (Home Range and Core Area)** The **home range** of an animal is the area it traverses in its normal activities of food gathering, mating and caring for young during a specified period; occasional excursions outside it are not included (Burt 1943). The **core area** is the part of the home range that is used most intensively, often containing the den, nest or main feeding sites.

**Definition 26.2.2 (Territory)** A **territory** is an area, or the resources within it, that an animal or a group defends against conspecifics, so that the owner has exclusive or priority access to it.

The home ranges of neighbors may overlap broadly, but territories, by definition, overlap little. Home ranges are estimated from the positions of animals obtained by direct observation, radio-tracking or, increasingly, satellite tags. The simplest estimate is the *minimum convex polygon*, the smallest convex polygon containing all the fixes; it is easy to compute but sensitive to outlying fixes and to the number of fixes, and it includes unused areas. *Kernel density* estimates place a smooth probability density around each fix and define the home range as the smallest area containing a given fraction (usually 95%) of the density and the core area as that containing 50%; more recent methods model the movement process itself (for example, as a random walk with attraction to a home site, the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process), so that autocorrelated fixes are not treated as independent.

### 26.2.1. Types of Territories

Territories differ in what they contain and how long they are held. A classical scheme, based on birds, distinguishes:

| Type | Contents | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| All-purpose | Mating, nesting and most of the food for the breeding season | Many songbirds (great tit, European robin) |
| Mating and nesting, food elsewhere | Nest site and mate; food taken outside | Red-winged blackbird (partly) |
| Nest site only | A small area around the nest | Colonial seabirds, gulls |
| Mating only | A small display site visited by females | Lekking birds, mammals and insects (**lek**, [7.10.13](/7.10.13)) |
| Feeding only | Food outside the breeding season | Winter territories of pied wagtails, nectar territories of sunbirds and hummingbirds |
| Roosting or shelter | A refuge | Burrows of fiddler crabs, crevices of reef fishes |

Territories may be held by individuals, pairs or groups (lion prides, chimpanzee communities, wolf packs, groups of cooperatively breeding birds), and for anything from hours (a sunspot defended by a butterfly) to a lifetime. Size varies with the resources they hold: other things being equal, territories that supply all of an animal's food are larger than those supplying only a nest, territories in poor habitat are larger than in rich habitat, and predators hold larger territories than herbivores of the same body size, because their food is sparser.

### 26.2.2. Advertising Ownership

Territories are advertised and defended by a graded series of behaviors, from long-range signals through threat displays at the boundary to chases and fights. Advertisement is usually cheaper than fighting and serves to keep intruders out without contact. Birds, frogs and many insects use song and calls, whose roles in territorial defense have been shown by removal and playback experiments: in great tits, territories from which the owners had been removed were reoccupied more slowly when loudspeakers played their song than when they played a control sound (Krebs 1977). Many mammals mark territories with scent from glands, urine or feces, often at boundaries or along paths. Scent marks persist after the owner has left, so they advertise ownership cheaply over large areas; Morris Gosling (1982) proposed that they allow an intruder to recognize the owner, by matching the odor of the marks to the odor of the animal it meets, and so to identify an opponent that is likely to escalate. Visual signals include the conspicuous displays of lizards, fiddler crabs and reef fishes, and electric fish use electric signals.

### 26.2.3. Interspecific Territoriality

Territories are usually defended against conspecifics, which compete for the same food, mates and nest sites. But many animals also defend territories against other species: yellow-headed blackbirds exclude red-winged blackbirds from the deepest parts of marshes (Orians and Willson 1964), and in many reef fishes, damselflies and birds, territory holders chase heterospecifics that use the same food or are mistaken for rivals. In a survey, interspecific territoriality was found in many families of birds, fishes and insects; it is most common between closely related species that overlap in resources and resemble each other in appearance or signals (Grether and colleagues 2009). It may persist because the species compete for resources, or because the species are too similar for the territory holders to distinguish them from conspecific rivals; the two explanations predict different evolutionary outcomes (divergence or convergence of signals) and are distinguished by experiments in which territory holders are presented with models of the other species.

## 26.3. The Economics of Territoriality

### 26.3.1. Economic Defensibility

Defense brings exclusive access to resources but costs time, energy and risk. Jerram Brown (1964) proposed that territorial behavior should be favored whenever its benefits exceed its costs.

**Definition 26.3.1 (Economic Defensibility)** A resource is **economically defensible** if the benefit an animal gains by excluding competitors from it exceeds the cost of defending it, measured in the same currency (such as energy or survival); territoriality is expected only for economically defensible resources (Brown 1964).

The idea predicts a range of resource levels within which defense pays. If resources are scarce, the extra gain from excluding others may not pay for the cost of defense, and the animal should abandon the area or forage without defending it. If resources are very abundant, the owner cannot use the extra resources that defense makes available, or so many intruders are attracted that defense becomes too costly. The size of territory that maximizes the net benefit depends on the shapes of the benefit and cost curves and need not change in a simple way with food density.

Defense is economical where benefit exceeds cost (shaded), and net benefit is greatest where the slopes of the two curves are equal (dots); with these curves the optimal territory is smaller in the rich habitat. Frank Gill and Larry Wolf (1975) tested the idea on golden-winged sunbirds (*Drepanorhynchus reichenowi*) that defend patches of *Leonotis* flowers in East Africa outside the breeding season. They measured the energy costs of foraging, perching and chasing in the laboratory and the time budgets of territorial birds in the field. A bird that excludes other nectar feeders raises the amount of nectar in its flowers and needs less time to collect its daily requirement: at 2 microliters per flower it must forage 4 hours a day, at 3 microliters only 2.7 hours. Raising the nectar from 2 to 3 microliters by defense saves 1.3 hours of foraging, which the bird spends perched, a net saving of $(1000 - 400) \times 1.3 = 780$ cal per day; defense takes about 0.28 hours per day, costing $(3000 - 400) \times 0.28 = 728$ cal. The birds made a small energetic profit by defending, and most were territorial when the flowers were economically defensible. When nectar was already abundant, the extra nectar saved little foraging time, because intake was limited by the time needed to probe each flower, and defense did not pay.

Pied wagtails (*Motacilla alba*) on the River Thames near Oxford defend winter feeding territories along the riverbank, where they eat insects washed ashore. The owner walks around its territory systematically, returning to each stretch about every 40 minutes, which allows the insects to renew. Sometimes an owner tolerates a second bird, a satellite, which reduces the return time to 20 minutes and so the owner's feeding rate, but helps to chase away intruders. Nicholas Davies and Alasdair Houston (1981) calculated the owner's feeding rate with and without a satellite from the food renewal rate and the intrusion rate on each day and predicted correctly on 34 of 40 days whether the owner would accept or evict the satellite. The study shows how different costs and benefits (time spent in defense, food lost to sharing) can be reduced to one currency, and it is an elementary case of the benefits of group living ([7.18.27](/7.18.27)).

Territory size is often adjusted to resources. Rufous hummingbirds (*Selasphorus rufus*) defending flowers on their migration stopovers, weighed on perches that were also balances, changed the size of their territories from day to day in ways that tended to maximize their daily weight gain (Carpenter, Paton and Hixon 1983). Sanderlings (*Calidris alba*) on Californian beaches defended feeding territories at intermediate densities of their crustacean prey, and abandoned defense when prey were scarce or when the number of intruding birds was very high (Myers, Connors and Pitelka 1979), as the economic argument predicts.

### 26.3.2. Costs and Benefits in the Field

The costs of territoriality can be measured by manipulating it. Catherine Marler and Michael Moore (1989, 1991) implanted testosterone into male Yarrow's spiny lizards (*Sceloporus jarrovi*) in summer, a season when they are weakly territorial. The implanted males patrolled more, performed more push-up displays, spent less time feeding and expended about a third more energy than control males, and they disappeared from the population faster; implanted males given extra food (mealworms) survived as well as controls, so the cost was energetic. In this species, territoriality is seasonal: males tolerate each other in early summer and become highly aggressive in autumn, when females are receptive ([7.18.19](/7.18.19) on hormones and aggression).

The benefits are measured as gains in survival or reproduction. In arctic ground squirrels (*Urocitellus parryii*), females mate with several males, but the first male to mate fertilizes almost all the eggs; territorial males mated first with females living on their territories in 20 of 28 cases (Lacey and Wieczorek 2001). Male pseudoscorpions (*Cordylochernes scorpioides*) disperse between dead trees under the wing covers of harlequin beetles, and large males defend a beetle's back, an area small enough to be guarded cheaply, against other males, gaining access to the females that travel with them (Zeh 1997). In chimpanzees (*Pan troglodytes*) at Ngogo in Uganda, males patrolling the boundary of their community's territory killed 21 members of a neighboring community over ten years, and then expanded their territory by about a fifth into the area of their victims (Mitani, Watts and Amsler 2010).

Territories held in winter affect success in the next breeding season. In American redstarts (*Setophaga ruticilla*) wintering in Jamaica, older males hold territories in black mangrove forest and exclude females and young males, which are forced into dry second-growth scrub. Birds in mangroves kept their body mass over the winter, departed earlier for their breeding grounds and, as shown by the carbon isotope ratios in their blood, which reflect the winter habitat, arrived earlier on the breeding grounds in New Hampshire and raised more young (Marra, Hobson and Holmes 1998; Norris and colleagues 2004). Such carry-over effects link the seasons of a migratory life cycle ([7.18.20](/7.18.20)).

## 26.4. Contests: Resource-Holding Potential and Assessment

### 26.4.1. Why Do Intruders Give Up Quickly?

Owning a good territory raises reproductive success, yet in many species an intruder gives up within seconds after a challenge, rather than fighting to take over. Three non-exclusive hypotheses explain why owners usually win.

1. *The arbitrary rule hypothesis*: ownership settles the contest by convention, as in the bourgeois strategy ([Definition 25.2.1](/7.18.25#def-25.2.1)).
2. *The resource-holding potential hypothesis*: owners win because they are better fighters.
3. *The payoff asymmetry hypothesis*: owners win because they value the territory more than intruders do, and so are prepared to fight harder.

**Definition 26.4.1 (Resource-Holding Potential)** The **resource-holding potential** (RHP) of an animal is its absolute ability to win a fight, determined by its size, strength, weapons, energy reserves, skill and experience (Parker 1974).

RHP is distinguished from *resource value*, the fitness gain that winning the resource would bring, which determines how much an individual should be prepared to pay to win, and from *aggressiveness* or motivation, which may depend on both. The arbitrary rule hypothesis was favored after Davies's (1978) experiments with speckled wood butterflies but was rejected for that species by the replication of Kemp and Wiklund (2003), described in [7.18.25](/7.18.25#sec-25.2.1); it has since found little support in any species.

Evidence for RHP comes from species in which territory owners are larger or stronger than nonterritorial males, as in rhinoceroses, fiddler crabs and many insects. RHP is not always body size. Male red-shouldered widowbirds (*Euplectes axillaris*) with larger and redder shoulder patches are more likely to hold territories than "floaters" of the same size. When residents and floaters were captured and paired in a cage unfamiliar to both, the former residents almost always dominated the floaters, even when their red shoulders had been painted black (Pryke and Andersson 2003): the patch advertises some quality that is expressed in fights even without the signal. In black-winged damselflies (*Calopteryx maculata*), territorial contests are long aerial chases without contact; the winners of territorial takeovers did not have heavier thoraxes but almost always had higher fat reserves than the losers (Marden and Waage 1990).

Some winners are neither larger nor stronger. Old male eggfly butterflies (*Hypolimnas bolina*), with ragged wings, persisted longer in aerial clashes with young rivals as they grew older (Kemp 2002), and older male song sparrows respond more intensely to simulated intrusions than younger males. An old animal has less future reproduction to lose, so it should value the current resource more relative to its future, a payoff asymmetry. Replacement experiments test the payoff asymmetry hypothesis directly. If a resident is removed, a replacement claims its territory, and the original resident is released after an interval, the payoff asymmetry hypothesis predicts that the longer the replacement has held the territory, the more it values it and the more likely it is to win. In red-winged blackbirds (Beletsky and Orians 1989) and in tarantula hawk wasps (*Hemipepsis ustulata*), this is what happens: a wasp released after a few minutes evicts its replacement in less than three minutes, but one released after an hour must fight long contests of ascending flights, lasting 25 minutes on average, which the replacement usually wins (Alcock and Bailey 1997). Captivity might also reduce the RHP of the removed residents; Joseph Tobias (1997) separated the two effects in European robins (*Erithacus rubecula*) by matching residents held captive for ten days against replacements that had held their territories for one day or for ten days. The former residents always won against one-day replacements and always lost against ten-day replacements: the outcome depended on the replacement's tenure, as the payoff asymmetry hypothesis predicts, not on the resident's time in captivity. One reason a resident values its territory is that it has settled its boundaries with its neighbors (Section 26.5).

### 26.4.2. Assessment Models

How do contestants decide when to give up? The war of attrition of [7.18.25](/7.18.25#sec-25.2.3) assumes that they choose a persistence time in advance and gain no information during the contest. Three later models make different assumptions about what contestants assess (Arnott and Elwood 2009).

**Algorithm 26.4.1 (Sequential Assessment Model)** (Enquist and Leimar 1983) Two contestants with unknown relative RHP repeatedly perform a display or fighting behavior that gives each a noisy sample of the difference in RHP between them. Each maintains a running estimate of its relative RHP and its uncertainty, and gives up when the estimate crosses a giving-up threshold that narrows as the number of samples increases. Contests may proceed through phases of increasingly costly and informative behaviors.

:::

Under *mutual assessment* of this kind, contests between closely matched opponents last longest, because more samples are needed to distinguish a small difference, and contests over more valuable resources last longer. In the cichlid *Nannacara anomala*, fights proceed from lateral display and tail beating to biting, mouth wrestling and circling; the larger fish nearly always won, and the duration and its variability increased as the contestants became more similar in weight (Enquist and colleagues 1990). Contests between red deer stags (*Cervus elaphus*) proceed through roaring contests, in which stags match each other's roaring rate, a demanding display that reflects condition, then a parallel walk, and only if these fail to settle the contest a pushing fight with locked antlers (Clutton-Brock and Albon 1979).

The *energetic war of attrition* (Payne and Pagel 1996) assumes *self-assessment*: each contestant persists until its own costs reach a threshold set by its own condition, without assessing the opponent; the contestant in better condition wins because it can keep going longer. Contest duration should then increase with the RHP of the loser, and contests between two strong opponents should last longer than between two weak ones. The aerial chases of *Calopteryx* damselflies fit this model: the fatter male nearly always wins, and the duration of a contest increases with the fat content of the loser.

The *cumulative assessment model* (Payne 1998) is also a self-assessment model, but for contests in which the opponents inflict costs on each other: each gives up when the accumulated damage reaches its threshold. Duration should increase with the RHP of the loser and decrease with the RHP of the winner, which inflicts costs faster. In house crickets (*Acheta domesticus*), which escalate from antennal fencing and mandible spreading to wrestling, duration was positively correlated with the loser's weight and negatively with the winner's, more strongly than with the weight difference (Briffa 2008).

| | Sequential assessment | Energetic war of attrition | Cumulative assessment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Decision based on | Estimated RHP relative to the opponent | Own accumulated costs | Own costs, including those inflicted by the opponent |
| Duration correlated with | Difference in RHP (negatively) | Loser's RHP (positively) | Loser's RHP (positively), winner's RHP (negatively) |
| Typical behavior | Ritualized displays, index signals | Matched noninjurious displays (chases, spiral flights) | Escalating, potentially injurious fights |

Distinguishing the models is harder than it seems. Under self-assessment, large differences in RHP usually involve a weak loser, who gives up quickly, so the duration also decreases with the RHP difference, mimicking mutual assessment (Taylor and Elwood 2003). The correct test examines the correlations of duration with the RHP of the winner and the loser separately. Studies that did so have found evidence for each model in different species, and for switches between them during a contest: in some spiders and crabs, opponents appear to assess each other during the early displays and to rely on self-assessment during the escalated phase.

### 26.4.3. Experience and Dominance

The outcome of a contest also depends on past contests. Animals that have recently won are more likely to win their next contest, and those that have lost are more likely to lose, even against new opponents (*winner and loser effects*); these effects are partly mediated by hormones, such as the rise of androgens after a victory, and they can by themselves generate linear dominance hierarchies in groups of initially equal animals (Hsu, Earley and Wolf 2006). In stable groups, animals recognize each other and the outcomes of earlier contests, and fights are replaced by brief threats and submissive signals; a dominance hierarchy is a stored record of past contests that saves the costs of fighting each time. The signals used in contests, from the reliable signals of fighting ability to signals of aggressive motivation, are treated in [7.18.39](/7.18.39).

## 26.5. Dear Enemies

Once territorial boundaries are settled, neighbors usually stop fighting over them and respond less aggressively to each other than to strangers.

**Definition 26.5.1 (Dear Enemy Effect)** The **dear enemy effect** is the reduced aggression of a territory owner toward established neighbors compared with unfamiliar individuals (Fisher 1954).

The effect is expected because a neighbor, with a territory of its own, threatens the owner less than a stranger looking for a territory, and because renegotiating boundaries with a familiar neighbor is wasteful (Temeles 1994). Territorial male Broadley's flat lizards (*Platysaurus broadleyi*) charge an unfamiliar intruder when it is still far away, but let familiar neighbors approach closely, and chase a stranger on average about 1.5 m but a neighbor only a few centimeters (Whiting 1999). Neighbor recognition requires individual recognition, which in birds is based on song ([7.18.17](/7.18.17)). In playback experiments with white-throated sparrows (*Zonotrichia albicollis*), territory owners responded weakly to the song of a neighbor played from the boundary they shared with it, but strongly to the same song played from the opposite boundary, and to the song of a stranger (Falls and Brooks 1975): the birds recognized not only who was singing but whether he was where he belonged. The reduced response saves energy and also physiological costs: in a cichlid fish, residents challenged by a familiar neighbor showed a smaller rise in androgens than residents challenged by a stranger (Aires and colleagues 2015).

The effect is not universal. In species in which neighbors are the main competitors for territory or mates, neighbors may be treated more aggressively than strangers, the *nasty neighbor effect*, as in banded mongooses (*Mungos mungo*), whose groups react more strongly to the scent of neighboring groups than to that of strangers (Müller and Manser 2007). The relative threat posed by neighbors and strangers, not familiarity alone, predicts the response.

The dear enemy effect has practical value. Translocations of the endangered Stephens' kangaroo rat (*Dipodomys stephensi*) in California had repeatedly failed. When Debra Shier and Ronald Swaisgood (2011) moved 99 animals to new reserves, keeping some with their former neighbors and mixing others with unfamiliar animals, the groups of familiar neighbors fought less, survived better and produced more offspring.

## 26.6. Dispersal

**Dispersal**, defined in [7.10.10](/7.10.10#def-10.2.1) as the movement of individuals from their place of birth to the place where they reproduce, is a behavioral decision with consequences for the individual and for the genetic structure of the population.

### 26.6.1. Natal and Breeding Dispersal

**Definition 26.6.1 (Natal Dispersal, Breeding Dispersal and Philopatry)** **Natal dispersal** is the movement of an individual from its birthplace to the site where it first breeds; **breeding dispersal** is movement between successive breeding sites. **Philopatry** is the tendency of an individual to remain at, or return to, its birthplace (or, for breeding philopatry, a previous breeding site).

Dispersal is distinguished from migration, a regular return movement between areas ([7.18.20](/7.18.20)), and from the daily movements within a home range. It is costly. The costs are energetic (the energy spent in moving), risk costs (predation and other mortality during travel through unfamiliar areas), time costs and opportunity costs (the loss of familiarity with a known area and of the benefits of staying) (Bonte and colleagues 2012). Ruffed grouse (*Bonasa umbellus*) fitted with radio transmitters ran at least three times the risk of being killed by a predator when they were in unfamiliar areas than when they were in familiar ones (Yoder, Marschall and Swanson 2004), and survival during dispersal is low in birds, mammals, fishes and insects alike. Why, then, do so many animals leave home?

### 26.6.2. Why Disperse?

Three main benefits have been proposed, which may act together.

*Avoiding inbreeding.* An individual that stays near its birthplace is likely to mate with relatives, and the offspring of close relatives suffer **inbreeding depression**. In laboratory populations of oldfield mice (*Peromyscus polionotus*), inbred females took longer to produce their first litter than outbred females (Margulis and Altmann 1997). Inbreeding can be avoided by recognizing and rejecting relatives as mates (kin recognition, [7.18.33](/7.18.33)), and in many species females prefer unfamiliar males: in spotted hyenas (*Crocuta crocuta*), females prefer males that have immigrated from other clans, so most young males leave their natal clan (Höner and colleagues 2007).

*Avoiding competition for local resources and mates.* Where resources near the birthplace are limited and occupied by parents and older siblings, young animals that move find vacancies more easily.

*Avoiding competition with relatives.* Even in a stable, uniform habitat where every site is as good as every other, offspring that stay at home compete with their siblings and their parent, and an individual that sends some of its offspring away reduces this competition among relatives. William Hamilton and Robert May (1977) showed that this alone selects for a surprisingly high rate of dispersal.

**Theorem 26.6.1 (Hamilton–May)** Consider a habitat of many sites, each holding one asexual adult. Each adult produces a large number of offspring and dies; a fraction $v$ of the offspring disperse to random sites and survive the journey with probability $s$, the rest stay at the natal site; each site is then taken by one offspring chosen at random from those present. The ESS dispersal fraction is

$$
v^* = \frac{1}{2 - s}, \tag{26.1}
$$
so that at least half of the offspring disperse however low the survival of dispersers.

**Proof** Let the population disperse a fraction $v$, and consider a rare mutant dispersing $v'$. At its own site, the mutant's offspring that stay, in proportion $1 - v'$, compete with immigrants arriving at a rate proportional to $sv$ (from the many other sites), and win the site with probability $(1 - v')/(1 - v' + sv)$. Its dispersing offspring, $sv'$ of them in total, arrive at sites where the competitors number $1 - v + sv$ (residents' stayers plus immigrants), and win $sv'/(1 - v + sv)$ sites in expectation. The mutant's fitness is therefore

$$
W(v', v) = \frac{1 - v'}{1 - v' + sv} + \frac{sv'}{1 - v + sv}.
$$
The first term is concave in $v'$, so $W$ is maximized where $\partial W/\partial v' = 0$. At $v' = v$, $\partial W/\partial v' = -sv/(1 - v + sv)^2 + s/(1 - v + sv)$, which vanishes when $1 - v + sv = v$, that is $v = 1/(2 - s)$. At this value the residents' own dispersal fraction is the best reply to itself, a strict Nash equilibrium and hence an ESS ([Proposition 25.1.1](/7.18.25#prop-25.1.1)). $\square$

The result is counterintuitive: even if 90% of dispersers die, an adult should send more than half of its offspring away, because an offspring that stays can at most replace its parent, and the more siblings stay, the more of them are wasted in competition for that one site. The model ignores many real features (sexual reproduction, variable habitat quality, finite numbers of offspring), and later models showed that kin competition favors dispersal more weakly when it is diluted by these factors; but it established that dispersal can evolve without any differences between sites, as an adaptation against competition among relatives.

The three benefits can be distinguished by their predictions. Inbreeding avoidance predicts that one sex disperses and the other stays (dispersal by either sex is enough to separate relatives), and that individuals stay where no relatives of the opposite sex are present. Kin competition predicts that dispersal increases with the relatedness of local competitors and with the density of relatives. In common lizards (*Zootoca vivipara*), juveniles dispersed more when their mother was present and in good condition and when they were born in large litters, consistent with kin competition (Léna and colleagues 1998).

### 26.6.3. Sex-Biased Dispersal

In most animals, one sex disperses farther or more often than the other. In Belding's ground squirrels (*Urocitellus beldingi*), young males travel on average about 250 m from their natal burrow before settling, young females only about 50 m (Holekamp 1984). Paul Greenwood (1980) noted that the direction of the bias differs between the two best-studied classes:

| | Mammals | Birds |
|---|---|---|
| Usual dispersing sex | Males | Females |
| Usual mating system | Polygynous, males compete for females | Socially monogamous |
| Which sex benefits from local familiarity | Females (resources for rearing young; help from kin) | Males (a territory that attracts females) |
| Exceptions | Chimpanzees, bonobos, spider monkeys, some bats (female-biased) | Many ducks and geese (male-biased; pairs form in winter) |

Greenwood explained the pattern by the *resource defense* versus *mate defense* mating systems: in birds, males defend territories that provide the resources females need, so males gain more by staying where they know the area and females disperse; in mammals, males compete for access to females rather than resources, so males gain little by staying, and females, which rear young with local resources and often with the help of female kin, stay. Inbreeding avoidance then requires only that one sex disperse. An alternative explanation is based on sex chromosomes: in both groups, the homogametic sex (male birds, ZZ; female mammals, XX) tends to stay. A comparative analysis of more than 250 species supported Greenwood's link between mating system and dispersal in mammals, polygynous species having male-biased dispersal, but found no relation with the mating system in birds, where sex-biased dispersal was instead associated with parental care and sexual dimorphism (Trochet and colleagues 2016). The contrast remains incompletely explained. Its consequences are clear: in most mammals, female group members are related and males are immigrants, which favors social bonds and cooperation among females ([7.18.35](/7.18.35)); in chimpanzees, with female-biased dispersal, the males of a community are more likely to be related and cooperate in territorial defense.

### 26.6.4. Who Disperses?

Dispersal is not a fixed trait. It is often condition-dependent: individuals in good condition, or born early in the season, may be more likely to disperse (and to survive dispersal), and dispersal decisions respond to density, the presence of relatives, habitat quality and the success of others (*informed dispersal*; Clobert and colleagues 2009). Individuals that disperse also differ from those that stay in morphology, physiology and behavior, a combination called a *dispersal syndrome*. Many insects are wing-dimorphic, with long-winged dispersers that delay reproduction and short-winged or wingless residents that reproduce earlier, a **trade-off** between dispersal and reproduction. Personality is involved too: in invasive mosquitofish (*Gambusia affinis*), less social individuals dispersed farther, and populations at the expanding front of an invasion were enriched with them (Cote and colleagues 2010), one of the consequences of consistent individual differences discussed in [7.18.25](/7.18.25#sec-25.5).

## Summary

- Habitat selection follows ideal free and despotic models: density-dependent settlement can equalize success between preferred and second-ranked habitats (blackcaps, red-winged blackbird females). Animals choose by structural cues, early experience, conspecific presence and public information (collared flycatchers); when cues become decoupled from quality, they fall into ecological traps (Definition 26.1.1), as insects laying eggs on asphalt.
- Home range, core area (Definition 26.2.1) and territory (Definition 26.2.2) differ in the degree of defense; home ranges are estimated by minimum convex polygons or kernel densities. Territories differ in content (all-purpose, nesting, mating, feeding), are advertised by song, scent and displays, and are sometimes defended against other species.
- A resource is defended if it is economically defensible (Definition 26.3.1): sunbirds defend nectar when the energy saved exceeds 728 cal per day; wagtails accept satellites when food renewal and intrusion rates are high; hummingbirds and sanderlings adjust territory size or defense to food. Testosterone-implanted lizards show the costs; ground squirrels, pseudoscorpions, chimpanzees and redstarts show the benefits.
- Owners win because of arbitrary rules (little support), higher resource-holding potential (Definition 26.4.1; widowbirds, damselflies) or payoff asymmetries (wasps, robins, old butterflies). Contests follow mutual assessment (Algorithm 26.4.1; cichlids, red deer) or self-assessment (energetic war of attrition in damselflies; cumulative assessment in crickets); the models are distinguished by the correlations of duration with loser and winner RHP. Winner and loser effects and hierarchies store past outcomes.
- Neighbors are treated as dear enemies (Definition 26.5.1; flat lizards, sparrow song playback) unless they are the main threat (nasty neighbors in banded mongooses).
- Natal and breeding dispersal and philopatry (Definition 26.6.1): dispersal is costly (grouse) but avoids inbreeding, local competition and competition with kin; with kin competition alone, at least half of the offspring should disperse (Theorem 26.6.1, Eq. (26.1)). Mammals usually have male-biased, birds female-biased dispersal (Greenwood); dispersal is condition-dependent and linked to dispersal syndromes and personality.

## Questions

1. Using the numbers in Section 26.3.1, calculate whether a golden-winged sunbird should defend its territory if defense raised the nectar level from 1 to 2 microliters, and from 3 to 4 microliters. What do the results imply about the range of resource levels at which territoriality is expected?

   **Answer** With foraging time $8/n$ hours, raising nectar from 1 to 2 microliters saves $8 - 4 = 4$ hours, worth $600 \times 4 = 2400$ cal, far more than the 728 cal cost of defense, so defense pays. From 3 to 4 microliters it saves $2.67 - 2 = 0.67$ hours, worth 400 cal, less than the cost, so defense does not pay. Defense pays below about 2.1 microliters per flower (where $4800/[n(n+1)] = 728$). The model gives only the upper limit; a lower limit arises when nectar is so scarce that even a defended territory cannot supply the bird's needs, or when the gain per unit area is too small to pay for defending an area large enough, and the bird should then abandon the area. Territoriality is therefore expected at intermediate resource levels.
2. Design a replacement experiment that distinguishes the resource-holding potential and payoff asymmetry hypotheses for why territory owners win, and state the predictions of each.

   **Answer** Remove residents and hold them in captivity under good conditions; allow replacements to settle; release the residents after varying combinations of captivity time and replacement tenure (as in Tobias's robins: residents held 1 or 10 days, replacements in place 1 or 10 days, in a factorial design), and record who wins. The RHP hypothesis, if captivity reduces fighting ability, predicts that the outcome depends on how long the resident was held, and, in general, that the winner can be predicted from measures of fighting ability (size, condition) independently of tenure. The payoff asymmetry hypothesis predicts that the probability that the replacement wins increases with its tenure, independently of the resident's captivity time, and that contests are longer and more escalated when both contestants have had long tenures. Measuring the condition of both contestants before release allows both effects to be estimated together.
3. Explain why a negative correlation between contest duration and the difference in size between opponents does not show that contestants assess each other. What analysis would?

   **Answer** Under self-assessment, the duration is set by the loser's own threshold, which increases with its size. Contests with a large size difference usually have a small loser (because a large difference requires one small contestant), so they are short, and a negative correlation with the difference appears without any assessment of the opponent. To test mutual assessment, analyze duration as a function of the winner's and the loser's sizes separately (or in a multiple regression): mutual assessment predicts effects of equal magnitude and opposite sign (duration depends on the difference), pure self-assessment predicts a positive effect of loser size and no effect of winner size, and cumulative assessment a positive effect of loser size and a weaker negative effect of winner size. Contests between size-matched pairs of different absolute sizes give a further test: under self-assessment, matched large pairs fight longer than matched small pairs; under mutual assessment, they do not.
4. In the Hamilton–May model, show that if dispersers always survive ($s = 1$), the ESS is $v^* = 1$, and explain why the ESS does not fall below $1/2$ as $s \to 0$.

   **Answer** Substituting $s = 1$ into Eq. (26.1) gives $v^* = 1$: if dispersal is free, an offspring that disperses competes at another site on equal terms, whereas one that stays competes with its siblings, so all should disperse (any site is as good, and competing against non-relatives does not reduce one's own inclusive success). As $s \to 0$, $v^* \to 1/2$. One might expect almost no dispersal when dispersers almost always die, but compare the marginal value of the two kinds of offspring. When few offspring disperse, the natal site is contested almost only by the parent's own stayers; it is already almost certainly won by the family, so one more stayer adds almost nothing (its marginal value is $sv/(1 - v + sv)^2$, small when $v$ is small). A disperser rarely survives, but if it does it competes at a site where few immigrants arrive and wins with high probability (its value is $s/(1 - v + sv)$). The two values are equal when $1 - v + sv = v$; for $s \to 0$ this requires $1 - v = v$: competition among stayers wastes offspring until as many disperse as stay.
6. (Open question) Conservation managers plan to translocate a territorial songbird to a restored habitat. Using the concepts of this chapter, list the behavioral factors that could make the translocation fail, and suggest measures to prevent failure.

   **Answer** Factors: (1) Habitat selection: the birds may not recognize the restored habitat as suitable if structural cues are missing or if conspecific cues are absent; they may leave or settle in nearby poorer habitat (an ecological trap if the restored site lacks a cue they need). Playback of song or decoys can provide conspecific cues. (2) Social structure: without established neighbors, birds will fight more to establish boundaries (no dear enemies), at an energetic cost; translocating groups of former neighbors together, as with Stephens' kangaroo rats, can reduce aggression. (3) Dispersal after release: translocated animals often disperse far from the release site; soft release (holding them in aviaries at the site for some time) and releasing young birds before natal dispersal can increase settlement. (4) Despotic exclusion: if the site is already partly occupied, dominants may exclude newcomers to poor areas. (5) Sex ratio and mate availability: if one sex disperses more, translocated populations may lose that sex. Monitoring the settlement and breeding success of released individuals is necessary to identify which factor operates.
