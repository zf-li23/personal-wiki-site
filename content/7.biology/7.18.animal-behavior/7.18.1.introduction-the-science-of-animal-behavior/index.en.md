A honey bee that stings an intruder at the hive dies within minutes, because the barbed sting and the attached venom gland are torn from her abdomen. A male digger bee spends his mornings excavating holes in the hard desert floor, and a male langur monkey that has just taken over a group attacks the infants of the females he has come to live with. Animal behavior is full of such actions, which are at first sight puzzling: why would an individual do something that harms itself or seems to harm its own interests? Answering questions of this kind requires two kinds of explanation. One describes how the behavior is produced (which stimuli trigger it, which neurons and hormones control it, how it develops), the other explains why animals that behave in this way have left more descendants than animals that did not.

This chapter introduces the science that seeks both explanations. We first ask what behavior is and why it is worth studying, then follow the history of the discipline: the naturalists, Darwin, the comparative psychologists and behaviorists who studied learning in the laboratory, the ethologists who studied instinct in the field, and the later rise of sociobiology, behavioral ecology and neuroethology. We then present the conceptual framework that organizes the whole course, Tinbergen's four questions and the distinction between **proximate cause** and **ultimate cause** introduced in [7.10.1](/7.10.1#sec-1.5.1), and the logic by which **natural selection** shapes behavior. The chapter ends with an integrative case study, mobbing by gulls, that shows the observational, experimental and comparative approaches at work. Methods are treated in more detail in [7.18.2](/7.18.2), the classical ethological concepts in [7.18.3](/7.18.3), and the genetic and developmental bases of behavior in [7.18.4](/7.18.4) and [7.18.5](/7.18.5).

## 1.1. What Is Behavior?

### 1.1.1. Definitions

Everyone recognizes behavior when they see it, but it is surprisingly hard to define. The *Oxford English Dictionary* lists a range of meanings, from "deportment, manners, moral conduct" to "the way in which a machine, substance, etc. acts or works" and "response to stimulus". The psychologists B. F. Skinner and D. O. Hebb defined behavior as all observable processes by which an animal responds to perceived changes in the internal state of its body or in the external world. When Daniel Levitis and colleagues asked about 175 behavioral biologists at scientific meetings to classify examples as behavior or not, they found no consensus, and proposed the following definition (Levitis et al. 2009).

**Definition 1.1.1 (Behavior)** *Behavior* is the internally coordinated response (action or inaction) of a whole living organism, an individual or a group, to internal or external stimuli, excluding responses more easily understood as developmental changes.

Each clause of the definition excludes something. A response must be coordinated by the organism, so being blown off a branch by the wind is not behavior, but clinging to the branch is. Inaction can be behavior: a hare that freezes when a hawk passes overhead is responding to a stimulus as surely as one that runs. The response is that of a whole organism, so the secretion of a hormone by a gland is physiology, not behavior, although it may cause behavior. Groups can behave: a honey bee swarm choosing a new nest site ([7.18.27](/7.18.27)) or a flock of starlings turning in the air acts as a unit. Finally, slow changes such as growth or metamorphosis are development rather than behavior, although the boundary is not sharp (is the seasonal molt of a bird into breeding plumage behavior?).

A second, complementary definition emphasizes interactions: behavior is the way an individual acts or interacts with other organisms and with its environment. The two definitions reflect two traditions in the field. The first looks inward, at the mechanisms that coordinate the response; the second looks outward, at the consequences of the response for survival and reproduction. As we shall see in §1.3, a complete understanding of any behavior needs both views.

### 1.1.2. Why Study Animal Behavior?

People have watched animals for as long as there have been people. The oldest known figurative paintings, on cave walls in Sulawesi, depict wild pigs and are more than 45,000 years old; the paintings of Lascaux and Chauvet in France show horses, bison and lions with an accuracy of posture and movement that only careful observers could achieve. Animals fill the myths and art of every culture, from the deer king of the Buddhist *jātaka* tales painted in the Mogao caves at Dunhuang to the insect essays of Jean-Henri Fabre, who described how a cicada spends four years underground before a few weeks of song in the sun.

Three reasons for studying behavior can be distinguished.

1. *Intrinsic interest.* Behavior is the most conspicuous and varied expression of life. In the words of E. O. Wilson (1929–2021), animal behavior "is a fundamental and extraordinarily interesting subject in its own right".
2. *A foundation for other disciplines.* Wilson continued that behavior "is also basic to other disciplines of biology, all the way from neuroscience and behavioral genetics to ecology and conservation biology". Behavior is where the nervous system meets the environment, and it is the level at which natural selection acts on most traits of animals.
3. *Practical value.* Humans have always depended on knowledge of animal behavior, first as hunters and later as herders and farmers. Today, behavior matters for the welfare of domestic, laboratory and zoo animals, for the control of pests and disease vectors (mosquitoes find hosts by their odor and heat), for conservation (reintroduced animals must be able to find food and avoid predators), and for biomedical research, in which mice, rats, zebrafish and flies serve as models of human behavior and its disorders.

A personal attachment to animals also drives much research. Jane Goodall's studies of chimpanzees at Gombe, Tanzania, which began in 1960, depended on the animals gradually learning to tolerate her presence; only then could she observe at close range the tool use, hunting and social relationships that changed our view of our closest relatives.

## 1.2. A Short History of the Study of Behavior

### 1.2.1. Early Naturalists

The systematic study of behavior begins with Aristotle (384–322 BCE), whose *History of Animals* records the habits of hundreds of species, among them the cuckoo's habit of laying its eggs in the nests of other birds ([7.18.32](/7.18.32)) and partnerships between species, such as the small bird said to pick food from the teeth of the Nile crocodile. In Rome, the physician Galen (c. 130–c. 210 CE), who treated gladiators and dissected many animals, showed that cutting nerves or the spinal cord abolished movement and sensation in the parts they supplied, and so located the control of behavior in the brain and nerves rather than the heart. For more than a millennium afterward, however, animals were regarded in the European tradition either as moral emblems or, following Descartes in the seventeenth century, as reflex machines without minds.

### 1.2.2. Darwin

The modern study of behavior begins with Charles Darwin. *On the Origin of Species* (1859) devoted a chapter to instinct, arguing that behavior varies among individuals, is partly inherited and can therefore evolve by natural selection like any anatomical structure; his examples included the slave-making ants, the cuckoo and the hexagonal cells of the honey bee comb. *The Descent of Man, and Selection in Relation to Sex* (1871) introduced **sexual selection** to explain why males of so many species court, display and fight ([7.18.28](/7.18.28), [7.18.29](/7.18.29)). *The Expression of the Emotions in Man and Animals* (1872) compared facial expressions and postures across species and argued that many human expressions are inherited from animal ancestors. Darwin thus made behavior a subject for evolutionary explanation and implied that the mental lives of humans and other animals differ in degree rather than in kind.

Darwin's student George Romanes collected stories of clever animals in *Animal Intelligence* (1882), but his anecdotal method readily credited animals with human reasoning. The reaction came from Conway Lloyd Morgan, whose rule of interpretation is still taught.

**Definition 1.2.1 (Morgan's Canon)** **Morgan's canon** (1894): in no case should an action be interpreted as the outcome of a higher psychological faculty if it can be interpreted as the outcome of one that stands lower in the psychological scale.

Morgan's own example was his fox terrier, which learned to open the garden gate by lifting the latch with its head. An observer seeing only the final performance might conclude that the dog understood how latches work; Morgan, who had watched the dog learn, knew that it had lifted the latch by accident while putting its head through the railings, and repeated what had worked. The canon is a principle of parsimony for psychological explanation. It does not say that animals lack higher faculties, only that we must exclude simpler explanations before invoking them, and it has the drawback that "lower" and "higher" faculties are hard to rank. The opposite error, attributing human thoughts and feelings to animals without evidence, is called *anthropomorphism*.

The same caution was taught by the case of Clever Hans, a horse in Berlin whose owner, Wilhelm von Osten, believed he had taught it arithmetic: asked "what is 3 plus 4?", Hans tapped his hoof seven times. In 1907 the psychologist Oskar Pfungst showed that Hans answered correctly only when the questioner knew the answer and was visible to the horse. Hans had learned to start tapping when the questioner leaned forward slightly and to stop at the questioner's tiny, involuntary upward movement of the head when the right number was reached. Hans was a remarkable reader of human body language, not a mathematician. The *Clever Hans effect*, unintended cueing of a subject by an experimenter who knows the expected answer, is the reason why modern studies of animal cognition are run "blind" ([7.18.2](/7.18.2), [7.18.43](/7.18.43)).

### 1.2.3. Comparative Psychology and Behaviorism

In the first half of the twentieth century the study of behavior split into two schools, divided by their methods, their animals and to some extent their continents.

The psychologists, mostly in North America, sought general laws of learning that would apply to all animals including humans, and studied them in controlled laboratory experiments. Edward Thorndike (1898) placed hungry cats in puzzle boxes that opened when the cat pulled a loop, and found that the time to escape fell gradually over trials; he summarized the result as the law of effect, that actions followed by satisfying consequences become more likely. Ivan Pavlov, who received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1904 for his work on the physiology of digestion, discovered that dogs come to salivate at signals that predict food and analyzed this **classical conditioning** in great detail. John B. Watson's manifesto of 1913, "Psychology as the behaviorist views it", demanded that psychology study only observable behavior and abandon introspection and talk of the mind; with Rosalie Rayner he conditioned an infant, "Little Albert", to fear a white rat (1920), an experiment that would never be permitted today. B. F. Skinner (*The Behavior of Organisms*, 1938) analyzed **operant conditioning**, the learning of actions by their consequences, with the operant chamber or "Skinner box" in which a rat presses a lever or a pigeon pecks a key for food.

**Definition 1.2.2 (Comparative Psychology and Ethology)** **Comparative psychology** is the study of behavior, especially learning, in controlled laboratory experiments that relate observable responses to stimuli, typically in a few species and with the aim of finding general laws. **Behaviorism** is the doctrine within psychology that its proper subject is observable behavior and its causes in the environment, not mental states. **Ethology** is the biological study of behavior under natural conditions, in relation to the animal's anatomy, physiology, ecology and evolution, and with attention to the inherited as well as the learned components of behavior.

The word ethology comes from the Greek *ēthos*, "character" or "custom", and *-logia*, "study"; Isidore Geoffroy Saint-Hilaire used *éthologie* for the study of animal habits in 1854. One way to describe the program of ethology is to interview animals in their own language: to describe what each species does in the conditions it evolved in before asking how and why.

Behaviorism produced powerful methods and many lasting results, but in its extreme form it claimed that the environment can shape any behavior in any animal. Watson boasted that given a dozen healthy infants he could train any one of them to become "doctor, lawyer, artist, merchant-chief and, yes, even beggar-man and thief". Later work showed that learning is constrained by the evolved biology of each species: rats readily learn to avoid a taste that precedes nausea but hardly learn to avoid a taste that precedes an electric shock ([7.18.41](/7.18.41)). History offers a further, harsher lesson about the confident application of behavioral science to human beings. In the same decades, the Portuguese neurologist António Egas Moniz introduced prefrontal leucotomy for psychiatric illness and received the Nobel Prize in 1949; tens of thousands of lobotomies were performed before the operation was discredited. Konrad Lorenz, one of the founders of ethology, joined the Nazi party in 1938 and in 1940 wrote that "domestication" degraded the genetic quality of human populations, in language that supported racial hygiene; he later expressed regret. Neither the environmentalism of the behaviorists nor the biological determinism of eugenics was supported by the evidence, and both caused real harm.

### 1.2.4. Classical Ethology

The ethologists, mostly European zoologists, began from the natural history of particular species. Charles Otis Whitman and Wallace Craig in the United States and Oskar Heinroth in Germany showed that displays and other behavior patterns are as characteristic of a species as its anatomy and can be used to reconstruct relationships among species, just as anatomy can. Heinroth (1911) described how goslings hatched in an incubator attach themselves to the first person they see. Craig (1918) distinguished the variable searching that precedes a goal from the stereotyped act that completes it, a distinction that became central to ethology ([7.18.3](/7.18.3)). Jakob von Uexküll (1909) insisted that each animal lives in its own perceptual world, its Umwelt, made of the few features of the environment that its sense organs and nervous system select.

Three men built the discipline.

| | Main animals | Main contributions |
|---|---|---|
| Konrad Lorenz (1903–1989) | Geese, jackdaws, ducks | Imprinting (1935); fixed action patterns and their comparative study; the "psychohydraulic" model of motivation |
| Niko Tinbergen (1907–1988) | Sticklebacks, gulls, digger wasps | Experimental analysis of sign stimuli with dummies; *The Study of Instinct* (1951); field experiments on adaptive value; the four questions (1963) |
| Karl von Frisch (1886–1982) | Honey bees, fishes | Color vision of bees (1914) and hearing in fishes; the waggle dance as a code for the distance and direction of food; the sun compass |

In 1973 the three shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine "for their discoveries concerning organization and elicitation of individual and social behaviour patterns", the first time the prize was given for the study of behavior. The ethologists compiled the ethogram, the catalog of the behavior patterns of a species ([7.18.2](/7.18.2)), and studied the stimuli that release behavior, the stereotyped motor patterns that follow, and the internal motivation that sets their threshold ([7.18.3](/7.18.3)).

The two schools are often contrasted as follows.

| | Ethology | Comparative psychology |
|---|---|---|
| Perspective | Evolutionary: behavior as an adaptation of each species | Mechanistic and developmental: general processes of learning |
| Setting | Primarily field-based, natural conditions | Primarily laboratory-based, controlled conditions |
| Species | A wide range of animals, chosen for their natural history | Few species, mostly rats, pigeons and primates |
| Emphasis | Species-specific, inherited components ("instinct") | Modification by experience ("learning") |
| Strength | Validity: behavior as it occurs in nature | Control: clear causal inference from experiments |

The contrast should not be exaggerated. Tinbergen was a superb experimenter, and some psychologists, such as Daniel Lehrman and T. C. Schneirla, studied behavior in a developmental and comparative frame. Lehrman's critique of Lorenz (1953) argued that the dichotomy between "innate" and "learned" behavior is misleading because every behavior develops through an interaction of genes and environment; this argument, and the work of Robert Hinde and William Thorpe in Britain, gradually merged the two traditions. We return to the modern resolution of the nature–nurture question in [7.18.3](/7.18.3), [7.18.4](/7.18.4) and [7.18.5](/7.18.5).

### 1.2.5. Sociobiology, Behavioral Ecology and Neuroethology

From the 1960s the questions about the evolution of behavior became sharper. Many naturalists, including V. C. Wynne-Edwards (1962), had explained behavior by its benefit to the group or the species, for instance that animals limit their reproduction to avoid overexploiting food. George C. Williams (*Adaptation and Natural Selection*, 1966) argued that selection among groups is usually much weaker than selection among individuals within groups (**group selection**, [7.10.3](/7.10.3)), so that adaptations should be explained by their effects on the reproductive success of individuals and ultimately on the transmission of their genes. W. D. Hamilton (1964) showed how behavior that helps relatives can spread (**kin selection**, **inclusive fitness**, [7.10.16](/7.10.16)); Robert Trivers developed theories of reciprocal altruism (1971), parental investment (1972) and parent–offspring conflict (1974); and John Maynard Smith and George Price (1973) introduced game theory and the **evolutionarily stable strategy** to the analysis of fighting. These ideas were synthesized in E. O. Wilson's *Sociobiology: The New Synthesis* (1975), popularized in Richard Dawkins's *The Selfish Gene* (1976), and turned into a field-based experimental science by John Krebs and Nicholas Davies (*Behavioural Ecology: An Evolutionary Approach*, 1978).

**Definition 1.2.3 (Sociobiology and Behavioral Ecology)** **Sociobiology** is the systematic study of the biological, especially evolutionary, basis of social behavior. **Behavioral ecology** is the study of how behavior contributes to survival and reproductive success in the animal's ecological and social environment, using natural selection theory, quantitative models (optimality and game theory) and field experiments.

The last chapter of *Sociobiology*, which applied the same reasoning to humans, provoked a fierce controversy about biological determinism. The field has since become less polemical; human behavior is discussed only briefly in this course ([7.18.47](/7.18.47)).

At the same time, physiologists began to study the neural basis of natural behavior in animals chosen because their behavior is conspicuous and their nervous systems accessible: the escape of moths from bats, the prey capture of toads, sound localization by barn owls, the electric signals of weakly electric fish and the songs of crickets and birds. This is **neuroethology**, the subject of [7.18.6](/7.18.6)–[7.18.21](/7.18.21). Its motto, a play on Theodosius Dobzhansky's "nothing in biology makes sense except in the light of evolution", might be that nothing in neuroscience makes sense except in the light of behavior. The newest tools of the field, from genomics and gene editing in non-model species to automated tracking and machine-learning analysis of posture ([7.18.2](/7.18.2)), have made it possible to combine mechanistic and evolutionary questions in the same study. This integrative approach is the approach of this course.

## 1.3. Levels of Analysis

### 1.3.1. Proximate and Ultimate Causes

Behavior can be explained at two levels, the proximate and the ultimate, already defined in [7.10.1](/7.10.1#sec-1.5.1). Ernst Mayr (1961) introduced the terms; the distinction itself is older. A **proximate cause** is the mechanism within the lifetime of the individual: the environmental stimuli that trigger a behavior and the genetic, developmental, neural and hormonal mechanisms that make it possible. Proximate questions ask *how* an animal performs a behavior. An **ultimate cause** is the evolutionary explanation: how the behavior increases the **fitness** of animals that perform it, and how it arose from the behavior of ancestors. Ultimate questions ask *why* the animal behaves this way.

A simple example shows the difference. Red-crowned cranes (*Grus japonensis*) breed in spring and early summer. "Increasing day length stimulates the release of reproductive hormones, which trigger breeding behavior" is a proximate explanation. "Chicks hatched in early summer have ample food and time to grow before the autumn migration, so breeding is most successful at this season" is an ultimate explanation. The two do not compete. The proximate explanation is true only because cranes have evolved a photoperiodic mechanism that times breeding to the season of plenty; the ultimate explanation is true only because such a mechanism exists. Confusing the two levels is the commonest error in discussions of behavior: a finding that a behavior is caused by a hormone does not show that it is not an adaptation, and a finding that it is adaptive says nothing about its mechanism.

### 1.3.2. Tinbergen's Four Questions

In a paper of 1963, "On aims and methods of ethology", Tinbergen argued that a complete biology of behavior must answer four distinct questions.

**Definition 1.3.1 (Tinbergen's Four Questions)** **Tinbergen's four questions** about any behavior are:

1. Mechanism (causation): which stimuli elicit the behavior, and which sensory, neural, hormonal and genetic mechanisms produce it?
2. Development (ontogeny): how does the behavior arise and change during the lifetime of the individual, through the interaction of genes and experience?
3. Function (adaptive value, survival value): how does the behavior contribute to survival and reproduction now?
4. Evolution (phylogeny): how did the behavior evolve from the behavior of ancestors, and how is it distributed among related species?

The first two are proximate questions and the last two ultimate ones. A second axis cuts across the first: mechanism and function concern the behavior as it is, at a given moment, whereas development and evolution concern how it came to be, over the life of the individual or the history of the lineage.

| | Static: the behavior as it is now | Dynamic: how the behavior came to be |
|---|---|---|
| Proximate | Mechanism: how does it work? | Development: how did it develop in the individual? |
| Ultimate | Function: what is it for? | Evolution: how did it evolve in the lineage? |

![Tinbergen's four questions arranged as a two-by-two table, with the black-headed gull's mobbing of predators as an example. Rows: proximate questions (about the individual's lifetime) and ultimate questions (about the history of selection). Columns: questions about the behavior as it is now and about how it came to be. Each cell gives the question and an example of an answer for mobbing; the arrows indicate that answers at one level constrain and inform answers at the others](./plots/four-questions.py)

Tinbergen's own word for the third question was "survival value", but the modern term "function" refers to reproduction as much as to survival. The question of function also differs subtly from that of evolution. Function concerns the current effect of a behavior on fitness, whereas the evolutionary question concerns the history of the trait, including the selective pressures that shaped it in the past, which need not be the same as its present use. The four questions are logically independent: we can study the mechanism of a behavior without knowing its function, and vice versa. But they are also linked, and the most illuminating studies combine them. Behaviors that spread because of their effects on fitness must have inherited mechanisms; the mechanisms available constrain what can evolve; and the developmental program determines how much a behavior can be adjusted to local conditions ([7.18.5](/7.18.5)).

### 1.3.3. An Integrative Example: Digger Bees

The four questions can be asked of any behavior. Males of the digger bee *Centris pallida* in the deserts of the southwestern United States patrol bare ground in spring, stop, and dig holes up to several centimeters deep. John Alcock showed that they dig to reach females that are about to emerge from the underground cells in which they developed; a male that reaches such a female mates with her immediately, often before she has fully left the soil. The function of digging is therefore to obtain matings, and competition among males is intense: several males may converge on the same spot and fight for the female.

How does a male know where to dig? Alcock and colleagues (1976) proposed that males smell the emerging females through the soil, a proximate hypothesis about mechanism. The olfactory receptors on the antennae and the olfactory circuits of the brain that make this possible develop from the egg through the larva and pupa under the control of genes and their environment (development), and their sensitivity to the odor of females is an evolutionary modification of the olfactory system of ancestral bees (evolution). One can ask further proximate questions (do older males find females more efficiently than young ones?) and further ultimate ones (have the legs of digger bees been modified for digging, compared with species that find mates at flowers?). Each answer makes the others more intelligible. Chapter [7.18.17](/7.18.17) develops a fuller example of the integrative approach with bird song, where the mechanism (the song system of the brain), the development (song learning in a **critical period**), the function (territory defense and mate attraction) and the evolution (the repeated origin of vocal learning) have all been studied in depth.

## 1.4. Natural Selection and the Evolution of Behavior

### 1.4.1. The Logic of Selection

Darwin's argument for evolution by natural selection, developed in [7.10.1](/7.10.1) and [7.4.23](/7.4.23), requires only three conditions:

1. Variation: individuals in a population differ in a characteristic.
2. Heredity: offspring tend to resemble their parents in that characteristic.
3. Differential reproductive success: individuals with some variants leave more surviving offspring than others.

When all three hold, the variants associated with greater reproductive success become more common in the population over generations. Evolution in the population-genetic sense is a change in the frequency of **alleles** or genotypes in a population; natural selection is one of its causes, alongside mutation, migration and genetic drift. A trait that has spread or is spreading by natural selection because it increases fitness relative to the alternatives is an **adaptation** ([7.10.3](/7.10.3)). Darwin showed that the necessary conditions are universal, and pointed to artificial selection by breeders of dogs and pigeons as proof that heritable variation in behavior and structure exists. Variation in behavior is heritable in many cases ([7.18.4](/7.18.4)), and natural selection on it is often strong.

Three points about this logic are essential for what follows.

- *Selection acts through individuals, and its consequences are recorded in genes.* Organisms do not replicate themselves; genes do, and organisms are the vehicles in which they travel (Dawkins 1976). Adaptive evolution therefore increases the frequency of alleles whose effects on the **phenotype** promote their own transmission. This gene-centered view explains why individuals can be selected to help relatives that carry copies of the same alleles (**kin selection**).
- *Selection does not work for the good of the species.* A behavior can spread because it benefits the individuals that perform it even if it harms the group or the species in the long run. Arguments of the form "animals do this to preserve the species" are almost always wrong, and the special cases in which selection favors behavior that benefits others at a cost to the actor require explicit explanation ([7.18.33](/7.18.33)–[7.18.36](/7.18.36)).
- *Selection is not directed.* It has no foresight and is not "trying" to achieve anything. It is the cumulative effect of the differential reproduction of individuals that happen to carry different variants.

Behaviors that seem to reduce the reproductive success of the individuals that perform them are called *Darwinian puzzles* by Rubenstein and Alcock. The suicidal sting of a worker honey bee is one: workers are sterile, and their sting protects a colony of sisters whose reproduction, through their mother the queen, transmits copies of the worker's genes ([7.18.36](/7.18.36)). Resolving such puzzles has driven much of the theory of behavioral ecology.

### 1.4.2. The Cost–Benefit Approach and Hypothesis Testing

To find out why a behavior has evolved, a behavioral biologist proposes a *hypothesis*, a tentative explanation, and derives from it *predictions*, observations or experimental outcomes that should occur if the hypothesis is true. Usually several hypotheses are possible. When only one of them can be correct, they are mutually exclusive (Rubenstein and Alcock call them alternative hypotheses); when several can be true at the same time, they are non-mutually exclusive. A good study states the hypotheses before collecting the data and is designed so that the outcome would discriminate among them ([7.18.2](/7.18.2)).

Hypotheses about adaptation are usually framed in terms of costs and benefits. The *fitness benefit* of a behavior is its positive effect on the number of surviving offspring of the individual (more generally, on the number of copies of its alleles passed to the next generation); the *fitness cost* is its negative effect. An adaptation has a better ratio of benefits to costs than the alternative behaviors that have occurred in the population. Because fitness itself is hard to measure, researchers use proxies such as the survival of offspring to fledging, the number of mates, the rate of food intake or the acquisition of a territory. A cost–benefit hypothesis predicts how a behavior should change when its costs or benefits change, and these predictions can be tested by comparing individuals, populations or species that face different conditions, or by manipulating the conditions experimentally. When costs and benefits can be expressed in the same currency, an optimality model predicts the best behavior quantitatively ([7.18.2](/7.18.2), [7.18.22](/7.18.22)).

### 1.4.3. A Darwinian Puzzle: Infanticide in Langurs

Hanuman langurs (*Semnopithecus entellus*) of India live in groups of several adult females and their young with one or a few adult males. Males from outside, often as a coalition from an all-male band, periodically attack a group, drive out the resident male and take his place. In the 1960s and 1970s observers saw newly arrived males attack and sometimes kill young infants of the group, although mothers defended their infants and the attacking males were sometimes injured. How could such behavior be adaptive?

Some primatologists proposed that infanticide was not adaptive at all but an abnormal, pathological product of crowding in langur populations that were fed by villagers and lived at unnaturally high densities (the *social pathology hypothesis*). Sarah Hrdy (1977) proposed instead that infanticide is a reproductive tactic of males (the *sexually selected infanticide hypothesis*). A langur mother nursing an infant does not ovulate for many months; if her infant dies, she resumes cycling within weeks or months. A male who has just taken over a group may hold it only for a year or two before he is ousted himself. By killing infants sired by his predecessor, he advances the time at which the mothers can conceive his own offspring, and so increases the number of offspring he can sire during his short tenure.

The two hypotheses make different predictions:

| Prediction | Social pathology | Sexually selected infanticide |
|---|---|---|
| Infanticide occurs mainly when | Population density is high | A new male has taken over a group |
| Victims are | Any infants, at random | Infants sired by other males, never the killer's own |
| Victims' age | Any | Mostly unweaned infants (killing an independent juvenile does not hasten conception) |
| Mothers after losing an infant | No particular change | Resume ovulating soon and conceive with the killer |

The data support the sexual selection hypothesis. Infanticide is associated with male takeovers, also in populations not fed by people; the victims are mostly young, unweaned infants; and when Carola Borries and colleagues (1999) determined paternity by DNA analysis of feces in a wild population at Ramnagar, Nepal, the attacking males were never the fathers of the infants they attacked and were in most cases the fathers of the mothers' next infants. Similar male infanticide after takeovers occurs in lions, in many other primates, in rodents, horses and some bats. Females in turn have evolved counterstrategies, such as defense of infants by mothers and coalitions of females, and in some species mating with many males so that every male could be the father ([7.18.30](/7.18.30)).

A simple model makes the cost–benefit argument quantitative. Suppose that, without infanticide, a female conceives again only when her current infant is weaned, an interval that depends on the age $a$ of the infant at the time of the takeover; with infanticide, she conceives after a short delay $d$. If the tenure of a male ends at a random time with mean $T$ (an exponential distribution), the probability that he is still resident when the female conceives is $\mathrm{e}^{-t/T}$, where $t$ is the waiting time to conception.

A: time from takeover to the birth of the new male's first infant with and without infanticide, as a function of the age of the infant at takeover; the gain is largest for young infants. The model shows when infanticide should be favored. The benefit is largest when the infant is young (a long period of lactational infertility remains) and vanishes for infants close to weaning, whose mothers would conceive soon anyway (beyond about 13.5 months in panel A) and when male tenure is short (a male who waits may be ousted before he sires anything). If males usually held groups for many years, the benefit would be small relative to the costs: injury in fights with mothers, and the risk of killing one's own offspring. These are the conditions under which infanticide by males is in fact observed across mammals.

The langur example illustrates a general point. That a behavior seems cruel or harmful to the species tells us nothing about whether it is an adaptation. Behavioral biology explains behavior; it does not justify it, and the explanation of infanticide as an adaptation of male langurs implies no moral judgment.

## 1.5. An Integrative Case Study: Mobbing in Gulls

Anyone who walks into a colony of nesting gulls is met by a storm of birds that circle, call loudly, dive at the intruder and sometimes strike or defecate on him. This behavior, in which prey animals approach and harass a predator rather than flee from it, is called mobbing. Many birds mob owls, hawks, snakes and mammals. For gulls the costs are obvious, in time, energy and the risk of being caught. What is the benefit?

### 1.5.1. Observation: the Predator Distraction Hypothesis

Hans Kruuk (1964) studied mobbing in a colony of black-headed gulls (*Chroicocephalus ridibundus*), which nest on the ground in dunes and marshes across Europe and Asia, and whose eggs and chicks are taken by carrion crows (*Corvus corone*), herring gulls (*Larus argentatus*), foxes and hedgehogs. His working hypothesis was that mobbing distracts predators from searching for nests (the *predator distraction hypothesis*), so that the benefit to the parents in offspring survival exceeds the costs. A first prediction is that mobbed predators should search less effectively. By watching crows hunting in the colony, Kruuk found that a crow being mobbed had to face the diving gulls continually and could not search the ground for nests at the same time. Crows did not attack or injure adult gulls, so the cost of mobbing them was small.

### 1.5.2. Experiment: Eggs Inside and Outside the Colony

Observation alone could not show that mobbing actually protects eggs, because nests inside and outside the colony differ in many other ways. Kruuk therefore laid out chicken eggs as stand-ins for gull eggs, one every 10 m along lines running from outside the colony to its center. Eggs outside the colony, where few gulls mobbed the predators, were found and eaten by crows and herring gulls far more often than eggs inside it, and the predators searching inside the colony were attacked by many more gulls. The proportion of eggs taken fell as the intensity of mobbing rose, as the hypothesis predicted. Kruuk also found that crows were less successful the more gulls joined the attack, so that mobbing is most effective as a group defense.

A further prediction follows if mobbing is a group defense. Birds that nest at higher density should be better protected. This was tested in arctic skuas (*Stercorarius parasiticus*), relatives of gulls that also nest on the ground and mob intruders, including great skuas (*Stercorarius skua*) that eat their eggs and chicks. In a population in Scotland, pairs nesting with many close neighbors hatched and fledged more chicks than pairs at low density, although their chicks grew somewhat more slowly, perhaps because of competition for food (Phillips et al. 1998). The benefit of mobbing in dense colonies thus comes with a cost, and an adaptation need not be perfect to be favored by selection; it need only be better than the alternatives.

### 1.5.3. Comparison: Divergence and Convergence

The **comparative method** ([7.10.3](/7.10.3)) tests adaptive hypotheses across species. If mobbing evolved because of predation on eggs and chicks, species whose eggs are safe from predators should not mob, even if they are closely related to mobbing species; and unrelated species that face similar predators should have evolved mobbing independently.

Most of the 50 or so species of gulls nest on the ground and mob predators, and the ancestral gull was almost certainly a ground nester. The black-legged kittiwake (*Rissa tridactyla*), however, nests on tiny ledges of vertical sea cliffs, where mammals cannot reach the nests and turbulent winds make it hard for predatory birds to approach. Esther Cullen (1957) found that kittiwakes do not mob, although they share many other traits with ground-nesting gulls. This is **divergent evolution**: a lineage that inherited mobbing from its ancestors lost it when the selective pressure that maintained it was removed. (Cullen's study showed that a whole suite of other kittiwake traits changed with cliff nesting; see [7.18.2](/7.18.2).)

Conversely, mobbing has evolved independently in many colonial animals that are only distantly related to gulls. Bank swallows (*Riparia riparia*) nest in colonies in sand banks and mob the snakes and jays that come to eat their eggs and nestlings; in colonies of California ground squirrels (*Otospermophilus beecheyi*), adults confront a hunting rattlesnake, wave their tails, which they heat to emit infrared radiation that the snake's pit organs detect, and kick sand at it (Rundus et al. 2007). This is **convergent evolution** ([7.10.2](/7.10.2)): similar selective pressures produce similar adaptations in unrelated lineages. Shared ancestry tends to make related species similar, and different selective pressures can override that similarity; similar selective pressures can make unrelated species similar.

Inferring the ancestral state requires a phylogeny. Of two scenarios for the origin of cliff nesting in kittiwakes, one in which the ancestral gull nested on the ground and cliff nesting arose once requires a single change, whereas one in which the ancestor nested on cliffs requires two changes (to ground nesting and back). Preferring the scenario with fewer changes applies the principle of parsimony, which usually, though not always, gives the most probable reconstruction.

![The comparative method applied to mobbing. A, B: two hypotheses for the evolution of nest sites in gulls. If the ancestral gull nested on the ground (A), cliff nesting in the kittiwake evolved once; if it nested on cliffs (B), two changes are needed. Parsimony favors A. C: divergent and convergent evolution. Ground-nesting gulls and the cliff-nesting kittiwake share ancestry but differ in mobbing because predation pressure differs; colonial swallows and gulls do not share ancestry but both mob, because both face predators that can reach their nests](./plots/gull-comparative.py)

Observation, experiment and comparison thus agree that mobbing in black-headed gulls is an antipredator adaptation: it reduces predation on eggs and chicks, it is maintained where nest predation is high, lost where it is low, and has arisen independently wherever colonial animals face predators that can reach their young. Comparative studies today use explicitly phylogenetic statistics, which correct for the non-independence of related species (**phylogenetically independent contrasts**, [7.10.26](/7.10.26); see [7.18.2](/7.18.2)).

## 1.6. Behavioral Biology as a Science

The examples of this chapter share a logic that runs through the whole course. A behavior that needs explaining is identified, often a Darwinian puzzle; competing hypotheses are formulated, at the proximate or the ultimate level or both; predictions are derived; and the predictions are tested by observation, experiment and comparison. A hypothesis whose predictions fail is rejected; one whose predictions are confirmed is accepted provisionally, until a better test or a better hypothesis comes along. Good studies consider alternatives before they collect data, use controls, measure behavior without knowing which treatment an animal received, and replicate their results.

Two opposite errors threaten the study of animal minds. One is the uncritical anthropomorphism of Romanes, which reads human motives into animal actions; the other is a dogmatic refusal to consider that animals might have any mental life at all. The modern position, sometimes called *critical anthropomorphism* (Gordon Burghardt), uses our intuitions about animals as a source of hypotheses, but tests them with the same rigor as any other hypothesis, guided by Morgan's canon and by the lesson of Clever Hans ([7.18.41](/7.18.41)–[7.18.46](/7.18.46)).

The course follows the order of Tinbergen's questions, from mechanism to function:

## Summary

- Behavior is the internally coordinated response, action or inaction, of a whole organism or group to internal or external stimuli (Definition 1.1.1). We study it for its own interest, as a foundation of neuroscience, ecology and conservation, and for its practical uses.
- History. Aristotle and Galen observed and dissected; Darwin made behavior a subject of evolution (instinct, **sexual selection**, the expression of emotions). The reaction to anecdotal anthropomorphism produced Morgan's canon (Definition 1.2.1) and the lesson of Clever Hans. Comparative psychology and behaviorism studied learning in the laboratory; **ethology** studied species-specific behavior in nature (Definition 1.2.2), culminating in the 1973 Nobel Prize to Lorenz, Tinbergen and von Frisch. From the 1960s, the gene-centered view of selection, kin selection and game theory produced sociobiology and behavioral ecology (Definition 1.2.3), and physiologists created **neuroethology**.
- Levels of analysis. Proximate causes (mechanism, development) and ultimate causes (function, evolution) are complementary, not alternative, explanations. **Tinbergen's four questions** (Definition 1.3.1) cross the proximate–ultimate axis with a static–dynamic one.
- Natural selection and behavior. Variation, heredity and differential reproduction suffice for adaptive evolution. Selection acts through individuals and genes, not for the good of the species, and has no foresight. Adaptive hypotheses are framed as costs and benefits in units of fitness and tested by their predictions.
- Infanticide in langurs is a reproductive tactic of males that take over groups: it shortens the time to their own offspring, is directed at unweaned infants sired by other males, and is favored when male tenure is short.
- Mobbing in gulls shows the three approaches: observation (mobbed crows search less), experiment (eggs inside the colony survive better), and comparison (the cliff-nesting kittiwake has lost mobbing; colonial swallows and ground squirrels have evolved it convergently).

## Questions

1. Red-crowned cranes breed in spring and early summer. Which of the following explanations are proximate and which ultimate? (a) Breeding is most likely to be successful in spring and early summer. (b) Increasing day length triggers the release of breeding hormones. (c) Ample food is available for chicks at this time. (d) The effect of increasing day length on the birds' photoreceptors triggers breeding.

   **Answer** (b) and (d) are proximate: they describe the mechanism (photoperiodic stimulation of the reproductive axis) by which breeding is timed. (a) and (c) are ultimate: they explain why selection has favored breeding at this season, namely that offspring produced then have the highest survival. The four statements are compatible, and a complete explanation includes both kinds.
2. Classify each of the following as behavior or not behavior by Definition 1.1.1, and explain: (a) a hare freezing when a hawk passes; (b) a sunflower head turning to follow the sun; (c) a caterpillar molting; (d) a flock of starlings wheeling in the sky; (e) a dead fish drifting downstream.

   **Answer** (a) is behavior: inaction coordinated by the nervous system in response to a stimulus. (b) is a coordinated response of a whole organism to an external stimulus, and by the letter of the definition counts as behavior, although most researchers restrict the term to animals or treat plant movements separately; the case shows that the boundary is a matter of convention. (c) is a developmental change and excluded. (d) is behavior of a group, coordinated by interactions among individuals. (e) is not behavior: the movement is not coordinated by the organism.
3. Discuss the strengths and weaknesses of ethology and comparative psychology as approaches to behavior. What did each contribute to the modern discipline?

   **Answer** Ethology studied many species in natural conditions, with attention to the evolution and adaptive value of species-specific behavior; its strengths were validity and breadth, its weaknesses the difficulty of controlling variables in the field and a tendency, in its early theory, to treat "innate" behavior as independent of experience. Comparative psychology achieved rigorous experimental control and quantitative laws of learning, but in few species, under artificial conditions, and with a tendency to ignore species differences and evolved constraints on learning. The modern discipline combines field and laboratory work, evolutionary and mechanistic questions, and regards all behavior as developing through the interaction of genes and experience, while using the experimental designs of psychology to test hypotheses inspired by natural history.
4. Formulate the infanticide hypothesis for Hanuman langurs from the point of view of a gene. Would an allele that predisposes males to kill unrelated infants after a takeover spread? Under what conditions would it not?

   **Answer** An allele that causes males to kill unweaned infants sired by other males after a takeover would be transmitted to more offspring than an allele that does not, because carriers sire their first offspring sooner and so, on average, sire more offspring before they lose the group. It would spread if this gain exceeds the costs: injuries inflicted by mothers and female coalitions, the risk of killing the carrier's own offspring (if paternity is uncertain), and possible female counterstrategies such as mating with many males or avoiding infanticidal males. It would not spread where male tenures are long relative to the period of lactational infertility, or where females can conceal paternity, as the model of §1.4.3 shows.
5. In the infanticide model of §1.4.3, how does the advantage of infanticide change if (a) mothers of killed infants take longer to resume cycling, (b) the new male's tenure is very long, (c) the male cannot tell whether an infant could be his own?

   **Answer** (a) A longer delay $d$ reduces the time saved and the probability $\mathrm{e}^{-d/T}$ that the male still holds the group when the female conceives, so the advantage shrinks. (b) As $T \to \infty$, both strategies sire the next infant with probability close to 1, and the benefit reduces to a modest gain in time; with costs, infanticide would not be favored. (c) If some infants could be his, killing them destroys his own reproductive success; selection then favors males that kill only after a takeover, when the infants cannot be theirs, which is what is observed, and females gain an incentive to confuse paternity.
6. Design a comparative test of the hypothesis that mobbing is an adaptation against nest predators, using gulls and terns. What confounding factors would you need to consider?

   **Answer** Score mobbing intensity in many species and relate it to nest predation risk (for instance, ground versus cliff or island nesting, and the presence of mammalian predators), predicting more intense mobbing where predation risk is high. Confounding factors include body size (large species can afford to attack predators), colony size and density (mobbing is more effective in groups), the type of predator (birds versus mammals), and above all shared ancestry: closely related species resemble each other for historical reasons, so species are not independent data points and the analysis must use phylogenetic methods such as **phylogenetically independent contrasts**. A stronger test asks whether changes in nesting habit along the phylogeny are associated with changes in mobbing, as in the kittiwake.
7. (Open question) Why do we need a science of animal behavior, rather than leaving behavior to neuroscience on the one hand and ecology on the other?

   **Answer** Behavior is the level at which the nervous system meets the environment and at which selection acts on most traits of animals; neither neuroscience nor ecology can define its questions without a description of what animals actually do and why. Neuroscience without behavioral analysis risks studying circuits in tasks that the animal never faces, and Tinbergen's four questions show that the meaning of a mechanism depends on the function it serves. Ecology without behavior cannot explain the decisions that individuals make about foraging, mating and grouping, from which population patterns arise. The integrative science of behavior connects these levels.
