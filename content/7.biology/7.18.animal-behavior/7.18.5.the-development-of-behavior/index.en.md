A newly hatched gosling follows the first moving object it sees and becomes attached to it for life. A young white-crowned sparrow that hears no adult song in its first two months sings an abnormal song forever. A desert locust nymph that is jostled by its neighbors for a few hours becomes gregarious, and its offspring are born ready to swarm. In each case, the behavior of the adult depends on what happened at a particular stage of development, through an interaction between the developing nervous system and the environment. This chapter is about such interactions: Tinbergen's question of ontogeny ([7.18.1](/7.18.1)).

The previous chapter showed how genes contribute to behavioral differences and how the environment acts through gene regulation ([7.18.4](/7.18.4)). Here we follow the developing individual. We first survey the sources of behavioral change during development, then the concept of the sensitive period and its best-studied example, imprinting. We then consider the effects of early social and physical conditions, including effects transmitted from mothers, and the opposite capacity of development to produce a normal phenotype despite perturbations. The second half of the chapter is about developmental plasticity: how a single genotype produces different behavioral phenotypes in different environments, often through switch-like mechanisms, and how such mechanisms evolve. Song learning, the best-studied case of behavioral development, has its own chapter ([7.18.17](/7.18.17)), and the general developmental biology of plasticity and polyphenism is covered in [7.5.38](/7.5.38#sec-38.8).

## 5.1. Sources of Behavioral Change during Development

Behavior changes during development for four main reasons, which usually act together.

| Source | Example |
|---|---|
| Maturation of the nervous system | Swimming in frog tadpoles develops normally in embryos that are immobilized by anesthesia and cannot practice |
| Hormonal changes | Female *Drosophila* become sexually receptive about two days after eclosion, when juvenile hormone from the corpora allata rises |
| Changes in body form | Metamorphosis changes the locomotion, diet and habitat of frogs and insects; growth changes what prey a predator can handle |
| Experience | Learning the features of parents, the song of the species, the location of food, the identity of neighbors |

The independence of some motor patterns from practice was shown in classic experiments on amphibians. Leonard Carmichael (1926) raised salamander and frog embryos in an anesthetic solution that prevented all movement during the period in which swimming normally develops; when they were placed in fresh water, they swam almost as well as controls within half an hour, the time needed for the anesthetic to wear off. Later work on *Xenopus* showed that the neural circuits for swimming, the spinal central pattern generators ([7.18.14](/7.18.14)), form in the absence of patterned activity. Such results do not show that development is independent of the environment (the embryos still needed a normal chemical and physical environment) but that this particular behavior does not require practice.

Hormones act on behavior in two ways that are treated in [7.18.19](/7.18.19): during sensitive periods of development they organize sex differences in the nervous system (organizational effects), and later they activate behavior in adults (activational effects). The development of song in zebra finches, in which estradiol acting on the brain of nestlings masculinizes the song system, is an example ([7.18.17](/7.18.17)). Female *Drosophila* show how hormones time behavioral maturation in insects: juvenile hormone from the corpora allata lowers the threshold for accepting a male, and implanting extra corpora allata into pupae makes females receptive earlier. After mating, a peptide transferred in the male's seminal fluid (the sex peptide) suppresses receptivity and increases egg laying.

## 5.2. Sensitive Periods

### 5.2.1. Definition

Many experiences affect development only if they occur at a particular stage of life. The concept comes from embryology, where it describes the short periods of rapid tissue differentiation during which a disturbance has drastic and irreversible effects (**critical period**, [7.5.38](/7.5.38#sec-38.4.1)). Konrad Lorenz applied it to behavior in 1935, to the brief period after hatching in which goslings learn the characteristics of their parent.

**Definition 5.2.1 (Sensitive Period)** A **sensitive period** is a phase of development during which a particular experience has a much larger effect on the development of a behavior, or of the underlying neural mechanism, than the same experience has earlier or later.

The older term "critical period" implied a sharply bounded window after which the effect of experience is zero and its result irreversible. Later research showed that sensitive periods typically begin and end gradually; that their timing and duration differ among species, individuals and functional systems; that they depend on the nature and intensity of the stimulation; and that what is learned is often modifiable later, although less easily than during the sensitive period. Most researchers therefore prefer "sensitive period" for behavior, and reserve "critical period" for cases with sharp limits and irreversible effects, such as the development of binocular vision in kittens studied by David Hubel and Torsten Wiesel ([7.18.10](/7.18.10)).

Sensitive periods occur in many systems:

| System | Sensitive period | Chapter |
|---|---|---|
| Filial imprinting in ducks, geese and chicks | First one to two days after hatching | §5.3 |
| Sexual imprinting in birds and mammals | Weeks to months, before sexual maturity | §5.3.4 |
| Song learning in white-crowned sparrows | About 10–50 days after hatching for memorizing the tutor song | [7.18.17](/7.18.17) |
| Olfactory imprinting on the home stream in salmon | Mainly at the parr–smolt transformation, before migration to sea | [7.18.21](/7.18.21) |
| Binocular vision and ocular dominance in cats and monkeys | First few months of life | [7.18.10](/7.18.10) |
| Maternal recognition of the young in sheep | A few hours after giving birth | §5.3.3 |

Why should learning be concentrated early in life? A common answer is that early life is when the young are reliably in the company of their parents and siblings and can learn what their own species and family look like, before they meet other species or strangers. Learning that remains open for too long risks being corrupted by later, less reliable experience. A second answer is that some kinds of learning are needed early, because their products are used at once (a duckling must follow its mother within hours of hatching).

### 5.2.2. How Sensitive Periods Begin and End

The onset of a sensitive period depends on the maturation of the sensory and neural systems that process the relevant stimuli. Filial imprinting cannot begin before the hatchling's visual system works and it can walk; song memorization cannot begin before the auditory system is mature.

Two hypotheses explain the end of a sensitive period. According to the *internal clock hypothesis*, sensitivity declines at a fixed developmental age, controlled by an internal timer independent of experience. According to the *competitive exclusion hypothesis* (Patrick Bateson), the sensitive period is self-terminating: experience during the period commits the developing neural representation to what has been experienced, so that later, competing experiences have less and less effect. The two hypotheses make different predictions about animals deprived of the relevant experience. If the end of the period is set by a clock, deprivation should not prolong it; if it is self-terminating, deprivation should prolong it, because nothing has yet been learned. In chicks and ducklings reared in darkness or in visual isolation, the sensitive period for imprinting is in fact extended, supporting the competitive exclusion hypothesis, although sensitivity eventually declines even in isolated birds, probably because fear of novel objects increases with age. In songbirds, isolation can also extend the period for song learning in some species, and a live tutor can be effective beyond the age at which tape-recorded song is no longer learned (Baptista and Petrinovich 1984).

## 5.3. Imprinting

### 5.3.1. Filial Imprinting

**Definition 5.3.1 (Filial Imprinting)** **Filial imprinting** is the process by which the young of precocial birds, and some mammals, learn the characteristics of a parent (or a substitute) during a sensitive period early in life and develop a social attachment to it, expressed by following it and by distress when separated from it.

Lorenz's demonstration became famous. He divided a clutch of greylag goose eggs: half were hatched by their mother, half in an incubator, where the first moving object the goslings saw was Lorenz himself. The goslings hatched by the goose followed her; those hatched in the incubator followed Lorenz as if he were their mother. When he put all the goslings together under a box and then lifted it, the two groups ran to their respective "parents". Similar attachments can be formed to a box, a ball, a flashing light or a moving model, although natural objects are more effective (§5.3.2).

Filial imprinting occurs in precocial birds such as ducks, geese, chickens and other galliforms, whose young can walk and feed themselves soon after hatching and must stay close to their parent to be protected. It has no exact counterpart in altricial birds, which remain in the nest for weeks and are fed by their parents. Its function is to ensure that the young stay with and recognize their own parent among other adults of the species.

Eckhard Hess (1959) measured the sensitive period for imprinting in mallard ducklings (*Anas platyrhynchos*). Ducklings hatched in the dark were exposed once, at different ages, to a moving model of a male mallard that called as it moved around a circular runway, and were later tested for their preference for the model over a female model. Imprinting was strongest in ducklings exposed at about 13–16 hours after hatching, and weak in those exposed after about 30 hours. Hess also reported that ducklings that had to work harder to follow the model, for example by climbing over hurdles, imprinted more strongly, which he called the law of effort. Later studies found that the sensitive period depends on the conditions of rearing and testing, as the competitive exclusion hypothesis predicts (§5.2.2).

Imprinting differs from ordinary associative learning ([7.18.41](/7.18.41)) in several ways: it requires no conventional reward, it occurs rapidly (a few hours of exposure can suffice), it is restricted to a sensitive period, and its product is relatively stable. Lorenz believed it to be instantaneous and irreversible, but it is neither: a strong imprinting can be reversed by prolonged exposure to a new object, although the first object often remains preferred in a later test. The neural substrate in domestic chicks, a forebrain region called the intermediate medial mesopallium, and the cellular changes that accompany imprinting are described in [7.16.38](/7.16.38#sec-38.6).

### 5.3.2. Predispositions and Embryonic Experience

Imprinting is not learning on a blank slate. Gabriel Horn, Mark Johnson and colleagues found that dark-reared domestic chicks that were briefly allowed to run in a wheel, without any visual experience of a specific object, developed a preference for a stuffed jungle fowl over a rotating red box or other artificial objects. The preference depended on the features of the head and neck region: a fowl whose body parts had been cut up and rearranged was as attractive as an intact one as long as the head was present. The predisposition guides the chick toward an appropriate object, and learning then specifies the characteristics of the particular individual it follows. The two processes depend on different brain regions, since lesions of the intermediate medial mesopallium abolish learned preferences but leave the predisposition intact.

Gilbert Gottlieb (1971, 1978) studied the auditory component of filial imprinting in ducklings. Mallard ducklings hatched in an incubator, which had never heard a duck, preferred the maternal call of their own species to that of other species in their first test. This would usually be called innate. But Gottlieb found that the embryos call from inside the egg during the last days before hatching, and that ducklings that were devocalized as embryos (by sealing their vocal membranes temporarily) and incubated in isolation lost their specific preference for the mallard call. The embryos had to hear their own and their siblings' calls, which contain the frequency modulation characteristic of the maternal call, to develop the normal preference. A preference that appears in its first test without any experience of the mother still depends on experience, though of an unexpected kind: an elegant confirmation of Lehrman's argument ([7.18.3](/7.18.3)).

### 5.3.3. Imprinting-Like Processes in Mammals and Insects

Mammals show processes with similar properties. In sheep, a mother learns to recognize the odor of her own lamb within a few hours after giving birth; afterward she lets only that lamb suckle and rejects others. The sensitive period is opened by the birth itself: stimulation of the vagina and cervix during birth releases oxytocin in the brain (**oxytocin**, [7.14.34](/7.14.34)), and artificial vaginocervical stimulation can induce a ewe that has already given birth to accept an alien lamb. Lambs in turn learn to recognize their mother within days.

Ants learn the odor of their colony early in adult life: workers of several species that have just emerged and are exposed for a few days to pupae or workers of another species will later care for that species rather than their own, a process exploited by slave-making ants, whose raids bring home pupae of host species that emerge into the slave-maker's nest and work for it ([7.18.36](/7.18.36)). Honey bee workers recognize nestmates by odors acquired in the nest, from the comb wax and nestmates.

### 5.3.4. Sexual Imprinting

The characteristics of the parents learned early in life can also shape mate preferences later (**sexual imprinting**, [7.10.11](/7.10.11)). Klaus Immelmann cross-fostered zebra finches with Bengalese finches; males raised by Bengalese finches courted Bengalese finch females in preference to females of their own species when they matured, even after having lived with zebra finches for long periods. Keith Kendrick and colleagues (1998) cross-fostered sheep and goats at birth: as adults, cross-fostered males preferred to socialize and mate with the foster species, and the preference persisted for years after they had lived in mixed flocks, whereas cross-fostered females were more flexible and readily shifted to their own species. The sensitive period for sexual imprinting usually lies later than that for filial imprinting, in the weeks before the young leave their parents.

Sexual imprinting has evolutionary consequences. Because mate preferences are learned from the parents, they can change quickly and can promote reproductive isolation between populations that differ in appearance ([7.10.11](/7.10.11)). Bateson (1982) found that Japanese quail (*Coturnix japonica*) reared with their siblings later preferred partners that were slightly different from their familiar siblings, typically first cousins, over both siblings and unrelated birds, consistent with a balance between avoiding inbreeding and avoiding outbreeding (optimal outbreeding).

## 5.4. The Early Environment

### 5.4.1. Social Experience in Rhesus Monkeys

The most famous, and most troubling, experiments on early social experience were Harry and Margaret Harlow's studies of rhesus macaques (*Macaca mulatta*) in the 1950s and 1960s. Infants separated from their mothers shortly after birth were given two artificial "surrogate mothers": a wire cylinder with a bottle from which the infant could feed, and a cylinder covered with soft terry cloth without a bottle. The infants spent most of their time clinging to the cloth mother, went to the wire mother only to feed, and ran to the cloth mother when frightened. Attachment was based on contact comfort rather than on food, contrary to the then dominant view that infants attach to the mother because she provides food.

Infants reared in complete social isolation for six months or a year developed severe and lasting abnormalities: they huddled in a corner, rocked, bit themselves, and were withdrawn or excessively aggressive when they later met other monkeys; as adults they could not mate normally, and females that became mothers often neglected or abused their infants. Infants reared with mothers but no peers were also not completely normal. Yet surprisingly little social experience sufficed to prevent the worst effects. Infants that were separated from their mothers but allowed to play with three other infants for just 15 minutes a day at first clung to each other, then began to play, and as adults showed nearly normal social and sexual behavior (Harlow et al. 1971). Even monkeys isolated for six months could be largely rehabilitated by daily contact with younger, three-month-old "therapist" monkeys, which approached and clung to them without threatening them (Suomi and Harlow 1972). These experiments, which caused great suffering, would not be approved today ([7.18.2](/7.18.2#sec-2.5.2)); they were influential in changing practices in human child care, such as allowing parents to stay with hospitalized children.

### 5.4.2. Development without Parents

Some animals develop normal social behavior with almost no contact with their parents. Chicks of the Australian brushturkey (*Alectura lathami*) hatch inside a large mound of decomposing leaves, warmed by the heat of decomposition, dig their way out and leave alone, never seeing a parent. In the laboratory, Ann Göth and Christopher Evans (2004) found that a naive chick approached a robotic model of another chick that pecked at the ground: a simple pecking movement was sufficient to elicit social approach, so the chicks need no extensive social experience to recognize their own species.

Brood parasites face a similar problem: a young brown-headed cowbird (*Molothrus ater*) is raised by a different species and must later recognize other cowbirds. Mark Hauber and colleagues (2001) found that young cowbirds respond selectively to the "chatter" call of adult cowbirds, which acts as a species-specific "password" that triggers learning about their own species. Hosts may have passwords too: superb fairy-wren mothers (*Malurus cyaneus*) call to their eggs during incubation, and the chicks incorporate a note from the call into their begging calls after hatching, which allows parents to distinguish their own chicks from those of Horsfield's bronze-cuckoo, which hatch too late to learn the call (Colombelli-Négrel et al. 2012).

### 5.4.3. Early Conditions, Maternal Effects and Later Life

Conditions early in life, including those experienced by the mother, influence behavior and fitness in adulthood. Many such influences act through the mother: the phenotype of an offspring depends not only on its own genotype and environment but also on the phenotype or environment of its mother (**maternal effect**, [7.4.11](/7.4.11)), through egg size and composition, hormones deposited in the yolk, or maternal care ([7.18.4](/7.18.4#sec-4.5.1)).

Two hypotheses about the consequences of poor early conditions are not mutually exclusive.

| Hypothesis | Claim | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Developmental constraint ("silver spoon") | Individuals born in poor conditions are permanently impaired and have lower fitness in any later environment | Female yellow baboons (*Papio cynocephalus*) in Amboseli born in drought years suffered larger drops in fertility in later droughts than females born in wet years (Lea et al. 2015) |
| Predictive adaptive response | Early conditions serve as a forecast of later conditions, and individuals adjust their development to match them, which is advantageous if the forecast is correct | Snowshoe hare mothers (*Lepus americanus*) exposed to predators have high glucocorticoid levels and produce offspring with heightened stress responses, which may suit the predator-rich environment they will encounter (Sheriff et al. 2009) |

A predictive response is favored only if early conditions are a reliable predictor of later ones, as in species with short lives or strongly autocorrelated environments. In long-lived species in unpredictable environments, such as savanna baboons, early conditions are a poor forecast and the constraint effects dominate. In humans, the Dutch "Hunger Winter" of 1944–1945 provides a natural experiment: men conceived or born during the famine did not differ in intelligence test scores at age 19 from men born elsewhere in the Netherlands, a sign of the robustness of brain development, but prenatal famine exposure was later associated with differences in DNA methylation and with metabolic disease in adulthood (**developmental origins of health and disease**, [7.5.38](/7.5.38#sec-38.6)).

## 5.5. Developmental Homeostasis and Plasticity

The ability of many animals to develop normally despite unusual genes or environments, as in the 15-minute play groups of rhesus monkeys or the parentless brushturkeys, reflects a property of developmental systems.

**Definition 5.5.1 (Developmental Homeostasis)** **Developmental homeostasis** is the capacity of developmental processes to produce a functional phenotype reliably, despite genetic or environmental perturbations.

Developmental homeostasis is the behavioral counterpart of **canalization** ([7.5.1](/7.5.1)), the buffering of development against variation. It is expected to evolve for traits whose optimal value does not depend on the environment: there is one best way for a duckling to follow its mother, and a developmental system that reaches it under a wide range of conditions is favored.

For other traits, the best phenotype depends on the environment, and selection favors **developmental plasticity** ([7.5.38](/7.5.38#sec-38.8.1)), the capacity of one genotype to produce different phenotypes in different environments. The relationship between environment and phenotype for a genotype is its **norm of reaction** ([7.4.3](/7.4.3)). Plasticity can be continuous, as when the amount a bird sings increases with food supply, or discontinuous, producing a few discrete phenotypes from the same genotype (**polyphenism**, [7.5.38](/7.5.38#sec-38.8.1)). Discrete behavioral phenotypes are usually produced by developmental switches.

## 5.6. Developmental Switches and Conditional Strategies

**Definition 5.6.1 (Developmental Switch and Conditional Strategy)** A **developmental switch** is a mechanism that channels development into one of two or more discrete pathways according to an environmental cue or the individual's state, such as its nutrition, size or social environment. A **conditional strategy** is a genetically based decision rule that specifies which of several alternative behaviors (tactics) to adopt depending on the individual's condition or environment.

A conditional strategy can produce the same discrete phenotypes as a genetic polymorphism such as the ruff's supergene ([7.18.4](/7.18.4#sec-4.4)), but by a different mechanism: all individuals have the same rule, and they differ because their conditions differ. The two are distinguished experimentally by changing the condition (for instance the food of larvae) and asking whether the phenotype changes.

### 5.6.1. Age Polyethism in Honey Bees

The age-related change of job in worker honey bees ([7.18.4](/7.18.4#sec-4.1.2)) is a developmental switch that remains reversible. Young workers clean cells and nurse larvae; at about three weeks of age they become foragers. The transition is accelerated by juvenile hormone, whose level in the hemolymph rises before the onset of foraging: treating young bees with juvenile hormone or its analogs makes them forage precociously, removing the corpora allata, the glands that produce it, delays foraging, and hormone replacement restores normal timing (Sullivan et al. 2000). Juvenile hormone and the yolk protein vitellogenin, which is high in nurses, mutually suppress each other, forming a double-repressor switch (Amdam and Omholt 2003).

The timing of the switch is regulated socially. In colonies made up only of young bees, some begin foraging at 5–7 days of age; adding older foragers to such colonies delays the transition, whereas adding young bees does not (Huang and Robinson 1992). Conversely, when the foragers are removed from a normal colony, young bees start foraging earlier and the forager force is restored within days; when nurses are removed, some foragers revert to nursing. The inhibition is mediated at least partly by ethyl oleate, a substance produced by foragers and passed to nurses with the nectar they regurgitate, which delays the maturation of nurses (Leoncini et al. 2004). Zhi-Yong Huang and Gene Robinson proposed an activator–inhibitor model: each bee matures at its own rate, driven by juvenile hormone, while contact with foragers inhibits maturation. The model explains how a colony maintains an appropriate division of labor without any central control.

### 5.6.2. Phase Change in Locusts

Locusts are grasshoppers that can switch between two phases that differ in behavior, color, morphology and physiology. At low density, the desert locust (*Schistocerca gregaria*) is solitarious: cryptically green or brown, shy, mostly active at night, and avoiding other locusts. At high density it becomes gregarious: the nymphs turn black and yellow, are active by day, are attracted to other locusts, and form marching bands and, as adults, the swarms that devastate crops across Africa and Asia. The behavioral change comes first. Solitarious nymphs become behaviorally gregarious within a few hours of being crowded, whereas the changes in color and morphology take one or more molts, and the reverse change back to solitarious behavior after isolation is slower.

Stephen Simpson, Malcolm Burrows and colleagues identified the cues and the mechanism. Either the sight and smell of other locusts or repeated touching of the outer surface of the hind femora, as happens when locusts jostle each other, is sufficient to induce gregarious behavior (Simpson et al. 2001). These stimuli cause a transient rise in serotonin in the thoracic ganglia; blocking serotonin receptors prevents gregarization, and serotonin or its agonists induce gregarious behavior in isolated locusts (Anstey et al. 2009). The switch is transmitted across generations: gregarious females add substances to the foam surrounding their eggs that cause hatchlings to be gregarious, a maternal effect that accelerates the buildup of swarms.

A: equilibrium gregariousness as a function of a crowding cue; positive feedback (gregarious locusts seek each other's company, which increases the cue they experience) creates a range of intermediate crowding in which both phases are stable, separated by an unstable state, so that the phase depends on history (hysteresis). B: time course of gregariousness when an isolated nymph is crowded for 24 hours and then isolated again; gregarization takes hours, solitarization days](./plots/locust-phase.py)

The ultimate explanation of phase change is that the best behavior depends on density. At low density, being cryptic and avoiding others reduces predation; at high density, when local food is exhausted, joining a marching band and moving on is better, and the conspicuous black and yellow coloring of gregarious nymphs, which feed on toxic plants, may serve as a warning to predators (**aposematism**, [7.10.17](/7.10.17)). Cannibalism also matters: in marching bands, locusts that stop are attacked from behind by others, which drives the band forward ([7.18.27](/7.18.27)).

### 5.6.3. Cannibal Salamanders, Horned Beetles and Cichlid Status

Larvae of the tiger salamander (*Ambystoma tigrinum*) usually develop into a typical morph that feeds on small invertebrates, but some develop into a cannibal morph with a broad head and large teeth that eats other salamander larvae. The cannibal morph develops more often when larvae are crowded, when they differ greatly in size (the largest larvae become cannibals), and when the larvae around them are not relatives: larvae raised with siblings, which they recognize by odor, are less likely to become cannibals (Pfennig and Collins 1993). Similar switches produce carnivorous morphs in spadefoot toad tadpoles that feed on fairy shrimp.

In many dung beetles of the genus *Onthophagus*, males come in two forms. Large males have long horns and fight for and guard the tunnels in which females lay their eggs on buried dung; small males are hornless and sneak past guarding males through side tunnels to mate with females. Whether a male develops horns depends on its size, which depends on the amount of dung its mother provided for it as a larva: horn length is a steep sigmoid function of body size, with a threshold size below which almost no horns develop (Emlen 1994). The threshold involves insulin signaling, which links nutrition to tissue growth: horn tissue is especially sensitive to insulin, and knocking down the insulin receptor reduces horn growth in large males (Emlen et al. 2012). Within the switch, the behavior is matched to the morphology: horned males guard, hornless males sneak ([7.18.28](/7.18.28)).

![A developmental switch in a dung beetle (simulated data after *Onthophagus taurus*). A: horn length against body size: a threshold separates small, hornless males from large, horned males, with few intermediates; the two forms use different mating tactics. B: the resulting distribution of horn lengths is bimodal, although body size is unimodally distributed](./plots/horn-threshold.py)

Not all switches are restricted to early development. Males of the cichlid fish *Astatotilapia burtoni* switch repeatedly between two states during adult life. Territorial males are brightly colored, aggressive and reproductively active; non-territorial males are dull, schooling with females, and reproductively suppressed. When a non-territorial male is given the chance to become territorial by the removal of a dominant male, he changes color within minutes, his behavior within hours, and the gonadotropin-releasing hormone neurons in his hypothalamus enlarge and increase their expression of the hormone within days, followed by growth of the testes; losing a territory reverses the changes (Fernald and colleagues). The switch is controlled by social status, which determines the reproductive tactic most profitable at that moment.

### 5.6.4. When Should a Switch Evolve?

Developmental switches producing discrete phenotypes are expected when the environment presents a few discrete options rather than a continuum, when intermediate phenotypes would do poorly (a half-horned beetle can neither fight nor sneak efficiently), and when a reliable cue predicts which phenotype will be best. The threshold of the switch is itself a heritable trait that can evolve: populations of *Onthophagus taurus* introduced to different continents have diverged in the body size at which horns appear, in response to differences in competition (Moczek and Nijhout 2003). Conditional strategies in which individuals in poor condition "make the best of a bad job" by adopting a tactic with lower average success are discussed in [7.18.28](/7.18.28), and the game-theoretical analysis of when alternative tactics coexist in [7.18.25](/7.18.25).

## 5.7. The Evolutionary Development of Behavior

Evolutionary developmental biology (evo-devo) showed that animals as different as flies and mice build their bodies with a shared "toolkit" of developmental genes, such as the **Hox genes** ([7.5.15](/7.5.15)), and that evolution often works by changing the regulation of these genes rather than inventing new ones ([7.10.19](/7.10.19)). The same principle applies to behavior. The *foraging* gene influences food-related behavior in flies, bees and nematodes ([7.18.4](/7.18.4#sec-4.3.1)); vasopressin and oxytocin, and their invertebrate relatives, regulate social and reproductive behavior in animals from worms to mammals; and the differences between species often lie in where and when the receptors for these molecules are expressed ([7.18.4](/7.18.4#sec-4.3.3)).

A striking evo-devo hypothesis concerns the origin of the division of labor in social insects. Mary Jane West-Eberhard proposed that the reproductive cycle of a solitary ancestral wasp or bee, alternating between a phase of egg development and a phase of foraging to provision the young, was split among the members of a colony: queens specialized in the reproductive phase and workers in the provisioning phase (the ovarian ground plan hypothesis). Robert Page and Gro Amdam extended it to honey bees (the reproductive ground plan hypothesis): the vitellogenin–juvenile hormone network that controls egg development in solitary insects regulates the transition from nursing to foraging in workers, and worker bees selected for differences in pollen hoarding differ in their ovary size and vitellogenin levels. Comparisons of gene expression in the brains of honey bees, paper wasps and ants have found that some pathways (related to metabolism and foraging) are shared among social insects, but also that each lineage has recruited different genes, including many that are new or rapidly evolving (Toth et al. 2010). Behavior is assembled from both ancient and novel parts.

## 5.8. Behavioral Development in Insects and Fishes

The development of behavior has been studied most in birds and mammals, but insects and fishes show the same principles, often in more accessible form.

In *Drosophila*, larvae spend most of their time feeding, and the third-instar larvae reach a maximum rate of feeding before leaving the food to pupate, some species burrowing into the soil, which reduces predation. Adult behaviors mature after eclosion: female receptivity develops over about two days, as described in §5.1, and in males of some Hawaiian *Drosophila*, which court on leks, the full repertoire of courtship and fighting behaviors appears only over several weeks after eclosion, when males begin competing for matings.

Social insects show developmental sequences linked to the needs of the colony: in honey bees, the sequence of jobs from cleaning to foraging is accompanied by physiological changes, such as the development of the hypopharyngeal glands that produce brood food in nurses, of the wax glands, which peak at about two weeks of age, and of the enzymes that convert nectar to honey (§5.6.1).

In fishes, behavior changes with each life stage. Salmon (*Salmo* and *Oncorhynchus*) hatch in the gravel of streams as alevins that live on their yolk sacs; the emerging fry become territorial parr that defend feeding stations in the stream; and at the parr–smolt transformation, controlled by thyroid hormones and cortisol, they lose their territoriality, form schools, become tolerant of salt water and migrate to the sea. During this transformation they learn the odor of their home stream, which they use to find it years later when they return to spawn (Hasler; [7.18.21](/7.18.21)). In cichlids, parental care and the response of young to their parents develop in a characteristic sequence, and the young of mouthbrooding species learn to flee into the parent's mouth at a threat.

## Summary

- Behavior changes during development through maturation of the nervous system, hormones, changes in body form and experience; some motor patterns, such as swimming in amphibian larvae, develop without practice.
- A sensitive period (Definition 5.2.1) is a stage during which a particular experience has an especially large effect. Sensitive periods begin as sensory and neural systems mature and often end because experience itself commits the developing representation (competitive exclusion); deprivation can extend them.
- Filial imprinting (Definition 5.3.1) attaches precocial young to a parent within the first day or two (peak about 13–16 h in mallards); it is guided by predispositions (the head and neck of a fowl) and depends on unexpected experiences (embryonic vocalizations in ducklings). Imprinting-like learning occurs in ewes recognizing their lambs and in ants learning colony odor. **Sexual imprinting** shapes later mate preferences, more rigidly in males than in females in sheep and goats.
- Early social experience is needed for normal social development in rhesus monkeys, but brief daily contact with peers suffices; brushturkeys and cowbirds develop species recognition without parents, guided by simple cues and "passwords".
- Early conditions and **maternal effects** influence later life through developmental constraint or predictive adaptive responses; the latter require early conditions to predict later ones.
- Developmental homeostasis (Definition 5.5.1) buffers development; **developmental plasticity** adjusts it to the environment, continuously or through discrete **polyphenism**.
- Developmental switches and conditional strategies (Definition 5.6.1):

  | System | Cue | Alternative phenotypes |
  |---|---|---|
  | Honey bee workers | Age, juvenile hormone, inhibition by foragers (ethyl oleate) | Nurse, forager (reversible) |
  | Desert locust | Crowding: sight, smell, touch of hind legs; serotonin | Solitarious, gregarious |
  | Tiger salamander | Density, size variation, kinship | Typical, cannibal |
  | *Onthophagus* beetles | Larval nutrition, body size threshold, insulin signaling | Horned guarders, hornless sneakers |
  | *A. burtoni* cichlids | Social status | Territorial, non-territorial (reversible) |

- Evo-devo shows conserved molecular toolkits for behavior (PKG, vasopressin and oxytocin, the vitellogenin–juvenile hormone network) alongside lineage-specific novelties.

## Questions

1. Design an experiment to distinguish the internal clock hypothesis from the competitive exclusion hypothesis for the end of the sensitive period for filial imprinting in chicks.

   **Answer** Rear chicks under two conditions from hatching: in normal visual conditions with exposure to patterned objects, and in darkness or in diffuse light without patterned visual experience. At different ages (for example 12, 24, 48, 72 and 96 hours), expose subsets of each group once to a standard imprinting object for a fixed time, and test their preference for it the next day. The internal clock hypothesis predicts that the decline in imprinting with age is the same in both groups; the competitive exclusion hypothesis predicts that the decline occurs later in the visually deprived chicks, since no competing representation has formed. An additional test: imprint chicks on object A at the peak, then expose them to object B at later ages; competitive exclusion predicts that the strength of the first imprinting determines how much B can be learned.
2. Gottlieb's devocalized ducklings lost their preference for the mallard maternal call. Does this show that the preference is "learned"? How does the experiment bear on the concept of instinct?

   **Answer** It shows that the normal development of the preference depends on a particular experience, hearing the embryonic calls of oneself and one's siblings, so the preference is not independent of experience as the term "innate" implied. But it is not learned in the ordinary sense either: the ducklings never heard their mother, and the experience they need is not of the object of the preference. The experiment shows that behaviors that appear in their first performance without obvious experience can depend on experiences that are part of the normal environment of development (species-typical experience); the question "innate or learned?" should be replaced by the question of which experiences, at which times, are necessary for the normal development of the behavior.
3. In Kendrick's cross-fostering experiments, male sheep and goats retained their preference for the foster species much more rigidly than females did. Propose an ultimate explanation for this sex difference.

   **Answer** One hypothesis: in polygynous ungulates, females are the choosier sex and invest heavily in each offspring, so a mistake in mate choice is costly for them, and selection may have favored females that can correct an early learned preference using later information (such as the response of potential mates or the cues of their own species), whereas males, which gain by mating with any receptive female and rarely suffer large costs from courting the wrong species, may have been under weaker selection to revise early preferences. Another possibility is that sexual imprinting in males evolved to promote choice of the best females of their species based on maternal phenotype, and the stability of early-learned preferences is a byproduct. The hypotheses could be tested by comparing species with different mating systems and parental investment patterns.
4. Explain why the honey bee model in §5.6.1 produces both foragers and nurses in a single-cohort colony, even though all bees are the same age. What would happen if the inhibition were removed?

   **Answer** Bees differ in their intrinsic rates of maturation. The fastest-maturing bees reach the threshold first and become foragers; as foragers accumulate, they inhibit the maturation of the remaining bees, so the rest remain nurses for longer, and a stable division of labor emerges from the interaction of individual variation and social inhibition. Without inhibition, every bee would reach the threshold at an age set by its own rate, so almost the whole cohort would become foragers within about a week or two, leaving no nurses, which would be disastrous for a colony with brood.
5. Using the bistable model of locust phase change, explain why a locust population can remain gregarious at a density lower than that at which it became gregarious. What practical consequence does this have for locust control?

   **Answer** In the bistable range of crowding, both the solitarious and the gregarious states are stable. A population that has become gregarious at high crowding remains on the gregarious branch as crowding decreases, because gregarious locusts seek each other's company, which keeps the crowding cue they experience high (positive feedback), until crowding falls below the lower limit of the bistable range; this is hysteresis. The maternal transmission of phase reinforces it. Practically, preventing outbreaks is much easier than stopping them: control should target areas where solitarious locusts are concentrating (for example after rains, when vegetation becomes patchy) before the switch occurs, since reducing density moderately in a gregarious population may not return it to the solitarious phase.
6. How would you determine whether the horned and hornless males of a dung beetle represent a genetic polymorphism or a conditional strategy?

   **Answer** Rear offspring of horned and hornless fathers under controlled feeding: provide larvae with different amounts of dung. If the morph is determined by a conditional strategy, the morph of the offspring depends on the amount of food (and resulting body size), not on the father's morph; offspring of horned fathers that receive little food are hornless, and offspring of hornless fathers that receive much food are horned. If it is a genetic polymorphism, offspring resemble their fathers independently of food. A breeding experiment can also estimate the heritability of the threshold body size, which in a conditional strategy may vary genetically (as in diverged populations of *O. taurus*) even though the morph itself is environmentally determined.
7. (Open question) Female yellow baboons born in drought years have reduced fertility in later droughts. What evidence would distinguish a developmental constraint from a predictive adaptive response in this case?

   **Answer** The two hypotheses make different predictions about the interaction between early and later conditions. A developmental constraint predicts that females born in poor conditions do worse in all later environments, or at least not better in any. A predictive adaptive response predicts a crossover: females born in drought should do better than females born in wet years when they later experience drought, and worse when they experience wet conditions. The Amboseli data show the opposite of the predictive prediction (females born in drought did worse in later droughts), supporting the constraint hypothesis, which is expected because conditions at birth do not predict conditions years later in an unpredictable savanna. Further evidence would come from physiological measures (for example, growth, immune function, age at first birth) and from experiments in shorter-lived species in which the environment can be manipulated.
