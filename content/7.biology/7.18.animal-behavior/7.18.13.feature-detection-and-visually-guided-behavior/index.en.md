An animal's sense organs are bombarded with far more information than it could ever use, and most of it is irrelevant. A toad sitting at dusk at the edge of a path sees grass moving in the wind, the shadows of branches, beetles, worms, the approaching silhouette of a heron and the looming foot of a hiker; it must respond to two or three of these and ignore the rest. The noctuid moth of the previous chapter solved a simpler version of the problem with an ear that detects only the pulsed ultrasound of bats ([7.18.12](/7.18.12)). This chapter examines how visual systems pick out the stimuli that call for action and convert them into orienting, striking or escape. Three classic systems provide the examples: the recognition of prey and predators by the common toad, analyzed by Jörg-Peter Ewert from the 1960s onward; the detection of objects on a collision course by the locust's lobula giant movement detector; and the interception of flying prey by dragonflies. A final section treats the use of optic flow for flight control in insects, the visual counterpart of the moth's simple but sufficient code.

The cellular physiology of vision, from phototransduction to cortical receptive fields, is treated in [7.14.39](/7.14.39)–[7.14.41](/7.14.41), [7.16.17](/7.16.17) and [7.16.18](/7.16.18), and the sensory ecology of vision in [7.18.10](/7.18.10). The escape circuits driven by looming detectors, and the related idea of a command neuron, follow in [7.18.14](/7.18.14).

## 13.1. Stimulus Filtering

### 13.1.1. Filtering in the Periphery and in the Brain

Von Uexküll's notion of the **Umwelt** ([7.18.9](/7.18.9)) expresses the fact that each species perceives only a small selection of the physical world. The selection is made by the nervous system at successive stages.

**Definition 13.1.1 (Stimulus Filtering)** **Stimulus filtering** is the selective transmission of biologically relevant features of the sensory input, and the suppression of the rest, by the properties of sense organs (peripheral filtering) and of central neurons and circuits (central filtering).

Peripheral filtering occurs because receptors respond only to a range of stimulus energies, frequencies or spatial patterns, and because their sensitivity is matched to the signals that matter:

- The moth's A1 receptor responds only to ultrasound, preferentially to pulses ([7.18.12](/7.18.12#sec-12.5.1)).
- Females of the parasitoid fly *Ormia ochracea*, which deposit their larvae on singing male crickets, have ears most sensitive at $4$–$5\,\mathrm{kHz}$, the carrier frequency of cricket song; males of the same species, which do not search for hosts, are not especially sensitive in this range (Robert and colleagues 1992). Females of another tachinid fly, *Therobia leonidei*, which parasitizes bush crickets, are tuned to the $20$–$30\,\mathrm{kHz}$ songs of their hosts, and they parasitize a species with long, many-syllable chirps (*Poecilimon mariannae*) more heavily than a congener that sings single syllables (Lehmann and Heller 1998): the parasitoid's ear is tuned to the host's song, and the most conspicuous songs pay the highest price ([7.18.16](/7.18.16)).
- Female plainfin midshipman fish (*Porichthys notatus*) become more sensitive to higher frequencies in the breeding season, when the hum of nesting males, with harmonics up to about $400\,\mathrm{Hz}$, is the signal that matters. The change lies in the hair cells of the saccule and is induced by steroid hormones (Sisneros and Bass 2003): the filter itself is seasonally retuned.

Central filtering operates on the output of the receptors. The star-nosed mole devotes about a quarter of the somatosensory cortex that represents its nose to the two small rays nearest the mouth, which carry only about $7\,\%$ of the touch receptors of the star; a disproportionate share of the brain analyses the part of the input that guides the final strike at prey ([7.18.11](/7.18.11)). The toad and the locust provide more detailed examples, in which the central filter can be followed from neuron to neuron.

### 13.1.2. Feature Detectors

In 1959 Jerome Lettvin, Humberto Maturana, Warren McCulloch and Walter Pitts published a paper with the memorable title *What the frog's eye tells the frog's brain*. Recording from the optic nerve fibers of frogs while moving objects in front of the eye, they distinguished four classes of retinal ganglion cells, one of which responded best when a small dark object entered its **receptive field** ([7.14.39](/7.14.39)), stopped and moved jerkily. They called these cells "bug detectors" and concluded that the eye does not transmit an image to the brain but tells it about a few behaviorally important features. The paper inspired a search for feature detectors throughout the nervous system.

**Definition 13.1.2 (Feature Detector)** A **feature detector** is a neuron that responds selectively to a particular complex configuration of a stimulus (such as a small object moving in a particular way, a looming object, or a pulse–echo pair with a particular delay), much more than to other stimuli of similar energy.

Feature detectors are found at many levels: in the retina (direction-selective ganglion cells), the insect optic lobe (looming detectors, Section 13.3), the auditory cortex of bats (delay-tuned neurons, [7.18.12](/7.18.12#sec-12.4.3)) and the temporal cortex of primates (face-selective cells). The toad shows how a feature detector is constructed from simpler elements and how much of the selectivity of behavior it explains.

## 13.2. Prey and Predator Recognition in the Toad

### 13.2.1. The Behavior

When a hungry common toad (*Bufo bufo*) sees a small object moving in its visual field, it responds with a characteristic sequence: it turns its head and body toward the object (orienting), walks toward it if it is far away (approaching), fixates it binocularly, snaps with its sticky tongue, swallows and wipes its mouth with a forelimb. Each element is released by particular stimulus conditions, and the sequence can be broken off at any point: if the object stops moving, the toad waits; if the object disappears after the snap has been triggered, the toad snaps, swallows and wipes its mouth nonetheless. The snap, once released, runs to completion like a **fixed action pattern** ([7.18.3](/7.18.3)). A large object approaching from above or a looming silhouette releases the opposite behaviors: the toad ducks, turns away, puffs itself up and raises itself on its legs, or flees.

The orienting turn is the most convenient measure of prey recognition, because it is the first and most frequent element. Ewert placed toads in a small glass cylinder and moved dummies, simple black cardboard shapes, around the cylinder at constant angular velocity on a white background, counting the number of orienting turns per minute. With such dummies the features that define "prey" and "threat" in the toad's world could be varied one at a time.

### 13.2.2. Worm and Antiworm

The most important result concerns the relation between the shape of an object and the direction of its motion. A narrow bar ($2.5°$ wide) moved in the direction of its long axis, like a crawling worm, elicits vigorous prey-catching; the same bar moved perpendicular to its long axis elicits few or no orienting turns and, if large, avoidance. Ewert called the two configurations *worm* and *antiworm*. The key variable is the length of the object along the direction of motion relative to its length across it, not its absolute orientation in space: a bar moving vertically along its long axis, like a caterpillar climbing a grass stalk, is also treated as prey. Varying the length $L$ of the bar systematically gives three characteristic curves:

| Configuration | Edge parallel to motion | Edge perpendicular to motion | Prey-catching as $L$ increases from $2.5°$ to $32°$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Worm | $L$ | $2.5°$ | Increases steadily: "wormier" is better |
| Antiworm | $2.5°$ | $L$ | Decreases from the start and soon vanishes |
| Square | $L$ | $L$ | Rises for small squares (like worms), falls for larger ones, and large squares elicit avoidance |

The toad's prey category is thus defined by a simple rule, which can be written as a comparison between two measurements: the extension of the object along its direction of motion (which favors prey) and its extension across the direction of motion (which disfavors it). The rule is invariant over the direction of movement, the position in the visual field and, within limits, the speed, and it is applied equally to stimuli that the toad has never encountered. The rule is also modulated by the state of the animal and by experience. Hungry toads respond more than satiated ones; toads that have been fed repeatedly from the experimenter's hand learn to orient toward the hand, a large object that would normally be avoided; and prey-catching is suppressed in a toad that has just been handled. Stimulus filtering in the toad is therefore not a fixed property of the eye.

### 13.2.3. Searching for the Detector

Where in the visual system is the worm–antiworm distinction made? Ewert and his colleagues recorded from neurons at each level of the visual pathway of immobilized toads while moving the same dummies as in the behavioral experiments, and asked which neurons, if any, discriminated the configurations as the whole animal does. The retina projects to two main targets: the optic tectum, the roof of the midbrain (homologous to the superior colliculus of mammals), and the thalamic-pretectal area of the diencephalon. The projection to the tectum is entirely crossed in amphibians and retinotopic: neighboring points in the visual field are represented at neighboring points of the tectal surface, forming a map of visual space.

- *Retina.* Four classes of ganglion cells, R1 to R4, differ in receptive field size (from a few degrees to about $15°$) and in their preferences for contrast, motion and dimming. None of them discriminates worm from antiworm: their responses depend mainly on the size of the object relative to the excitatory center of the receptive field and on the inhibitory surround (**lateral inhibition**, [7.14.39](/7.14.39)). The frog's "bug detectors" (class R2) respond well to small objects of any configuration.
- *Thalamic-pretectal area.* Of at least ten classes of neurons, the TP3 class responds best to large moving objects and to antiworm bars, and weakly to worm bars.
- *Optic tectum.* Of eight classes (T1 to T8), the T5 neurons respond to moving objects. The T5(1) subclass responds to an increase of the extension of the object parallel to its direction of motion but also to squares, and discriminates poorly. The T5(2) subclass shows the selectivity of the whole animal: its firing increases with the length of a worm bar, decreases with the length of an antiworm bar and first rises then falls with the size of a square.

Three further kinds of evidence connect the T5(2) neurons to prey-catching. First, electrical stimulation through electrodes implanted in the tectum of freely moving toads elicits orienting, snapping and swallowing, directed toward the part of the visual field that corresponds to the stimulated point on the retinotopic map, as if prey were present there; stimulation of the thalamic-pretectal area elicits avoidance and ducking. Second, T5(2) neurons project to premotor regions in the medulla and spinal cord that control orienting and snapping. Third, the selectivity of T5(2) cells depends on inhibition from the pretectum. After lesions of the thalamic-pretectal area, tectal neurons respond much more strongly, T5(2) neurons lose their configurational selectivity, and the toads, tested behaviorally, snap at every moving object, large or small, worm or antiworm, including the experimenter's hand, and no longer show avoidance. They also snap at their own moving limbs and misjudge distances: the pretectum normally distinguishes movement of objects from image motion caused by the toad's own movements.

The interpretation is that T5(2) prey selectivity is computed by subtraction: excitation from tectal neurons that respond to object extension in the direction of motion (T5(1)) minus inhibition from pretectal neurons that respond to extension across the direction of motion and to large size (TP3). The model reproduces the three behavioral curves and the effect of the lesion.

### 13.2.4. Feature Detectors Reconsidered

Ewert's work gave neuroethology one of its paradigms, but its interpretation has changed. In the 1970s T5(2) neurons were often presented as "prey detectors" and the analogous pretectal neurons as "predator detectors", single cells that recognize a behavioral category and release the corresponding behavior, as the ethologists' **innate releasing mechanism** was supposed to do ([7.18.3](/7.18.3)). Several observations argue against such a literal reading:

- The selectivity of T5(2) neurons is graded. They respond best to worms but also to small squares and weakly to antiworms; the categorical decision "prey or not" is made by the population of T5(2) cells and the circuits they drive, not by any one cell.
- The selectivity is constructed by a network that includes inhibition from the pretectum, modulatory inputs from the telencephalon (the striatum and the medial pallium, which mediate the effects of learning and motivation) and the state of the animal. Hungry and satiated toads, or naive and hand-fed ones, process the same stimulus differently.
- A single class of neurons is necessary but not sufficient for a behavior. Ewert himself later replaced the idea of a "prey detector" with that of a *command releasing system*: a combination of neurons whose joint activity, read out by premotor circuits, releases one element of the behavior sequence.

The same development occurred in other systems. Delay-tuned neurons in the bat's cortex, face cells in the monkey's temporal cortex and the "grandmother cells" postulated by theorists are all selective, but what an animal recognizes is represented by the joint activity of many such neurons, a **population code**. The value of the feature-detector concept lies elsewhere: it emphasizes that the nervous system extracts behaviorally relevant variables early and explicitly, and it guides the experimenter to look for neurons whose tuning matches the behavior. The question that follows, whether there are neurons that do not merely detect a stimulus but command a behavior, is taken up in [7.18.14](/7.18.14).

## 13.3. Detecting Collision: Looming

### 13.3.1. The Locust LGMD

An object approaching on a collision course produces a characteristic image: it expands slowly at first and then explosively in the last moments before contact. Detecting this "looming" is vital for an animal that may be struck by a predator or collide with obstacles or conspecifics, and it is one of the visual computations that appear in almost every animal with eyes.

**Definition 13.3.1 (Looming Detection)** **Looming detection** is the detection of objects approaching on a collision course from the expansion of their image on the retina, as distinct from objects that recede, pass by or are merely large.

The best-understood looming detector is a single identified neuron in the optic lobe of locusts.

**Definition 13.3.2 (LGMD)** The **lobula giant movement detector** (LGMD) is a large identified interneuron in the third optic neuropil (the lobula) of locusts and other orthopterans, which responds selectively to objects approaching on a collision course and drives, through its one-to-one synapse onto the descending contralateral movement detector (DCMD), the escape jumps and the collision-avoidance steering of flight.

The LGMD has a huge fan-shaped dendritic field that receives excitatory input from retinotopic motion-sensitive columns covering the whole eye, and two smaller fields that receive inhibitory inputs. Its spikes are relayed by the DCMD, whose large axon conducts them in about $10\,\mathrm{ms}$ to the thoracic motor centers. The response to an approaching object rises as the object nears and peaks shortly before collision; the response to a receding object, to a translating object of the same size, or to whole-field motion is much weaker. Two mechanisms produce this selectivity. Lateral inhibition between neighboring inputs suppresses responses to edges that move slowly across the eye, while the rapid expansion of a looming image outruns the inhibition. And a feed-forward inhibition proportional to the size of the image arrives at the inhibitory dendrites and switches off the response when the image becomes very large.

### 13.3.2. The Eta Function

Gabbiani, Laurent and colleagues described the LGMD response quantitatively. An object of half-size $l$ approaching at constant speed $v$ subtends at time $t$ before collision ($t < 0$) the angle

$$
\theta(t) = 2\arctan\frac{l}{v\,\lvert t\rvert}.
\tag{13.1}
$$
The image grows slowly at first and very rapidly near collision; the time course depends only on the ratio $l/\lvert v\rvert$, which has the dimension of time. The firing rate of the LGMD was found to follow

$$
\eta(t) = C\,\dot\theta(t - \delta)\,\mathrm{e}^{-\alpha\,\theta(t - \delta)},
\tag{13.2}
$$
the product of the angular velocity of the image and a negative exponential of its angular size, with a delay $\delta$ of a few tens of milliseconds. Early in the approach the angular velocity term dominates and the response grows; late in the approach the exponential term, which corresponds to the size-dependent inhibition, overwhelms it and the response falls. The peak of $\eta$ occurs when the image reaches a fixed angular size $\theta_{\mathrm{thr}}$, independent of the size and speed of the object; the neuron's peak firing therefore occurs a fixed delay after the object reaches the threshold angle, and the time of the peak before collision is a linear function of $l/\lvert v\rvert$. This linear relation is what was observed in recordings, with threshold angles between about $15°$ and $40°$ in different preparations.

How does a neuron multiply? Gabbiani and colleagues (2002) showed that the LGMD implements Eq. (13.2) by combining logarithmic and exponential transformations: its excitatory inputs, summed on the dendrite, produce a membrane potential approximately proportional to $\log\dot\theta$; the inhibitory inputs subtract a term proportional to $\theta$; and the conversion of membrane potential into firing rate at the spike-initiation zone is approximately exponential. Since $\exp(\log\dot\theta - \alpha\theta) = \dot\theta\,\mathrm{e}^{-\alpha\theta}$, the neuron multiplies by adding in the logarithmic domain. Blocking the inhibition pharmacologically abolished the peak, and the response kept growing until collision, as the model predicts.

A: angular size of the approaching object (dotted, scaled to 180°) and the modeled LGMD response η (solid, each normalized to its peak) against time to collision, for three ratios of half-size to speed, l/|v| = 10, 30 and 50 ms (red, orange, blue). Fast or small objects (low l/|v|) loom late and produce a late, sharp peak; large or slow ones (high l/|v|) produce an early, broad response. B: time of the response peak before collision against l/|v|. The relation is linear: the peak always occurs 27 ms after the image has reached a fixed angle of about 24°, so the neuron signals a fixed angular threshold, giving the locust a warning time that grows with the object's size-to-speed ratio.](./plots/loom-eta.py)

The behavioral output fits the neuron. A locust's escape jump is prepared in stages: the hindlegs are flexed and the tibiae locked in a "cocked" position, energy is stored in elastic cuticle, and the legs are then released. Fotowat and Gabbiani (2007) recorded DCMD activity in freely behaving locusts confronted with looming stimuli. The cocking of the legs began when the DCMD firing rate rose, the number of spikes during the rising phase determined whether the locust jumped, and the takeoff occurred close to the peak and decline of the firing, when the stimulus had crossed its threshold angle. In flight, DCMD activity triggers steering away from the approaching object and, if it is close, gliding dives. In a swarm of thousands of locusts, this circuit prevents collisions; the LGMD habituates to repeated stimuli in the same part of the visual field, which may help locusts in swarms ignore their neighbors' predictable movements.

### 13.3.3. Looming Detectors in Flies and Vertebrates

*Drosophila* escapes from looming objects with a jump and takeoff. Card and Dickinson (2008), filming flies at high speed as a black disk expanded toward them, discovered that flies prepare their takeoff in the roughly $200\,\mathrm{ms}$ before the jump: they shift their center of mass by repositioning the middle legs so that the jump carries them away from the threat, a behavior that requires the visual system to encode the direction of the looming object. Several classes of visual projection neurons that connect the optic lobe to the central brain are tuned to looming. One class (LPLC2) responds only to the radial expansion of dark edges from a point, as produced by an object on a collision course (Klapoetke and colleagues 2017), and another (LC4) responds to the angular velocity of expansion. Both converge onto the giant fiber neuron, which drives the fastest form of takeoff, and the timing of a single spike in the giant fiber relative to the onset of a slower, parallel pathway decides between two kinds of escape ([7.18.14](/7.18.14)).

Looming detection also triggers defensive behavior in vertebrates. Larval zebrafish perform escape bends in response to expanding dark disks, mediated by tectal neurons that project to the Mauthner cell ([7.18.14](/7.18.14)). Mice exposed to a dark disk expanding overhead, the image of a swooping bird of prey, either flee to a shelter or freeze, depending on whether a shelter is available, through neurons of the superior colliculus that project to the amygdala and the periaqueductal gray (Yilmaz and Meister 2013). Human infants blink and pull back their heads from looming patterns within the first weeks of life. Such generality suggests that the problem of detecting an approaching object has a small number of good solutions, and that the threshold-angle computation of Eq. (13.2) is one of them.

## 13.4. Interception: Dragonflies

### 13.4.1. The Problem of Catching a Flying Insect

Dragonflies are among the most successful aerial predators: they catch flying insects on the wing, and in laboratory and field studies perched dragonflies succeed in more than $90\,\%$ of their attempts (Olberg and colleagues 2000). Catching a flying target requires the predator to solve a guidance problem: where to head when the prey is moving. Engineers designing guided missiles have analyzed three solutions:

- *Pure pursuit.* The pursuer always heads directly at the present position of the target. This is easy to compute, but the pursuer ends up behind the target, turning ever more sharply, and the path is long.
- *Constant bearing* (parallel navigation). The pursuer steers so that the line of sight to the target keeps a constant direction in space. If the target moves in a straight line at constant speed, this puts the pursuer on a collision course, the shortest path to interception. A constant bearing is detected visually as a target image that does not drift across the eye, so the rule can be implemented by steering so as to cancel the drift of the image.
- *Proportional navigation.* The pursuer turns at a rate proportional to the rate of rotation of the line of sight, $\dot\gamma = N\dot\lambda$, with a navigation constant $N$ usually between 2 and 5. It approximates constant bearing and copes better with targets that change course.

Perched dragonflies (*Libellula*, *Plathemis*) take off and fly a nearly straight interception course, keeping the image of the prey at a constant position in the dorsal part of the eye, where a region of high acuity, the dorsal fovea, looks forward and upward. Peregrine falcons attacking lures follow proportional navigation with $N \approx 3$ (Brighton and colleagues 2017), and some robber flies use proportional navigation with an additional bias that leads them to anticipate the target. The diversity of predators that use the same guidance laws, from insects to birds, again suggests that physics leaves few good solutions.

### 13.4.2. Internal Models

Mischiati and colleagues (2015) filmed dragonflies (*Plathemis lydia*) catching artificial prey, small beads pulled along a fishing line at constant or variable speed, at high speed, while recording the movements of the head and body in three dimensions. Two results showed that the dragonfly does not simply react to the image of the prey.

First, the head moves to keep the prey image on the fovea, but it does so even during rapid rotations of the body that the dragonfly makes to align itself with the prey: the head counter-rotates in anticipation of the body's own movement, with no delay, too early to be a reaction to visual feedback. This requires a forward model of the effect of the dragonfly's own motor commands on its body orientation, an internal model of the kind postulated in human motor control, based on an **efference copy** of the motor command ([7.16.29](/7.16.29#sec-29.3)).

Second, the head also anticipates the motion of the prey: its movements lead the image of a prey moving at constant speed, so that the image stays on the fovea with little error, but errors grow when the prey changes speed unexpectedly. The dragonfly predicts where the prey will be, and the steering of the body follows the head. Interception is therefore not a reflex driven by the current image but a predictive control process, with the head acting as a stabilized platform for the sensors.

On the neural side, the dragonfly's lobula contains small-target motion detectors that respond to small dark objects moving against cluttered backgrounds and suppress responses to large objects and whole-field motion; some of them show predictive gain modulation, enhancing their sensitivity just ahead of a moving target (Wiederman and colleagues 2017). Their output converges onto eight pairs of target-selective descending neurons that carry the information to the thoracic flight motor centers; each has a receptive field in the dorsal fovea and a preferred direction, and the population vector of their activity encodes the direction of the prey's motion with enough precision to steer the interception (Gonzalez-Bellido and colleagues 2013). The motor system thus reads a population code from a small set of feature detectors, combining the ideas of Sections 13.1 and 13.2.

## 13.5. Optic Flow and Flight Control

A flying insect moves through a world that streams past its eyes. The pattern of image motion across the whole visual field, the optic flow, depends on the insect's own rotation and translation and on the distances of the objects around it: rotation produces flow that is independent of distance, whereas translation produces flow whose speed is proportional to the speed of flight and inversely proportional to distance. Insects, whose compound eyes have low spatial resolution and closely spaced eyes that provide little stereoscopic depth information, use optic flow to control flight.

### 13.5.1. The Optomotor Response

An insect placed in a rotating striped drum turns in the direction of the rotation, an optomotor response that stabilizes its course against involuntary turns. Hassenstein and Reichardt (1956) analyzed the turning of the beetle *Chlorophanus*, walking on a Y-maze globe inside a drum, in response to sequences of stimuli at neighboring **ommatidia**. The beetle turned in the direction of a sequence of light changes between two neighboring ommatidia, and the strength of its response depended on the product of the signals with a delay; the scheme that explains this, the **Reichardt detector**, correlates the delayed signal of one receptor with the undelayed signal of its neighbor and subtracts the mirror-symmetric term ([7.16.17](/7.16.17)). In flies, the elementary motion detectors have since been identified as the T4 and T5 cells of the optic lobe, and the large tangential cells of the lobula plate (the horizontal and vertical system cells) integrate their outputs over large parts of the visual field. Each tangential cell has a receptive field whose local preferred directions match the flow field produced by rotation about a particular axis (Krapp and Hengstenberg 1996): these cells are matched filters for self-motion, feature detectors on the scale of the whole visual field, and they drive the head and wing movements that compensate for rotation ([7.17.27](/7.17.27#sec-27.5.6)).

### 13.5.2. Bees: Centering, Landing and Odometry

Srinivasan and colleagues discovered that honey bees use translational optic flow, the image speed of nearby surfaces, to control their flight. Bees flying through a tunnel to a food source flew along its midline. When one wall of the tunnel was moved in the direction of flight, the bees flew closer to it, and when it was moved against the direction of flight, they flew farther away (Srinivasan and colleagues 1991). The bees balanced the image speeds on the two eyes: moving a wall in the direction of flight reduces the image speed it produces, so the bee must approach it to restore the balance. The rule requires neither a measurement of distance nor of flight speed. A similar rule controls flight speed: in a tunnel that narrows, bees slow down, keeping the image speed of the walls roughly constant.

Landing follows from the same principle. A bee landing on a flat surface keeps the angular speed of the ground image, $\omega = v/h$ for forward speed $v$ at height $h$, approximately constant while descending at a roughly constant angle. With $\omega$ constant, forward speed is automatically proportional to height, and both decrease exponentially as the bee approaches the ground, so that the bee arrives with almost zero speed without ever measuring its speed or its height (Srinivasan and colleagues 2000).

Optic flow also tells a bee how far it has flown. Bees trained to collect food at the end of a narrow tunnel lined with a fine pattern, which generates intense optic flow, signal in their waggle dances a distance many times greater than the actual length of the tunnel, and bees searching for food in a tunnel search at the position where the accumulated image motion equals that experienced in training (Esch and Burns 1996; Srinivasan and colleagues 2000). The bee's odometer integrates image motion, not energy expenditure or the number of wingbeats; how this measurement is combined with a compass in path integration is discussed in [7.18.21](/7.18.21).

These controllers have been copied by engineers, who use optic flow sensors for the guidance of small drones, as the LGMD has been copied in collision detectors for vehicles. Their attraction is the same as for the insects: they solve a control problem with a few variables that can be computed cheaply from a low-resolution image, without reconstructing the three-dimensional world.

## Summary

- Stimulus filtering (Definition 13.1.1) selects behaviorally relevant inputs at the periphery (moth A1, ears of parasitoid flies tuned to their hosts, the seasonally retuned saccule of the midshipman) and in central circuits (cortical magnification in the star-nosed mole).
- Feature detectors (Definition 13.1.2) respond to complex stimulus configurations: frog "bug detectors", bat delay-tuned neurons, looming detectors, face cells.

| System | Behavior | Key neurons | Computation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Toad | Prey catching vs avoidance; worm, antiworm, square dummies | Retina R1–R4 (unselective); tectal T5(2) (prey); pretectal TP3 (large and antiworm) | T5(2) ≈ T5(1) − TP3; tectal stimulation releases prey catching, pretectal lesions abolish selectivity and avoidance |
| Locust | Escape jumps, collision avoidance in flight | LGMD → DCMD (Definition 13.3.2) | $\eta = \dot\theta\,\mathrm{e}^{-\alpha\theta}$ (Eq. (13.2)), peak at a threshold angle; multiplication by log–exp |
| *Drosophila* | Postural preparation and takeoff | LPLC2, LC4 → giant fiber | Size and velocity of expansion; spike timing selects escape mode ([7.18.14](/7.18.14)) |
| Dragonfly | Interception of flying prey | Small-target motion detectors → 8 pairs of target-selective descending neurons | Constant bearing; predictive head control with internal models; population vector |
| Flies, bees | Course stabilization, centering, landing, odometry | T4/T5 elementary motion detectors (Reichardt), lobula plate tangential cells | Rotational flow canceled; translational flow balanced; $\omega = v/h$ constant in landing |

- Feature detectors are best seen as elements of population codes and of circuits shaped by inhibition, modulation and learning, not as single cells that recognize a category (Ewert's command releasing systems).
- Looming detection (Definition 13.3.1) is a general visual computation found in insects, fish, mice and humans; interception laws (pursuit, constant bearing, proportional navigation) recur from insects to falcons.

## Questions

1. A toad with a lesion of the thalamic-pretectal area snaps at a large square that an intact toad would avoid. What does this result show about how prey selectivity is generated, and why is it not sufficient to conclude that the pretectum contains "predator detectors"?

   **Answer** The result shows that the configurational selectivity of prey catching depends on inhibition from the pretectum onto the tectum: without it, tectal neurons (including T5(2)) respond to all moving objects in proportion to size, and so does the toad. It does not establish predator detectors, because the lesion experiment shows only that pretectal neurons are necessary for the suppression of prey catching to large objects. To show that they signal predators, one would need to show that their activity is sufficient to elicit avoidance (electrical stimulation of the pretectum does elicit avoidance), that their selectivity matches the stimuli that elicit avoidance in behavior, and that their output reaches the motor circuits of avoidance. Even then, the recognition would be performed by a population of neurons and their interactions, not by single "detector" cells.

2. Using Eq. (13.1), show that a looming image grows by half of its final size in the last few percent of the approach. For an object with $l/\lvert v\rvert = 20\,\mathrm{ms}$, at what time before collision does it subtend $24°$, and when does the LGMD response peak if $\delta = 27\,\mathrm{ms}$?

   **Answer** For small angles $\theta \approx 2l/(v\lvert t\rvert)$, so the angular size is inversely proportional to the time to collision: halving the remaining time doubles the size, and most of the expansion occurs in the last moments. An object subtends $24°$ when $\tan 12° = l/(v\lvert t\rvert)$, that is, $\lvert t\rvert = (l/\lvert v\rvert)/\tan 12° = 20/0.2126 \approx 94\,\mathrm{ms}$ before collision. The response peak follows $27\,\mathrm{ms}$ later, about $67\,\mathrm{ms}$ before collision, which leaves time for a jump that takes some tens of milliseconds to execute.

3. Why does a pure-pursuit strategy lead the pursuer into an increasingly tight turn behind its target, whereas a constant-bearing strategy produces a straight path?

   **Answer** In pure pursuit the pursuer heads at the target's present position; as the target moves sideways, the pursuer's heading must keep rotating to follow it, and the pursuer swings in behind the target, so that the final approach is a tail chase with the rotation rate increasing as the distance falls. In constant bearing the pursuer heads at the point where the target will be when the two meet; if the target moves straight at constant speed, this point is fixed and the pursuer's path to it is straight. The line of sight translates parallel to itself instead of rotating, which is why the target image stays fixed on the pursuer's eye.

4. Describe the experiment by which Srinivasan and colleagues showed that bees center in a tunnel by balancing image speed rather than by measuring distances, and predict what bees do if both walls move in the direction of flight at the bee's own speed.

   **Answer** Bees flew through a tunnel with patterned walls to a feeder; with stationary walls they flew along the midline. Moving one wall in the bee's direction of flight reduced the image speed on that side, and the bees flew closer to that wall; moving it against the direction of flight increased the image speed, and the bees flew closer to the opposite wall. Since the distances to the walls were not changed, the bees must have been balancing image speeds. If both walls move with the bee at its own speed, neither eye sees translational flow: the centering cue disappears and the bee's lateral position becomes unstable, and since the same cue regulates flight speed, bees would be expected to speed up in an attempt to restore image motion.

5. (Open question) Dragonflies anticipate both their own body rotation and the movement of their prey. Design experiments that separate the two kinds of prediction.

   **Answer** Prediction of self-motion: perturb the dragonfly's body during an interception (for example by a brief air puff or a mechanical push of a tethered animal) and compare head stabilization after unexpected perturbations with that during the dragonfly's own voluntary rotations; self-generated rotations should be compensated without delay, external ones only after the visual and mechanosensory latency. Prediction of prey motion: use prey whose speed or direction changes at unpredictable moments (as Mischiati and colleagues did with variable-speed prey) and measure the tracking error immediately after the change; a predictive controller shows a transient error in the direction of the old trajectory, whereas a purely reactive one shows a constant lag. Recording from the target-selective descending neurons during such trials would show whether their activity leads or lags the prey image.
