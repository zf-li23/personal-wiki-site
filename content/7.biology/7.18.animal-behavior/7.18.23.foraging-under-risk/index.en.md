The models of [7.18.22](/7.18.22) assumed a forager that knows its environment, gains energy at a predictable rate and has nothing to fear. Real foragers know little about the patches they are about to visit, meet food at random, may die of starvation if they are unlucky, and are hunted while they feed. This chapter adds these complications one at a time. Uncertainty about the environment makes information valuable, so foragers sample; uncertainty about the outcome of a choice makes the variance of rewards matter as well as their mean; and the forager's internal state, its reserves and the time left before night, changes the value of food, a problem solved by dynamic programming. The solutions explain why small birds in winter fatten in the afternoon, why some of them store food instead, and how a few food-storing birds came to be the best-studied case of a link between ecology, behavior and the brain. The last part of the chapter weighs food against danger: the rule by which a growing fish should choose its habitat, the measurement of fear by the food an animal leaves behind, and the "landscape of fear" that predators create.

The tools used here are those of the **optimal foraging theory** of [7.18.22](/7.18.22) with two additions from other courses: the **exploration–exploitation trade-off** of reinforcement learning ([7.16.39](/7.16.39)) and **dynamic programming** ([4.1.13](/4.1.13)). Memory for caches is treated here only as far as it explains the ecology of food storing; the cognitive work on episodic-like memory in scrub jays and the comparative study of spatial memory are in [7.18.42](/7.18.42), and the competition among foragers, including the producer–scrounger game, in [7.18.25](/7.18.25).

## 23.1. Information and Sampling

### 23.1.1. Assessing a Patch

A forager arriving at a patch usually does not know how much food it contains, and must infer it from what it finds. Lima (1984) trained wild downy woodpeckers (*Dryobates pubescens*) in winter to search for sunflower seeds in holes drilled in hanging logs, each with 24 holes covered by tape. In each experiment, some logs were empty and the others contained the same number of seeds, and the woodpeckers could not tell in advance which logs were empty. When the full logs had a seed in every hole, a single empty hole showed that a log was empty, and the birds left empty logs after an average of 1.7 holes. When full logs had seeds in only 6 or 12 of their 24 holes, an empty hole was weaker evidence, and the question was how many empty holes a bird should open before giving up. Lima calculated the rate-maximizing numbers to be 6 (for logs with 6 seeds) and 3 (for logs with 12), and the woodpeckers opened on average 6.3 and 3.5.

**Example 23.1.1 (Bayesian Assessment of a Log)** The woodpecker's problem is one of **Bayesian inference** ([3.2.7](/3.2.7)). Suppose half the logs are full, each with $s$ seeds in 24 holes, and the bird has opened $k$ holes and found all of them empty. The probability of this observation is 1 for an empty log and $\binom{24 - s}{k}\big/\binom{24}{k}$ for a full one, so by Bayes' theorem the probability that the log is full is

$$
\pi_k = \frac{\binom{24-s}{k}\big/\binom{24}{k}}{1 + \binom{24-s}{k}\big/\binom{24}{k}}.
$$

For $s = 6$ and $k = 6$, the likelihood ratio is $\binom{18}{6}/\binom{24}{6} = 18\,564/134\,596 \approx 0.138$ and $\pi_6 \approx 0.12$; for $s = 12$ and $k = 3$, it is $\binom{12}{3}/\binom{24}{3} = 220/2024 \approx 0.109$ and $\pi_3 \approx 0.10$. The numbers of empty holes the woodpeckers tolerated in the two treatments, which differ by a factor of two, both brought the probability that the log was worth staying in down to about one in ten. A forager that leaves when its estimate of the patch's value falls below a threshold set by the quality of the rest of the habitat is following a Bayesian version of the **marginal value theorem** (Theorem 22.5.2 of [7.18.22](/7.18.22)): the marginal rate that matters is the expected rate, given what has been found so far.

:::

How patches are assessed has a strong effect on the best leaving rule. When the patches differ greatly in quality and the forager can learn a patch's quality quickly, it should stay longer in a patch after a capture, because a capture is evidence that the patch is good ("leave when you have found nothing for a while"); when all patches start alike and simply deplete, a capture means less food remains and the forager should leave sooner after successes ("leave after a fixed number of items"). Iwasa, Higashi and Yamamura (1981) showed that the optimal rule depends on the statistical distribution of prey among patches, and parasitoid wasps searching for host larvae, whose clustering varies from species to species, use rules of both kinds.

### 23.1.2. Exploration and Exploitation

When a forager has several options whose payoffs it does not know, it faces the **exploration–exploitation trade-off** ([7.16.39](/7.16.39)): every choice of the apparently best option exploits current knowledge, and every choice of another option gathers information that may pay later. The simplest form of the problem is the two-armed bandit, a version of the **multi-armed bandit** of reinforcement learning ([4.5.3](/4.5.3)). Krebs, Kacelnik and Taylor (1978) gave great tits two feeding places, each of which delivered a mealworm with a fixed probability per peck, the two probabilities being unknown to the bird. The birds first sampled both places more or less equally and then switched to the better one, and the length of the sampling period depended on the difference between the places and on the length of the session, roughly as predicted by a model of optimal sampling: when the options differed greatly, or the session was short, sampling ended sooner. In the long run the birds chose the better option almost exclusively, as predicted when the payoffs do not change.

Real environments change, and an animal that stopped sampling would miss improvements in the alternatives. This is one explanation for the partial preferences observed in tests of the prey model ([7.18.22](/7.18.22#sec-22.5.2)) and for the occasional visits of animals to options that are currently worse. Learning rules of the kind studied in [7.16.39](/7.16.39), in which choice probabilities depend on estimated values through a softmax function, produce continued sampling whose amount is set by a single parameter, and fit the choices of birds and bees in changing environments well.

## 23.2. Risk-Sensitive Foraging

### 23.2.1. Why Variance Matters

The currency of [7.18.22](/7.18.22), the mean rate of energy gain, ignores the variability of the outcome. Variability matters whenever fitness is not a linear function of the food obtained. A small bird in winter must end the day with enough reserves to survive the night: an extra seed above that threshold is worth little, and a seed below it may be the difference between life and death. Fitness is then a convex function of food below the threshold and a concave one above it, and by **Jensen's inequality** ([2.2.5](/2.2.5)) the expected fitness of a variable outcome is greater than the fitness of its mean where the function is convex and smaller where it is concave.

**Definition 23.2.1 (Risk-Sensitive Foraging)** **Risk-sensitive foraging** is foraging in which the choice between options depends on the variance of their payoffs as well as their means. A forager that prefers a less variable option with the same mean is *risk-averse*; one that prefers the more variable option is *risk-prone*.

In economics, risk refers to variance in the outcome, not to the danger of being killed, which in behavioral ecology is called predation risk; the two kinds of risk interact, as we shall see.

**Definition 23.2.2 (Energy Budget Rule)** The **energy budget rule** (Stephens 1981) states that a forager should be risk-averse when its expected energy gain exceeds its requirement (a positive energy budget) and risk-prone when the expected gain falls short of the requirement (a negative energy budget).

The rule follows from a simple model. Let the energy gained by the end of the day be a normal random variable with mean $m$ and standard deviation $s$, and let the forager survive the night if its gain reaches the requirement $R$. Then

$$
\mathbb{P}(\text{survive}) = \Phi\!\left(\frac{m - R}{s}\right), \tag{23.1}
$$

where $\Phi$ is the standard normal distribution function (the z-score model of Stephens and Charnov 1982). For options with the same mean, survival is maximized by the smallest $s$ when $m > R$ (the argument of $\Phi$ is positive and is largest when divided by a small number) and by the largest $s$ when $m < R$ (a negative argument approaches zero as $s$ increases). An animal that expects to fall short can survive only by gambling on a lucky outcome.

C: the reason, Jensen's inequality on an S-shaped fitness function: where the function is convex, the mean fitness of two outcomes (filled dot) exceeds the fitness of their mean (open dot); where it is concave, the reverse.](./plots/risk-sensitivity.py)

### 23.2.2. Tests of the Energy Budget Rule

Caraco and coauthors (1980, 1990) offered yellow-eyed juncos (*Junco phaeonotus*) in aviaries a series of choices between a feeder that always gave the same number of seeds and one that gave a variable number with the same mean, for example 3 seeds for certain versus 0 or 6 seeds with equal probability. In one experiment the birds were tested at 19 °C, when the fixed reward was enough to meet their daily energy needs, and at 1 °C, when it was not. As predicted, the birds were risk-averse at 19 °C and risk-prone at 1 °C. Common shrews (*Sorex araneus*), which have very high metabolic rates and can starve within hours, became risk-prone when their energy budget was made negative (Barnard and Brown 1985), and bumblebee workers (*Bombus*) became risk-prone when the honey reserves of their colony were experimentally depleted, the colony's reserves taking the place of an individual's body reserves (Cartar and Dill 1990).

The energy budget rule is supported in many but not all studies, and a review found an important complication (Kacelnik and Bateson 1996). When the variability is in the *amount* of food, animals are usually risk-averse, and they shift toward risk-proneness when hungry, as the rule predicts. When the variability is in the *delay* to food, for example a reward after either 2 s or 18 s instead of always after 10 s, animals of all species tested are strongly risk-prone, whatever their budget. The likely reason lies in the mechanisms of timing and learning: animals discount delayed rewards roughly hyperbolically, so that the value of the average of a short and a long delay is lower than the average of their values, and their estimates of intervals have errors proportional to the interval (scalar timing, [7.18.43](/7.18.43)). Risk-sensitivity thus illustrates the theme of this course that functional explanations must be combined with an understanding of mechanism.

## 23.3. State-Dependent Decisions

### 23.3.1. Decisions That Depend on Reserves and Time

The energy budget rule already shows that the best choice depends on the animal's state. The dependence is clearest when the forager has several decisions to make before a deadline. Suppose, as in the example above, that a bird needs 8 units at dusk and has two choices left. From 6 units, it should take the safe option twice and reach 8 for certain, although with only one choice left it would have to gamble. The best decision now depends on both the current reserves and the time remaining (Houston and McNamara 1982). Hungry animals generally take more risks, and the same animal may switch from risk-prone to risk-averse during a day if it has good luck early on.

Problems of this kind, in which an animal's behavior changes its state and the state changes the value of each behavior, are solved by stochastic dynamic programming (Houston and McNamara 1999; Mangel and Clark 1988).

**Formula 23.3.1 (Dynamic Programming Equation)** Let $x$ be the state of the animal (its energy reserves), $t = 0, 1, \dots, T$ the time, and $\Phi(x)$ the reproductive value of state $x$ at the final time $T$ (for example, the probability of surviving the night). If in period $t$ the animal chooses action $a$, it survives the period with probability $1 - \mu_a(x)$ and moves to state $x'$ with probability $p_a(x' \mid x)$. The maximal expected terminal reward from state $x$ at time $t$, $V(x, t)$, satisfies

$$
V(x, t) = \max_a \Big\{ \big(1 - \mu_a(x)\big) \sum_{x'} p_a(x' \mid x)\, V(x', t + 1) \Big\}, \qquad V(x, T) = \Phi(x), \tag{23.2}
$$

and the optimal action in each state and period is the one that attains the maximum. The equation is solved backward from $T$, the principle of optimality of **dynamic programming** ([4.1.13](/4.1.13)); it is the finite-horizon form of the **Bellman optimality equation** ([7.16.39](/7.16.39)), with survival multiplying the value instead of a discount factor.

The method has three advantages over static models. It allows for the effect of present behavior on future options; it uses a currency, survival or reproductive success, that is closer to fitness than the rate of gain; and it predicts sequences of behavior, which can be compared with observed daily routines.

### 23.3.2. A Winter Day

Small birds in winter face a daily problem. A great tit of about 20 g loses 10–15% of its body mass overnight in winter, almost all of it fat, and must regain it during the short day (Owen 1954). One might expect such a bird to put on fat as fast as possible every morning, as insurance against starvation. In fact birds gain mass slowly in the morning and rapidly in the afternoon, and they are heaviest on the coldest days, which implies that on other days they carry less than they could. Lima (1986) and McNamara and Houston (1990) explained this with a trade-off: fat is insurance against starvation, but a fatter bird takes off more slowly and climbs at a shallower angle when attacked, and a bird that forages more to gain fat is more exposed to predators. The best routine is then to keep reserves low while there is plenty of time left to feed, and to build them up later, so that the predation cost of carrying fat is incurred for only a short time before night (figure below).

### 23.3.3. Hunger and Danger

State also changes how an animal weighs food against danger. Milinski and Heller (1978) offered three-spined sticklebacks (*Gasterosteus aculeatus*) a choice between swarms of water fleas of different densities. Very hungry fish attacked the densest swarms, where the potential feeding rate was highest; less hungry fish preferred low densities. A fish attacking a dense swarm must concentrate to single out one moving target and is less able to watch for predators (Milinski 1984), so a hungry fish, whose risk of starvation is high, trades vigilance for intake, while a satiated fish does the reverse. When a model kingfisher was flown over the tank, even hungry fish switched to the low densities. Similarly, normally shy birds come to garden feeders in the open on very cold days. The traditional view that an animal's internal state controls its behavior can be turned around: behavior is the means by which an animal controls its state in the best way, and models like Eq. (23.2) predict how.

## 23.4. Body Reserves and Food Storing

### 23.4.1. Fat as Insurance

The insurance model of reserves makes clear predictions (figure below). Reserves should be larger when the requirement is less predictable, larger when the cost of carrying them is lower, and smaller when predators are more dangerous.

- *Unpredictability.* Captive great tits transferred from a constant to a variable environment, in which the length of the night without food varied at random, became heavier, and lost the extra mass when returned to the constant environment (Bednekoff and Krebs 1995). European starlings deprived of food for fixed periods at unpredictable times put on more mass than birds deprived at a predictable time of day (Witter, Swaddle and Cuthill 1995).
- *Predation.* In Wytham Woods near Oxford, the winter mass of great tits fell by about 0.5 g when sparrowhawks (*Accipiter nisus*) recolonized the wood in the 1980s after the decline caused by organochlorine pesticides, and mass declines in other parts of Britain coincided with the local return of the hawks (Gosler, Greenwood and Perrins 1995). Presenting a model hawk at feeders caused a similar decline in individual birds, which shows that the change was a strategic adjustment rather than the selective removal of heavy birds.
- *Access to food.* Dominant great tits, which have priority at feeders and can rely on getting food when they need it, carry less fat than subordinates.

### 23.4.2. Food Caching

Many animals store food outside their bodies.

**Definition 23.4.1 (Food Caching)** **Food caching** (food hoarding) is the storage of food for later use. In *larder hoarding* the food is kept in one or a few stores that the owner can defend; in *scatter hoarding* it is hidden in many small, dispersed caches, each too small to be worth defending, whose protection depends on concealment.

Caching is widespread. Among arthropods, spiders wrap surplus prey in silk; harvester ants store seeds in granaries; honeypot ants (*Myrmecocystus*) store nectar in the swollen crops of living workers, the repletes; honeybees store honey and pollen; and dung beetles bury dung balls as food for their larvae. Among vertebrates, rodents are the champions: squirrels scatter-hoard nuts, red squirrels and chipmunks keep larders, beavers anchor branches in the mud of their ponds as a winter food store, and pack rats and pikas gather hay piles. Hawks, owls and shrikes store prey (shrikes impale it on thorns), and the acorn woodpecker (*Melanerpes formicivorus*) of western North America drills holes in a "granary" tree, often tens of thousands of them, into each of which a group of woodpeckers wedges an acorn and which it defends collectively. The most remarkable scatter-hoarders are birds of two families, the crows and jays (Corvidae) and the tits and chickadees (Paridae). A Clark's nutcracker (*Nucifraga columbiana*) harvests pine seeds in autumn, carries up to 100 at a time in a pouch under its tongue, and buries about 30,000 of them in 2500–4000 caches over a large area, retrieving them over the winter and spring and even to feed its young in the next breeding season. Some tits store 100,000–500,000 seeds in a winter, each in a separate place.

Why store food outside the body rather than as fat?

1. Fat increases body mass and with it the risk of predation and the cost of locomotion; a cache weighs nothing (panel C of the figure above).
2. When food is superabundant for a short time, as in a mast year of acorns or pine seeds, far more can be gathered than could be stored as fat.
3. Large stores can support a group through the winter, as in acorn woodpeckers, beavers and honeybees.
4. Stored food can be given to offspring.

Caches have their own costs: the time and energy spent storing, the risk of exposure while caching, decay, and losses to thieves, both of other species and of the same species. A scatter-hoard is a public good in the sense that anyone who finds a cache can eat it, and Andersson and Krebs (1978) pointed out that caching can evolve only if the cacher has a better chance than others of recovering its own caches; otherwise individuals that did not cache but pilfered would do better. Brodin and Ekman (1994) tested this in the field by giving individual willow tits (*Poecile montanus*) in Sweden sunflower seeds labeled with the radioactive isotope ³⁵S. The birds cached the seeds, and the isotope was incorporated into the growing feathers of whichever bird later ate a labeled seed, so that autoradiographs of feathers revealed who had recovered the seeds and when. Over two months, the birds that had cached the labeled seeds were about five times more likely to recover them than other members of their flock. Cachers also reduce pilfering by behavior: they cache out of sight of others, re-cache food when they have been watched, and gray squirrels (*Sciurus carolinensis*) make more "empty" deceptive caches when watched by a conspecific (Steele et al. 2008); scrub jays even adjust their caching to what a particular observer has seen ([7.18.42](/7.18.42)).

Scatter-hoarding has large ecological consequences. Seeds that are cached and not recovered are planted, often in good sites for germination: jays plant oaks, squirrels plant walnuts and hickories, nutcrackers plant whitebark pine, which depends almost entirely on them for dispersal. These are **mutualism** with a cost to the plant, which loses most of the seeds it produces to the dispersers.

## 23.5. Food-Storing Birds: From Behavioral Ecology to Neuroscience

### 23.5.1. Memory for Caches

Do food-storing birds find their caches by memory, or by searching places where caches are likely? Sherry, Krebs and Cowie (1981) exploited the fact that the visual pathways of birds cross almost completely at the optic chiasm, so that information from one eye is stored mainly in the opposite hemisphere. Marsh tits (*Poecile palustris*) were allowed to store seeds in moss trays in an aviary with one eye covered by a small cap, and to retrieve them 24 hours later with either the same eye covered or the cap switched to the other eye. With the same eye open, the birds concentrated their searches at the cache sites; with the other eye open, they searched at random. Memory, not a search rule, guided retrieval. Clark's nutcrackers remember their caches for months: in the laboratory they recovered caches far above chance after 285 days (Balda and Kamil 1992).

Is the memory of food-storers better than that of related species, or do they simply use it more? Comparing four North American corvids that depend on stored food to different degrees, Kamil, Balda and colleagues found that the Clark's nutcracker, the most dependent, outperformed the others in tests of spatial memory, but not in tests of memory for colors (Balda and Kamil 2006). The specialization seems to concern spatial memory specifically, a conclusion discussed further in [7.18.42](/7.18.42).

### 23.5.2. The Hippocampus

In mammals the hippocampus is essential for spatial memory, and birds have a homologous structure in the dorsomedial telencephalon that is needed for the recovery of caches (Sherry and Vaccarino 1989). Comparisons across species, correcting for body and brain size, showed that within families of passerine birds the food-storing species have a larger hippocampus relative to the rest of the forebrain than non-storers (Krebs et al. 1989; Sherry et al. 1989). Within one species, black-capped chickadees (*Poecile atricapillus*) from populations with harsher winters, which depend more on stored food, have a larger relative hippocampus with more neurons than birds from milder places, whether the harshness comes from the cold or from short days (Roth and Pravosudov 2009; Roth et al. 2011). Experience matters too: in young marsh tits, the hippocampus grew only if the birds were allowed to store and retrieve food or perform a similar spatial task (Clayton and Krebs 1994), and in adult chickadees the relative size of the hippocampus and the recruitment of new neurons vary with the seasons (**adult neurogenesis**). That brain tissue shrinks when it is not needed suggests that it is costly to maintain, the same argument as in the evolution of large brains ([7.18.8](/7.18.8)). A similar plasticity has been found in people: London taxi drivers, who spend years learning the city's streets, have an enlarged posterior hippocampus (Maguire et al. 2000).

### 23.5.3. Barcodes for Memories

How does the hippocampus store tens of thousands of separate events? Recordings from food-storing birds have begun to answer the question. Chettih, Mackevicius, Hale and Aronov (2024) recorded large populations of hippocampal neurons in black-capped chickadees caching and retrieving seeds at 128 sites in an arena. As in other animals, many neurons were **place cells**, firing when the bird was at a particular location ([7.16.44](/7.16.44)). But at the moment of caching, the population produced a burst of activity that was specific to that cache: a sparse pattern, a "barcode", that differed even between caches made at the same site or at neighboring sites, was largely unrelated to the place code, and was reactivated when the bird later retrieved the seed from that cache. The barcodes appear to be a way of indexing individual memories, the "what happened here" of each caching event, separately from the map of where things are; they are a candidate mechanism for the episodic-like memory of food-storers, discussed in [7.18.42](/7.18.42).

The food-storing birds thus link the levels of explanation that run through this course: an ecological problem (surviving winter on scattered food), an economic analysis (when to cache rather than fatten), a behavioral capacity (memory for thousands of places), a brain specialization (the size of the hippocampus) and a neural code (barcodes).

## 23.6. Feeding and Danger

### 23.6.1. A Trade-Off in Every Meal

Foraging and predation are linked in almost every animal that is not a top predator. Lima, Valone and Caraco (1985) watched gray squirrels eating pieces of cookies set out on a table in a park. The squirrels often carried each piece to a tree before eating it, which wastes time but reduces exposure to predators; they were more likely to do so when the table was close to the trees, so that the trip was cheap, and when the pieces were large, so that eating them in the open would take long. The squirrels behaved as if they traded the rate of intake against the probability of being caught, according to the costs of each.

A general formulation is due to Gilliam (Werner and Gilliam 1984). Consider a juvenile animal that grows until it reaches a size at which it can reproduce or is safe from predators, and that can choose among habitats with different growth rates $g$ and mortality rates $\mu$.

**Formula 23.6.1 (Gilliam's Rule)** If an animal must grow from size $L_0$ to size $L_1$ before reproducing, and the mortality rate $\mu_i(L)$ and growth rate $g_i(L)$ in each habitat $i$ depend on size, then its probability of surviving to $L_1$ is

$$
\exp\!\left(-\int_{L_0}^{L_1} \frac{\mu_{i(L)}(L)}{g_{i(L)}(L)}\,\mathrm{d}L\right), \tag{23.3}
$$

where $i(L)$ is the habitat used at size $L$. Survival is maximized by choosing at each size the habitat that minimizes $\mu/g$, the mortality incurred per unit of growth.

The rule follows because survival over a small increment of size $\mathrm{d}L$, which takes time $\mathrm{d}L/g$, is $\exp(-\mu\,\mathrm{d}L/g)$. It has a simple interpretation: a habitat with high growth but high mortality is worth using only if it shortens the dangerous period enough to compensate.

Werner and coauthors (1983) tested the rule with bluegill sunfish (*Lepomis macrochirus*) in experimental ponds in Michigan. Bluegills grew fastest by feeding on benthic invertebrates in open areas of the pond, and in ponds without predators they fed mostly there. When largemouth bass (*Micropterus salmoides*), which can swallow only small bluegills, were added, the small sunfish moved into the vegetation and fed on plankton, reducing their food intake by about a third and their seasonal growth by 27%, while larger sunfish continued to feed in the open. As they grew, the small fish moved out of the vegetation at a size close to that predicted by Gilliam's rule.

### 23.6.2. Measuring Fear: Giving-Up Densities

How can one measure the cost of predation risk to a forager? Brown (1988) proposed an elegant method based on the marginal value theorem. A forager exploiting a patch whose returns diminish should leave when its rate of gain falls to the sum of the costs of staying: the metabolic cost of foraging, the cost of the risk of predation, and the missed opportunity of doing something else. If one provides artificial patches, such as trays of seeds mixed with sand, and measures the amount of food left behind when the forager quits, this *giving-up density* is a measure of the forager's total cost of foraging there; since the metabolic and opportunity costs are similar in nearby patches, differences in giving-up density between patches reflect differences in perceived predation risk.

**Definition 23.6.1 (Giving-Up Density)** The **giving-up density** of a food patch is the density of food remaining when a forager stops exploiting it. Under the marginal value theorem, a forager quits when its harvest rate $H$ equals the sum of its metabolic cost $C$, predation cost $P$ and missed-opportunity cost $M$ of foraging there, $H = C + P + M$, so that differences in giving-up density among otherwise similar patches measure differences in predation cost.

Giving-up densities have mapped fear in many animals. Gerbils in the Negev Desert left more seeds in trays in the open than under bushes, more on moonlit nights than on dark ones, and more when barn owls were flown over their enclosure (Kotler, Brown and Hasson 1991). Fox squirrels, gray squirrels, rodents, ibexes and primates all leave more food in patches far from cover or close to places where predators have been seen.

### 23.6.3. The Landscape of Fear

Predators affect their prey not only by killing them but by changing their behavior, and the second effect can be the larger.

**Definition 23.6.2 (Landscape of Fear)** The **landscape of fear** is the spatial (and temporal) variation in the predation risk perceived by prey, as reflected in their behavior: their use of habitats, vigilance, movement and foraging (Laundré, Hernández and Altendorf 2001).

Examples:

- *Dugongs and tiger sharks.* In Shark Bay, Western Australia, dugongs (*Dugong dugon*) feed on seagrass either by cropping leaves or by excavating the rhizomes from the sediment, which gives more energy but leaves the animal with its head buried and its vision obstructed. When tiger sharks (*Galeocerdo cuvier*) were abundant, dugongs excavated less and cropped more (Wirsing, Heithaus and Dill 2007).
- *Zebras and lions.* In a Kenyan conservancy, plains zebras (*Equus quagga*) used grassland, where grazing is best, less on days when lions had been there and less at night, when lions hunt, and when they were in grassland at night they moved faster and turned more sharply (Fischhoff et al. 2007).
- *Wolves and elk.* After wolves (*Canis lupus*) were reintroduced to Yellowstone National Park in 1995, female elk (*Cervus canadensis*) increased their vigilance, used less open grassland when wolves were near, and moved into wooded areas (Laundré et al. 2001; Creel et al. 2005). Reductions in elk diet quality and in pregnancy rates were attributed to these behavioral changes (Creel et al. 2007; Christianson and Creel 2010). Later studies questioned the size of these effects: GPS-collared elk that encountered wolves frequently did not have lower body fat or pregnancy rates than others (Middleton et al. 2013), and the recovery of aspen and willow, once attributed to elk avoiding risky places, appears to depend more on the reduction in elk numbers. How strongly fear alone, as opposed to killing, affects large herbivores remains debated.
- *Song sparrows and playbacks.* The cleanest demonstration that fear alone affects reproduction comes from an experiment in which all direct predation was prevented. Zanette and coauthors (2011) protected nests of song sparrows (*Melospiza melodia*) on islands in British Columbia from predators with electric fences and nets, and broadcast recordings of predator calls or, as a control, of non-predators. Parents hearing predators laid fewer eggs, fed their nestlings less and produced 40% fewer young. Playbacks of large carnivores (dogs) to raccoons on islands made them forage less in the intertidal zone, and the intertidal crabs and fish that raccoons eat increased (Suraci et al. 2016): fear of predators can cascade through a food web.

The ecology of fear shows that predation shapes foraging even where predators kill few animals. Whether prey respond to the long-term riskiness of a place or to the immediate presence of a predator can be distinguished: in Zambia, zebras, wildebeest and oribi were more vigilant in areas heavily used by predators over the long term, but not when a predator happened to be close at the time of observation (Dröge et al. 2017). The neural organization of defensive behavior according to the imminence of the threat is described in [7.18.8](/7.18.8#sec-8.3), and the ways in which prey escape once a predator attacks in [7.18.24](/7.18.24).

## Summary

- Foragers sample to learn about patches and options. Downy woodpeckers give up on a log after a number of empty holes that brings the posterior probability of its being full down to about 0.1 (Example 23.1.1); great tits facing a two-armed bandit sample, then exploit the better option, an instance of the **exploration–exploitation trade-off**.
- When fitness is a nonlinear function of food, variance matters (**Jensen's inequality**). **Risk-sensitive foraging** (Definition 23.2.1) follows the **energy budget rule** (Definition 23.2.2, Eq. 23.1): risk-averse with a positive budget, risk-prone with a negative one (juncos at 1 °C and 19 °C, shrews, bumblebees). Animals are nearly always risk-prone for variable delays, a consequence of the mechanisms of timing.
- State-dependent decisions are solved by **dynamic programming** (Formula 23.3.1). Models of a winter day predict that small birds keep reserves low in the morning and fatten in the afternoon, trading starvation against predation.
- Fat is insurance: reserves increase with the unpredictability of food and decrease with predation risk (great tits after the return of sparrowhawks). **Food caching** (Definition 23.4.1) stores food without adding mass; it can evolve only if cachers recover their own caches preferentially (willow tits, ³⁵S), and it plants seeds.
- Food-storing birds remember caches (eye-cap experiment; nutcrackers after 285 days), have a larger hippocampus that grows with experience, and encode each cache by a hippocampal barcode separate from the place code.
- Foragers trade intake against danger (squirrels, sticklebacks). **Gilliam's rule** (Formula 23.6.1): minimize $\mu/g$, as bluegill sunfish do when bass are present. The **giving-up density** (Definition 23.6.1) measures the cost of predation risk. The **landscape of fear** (Definition 23.6.2) changes habitat use and reproduction (dugongs, zebras, elk, song sparrows), although its strength in large mammals is debated.

## Questions

1. (Calculation) In Example 23.1.1, how many empty holes should a woodpecker tolerate on logs that contain 3 seeds in 24 holes, if it uses the same threshold of a posterior probability of about 0.1 that the log is full?

   **Answer** The likelihood ratio after $k$ empty holes is $\binom{21}{k}/\binom{24}{k}$. For $k = 10$ it is $\binom{21}{10}/\binom{24}{10} = 352\,716/1\,961\,256 \approx 0.180$, giving $\pi \approx 0.15$; for $k = 12$ it is $\binom{21}{12}/\binom{24}{12} = 293\,930/2\,704\,156 \approx 0.109$, giving $\pi \approx 0.098$. The bird should open about 12 empty holes, half the log. The sparser the food in good patches, the more evidence is needed to reject a patch and the more time is spent on empty ones, which lowers the overall rate; this is why patch assessment matters most when good patches are poor.

2. Why are animals risk-prone when choosing between a fixed and a variable delay to food, even when well fed? Show with an example that a hyperbolic discounting function makes a variable delay more attractive than a fixed delay with the same mean.

   **Answer** If the value of a reward after delay $d$ is $v(d) = A/(1 + kd)$, the value is a convex function of $d$, so by Jensen's inequality the mean value of a variable delay exceeds the value of its mean delay. With $A = 1$ and $k = 1\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$, a fixed delay of 10 s has value $1/11 \approx 0.091$; delays of 2 s or 18 s with equal probability have mean value $\tfrac12(1/3 + 1/19) \approx 0.193$, about twice as large. The short delays are valued much more than the long ones are devalued. The functional reason for hyperbolic discounting may be that in nature rewards that are delayed are often lost to competitors or interruptions, and the mechanism is that intervals are estimated with errors proportional to their length.

3. In the dynamic programming model of a winter day, explain why birds that are far below the requirement late in the day stop foraging, and why this prediction should be tested with caution.

   **Answer** The terminal reward is all-or-none: a bird that cannot reach 22 units by dusk will die anyway, so foraging cannot increase its probability of surviving the night, while it adds predation risk during the remaining day; resting, or any action, gives the same zero terminal value, and the model's tie-breaking makes it rest. In reality the step function is an idealization: a bird a little below the threshold may survive by lowering its body temperature (nocturnal hypothermia, which chickadees use), by finding a warmer roost, or by feeding at dawn, so the fitness function is smoother and continued foraging pays. The prediction depends on the shape of the terminal reward, which should be measured rather than assumed.

4. Using Formula 23.6.1, explain why a small bluegill should stay in vegetation even though it grows slower there, and predict the effect on the switching size of (a) doubling the growth rate in open water and (b) adding a second predator that eats fish of all sizes in both habitats equally.

   **Answer** While small, the fish's mortality in open water is so high that $\mu_1/g_1 > \mu_2/g_2$: the extra growth does not compensate for the extra risk per millimeter grown. (a) Doubling $g_1$ halves $\mu_1/g_1$ at every size, so the curves cross at a smaller size and the fish should move into open water earlier. (b) A mortality $\mu_0$ added to both habitats changes the ratios to $(\mu_1 + \mu_0)/g_1$ and $(\mu_2 + \mu_0)/g_2$. Since $g_1 > g_2$, the added term is smaller in the fast-growth habitat, so open water becomes relatively more favorable and the switch occurs at a smaller size: when the background risk is high, growing fast through the vulnerable sizes is worth more.

5. Why must a scatter-hoarder have an advantage in recovering its own caches for caching to evolve, and how did Brodin and Ekman's experiment measure this advantage?

   **Answer** If caches were found equally by all members of a population, a cacher would bear the costs of caching while sharing the benefits with non-cachers, who would do better; caching would not spread, like a public good exploited by free riders. It evolves only if the cacher's probability of recovering its caches exceeds that of others enough to repay the costs. Brodin and Ekman labeled seeds with ³⁵S and gave them to individual willow tits to cache; the isotope was incorporated into the growing feathers of any bird that ate a labeled seed, so autoradiographs of feathers from members of the flock showed who had recovered the seeds. Cachers were about five times more likely than flock mates to recover their labeled seeds.

6. (Open question) Design an experiment using giving-up densities to test whether wild mice perceive the risk of owl predation to be higher under a full moon.

   **Answer** Place pairs of seed trays (a fixed quantity of seeds mixed with sand, so that harvest rate declines as seeds are depleted) in the open and under shrubs at many stations; record the seeds left after each night over a lunar cycle, with cameras to identify the forager species. Prediction: giving-up densities are higher in the open on moonlit nights, with a smaller or no effect under shrubs (an interaction of moonlight and microhabitat). Controls: artificial illumination on dark nights to separate light from other lunar effects; measure temperature (metabolic cost) and alternative food (missed-opportunity cost), which also affect giving-up densities; and, if possible, experimentally vary owl presence (tethered or flown owls, or owl calls) to show that the response is to predators rather than to light itself.
