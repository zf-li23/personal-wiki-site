Predators kill a large fraction of the animals that are born, and almost every animal is potentially someone's dinner. The previous two chapters considered predation from the forager's point of view: which prey to take ([7.18.22](/7.18.22)) and how to balance feeding against the danger of being eaten ([7.18.23](/7.18.23)). This chapter takes the prey's side. Prey defend themselves at every stage of an encounter with a predator: they avoid being detected, avoid being recognized as food, advertise that they are not worth attacking, imitate animals that are not, escape once attacked, and resist being subdued or eaten. Each defense in turn selects for counter-adaptations in the predator, so that predators and prey are locked in an **evolutionary arms race** ([7.10.17](/7.10.17)).

The evolutionary theory of these interactions, the definitions of **aposematism**, **Batesian mimicry** and **Müllerian mimicry**, and models of coevolution and of the frequency dependence of mimicry are in [7.10.17](/7.10.17); the camouflage, startle displays, chemical defenses and mimicry of insects, including the bombardier beetle, are described in [7.17.39](/7.17.39#sec-39.4); and the neurotoxins of animals and the molecular basis of resistance to them in [7.18.6](/7.18.6#sec-6.4). Here we concentrate on behavior: the experiments that showed how defenses work, the learning and decision-making of predators that select them, and the decisions of prey about when to hide and when to flee. The neural mechanisms of fear and escape are treated in [7.18.8](/7.18.8#sec-8.3) (the amygdala), [7.18.14](/7.18.14) (escape circuits) and [7.18.19](/7.18.19) (stress hormones), group defenses such as vigilance, dilution and the selfish herd in [7.18.27](/7.18.27), and alarm signals in [7.18.39](/7.18.39).

## 24.1. The Arms Race between Predators and Prey

### 24.1.1. Running to Stay in Place

Selection for efficient predators favors better-defended prey, which in turn favors better predators. If both parties improve, the success of each may stay the same while their tactics change, the "Red Queen" dynamics of the **Red Queen hypothesis** ([7.10.14](/7.10.14)). Testing for such dynamics requires comparing past, present and future generations, which became possible in a remarkable study of the water flea *Daphnia magna* and its bacterial parasite *Pasteuria ramosa* (Decaestecker et al. 2007). Both produce resting stages that accumulate in lake sediments, so that layers deposited over about 40 years form a living archive. Hosts hatched from the eggs of one layer were more often infected by parasites from the same layer than by parasites from earlier layers, to which they had adapted, or from later layers, which had adapted to later hosts; and the infectivity of contemporary parasites did not change over the decades. Host and parasite had been running to stay in the same place.

The selection pressures on the two sides of a predator–prey interaction are usually unequal.

**Definition 24.1.1 (Life–Dinner Principle)** The **life–dinner principle** (Dawkins and Krebs 1979) is the asymmetry of selection in many predator–prey interactions: a prey that fails to escape loses its life, and with it all future reproduction, whereas a predator that fails to catch one prey loses only a meal. "The rabbit runs faster than the fox, because the rabbit is running for his life while the fox is only running for his dinner."

The principle predicts that prey will often be "ahead" in an arms race, as rabbits mostly are. It does not hold when a predator's failure is costly too, for example when a predator that misjudges a venomous prey is itself killed, or when a specialized predator depends on one prey species.

### 24.1.2. Defenses Along the Predation Sequence

A predatory encounter proceeds through a sequence of stages (Endler 1986), and a defense can act at any of them:

| Stage of the encounter | Predator's task | Prey defenses |
|---|---|---|
| Encounter | Search where prey are | Avoid the predator's places and times (refuges, burrows, nocturnality), spacing out |
| Detection | See, hear or smell the prey | **Crypsis**: background matching, disruptive coloration, countershading, transparency; silence and stillness |
| Identification | Recognize it as edible | **Masquerade**; polymorphism (against search images); warning signals; Batesian and Müllerian mimicry |
| Approach and attack | Close the distance | Vigilance; signals that the predator has been detected; flight; startle displays |
| Subjugation | Catch and subdue | Armor, spines, active defense, autotomy, death feigning, distress calls |
| Consumption | Eat and digest | Toxins, distastefulness, resistance to digestion |

**Definition 24.1.2 (Primary and Secondary Defense)** A **primary defense** operates whether or not a predator is present and reduces the chance that an encounter begins or that the prey is detected or recognized (living in refuges, crypsis, masquerade, aposematism, mimicry). A **secondary defense** operates only during an encounter with a predator, after the prey has been detected (flight, startle displays, active defense, autotomy, death feigning, the release of toxins).

The later a defense acts, the more costly the encounter is likely to be for the prey, which explains why primary defenses are the commonest. Davies, Krebs and West (2012) emphasize two questions that must be answered for each proposed defense. Is it really a defense, or a story? Thayer (1909) proposed that flamingos are pink to blend with the sunset; the idea that a moth resembles bird droppings to avoid birds may sound no more plausible until it is tested by experiment. And how could the defense have begun? An arms race needs a starting point at which even a crude counter-adaptation reduces predation.

## 24.2. Avoiding Detection and Recognition

### 24.2.1. Crypsis and the Behavior That Makes It Work

**Crypsis**, the prevention of detection, takes several forms ([7.17.39](/7.17.39#sec-39.4.1)): **background matching**, in which the color and pattern of the animal resemble a random sample of the background; **disruptive coloration**, in which contrasting marks at the edge of the body break up its outline; **countershading**, a darker dorsal than ventral surface that cancels the shading produced by light from above; and transparency.

Each of these hypotheses has been tested experimentally, often with artificial prey exposed to wild birds.

- *Disruptive coloration.* Cuthill and coauthors (2005) pinned triangular paper "moths" with a dead mealworm as body to oak trees. Moths with wings printed from photographs of oak bark survived bird predation better than plain brown or black moths, and moths whose bold markings touched the wing edges survived better still than moths with the same markings placed inside the wing, where they did not break up the outline. Disruptive patterns worked even when the contrasting colors did not occur in the background (Stevens et al. 2006).
- *Countershading.* Pastry "caterpillars" pinned on branches survived better when countershaded than when uniformly dark, uniformly light, or reverse-shaded (Rowland et al. 2008). The eyed hawkmoth caterpillar (*Smerinthus ocellata*), which rests upside down under twigs, is darker on its ventral surface, the one exposed to the light, and looks flat and inconspicuous; turned the right way up, its shading makes it look rounded and conspicuous.
- *Transparency and mirrors.* In the open ocean, where there is nothing to hide behind, many animals are transparent (jellyfish, larval fishes, glass squids) or silvered. The scales of open-ocean fishes contain stacks of guanine platelets arranged so that the fish reflect light in a way that matches the background from the angles at which predators approach, better than a simple mirror (Brady et al. 2015).
- *Counterillumination.* Midwater fishes and squids seen from below against the downwelling light would appear as dark silhouettes; many emit light from ventral photophores matched in intensity and spectrum to the light from above. The Hawaiian bobtail squid (*Euprymna scolopes*) does so with a light organ housing luminous bacteria (*Aliivibrio fischeri*), and adjusts its light output to the moonlight.

Crypsis is a property of the animal in its setting, so behavior is part of it. A cryptic animal must keep still, choose a matching background and orient itself correctly. Different species of underwing moths (*Catocala*) choose bark that matches their forewings, and *C. relicta*, whose forewings are whitish, prefers birch bark and rests head-up, so that the dark bands of its wings align with the dark marks of birch bark. Pietrewicz and Kamil (1977) showed blue jays (*Cyanocitta cristata*) slides of moths and trained them to peck at the screen when a moth was present and at a second key when it was absent; the jays detected *C. relicta* 10–20% less often on birch than on darker bark, and least often when it rested head-up. Peppered moths (*Biston betularia*), the classic case of industrial melanism ([7.10.5](/7.10.5)), rest in the shaded patches beneath the joints of branches rather than on open trunks, where birds find them more easily (Howlett and Majerus 1987).

The slide experiments turned the predator's problem into one of **signal detection theory** ([7.16.47](/7.16.47)): each slide is a "trial" in which the bird must decide whether a target is present, a miss costs it food and a false alarm costs it time. The prey's crypsis determines the discriminability $d'$ of moth from bark, and the predator's criterion depends on the costs of the two errors and the frequency of prey, as in any detection task. The same framework applies to mimicry (§24.4.2).

### 24.2.2. Dynamic Camouflage in Cephalopods

Cuttlefish, squid and octopuses change their appearance in a fraction of a second. Their skin contains chromatophores, elastic sacs of yellow, red or brown pigment, each expanded by radial muscles under direct control of motor neurons from the brain, so that the skin can be patterned at the resolution of single cells; reflecting cells beneath them (iridophores and leucophores) add iridescent colors and white; and many octopuses and cuttlefish raise papillae that change the texture of the skin (Messenger 2001). The resulting body patterns fall into a few classes (Hanlon 2007): uniform patterns on uniform backgrounds; mottle patterns, small-scale light and dark blotches, on backgrounds with small-scale variation; and disruptive patterns, large high-contrast components, on backgrounds containing objects about the size of the animal's own white mantle square. Experiments with checkerboard backgrounds showed that cuttlefish (*Sepia officinalis*) choose the pattern from a few visual cues, such as the size and contrast of light objects in the background, rather than copying the background point by point. The unusual organization of the cephalopod nervous system, with most neurons outside the central brain, is described in [7.18.8](/7.18.8).

A puzzle remains: nearly all cephalopods have a single visual pigment and cannot discriminate colors, yet they match the colors of their backgrounds remarkably well. Two hypotheses are being tested. Cephalopod skin contains opsins, and in the octopus *Octopus bimaculoides* light causes chromatophores to expand in isolated skin, which suggests that the skin itself senses light (Ramirez and Oakley 2015). Alternatively, the U-shaped or W-shaped pupils of many cephalopods, which admit light off the optical axis, could allow them to use chromatic aberration, the wavelength dependence of the focal length, to infer the color of an object from the depth at which it is in focus (Stubbs and Stubbs 2016). The mimic octopus (*Thaumoctopus mimicus*) of Indonesia goes beyond camouflage: it imitates the shapes and movements of venomous sea snakes, lionfish and flatfish (Norman et al. 2001).

### 24.2.3. Search Images and Apostatic Selection

Predators also change. Luuk Tinbergen (1960), studying birds feeding their young in Dutch pine woods, found that a cryptic insect species was absent from the nestlings' diet when it first appeared in spring, and then suddenly appeared in it in large numbers. He proposed that the birds had learned to see it.

**Definition 24.2.1 (Search Image)** A **search image** is a perceptual change in a predator that has recently encountered a particular type of cryptic prey, by which it becomes better at detecting that type, often at the expense of detecting others.

Tinbergen's observation could also be explained by a change in the birds' preference rather than their perception: they might have seen the insects all along and started to eat them only when they became abundant enough to be profitable (Royama 1970). Marian Dawkins (1971) separated perception from preference by keeping the prey constant and changing only the background. Domestic chicks given colored rice grains on a background of the same color at first pecked mostly at the background stones and found few grains, but after a few minutes they began to find them, and by the end of the trial they ate cryptic grains as fast as conspicuous ones. Since the prey were identical, the improvement was in detection. Pietrewicz and Kamil (1979) then showed that blue jays shown slides containing either of two species of *Catocala* improved their detection over a run of slides of the same species, but not when the two species were presented in random order: switching between prey types prevented the search image from forming.

If predators form search images for the commonest prey, rare forms are overlooked, and prey populations can be maintained polymorphic.

**Definition 24.2.2 (Apostatic Selection)** **Apostatic selection** is predation that removes common forms of prey disproportionately to their frequency, so that rare forms have a survival advantage; it is a form of **negative frequency-dependent selection** ([7.10.5](/7.10.5)) that can maintain polymorphism in prey.

Many cryptic insects are polymorphic: about half of the North American species of *Catocala* have several forewing morphs, some as many as nine. Bond and Kamil (1998) tested apostatic selection in a "virtual ecology" experiment, in which blue jays hunted digital images of moths on a computer screen against a bark-like background. Each day the moths detected by the jays were "killed", and the population was restored to its original size with the morphs in the proportions of the survivors. Starting from equal numbers of three morphs, one more cryptic than the others, the most cryptic morph increased but did not take over: the population converged to about 75% of the cryptic morph and 12.5% of each of the others, a stable polymorphism at which all morphs suffered the same risk of detection. When the jays' prey were allowed to evolve with mutation and recombination of digital "genes" for pattern, the moths became both harder to detect and more variable over the generations (Bond and Kamil 2002).

### 24.2.4. How Little Concealment Is Enough?

The jay experiments show that crypsis and polymorphism work, but camouflage as perfect as that of an underwing moth cannot have arisen in one step. Would a slight concealment, adding only a second to the time a predator needs to identify a prey, already be favored? Erichsen and coauthors (1980) answered the question with the conveyor belt used to test the prey model ([7.18.22](/7.18.22#sec-22.5.2)). Great tits were offered three kinds of item: small conspicuous prey (half a mealworm in a clear straw), large "cryptic" prey (a whole mealworm in an opaque straw) and inedible "twigs" (opaque straws containing string). A bird needed only 3–4 s to examine an opaque straw and tell a twig from prey, yet when twigs were common, the birds largely ignored the large cryptic prey and specialized on the less profitable conspicuous ones, as the prey model predicts once the time spent examining twigs is included in the handling time of cryptic prey. A prey need only be concealed well enough to make some other prey more profitable to the predator; even crude concealment can be the first step of an arms race.

### 24.2.5. Masquerade

**Masquerade** ([7.17.39](/7.17.39#sec-39.4.1)), the resemblance to an inedible object, acts at the stage of identification rather than detection: the masquerader is seen but mistaken for a twig, a leaf, a stone or a bird dropping. Skelhorn and coauthors (2010) showed that domestic chicks that had encountered real twigs were slower to attack twig-like caterpillars than chicks without that experience, so that the protection depends on the predator's knowledge of the model object. Predators use crypsis and masquerade too. Stargazers (Uranoscopidae) lie buried in sand with only their eyes and upturned mouth exposed and engulf small fish that swim over them; crab spiders (*Thomisus*, *Misumena*) change color over days to match the flowers on which they wait for pollinators, a compromise between being invisible to their prey and to their own bird predators; and many anglerfishes look like sponge-covered rocks.

## 24.3. Warning Signals

### 24.3.1. Why Be Conspicuous?

Many animals do the opposite of hiding. Wasps, ladybirds, monarch butterflies, poison frogs, coral snakes and skunks are conspicuously colored, usually in red, orange or yellow with black, and they are defended by stings, toxins, distastefulness or noxious sprays. Alfred Russel Wallace suggested to Darwin in 1867 that bright colors advertise distastefulness, the idea now called **aposematism** ([7.10.17](/7.10.17)). Among poison frogs (Dendrobatidae), bright coloration and high toxicity evolved several times independently from cryptic, palatable ancestors with a generalized diet, each time together with a diet specialized on ants and mites, the sources of the frogs' alkaloids (Santos et al. 2003).

Experiments show why conspicuousness helps distasteful prey. Gittleman and Harvey (1980) gave domestic chicks distasteful breadcrumbs (dipped in quinine and mustard) that were either the same color as the background or a contrasting color. The chicks took more conspicuous crumbs at first, but learned to avoid them faster, and over the whole experiment more of the cryptic crumbs were eaten. Avoidance learning of conspicuous noxious prey is often fast and lasting: blue jays that had once vomited after eating a monarch butterfly avoided monarchs thereafter (Brower 1958), and some predators avoid warning patterns without any experience: hand-reared great kiskadees (*Pitangus sulphuratus*) avoided wooden rods painted with the red and yellow rings of coral snakes (Smith 1975). Conspicuous signals have at least three advantages for a defended prey: they are learned faster, they are remembered longer, and once learned they are less likely to be attacked by mistake, since a predator that must decide quickly makes fewer recognition errors when the prey is distinctive (Guilford 1986).

### 24.3.2. Learning to Avoid Multimodal Signals

Warning signals are often multimodal: ladybirds are red and black and smell of methoxypyrazines, tiger moths combine color with ultrasonic clicks, and many aposematic insects produce sounds or odors when handled. Rowe and Guilford (1996) found that the smell of pyrazine, which is not itself aversive to chicks, made naive chicks avoid yellow and red food, but not green food, a "hidden" color aversion released by the odor. In learning experiments, chicks offered distasteful yellow crumbs learned to avoid them faster when the crumbs smelled of pyrazine than when they were yellow alone. The **Rescorla–Wagner rule** ([7.16.39](/7.16.39)) predicts part of this result, because two cues that both predict the aversive taste acquire a larger summed associative strength than one; but it also predicts that the color learned in compound with the odor should acquire less strength than the color learned alone (overshadowing), whereas the chick experiments found the opposite, an enhancement of learning about the color in the presence of the odor (potentiation). Warning signals may have evolved to exploit features of predator learning that a simple associative rule does not capture.

### 24.3.3. The Evolution of Warning Signals

Aposematism poses an evolutionary puzzle ([7.10.17](/7.10.17)). Imagine a population of distasteful but cryptic caterpillars in which a conspicuous mutant appears. The mutant is more likely to be noticed and attacked; even if the predator learns from the experience, it is unlikely ever to meet another conspicuous individual, since the mutant is rare. Selection on a warning signal is **positive frequency-dependent selection**: a signal protects only when it is common enough for predators to learn it. How can a new warning signal pass through the initial stage of rarity?

Several solutions have been proposed and supported:

1. *Kin selection.* Fisher (1930) noted that distasteful caterpillars often live in family groups: if a predator learns from killing one, its siblings, who share the gene for the signal, are spared. Among British butterflies, the caterpillars of 9 aposematic species and no cryptic species lived in family groups, whereas 11 aposematic and 44 cryptic species were solitary (Harvey et al. 1983), and models show that **kin selection** in groups can favor the signal. But a phylogenetic analysis of butterflies found that aposematism usually evolved before gregariousness, not after it (Sillén-Tullberg 1988), so grouping cannot have been the general starting point.
2. *Survival of the sampled individual.* Many aposematic insects have tough bodies and are tasted and released rather than killed; Wiklund and Järvi (1982) found that most distasteful insects attacked by naive birds survived. If the attacked individual survives, it benefits directly from being memorable.
3. *Neophobia and dietary conservatism.* Many birds are reluctant to eat novel foods, and some individuals refuse them for long periods. In experiments in which wild-caught European robins (*Erithacus rubecula*) were offered palatable pastry prey, novel red and yellow "mutants" were sampled later than blue or green ones and more often spread to fixation in the experimental populations (Thomas et al. 2003). Predator biases can let a new signal accumulate before predators learn it.
4. *Defense first, signal later.* If distastefulness and the ability to survive attacks evolved first, conspicuous signals could spread later. The desert locust *Schistocerca gregaria* shows both states: at low density its nymphs are green, cryptic and avoid toxic plants; at high density, when crypsis is impossible, they become black and yellow, aggregate, and eat toxic plants, and predators learn to avoid them faster in this aposematic form (Sword et al. 2000).

### 24.3.4. The Costs and Honesty of Warning Signals

Warning signals are not free. In the wood tiger moth (*Arctia plantaginis*), whose caterpillars have an orange patch of variable size, more orange caterpillars are better protected against birds, but in cold conditions caterpillars with more black absorb more heat and grow faster, so that the size of the patch reflects a **trade-off** between warning and thermoregulation; adults reared on food with more toxic iridoid glycosides were less brightly colored, which suggests that detoxification and pigment production compete for resources (Lindstedt et al. 2009, 2010). Conspicuousness also conflicts with crypsis toward predators that are not deterred, and the balance can shift with the predator community: male guppies (*Poecilia reticulata*), whose colors are favored by **sexual selection**, are duller in streams with dangerous fish predators and evolve brighter colors within a few generations when moved to streams without them (Endler 1980).

Is the strength of a warning signal an honest indication of the strength of the defense? Signals of a fixed form are conventional and could in principle be cheated, as Batesian mimics do. Within some groups, more conspicuous species or populations are more toxic: in the strawberry poison frog (*Oophaga pumilio*), whose color varies greatly among the islands of Bocas del Toro in Panama, brighter populations are more toxic (Maan and Cummings 2012). Among mammalian carnivores, species with bold black-and-white patterns, such as skunks and honey badgers, are those with noxious anal-gland sprays or a strong ability to fight, and they live in open habitats where other defenses are limited (Stankowich, Caro and Cox 2011). The honesty of signals in general is the subject of [7.18.37](/7.18.37) and [7.18.39](/7.18.39).

## 24.4. Mimicry

### 24.4.1. Batesian Mimicry

**Mimicry** ([7.10.17](/7.10.17)) involves three parties: a mimic, a model and the receiver, here the predator, that confuses them. In **Batesian mimicry**, named after Henry Walter Bates, who described it in Amazonian butterflies in 1862, a palatable mimic resembles a defended model. Examples are found in most animal groups: hover flies and clearwing moths (Sesiidae) resemble stinging wasps and bees, ant-mimicking jumping spiders wave their front legs like antennae, and the harmless scarlet kingsnake (*Lampropeltis elapsoides*) of the southeastern United States has red, black and yellow bands like the venomous eastern coral snake (*Micrurus fulvius*).

Experiments with coral snake mimics showed that the resemblance protects only where the model occurs. Pfennig and coauthors (2001) placed plasticine replicas of snakes, ringed like the kingsnake or plain brown, at sites inside and outside the range of coral snakes in North Carolina and Arizona, and counted the marks left by attacking carnivores. Outside the coral snake's range, the ringed replicas were attacked more than the brown ones; inside it, they were attacked less. Across the New World, red–black banded coloration evolved many times independently in nonvenomous snakes, and the shifts to mimetic coloration are concentrated in places and times where coral snakes were present (Davis Rabosky et al. 2016). Because coral snake bites can kill a predator, the predator cannot learn from experience; avoidance of the pattern must be largely innate, as in the kiskadees of §24.3.1.

### 24.4.2. Mimicry as a Problem of Signal Detection

Mimicry is a contest of perception. The predator must decide whether an item with the warning pattern is a mimic, worth eating, or a model, costly to attack, on the basis of imperfect information, and the theory of signal detection predicts its best decision rule (Oaten, Pearce and Smyth 1975; Sherratt 2002).

**Formula 24.4.1 (Optimal Attack Criterion)** Let the predator perceive an appearance $x$ that is distributed as $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ for models and $\mathcal{N}(d, 1)$ for mimics, where $d$ measures the imperfection of the mimicry. Let a proportion $m$ of the patterned prey be mimics, the benefit of eating a mimic be $B$ and the cost of attacking a model be $C$. Attacking all items with $x > c$ maximizes the expected payoff when, at the criterion, the likelihood ratio of mimic to model equals the ratio of the expected costs and benefits, $\mathrm{e}^{dc - d^2/2} = (1 - m)C/(mB)$, that is, when

$$
c^* = \frac{d}{2} + \frac{1}{d}\ln\frac{(1 - m)C}{mB}, \tag{24.1}
$$

and a mimic is then attacked with probability $\mathbb{P}(x > c^* \mid \text{mimic}) = 1 - \Phi(c^* - d)$.

The formula gives the classical results of mimicry theory in a single expression. Mimics are attacked more often when they are common (large $m$ lowers $c^*$), which is the **negative frequency-dependent selection** that limits Batesian mimics and favors polymorphic mimicry, as in the African swallowtail *Papilio dardanus*, whose females resemble several different models. Mimics are protected better when the model is more noxious (large $C$), and when they resemble the model closely (small $d$). Most interestingly, an imperfect mimic can be well protected if the model is noxious enough: when the cost of a mistake is high, a predator should avoid anything that looks even remotely like the model, which helps to explain why many hover flies resemble wasps only crudely ([7.17.39](/7.17.39#sec-39.4.4)). The model also predicts selection on the predator to discriminate better, and on the model to diverge from its mimics, an arms race that the mimic tends to win because a model that changes its appearance loses the protection of the predators' learning (Nur 1970).

### 24.4.3. Müllerian Mimicry and Mimicry Rings

In **Müllerian mimicry**, defended species resemble each other and share the cost of educating predators. Mimicry rings of many species are common: the distasteful *Heliconius* butterflies of the Neotropics form local rings with other butterflies and moths, and the two co-mimics *H. erato* and *H. melpomene* have converged on the same color pattern in each of some 25 geographic races ([7.17.39](/7.17.39#sec-39.4.4)); in the tropical Pacific, several species of dorid nudibranchs, which store toxins from the sponges and ascidians they eat, share color patterns with each other and with distasteful polyclad flatworms. That predators treat co-mimics as equivalent has been shown directly. Naive domestic chicks that learned to avoid one species of Peruvian poison frog, *Ranitomeya imitator* or *R. variabilis*, also avoided the other, which has a similar pattern (Stuckert et al. 2014). In the field, *Heliconius erato* butterflies painted to look like a race from another region survived less well than controls painted without changing their pattern (Benson 1972), and butterflies transferred across the boundary between two races survived less well and carried more beak marks (Mallet and Barton 1989).

Real cases lie on a continuum between the two kinds of mimicry, because co-mimics differ in how well they are defended; a weakly defended species can be a partial parasite of a strongly defended one, and whether it benefits or harms its model depends on how predators weigh their experiences with each.

### 24.4.4. Behavioral Mimicry

Appearance is often not enough: the mimic must also behave like the model. The tephritid fly *Zonosemata vittigera* has banded wings that it waves in a way that resembles the leg-waving threat display of the jumping spiders that prey on flies. To test whether the display deters spiders, Greene, Orsak and Whitman (1987) exchanged wings between tephritids and houseflies. Tephritids carrying their own wings, removed and glued back on as a control, repelled spiders in 16 of 20 encounters; tephritids given housefly wings were eaten, although they waved them normally; and houseflies given tephritid wings gained no protection, because they did not wave them. Both the pattern and the movement were needed. Hover flies that mimic honeybees hover and fly like them; ant-mimicking spiders walk in the zigzag manner of ants; and burrowing owls (*Athene cunicularia*), which nest in the burrows of ground squirrels, produce a hiss in their nests that resembles the rattle of a rattlesnake, to which ground squirrels respond with caution (Rowe, Coss and Owings 1986).

## 24.5. Chemical Defense: Poisons, Venoms and Sprays

### 24.5.1. Poisons and Venoms

Chemical weapons are widespread among animals, and they are often misdescribed in popular accounts, which rank "the most poisonous animals" without distinguishing how a toxin is delivered or how its toxicity was measured.

**Definition 24.5.1 (Poison, Venom and Toxungen)** A **poison** is a toxic substance that an organism carries in its body or on its surface and that harms another organism only when contacted, touched or eaten; delivery is passive. A **venom** is a toxic secretion that is introduced into another organism through a wound made by a specialized structure (a sting, fang, spine, harpoon or nematocyst). A *toxungen* is a toxic secretion that is actively sprayed, spat or smeared onto another organism without a wound (Nelsen et al. 2014).

By these definitions, poison frogs, pufferfish, monarch butterflies and the birds of the genus *Pitohui* of New Guinea are poisonous; snakes, scorpions, spiders, cone snails, stonefishes and box jellyfish are venomous; and skunks, spitting cobras, bombardier beetles and many ants use toxungens. The hooded pitohui (*Pitohui dichrous*) carries batrachotoxins in its skin and feathers, obtained from melyrid beetles of the genus *Choresine* that it eats (Dumbacher et al. 1992, 2004); it is poisonous, not venomous, and it is not the only toxic bird: the related *Ifrita kowaldi* carries the same toxins, and the spur-winged goose (*Plectropterus gambensis*) can accumulate cantharidin from the blister beetles it eats. The most toxic poison frog known is the golden poison frog (*Phyllobates terribilis*) of Colombia, a single wild individual of which can carry about a milligram of batrachotoxins (Myers, Daly and Malkin 1978), enough to kill several people. Comparisons of "toxicity" should be made with care, because the lethal dose depends on the test animal and the route of administration, and the danger of an animal to people depends on how often it delivers its toxin, not only on how potent it is.

### 24.5.2. Where Toxins Come From

Animals obtain their chemical defenses in three ways ([7.17.39](/7.17.39#sec-39.4.3)):

- *Synthesis.* Many venoms are mixtures of peptides and proteins made by the animal itself, and some poisons, such as the cyanogenic glycosides of burnet moths and the steroids (bufadienolides) of toads, are also synthesized.
- *Sequestration from food.* Monarch caterpillars store cardenolides from milkweeds, poison frogs alkaloids from mites and ants, nudibranchs toxins from sponges, and pitohuis batrachotoxins from beetles. Sequestered defenses depend on a reliable food supply: nudibranchs that eat sponges, a constant source of toxins, are nearly all brightly colored, whereas animals that acquire saxitoxin from dinoflagellates, whose abundance fluctuates greatly, are usually not aposematic (Bradbury and Vehrencamp 2011).
- *Symbionts.* Tetrodotoxin (TTX) in pufferfish, newts, the blue-ringed octopus (*Hapalochlaena*) and other animals is produced, at least in part, by bacteria and accumulated through the food chain ([7.18.6](/7.18.6#sec-6.4.1)).

### 24.5.3. Venoms: Prey Capture and Defense

Venoms serve two functions, to subdue prey and to defend against predators, and the two select for different properties. Venoms used against prey act fast and immobilize: most block neuromuscular transmission, as the **neurotoxin** cocktails of elapid snakes and cone snails do ([7.18.6](/7.18.6#sec-6.4)), or disrupt the blood. Many viper venoms are not simply anticoagulant, as is often said; they contain enzymes that activate clotting factors, which consumes fibrinogen and platelets and, paradoxically, leaves the blood unable to clot, causing internal bleeding (venom-induced consumption coagulopathy), together with metalloproteinases that damage the walls of blood vessels. Venoms used in defense must hurt immediately, because a predator that dies later does not learn and does not stop its attack. The sting of a stonefish is notoriously painful; the venom of the Texas coral snake (*Micrurus tener*) contains MitTx, a toxin that activates the acid-sensing ion channels of pain neurons (Bohlen et al. 2011); and in spitting cobras, which spray venom at the eyes of attackers, the evolution of spitting (three times independently) was accompanied by an increase in venom phospholipases that intensify the pain caused by the venom's cytotoxins (Kazandjian et al. 2021).

Venom is expensive to make, and venomous animals use it sparingly. Snakes often deliver "dry" defensive bites without venom; some scorpions first inject a small volume of a "prevenom" that causes pain but contains few of the costly lethal peptides, and inject the main venom only if the threat persists; and scorpions with large pincers use them rather than the sting to subdue small prey (the venom optimization hypothesis; Wigger, Kuhn-Nentwig and Nentwig 2002). The geography cone deploys different venoms for hunting and for defense ([7.18.6](/7.18.6#sec-6.4.2)).

### 24.5.4. The Problem of Being Tasted

A defense that works only after the prey has been eaten is of no use to the individual eaten. An animal whose toxins are inside its body must be attacked, and often injured or killed, before the predator learns. Several features reduce this cost. Many distasteful insects concentrate their defensive chemicals in the cuticle or in secretions released on contact, so that a predator rejects them after tasting, and bird predators often hold an insect in the beak, taste it, and release it alive. And many animals deliver their defense at a distance, as toxungens:

- Striped skunks (*Mephitis mephitis*) spray a mixture of thiols and thioacetates from anal glands, accurately for several meters; the spray is preceded by warning displays (foot stamping, raised tail, and in spotted skunks a handstand), and skunks spray only when these fail.
- Formicine ants spray formic acid from the acidopore, and whip scorpions (*Mastigoproctus*) a spray containing concentrated acetic acid.
- Bombardier beetles eject a boiling mixture of benzoquinones from a reaction chamber ([7.17.39](/7.17.39#sec-39.4.3)).

### 24.5.5. Resistance

Every chemical defense selects for resistance in predators. Resistance by **target-site insensitivity** ([7.18.6](/7.18.6#sec-6.4.3)) has evolved in garter snakes (*Thamnophis sirtalis*) that eat TTX-laden rough-skinned newts (*Taricha granulosa*), the classic predator–prey arms race with a geographic mosaic of more and less escalated populations ([7.10.17](/7.10.17)); in monarch butterflies, insensitive to the cardenolides they store; and in mongooses, honey badgers, hedgehogs and pigs, whose muscle **nicotinic acetylcholine receptors** carry substitutions that prevent the binding of snake α-neurotoxins (Barchan et al. 1992; Drabeck et al. 2015). Resistance is a behavioral matter too: a garter snake that eats a toxic newt may be paralyzed for hours, and resistant snakes assess newts, rejecting individuals that are too toxic.

## 24.6. Escape and Last-Ditch Defenses

### 24.6.1. When to Flee

An animal that sees a predator approaching must decide when to flee. Fleeing too early wastes time and energy and gives up feeding, mating or resting; fleeing too late risks capture.

**Definition 24.6.1 (Flight Initiation Distance)** The **flight initiation distance** of a prey animal is its distance from an approaching predator (or a human observer used as a stand-in) at the moment it begins to flee.

Ydenberg and Dill (1986) analyzed the decision economically.

**Formula 24.6.1 (The Economic Model of Escape)** Let $R(D)$ be the expected cost of remaining when the predator is at distance $D$ (the probability of being captured times the loss of fitness), which increases as the predator approaches, and $F(D)$ the cost of fleeing at distance $D$ (the opportunities given up by leaving now), which decreases as the predator approaches because less is lost by leaving later. The prey should flee at the distance $D^*$ at which

$$
R(D^*) = F(D^*). \tag{24.2}
$$

The model predicts that flight initiation distance increases with the danger posed by the predator (its speed, size and directness of approach, and the distance to a refuge) and decreases with the value of what the prey would give up. Both predictions are well supported. Across studies, animals flee earlier from faster and larger predators, from predators that approach directly or quickly, and when farther from a refuge, and they flee later when feeding on rich food or when guarding mates or young (Stankowich and Blumstein 2005). Lizards fled later from an approaching person when feeding on a mealworm than when not, and crayfish fed on more valuable food allowed a model predator to come closer. Cooper and Frederick (2007) extended the model by maximizing fitness directly, including the prey's initial fitness: an animal with more to lose, such as one in good condition, should be more cautious. Experience also matters. Birds in cities, which are approached by people that never attack, have shorter flight initiation distances than rural birds of the same species (Møller 2008), and animals on islands without predators often allow a very close approach. The neural decision to escape, and its bias toward false alarms when errors are asymmetric, are treated in [7.18.8](/7.18.8#sec-8.3) and [7.18.14](/7.18.14).

### 24.6.2. Startle Displays and Eyespots

When a cryptic animal has been discovered, it may suddenly reveal a conspicuous pattern.

**Definition 24.6.2 (Deimatic Display)** A **deimatic display** (startle display) is a sudden display of conspicuous colors, patterns, postures or sounds by a prey that has been discovered or touched, which causes the predator to hesitate or retreat; unlike a warning signal, it need not be backed by a defense.

The underwing moths of §24.2.1 have cryptic forewings that cover brightly colored hindwings, exposed when the moth is disturbed. Schlenoff (1985) trained blue jays to remove model moths from a board to obtain food. When the models suddenly revealed brightly patterned "hindwings", jays trained on models with gray hindwings startled; jays trained on bright models did not startle at gray ones; and the jays showed **habituation** to a repeated pattern but startled again at a novel bright one. The startle effect therefore favors diversity of hindwing patterns among the sympatric species of *Catocala*. Peacock butterflies (*Aglais io*), which are palatable, flick open their wings to reveal four large eyespots and produce a hissing sound with ultrasonic clicks; birds attacked butterflies whose eyespots had been painted over more often than controls (Vallin et al. 2005), and mice approaching them in the dark backed away when they began to flick (Olofsson et al. 2011). The large caterpillar of the walnut sphinx (*Amorpha juglandis*) whistles through its spiracles when pecked, and birds that pecked it flew away (Bura, Rohwer and Gibson 2011).

Why are eyespots effective? The traditional explanation is that they resemble the eyes of the predator's own enemies, such as owls. Experiments with artificial prey found that conspicuous circles survived no better than other conspicuous shapes of the same contrast, and that eye-like circles were no more effective than other circles (Stevens et al. 2007, 2008), which suggests that conspicuousness and novelty are what matter. Other experiments found that great tits were deterred by images of eyespots about as much as by images of owl eyes, and more than by other patterns, supporting eye mimicry (De Bona et al. 2015). Both mechanisms may operate, depending on the eyespot and the predator. Small marginal eyespots, in contrast, deflect attacks away from the body ([7.17.39](/7.17.39#sec-39.4.2)).

### 24.6.3. Telling the Predator It Has Been Seen

Predators that rely on surprise gain little from attacking a prey that has seen them, and prey often signal to predators that they have been detected or that they would be hard to catch. Such signals benefit both parties if they are honest. Many lizards perform push-up displays when they see a predator, and in *Anolis cristatellus* the number of push-ups performed toward a model snake was correlated with the lizard's running endurance (Leal 1999), so the display carries information about the difficulty of capture. Deer flash the white underside of their tails, kangaroos thump the ground, and fish and ungulates approach and inspect predators, which then often leave: cheetahs and leopards abandon hunting in an area after being inspected by their prey. The best-known example is stotting, the stiff-legged leaping of gazelles and springbok fleeing a predator. Caro (1986) tested eleven hypotheses for stotting in Thomson's gazelles (*Eudorcas thomsonii*); his observations that solitary gazelles stot, and that stotting gazelles turn their white rumps toward the predator rather than toward other gazelles, rejected the hypotheses that stotting warns conspecifics and supported the hypothesis that it signals to the predator. Cheetahs abandoned more hunts when the gazelle stotted, and African wild dogs chose to chase gazelles that stotted at lower rates, which were caught more easily (FitzGibbon and Fanshawe 1988). The signaling theory of these displays, their costs and the evidence that they are honest are discussed in [7.18.39](/7.18.39).

### 24.6.4. When the Predator Strikes

Once caught, prey have a few last resorts:

- *Autotomy*: shedding a body part, such as a lizard's tail, which continues to twitch and holds the predator's attention while the prey escapes. The tails of many juvenile skinks are bright blue, which draws attacks to the expendable part; autotomy has costs, since the tail stores fat and its loss lowers social status and future escape.
- *Death feigning* (thanatosis): becoming immobile, as do hognose snakes, which roll over with mouth open, Virginia opossums and many insects ([7.17.39](/7.17.39#sec-39.4.3)); many predators stop attacking motionless prey or lose interest in them.
- *Distraction*: ground-nesting shorebirds such as killdeer perform a "broken-wing" display that leads a predator away from their nest; cephalopods release clouds of ink, some shaped like a decoy of their own body.
- *Distress calls*: loud, harsh screams given when seized, which may startle the predator or attract other predators that interfere with it, giving the prey a chance to escape.

## 24.7. Armor and Inducible Defenses

The most direct defense is to be impossible to swallow or to bite through. Turtles, armadillos, pangolins, chitons, boxfishes and many beetles are armored; porcupines, hedgehogs, tenrecs and pufferfishes are spiny; and many prey make themselves bigger, as pufferfishes do by inflating with water. Armor has costs in speed, maneuverability and growth. The three-spined stickleback (*Gasterosteus aculeatus*) illustrates the trade-off: marine populations are covered by a row of bony lateral plates that protect against predatory fish, whereas freshwater populations, which face fewer fish predators and more insect predators that grasp prey, have repeatedly evolved reduced plating, mostly by the spread of a low-plate allele of the gene *Eda* that was already present at low frequency in the sea (Colosimo et al. 2005).

Where predation risk varies, many prey develop defenses only when predators are present, an **induced defense**. Water fleas (*Daphnia*) grow helmets and neck spines when exposed to chemical cues (kairomones) released by predatory insect larvae or fish; crucian carp (*Carassius carassius*) grow a deeper body, too deep for the mouth of a pike, when kept with pike (Brönmark and Miner 1992); tadpoles develop deeper tails and smaller bodies in the presence of dragonfly larvae (McCollum and Van Buskirk 1996); and marine snails thicken their shells when they smell crabs feeding on their conspecifics (Trussell 1996). Inducible defenses evolve when predation risk is variable and predictable from reliable cues, and when the defense is costly in the absence of predators; tadpoles with deep tails swim more slowly and grow more slowly, and they do better than undefended tadpoles only when dragonflies are present. Behavior is the most flexible of all inducible defenses: the reduction of activity, the shift to refuges and the increase of vigilance in the presence of predators described in [7.18.23](/7.18.23#sec-23.6) are the behavioral counterparts of helmets and thick shells.

## Summary

- Predators and prey are engaged in an **evolutionary arms race**, sometimes with Red Queen dynamics (*Daphnia* and *Pasteuria*); the **life–dinner principle** (Definition 24.1.1) makes selection stronger on prey. Defenses act at every stage of the predation sequence: **primary defenses** before an encounter and **secondary defenses** during it (Definition 24.1.2).
- **Crypsis** includes **background matching**, **disruptive coloration** and **countershading**, each confirmed with artificial prey; it requires behavior (choice of background and orientation, stillness). Open-ocean animals use transparency, mirrors and counterillumination; cephalopods change color and texture with neurally controlled chromatophores despite being color-blind.
- Predators form **search images** (Definition 24.2.1); polymorphic prey resist them, and **apostatic selection** (Definition 24.2.2) maintains polymorphism (Bond and Kamil's virtual moths). Even slight concealment pays (great tits on the conveyor belt). **Masquerade** acts at identification.
- **Aposematism** works because conspicuous noxious prey are learned faster, remembered longer and mistaken less; multimodal signals speed learning, sometimes beyond what the **Rescorla–Wagner rule** predicts. New warning signals overcome their initial rarity through kin selection, survival of tasted individuals, neophobia and dietary conservatism, or prior defense; warning signals have costs (wood tiger moths, guppies).
- **Batesian mimicry** protects only where the model is present (coral snake replicas). As a signal-detection problem (Formula 24.4.1), mimics are protected better when rare, close to the model and when the model is very noxious. **Müllerian mimicry** forms mimicry rings; behavioral mimicry (tephritid flies, burrowing owls) adds movement to appearance.
- **Poisons**, **venoms** and toxungens (Definition 24.5.1) differ in delivery; toxins are synthesized, sequestered from food or made by symbionts. Prey venoms immobilize, defensive venoms hurt; venom is metered. Sprays avoid the cost of being tasted. Predators evolve **target-site insensitivity**.
- Prey flee at the **flight initiation distance** (Definition 24.6.1) where the cost of staying equals the cost of fleeing (Formula 24.6.1). **Deimatic displays** (Definition 24.6.2) and eyespots startle predators; push-ups and stotting tell predators that pursuit would fail. Armor and **induced defenses** complete the repertoire.

## Questions

1. (Calculation) Using Formula 24.4.1 with $d = 1$, $B = 1$ and $C = 4$, find the optimal criterion and the probability that a mimic is attacked when mimics make up 20% and 50% of the patterned prey.

   **Answer** For $m = 0.2$, $\ln[(0.8 \times 4)/(0.2 \times 1)] = \ln 16 \approx 2.77$, so $c^* = 0.5 + 2.77 = 3.27$ and a mimic is attacked with probability $1 - \Phi(2.27) \approx 0.012$. For $m = 0.5$, $\ln 4 \approx 1.39$, $c^* = 1.89$ and the probability is $1 - \Phi(0.89) \approx 0.19$. Increasing the proportion of mimics from one fifth to one half raises the mimics' risk sixteenfold, the negative frequency dependence that limits Batesian mimics.

2. Explain why a new conspicuous mutant in a population of distasteful, cryptic prey is expected to be at a disadvantage, and evaluate the evidence for two of the proposed solutions.

   **Answer** Predators must learn that a pattern signals distastefulness by attacking a number of individuals that carry it; while the pattern is rare, the conspicuous individuals are detected more often than cryptic ones but are too few for predators to learn about them, so they suffer the costs of conspicuousness without its benefit (positive frequency dependence). Kin selection in family groups (Fisher) is supported by the association of aposematism with gregariousness among British butterfly caterpillars, but phylogenetic analysis shows that aposematism usually evolved before gregariousness, so it cannot be the general explanation. Survival of tasted prey is supported by observations that most distasteful insects attacked by naive birds are released alive; dietary conservatism is supported by experiments in which robins sampled novel red and yellow prey late, allowing them to increase.

3. Design an experiment to test whether the eyespots of a butterfly protect it by mimicking the eyes of predators or by being conspicuous.

   **Answer** Present a bird predator (for example, wild-caught great tits in an aviary) with artificial or real prey bearing (i) natural eyespots, (ii) circles of the same size and contrast but without eye-like features (no pupil or highlight, reversed contrast), (iii) non-circular marks of the same area and contrast (bars, squares), and (iv) no marks. Measure latency to attack, attack rate and the startle response. Eye mimicry predicts (i) > (ii) and (iii); conspicuousness predicts (i) ≈ (ii) ≈ (iii) > (iv). Additional controls: equalize the luminance contrast of all marks, test birds of different experience with owls, and present eyespots in pairs versus singly, since paired eyes are a stronger cue of a face.

4. (Calculation) In the escape model (Formula 24.6.1 with $R(D) = r\mathrm{e}^{-D/10}$ and $F(D) = fD$), show that the flight initiation distance $D^*$ increases with $r$ and decreases with $f$, and estimate $D^*$ for $r = 1$, $f = 0.01$.

   **Answer** $D^*$ solves $r\mathrm{e}^{-D/10} = fD$. The left side decreases and the right side increases in $D$, so there is one crossing. Increasing $r$ raises the left side at every $D$, so the crossing moves to larger $D$; increasing $f$ raises the right side, so the crossing moves to smaller $D$. For $r = 1$, $f = 0.01$: try $D = 17$: $\mathrm{e}^{-1.7} = 0.183$ and $0.17$; $D = 18$: $0.165$ and $0.18$; the crossing is near $D^* \approx 17.5\,\mathrm{m}$.

5. Distinguish poison, venom and toxungen, and explain why defensive venoms are typically painful while venoms used for prey capture need not be.

   **Answer** A poison harms only when touched or eaten (pufferfish, poison frogs, pitohuis); a venom is injected through a wound by a specialized apparatus (snakes, cone snails, scorpions); a toxungen is sprayed or spat without a wound (skunks, spitting cobras, bombardier beetles). A venom used against a predator must stop the attack immediately and make the predator avoid the prey in future; a slowly acting lethal toxin would kill the predator only after it had already eaten or injured the prey, and a dead predator learns nothing. Pain is immediate and memorable. A venom used against prey must immobilize it so that it cannot escape or injure the predator; pain is irrelevant, and many prey-capture venoms (cone snail cabals that sedate fish) avoid it.

6. Why might an inducible defense evolve instead of a permanent one? Use the example of tadpoles and dragonfly larvae.

   **Answer** A defense that is costly when predators are absent should be produced only when they are present, provided that predation risk varies among places or times, that the prey can detect a reliable cue of the predator (kairomones released when dragonfly larvae feed on tadpoles), and that the defense can be produced quickly enough to be useful. Tadpoles with deep tails and small bodies escape dragonfly strikes better but grow and swim more slowly; in ponds without dragonflies, undefended tadpoles grow faster and metamorphose earlier and larger, while in ponds with dragonflies defended tadpoles survive better. A fixed phenotype would be wrong in one of the two environments; a plastic one does well in both.
