Every behavior has a direction. A woodlouse that ends up under a damp log, a moth that turns away from a hunting bat and a bat that closes in on the moth are all solving the same problem: converting stimuli that carry information about where things are into movements that change the animal's position relative to them. This chapter begins the series of classic neuroethological systems ([7.18.6](/7.18.6)) with orientation. We first classify orienting movements, following the scheme of Fraenkel and Gunn (1940) that still provides the vocabulary of the field, and examine whether orienting requires a nervous system at all, using the ciliate *Paramecium*. We then turn to the most elaborate orientation system known, the active sonar of bats, and to the countermeasures of their insect prey. The bat–moth interaction is one of the oldest topics of **neuroethology**: Kenneth Roeder's analysis of the moth ear in the 1950s and 1960s was among the first studies to link the properties of identified sensory neurons to a natural behavior and to its survival value, and it now provides one of the best-documented examples of an **evolutionary arms race** studied at the level of neurons.

The sensory physiology of hearing, including the cochlea, the barn owl and the Jeffress model, is treated in [7.14.42](/7.14.42), [7.16.24](/7.16.24) and [7.18.11](/7.18.11); insect ears and the sonar equation that explains why moths hear bats before bats detect moths are in [7.17.27](/7.17.27#sec-27.2.4). Large-scale navigation, with sun, star and magnetic compasses, follows in [7.18.21](/7.18.21).

## 12.1. Classifying Orienting Movements

### 12.1.1. Primary and Secondary Orientation

Almost every animal holds its body in a characteristic position in space: a fish swims back up, a flatworm glides with its ventral side on the substrate, a flying locust keeps its body level. Maintaining this position against disturbance is called *primary orientation*. *Secondary orientation* is the orientation of the animal, or of its movement, with respect to a particular stimulus in the environment: a light, a source of odor, a sound, a gradient of humidity. The distinction is practical rather than absolute, because the same reference stimulus may serve both functions; the direction of gravity and the brightness of the sky, for example, tell an animal both which way is up and where to go.

Secondary orientations differ in how much spatial information the animal extracts from the stimulus. At one extreme the animal only measures the intensity of the stimulus at its present position and changes its locomotion accordingly; at the other it measures the direction of the stimulus and steers relative to it. Fraenkel and Gunn's classification, built on earlier work by Kühn (1919), arranges orienting movements along this axis.

### 12.1.2. Kineses

**Definition 12.1.1 (Kinesis)** A **kinesis** is an undirected locomotor response in which the speed of movement or the frequency of turning depends on the intensity of a stimulus, without the body being oriented with respect to the direction of the stimulus. When the speed (or the proportion of time spent moving) depends on intensity, the kinesis is an *orthokinesis*; when the rate of turning depends on intensity, it is a *klinokinesis*.

The classic example is the humidity response of woodlice (*Porcellio scaber*), which lose water rapidly through their cuticle. Gunn and colleagues observed woodlice in a choice chamber with dry air on one side and moist air on the other. The animals did not turn toward the moist side; their paths were as random as before. But they moved faster and more continuously in dry air and slowed down or stopped in moist air, and within an hour most of them were on the moist side. A kinesis is enough to produce aggregation in a favorable place, as long as the animals spend more time where they move slowly.

The logic can be made quantitative. Consider an animal that moves at speed $v(x)$ and changes direction at random at rate $\lambda(x)$, both of which may depend on the local intensity of the stimulus at position $x$. For such a *velocity-jump process* in a closed arena, the stationary density of animals is

$$
\rho(x) \propto \frac{1}{v(x)},
\tag{12.1}
$$
independent of the turning rate. Orthokinesis therefore works: animals accumulate where they are slow, exactly in proportion to the slowing. Klinokinesis in its simplest form, a turning rate that depends only on the local intensity, does not, which puzzled early workers who saw flatworms accumulate in the dark by turning more often in bright light. Ullyott (1936) found the explanation: the turning rate of the flatworm *Dendrocoelum lacteum* rises when the light *increases* and then declines as the animal adapts. The worm compares the present intensity with that of a moment ago, and turns more often when things are getting worse. Turning that depends on the temporal change of a stimulus produces a biased random walk that climbs or descends gradients, the same mechanism by which bacteria perform **chemotaxis** ([7.3.15](/7.3.15)), with a memory of a few seconds provided by receptor methylation ([7.6.22](/7.6.22)).

A: density of walkers along the gradient at the end of the simulation. Orthokinesis (blue; speed falls linearly with humidity) produces accumulation on the humid side that follows the prediction density ∝ 1/v (dotted line, Eq. (12.1)). Kineses are common in invertebrates that live in patchy microhabitats: woodlice and humidity, flatworms and light, many insect larvae and temperature. They are cheap, requiring only one receptor and no comparison between body sides, but they are slow and imprecise.

### 12.1.3. Taxes

**Definition 12.1.2 (Taxis)** A **taxis** is an orienting movement of a freely moving organism in which the long axis of the body is aligned with the direction of a stimulus, so that the animal moves toward (positive taxis) or away from (negative taxis) its source. Taxes are named by the stimulus: **phototaxis** (light), chemotaxis (chemicals), geotaxis or gravitaxis (gravity), phonotaxis (sound), thermotaxis (heat), rheotaxis (water current), anemotaxis (wind), galvanotaxis (electric current) and thigmotaxis (contact with a surface).

In sessile organisms, which cannot move but can bend, the corresponding growth or bending movements are called tropisms (phototropism of plant shoots, [7.13](/7.13)). A taxis requires that the animal determines the direction of the stimulus, and there are three ways to do so:

| Mechanism | How direction is found | Sensory requirement | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Klinotaxis* | Successive comparison: the animal swings its head or body from side to side and compares intensities at successive moments | One receptor, or receptors whose sensitivity is directional | Fly larvae (maggots) moving away from light, *Euglena* |
| *Tropotaxis* | Simultaneous comparison: paired receptors on the two sides; the animal turns toward the more strongly stimulated side until stimulation is balanced | Paired receptors | Many beetles and isopods walking toward or away from a light |
| *Telotaxis* | Fixation: the animal heads directly toward one source, ignoring others, using receptors that resolve direction (an image-forming eye) | An eye or ear that resolves direction | Bees flying to one flower; hunting mantises |

Experiments distinguish these mechanisms. An animal that orients by tropotaxis and has one eye blinded walks in circles toward its intact side under uniform light, because the blinded side always reports less light; a telotactic animal walks straight toward the light even with one eye, because it fixates the source with the intact eye. Given two equal lights, a tropotactic animal walks along the bisector between them, since that is where its two sides are balanced; a telotactic animal heads for one of them, sometimes switching from one to the other along a zigzag path. Honey bees show both behaviors, but in most trials choose one source, as an animal flying to a flower should. Many animals combine mechanisms: a female cricket walking to a singing male compares the two ears (tropotaxis) but also makes small zigzags that sample the sound field over time ([7.18.16](/7.18.16)).

### 12.1.4. Transverse Orientations

**Definition 12.1.3 (Transverse Orientation)** A **transverse orientation** is the maintenance of the body axis, or of the direction of movement, at a fixed angle other than 0° or 180° to the direction of a stimulus.

Two kinds are distinguished:

- The *dorsal light response* (dorsal light reaction) keeps the back turned toward the brightest part of the visual field and is a form of primary orientation: most flying insects and swimming fish keep their dorsal side up because the sky is brighter than the ground. Backswimmers (*Notonecta*) swim upside down, keeping their ventral side toward the light (a ventral light response). Von Holst showed in fish that light and gravity are combined: a fish lit from the side tilts its back toward the light by an angle that depends on both the light intensity and the gravitational signal from the otoliths, and after removal of the utricles it orients by light alone and can be made to swim on its side or upside down by lighting it from the side or below.
- The light-compass reaction is a form of secondary orientation that keeps the direction of locomotion constant.

**Definition 12.1.4 (Light-Compass Reaction)** The **light-compass reaction** (menotaxis) is the maintenance of a straight course by keeping a distant light source, usually the sun, at a constant angle to the direction of locomotion.

The light-compass reaction was demonstrated in ants by Santschi (1911) with a simple trick: when he shaded an ant returning to the nest from the real sun and showed it the sun's image in a mirror on the opposite side, the ant turned around. In a related experiment (Brun 1914), when an ant is confined in a dark box for two hours and then released, it walks off at an angle that differs from its original course by roughly the angle through which the sun has moved in the meantime: it held the angle to the sun constant without compensating for the sun's movement. Many animals do compensate, using an internal clock; this time-compensated sun compass, used by bees, ants, birds and fish, is treated in [7.18.21](/7.18.21).

A light-compass reaction gives a straight path only if the light source is far away, so that its rays are parallel. If the light is a lamp a few meters away, its bearing changes as the animal moves, and an animal that keeps the angle $\alpha$ between its heading and the light constant follows a logarithmic spiral: inward for $\alpha < 90°$, a circle for $\alpha = 90°$ and outward for $\alpha > 90°$. This geometry has long been the textbook explanation of why moths and other night-flying insects circle lamps and eventually crash into them: they mistake the lamp for the moon.

The explanation was plausible but untested until Fabian and colleagues (2024) filmed insects of several orders around artificial lights with high-speed stereo cameras and reconstructed their flight in three dimensions. The insects did not fly spirals at a fixed angle to the light. Instead they consistently tilted their dorsal side toward the light, a dorsal light response. Because thrust is produced roughly perpendicular to the back, an insect that turns its back to a nearby lamp produces thrust tangential to the lamp and orbits it; when the lamp is above, the insect pitches up and stalls; when it is below, the insect flips over and dives into the ground. The trapping effect of lamps is thus a misfiring of primary orientation, which evolved under a sky that is brighter than the ground, rather than of the light compass. How a lamp is oriented and shielded therefore changes how strongly it traps insects, an observation with practical consequences for reducing the impact of artificial light at night.

## 12.2. Orientation Without a Nervous System

### 12.2.1. The Avoidance Reaction of *Paramecium*

Orientation does not require a nervous system. At the beginning of the twentieth century Herbert Spencer Jennings, in *Behavior of the Lower Organisms* (1906), described in detail how the ciliate *Paramecium*, a single cell about $200\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ long, finds favorable conditions. When a swimming paramecium bumps into an obstacle or enters water that is too acidic, too hot or too salty, it stops, swims backward briefly, turns through an angle and swims forward again in a new direction: the *avoidance reaction* (Jennings's "motor reaction"; in Fraenkel and Gunn's terms a phobotaxis). By repeated avoidance reactions at the edges of unfavorable zones, paramecia become trapped in water of moderate acidity around a bubble of carbon dioxide or a drop of weak acid. This is adaptive because decaying bacteria, their main food, acidify the water around them, while strong acid damages the cell. Jennings, against the behaviorists of his time, emphasized that the behavior of even these "lower organisms" depends on internal state and recent experience, not only on the stimulus.

The mechanism, worked out mainly by Hans Machemer, Roger Eckert and Yoshiro Naitoh, is electrical. The paramecium is covered by several thousand **cilia** ([7.3.14](/7.3.14)) that normally beat toward the rear and slightly to the right, driving the cell forward along a left-handed helix. Mechanosensitive channels are distributed unequally over the cell surface:

- Touching the anterior end opens mechanosensitive $\ce{Ca^2+}$ channels, which are concentrated there; the inward calcium current depolarizes the membrane. The depolarizing receptor potential spreads almost without decrement over the small cell and opens voltage-gated $\ce{Ca^2+}$ channels in the ciliary membrane. Their regenerative opening produces a graded **action potential** carried by calcium, which raises the intraciliary $\ce{Ca^2+}$ concentration from about $10^{-7}$ to about $10^{-6}\,\mathrm{M}$. At this concentration the ciliary axoneme reverses its power stroke, and the cell swims backward. As calcium is pumped out and the channels inactivate, forward beating resumes after a turn.
- Touching the posterior end opens mechanosensitive $\ce{K+}$ channels, which hyperpolarize the membrane. Hyperpolarization increases the frequency of the normal backward beat, and the cell accelerates forward, away from whatever touched its tail.

The cell membrane is thus at the same time the sense organ, the integrating "neuron" and the switch of the motor organelles. Genetic dissection confirmed the scheme: *pawn* mutants (Kung and colleagues), named after the chess piece that can only move forward, lack functional voltage-gated $\ce{Ca^2+}$ channels in their cilia and cannot swim backward, whereas other mutants that cannot inactivate the calcium current swim backward for long periods. The voltage-gated calcium channel of the cilia and its coupling to motility is an ancient design: the same principle of a membrane potential that switches the direction of locomotion is found in the electrical signaling of many protists, and the ciliate channel belongs to the same family of four-domain voltage-gated channels as the calcium and sodium channels of animal neurons ([7.6.30](/7.6.30)).

### 12.2.2. Galvanotaxis

If a direct current flows through the water, paramecia swim toward the cathode with the anterior end first, and they reverse direction when the polarity of the field is reversed. This *galvanotaxis* hardly occurs in nature, but it shows particularly clearly how the physics of a stimulus is coupled to an orienting response. The key is that the cell is a resistor placed in a conducting medium. The resistance of the membrane is several hundred times that of the cytoplasm, so the cytoplasm is nearly isopotential, while the potential outside the cell varies along it with the applied field. Hence the membrane potential, the difference between inside and outside, changes in opposite directions at the two ends.

**Example 12.2.1** A paramecium $200\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ long lies parallel to a uniform field of $2\,\mathrm{V\,cm^{-1}}$, with its resting membrane potential at $-40\,\mathrm{mV}$. Over the length of the cell the external potential changes by $2\,\mathrm{V\,cm^{-1}} \times 0.02\,\mathrm{cm} = 40\,\mathrm{mV}$. Taking the external potential at the middle of the cell as $0$, it is $-20\,\mathrm{mV}$ at the end facing the cathode and $+20\,\mathrm{mV}$ at the end facing the anode, while the internal potential is about $-40\,\mathrm{mV}$ everywhere. The membrane potential $V_{\mathrm{m}} = V_{\mathrm{in}} - V_{\mathrm{out}}$ is therefore $-40 - (-20) = -20\,\mathrm{mV}$ at the cathodal end, a depolarization of $20\,\mathrm{mV}$, and $-40 - 20 = -60\,\mathrm{mV}$ at the anodal end, a hyperpolarization of $20\,\mathrm{mV}$; in between it varies linearly. Ciliary reversal requires a depolarization of more than about $5\,\mathrm{mV}$, which here occurs only where the external potential is below $-5\,\mathrm{mV}$, that is, on the cathodal $15/40$ of the cell (about $75\,\mu\mathrm{m}$). The remaining $5/8$ of the cell is hyperpolarized or only slightly depolarized and beats backward, faster than normal near the anodal end.

:::

If the cell is oblique to the field, the depolarized and hyperpolarized regions lie on different sides of its axis, and the asymmetric beating of the reversed and the accelerated cilia produces a torque that turns the anterior end toward the cathode. The homodromic orientation (anterior end toward the cathode) is the only stable one, and because more cilia beat backward than forward, the cell swims toward the cathode. The behavior is a taxis in the strict sense, achieved by a single cell whose "computation" consists of Ohm's law and the voltage dependence of its calcium channels.

## 12.3. Gravity and Posture in Vertebrates

The direction of gravity is the most reliable reference in the environment, and nearly all animals orient to it: they show geotaxis (gravitaxis) in their movements and keep a primary orientation of the body relative to it. Gravity is sensed by organs in which a dense mass rests on mechanoreceptors: the **statocysts** of most invertebrates ([7.17.44](/7.17.44)) and the otolith organs of the vertebrate inner ear.

In the vertebrate utricle and saccule, a gelatinous membrane loaded with calcium carbonate crystals, the otolithic membrane, lies on a patch of sensory epithelium, the macula. Its receptor cells are **hair cells** ([7.14.42](/7.14.42#sec-42.3.3)), each with a bundle of stereocilia graded in height toward a single kinocilium. Deflection of the bundle toward the tallest stereocilia stretches the tip links, opens the mechanotransduction channels and depolarizes the cell, increasing the firing of its afferent fiber; deflection in the opposite direction closes the channels that are open at rest and decreases firing. When the head tilts, the otoliths slide under gravity relative to the macula, and behavioral and physiological experiments have shown that it is this shearing force, not the pressure of the otoliths on the hair cells, that is the adequate stimulus. Because the hair cells of a macula are oriented in many directions, the population of afferents encodes the direction of tilt as a vector ([7.14.42](/7.14.42#sec-42.5.3)).

The vestibular signals drive postural reflexes of the neck, trunk and limbs and the eye movements that stabilize gaze, and they are combined with vision, as von Holst's experiments on the dorsal light response of fish showed (Section 12.1.4). The hair cell itself is one of the great conserved inventions of the vertebrates: the same cell, with different accessory structures, detects gravity and acceleration in the otolith organs and semicircular canals, water movements in the **lateral line** and sound in the cochlea ([7.18.11](/7.18.11)). In the bat it is pushed to the limits of its performance.

## 12.4. Echolocation in Bats

### 12.4.1. The Discovery

The story of bat sonar begins in the 1790s with Lazzaro Spallanzani, who found that blinded bats flew through rooms strung with silk threads and caught insects as well as sighted ones, and Charles Jurine, who showed that bats with their ears plugged collided with obstacles. Since no one could hear bats making any sound, the results were set aside for more than a century; the physiologist Hartridge suggested in 1920 that bats might use high-frequency sound, but he had no means of detecting it. In 1938 Donald Griffin, an undergraduate at Harvard who had been banding bats, brought them to the physicist George Washington Pierce, who had built one of the few devices capable of detecting ultrasound. The bats were emitting a stream of calls far above the range of human hearing.

With his fellow student Robert Galambos, Griffin then repeated the classic experiments with modern controls (Griffin and Galambos 1941). Bats flying through a curtain of wires avoided them in darkness; plugging their ears or covering their mouths so that they could not call made them collide with the wires; and when the ears were plugged on one side only, avoidance was impaired. Playing loud ultrasound into the room "jammed" the bats, which then crashed into obstacles, whereas loud sounds at audible frequencies had no effect. Griffin coined the term **echolocation** ([7.14.42](/7.14.42#def-42.6.2)) for orientation by the echoes of self-produced sounds.

Griffin's most important later discovery came from recording bats in the field (Griffin, Webster and Michael 1960). Echolocation was not only a collision-warning system: little brown bats (*Myotis lucifugus*) flying in a room with fruit flies caught up to ten flies a minute, and the sequences of calls recorded during each capture showed that the bat detected, tracked and intercepted each insect acoustically. His book *Listening in the Dark* (1958) founded a field that now includes some 1,450 species of bats, most of which echolocate (the Old World fruit bats, Pteropodidae, do not use laryngeal echolocation, although *Rousettus* echolocates with tongue clicks), together with toothed whales, a few cave-dwelling birds and some shrews and tenrecs.

### 12.4.2. Sonar Signals and What They Measure

An echo carries several kinds of information about a target:

| Echo property | Target property | Physical relation |
|---|---|---|
| Delay $\Delta t$ after the call | Range $D$ | $D = c\,\Delta t/2$: $17\,\mathrm{cm}$ per millisecond in air |
| Difference between the ears in time, level and spectrum | Direction | As in passive sound localization ([7.18.11](/7.18.11)); pinna and nose-leaf shape the beam |
| Frequency shift of a constant-frequency component | Relative velocity | **Doppler effect** ([5.1.7](/5.1.7)): $\Delta f \approx 2vf/c$ |
| Periodic modulation of amplitude and frequency ("glints") | Wingbeat of an insect | Moving wings change the reflecting area and add Doppler shifts |
| Spectral interference pattern | Size, shape, texture | Reflections from different parts of the target interfere |

Because the echo returns from a target the size of an insect only if the wavelength is comparable to or smaller than the target, bats use ultrasound: at $50\,\mathrm{kHz}$ the wavelength in air is $343/50\,000 \approx 7\,\mathrm{mm}$. High frequencies have a price, because atmospheric absorption increases steeply with frequency, from about $0.5\,\mathrm{dB\,m^{-1}}$ at $20\,\mathrm{kHz}$ to about $3\,\mathrm{dB\,m^{-1}}$ at $100\,\mathrm{kHz}$, and the echo suffers the loss twice. Echolocation is therefore a short-range sense, effective for insects over a few meters (see the worked sonar example in [7.17.27](/7.17.27#sec-27.2.5)), and the choice of frequency is a compromise between detecting small targets and seeing far.

Bat calls fall into three broad classes:

- *Frequency-modulated (FM)* calls are short (about $0.5$–$10\,\mathrm{ms}$) and sweep downward through an octave or more, for example from $55$ to $25\,\mathrm{kHz}$ in the big brown bat *Eptesicus fuscus*. Their broad bandwidth makes them ideal for measuring delay: like a radar chirp, a broadband signal has a sharp autocorrelation peak.
- *Quasi-constant-frequency (QCF)* calls are long (about $10$–$50\,\mathrm{ms}$) and narrowband; they are used by bats hunting in open air, where long calls concentrate energy in one frequency band and maximize detection range.
- *CF–FM* calls combine a long constant-frequency component ($10$–$100\,\mathrm{ms}$) with a brief terminal FM sweep. They are characteristic of the horseshoe bats (Rhinolophidae), the Old World leaf-nosed bats (Hipposideridae) and the mustached bat *Pteronotus parnellii*, which hunt fluttering insects close to vegetation.

The design of each call matches the task. A bat hunting an insect passes through three phases. In the *search phase* it emits calls every $50$–$200\,\mathrm{ms}$, usually one per wingbeat, so that the costs of calling are paid by the flight muscles. When it detects a target, in the *approach phase*, it shortens and broadens the calls and calls more often. In the final $100$–$200\,\mathrm{ms}$ before capture, the *terminal buzz*, it calls up to about $200$ times per second with calls less than a millisecond long. Two geometric rules explain much of this sequence. A call must end before its own echo begins, or the bat would be deafened by its own voice (the middle-ear muscles contract during each call and relax just in time for the echo); hence call duration falls in proportion to target distance. And the next call should not be emitted until the echo of the previous one has returned and been processed; hence the calling rate rises as the distance falls.

### 12.4.3. Ranging with FM Signals

How precisely can a bat measure range? James Simmons trained big brown bats to choose the nearer of two targets and then replaced the real targets with "phantom" targets: microphones that picked up the bat's call and loudspeakers that played it back after an electronically set delay. Bats discriminated two phantom targets whose echo delays differed by about $60\,\mu\mathrm{s}$, corresponding to a range difference of about $1\,\mathrm{cm}$. In "jitter" experiments, in which the delay of the phantom echo alternated from call to call, Simmons reported that bats could detect jitter of less than a microsecond, and in some experiments of tens of nanoseconds; these extreme values are disputed, because the bats may have used spectral cues rather than delay, but there is no doubt that bats measure echo delay with a precision of microseconds.

The neural basis begins in the midbrain. Neurons in the inferior colliculus respond to each FM sweep with one or two spikes whose latency is extraordinarily constant and almost independent of the intensity of the call or echo (Pollak and colleagues): they act as time markers for the call and for the echo. In the auditory cortex of the mustached bat, Suga and colleagues found neurons that respond weakly to the call or the echo alone but strongly to an echo that follows the call at a particular delay; these delay-tuned neurons are arranged in a map of target range from about $7\,\mathrm{cm}$ to $3\,\mathrm{m}$, built by **coincidence detection** between a delayed response to the call and a response to the echo ([7.16.24](/7.16.24#sec-24.6)). The behavioral range over which mustached bats react to prey matches the range of this map.

### 12.4.4. CF Signals: Doppler-Shift Compensation and the Acoustic Fovea

The long constant-frequency calls of horseshoe bats measure something that FM calls cannot: motion. When a bat flies toward an object, the echo returns at a higher frequency. For a bat moving at speed $v$ toward a stationary reflector, the frequency of the echo is

$$
f_{\mathrm{echo}} = f_{\mathrm{call}}\,\frac{c + v}{c - v} \approx f_{\mathrm{call}}\left(1 + \frac{2v}{c}\right),
\tag{12.2}
$$
because the bat is a moving source when it calls and a moving receiver when it hears the echo. At $5\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ the echo of an $83\,\mathrm{kHz}$ call returns about $2\times5\times83\,000/343 \approx 2.4\,\mathrm{kHz}$ higher.

Schnitzler (1968) discovered that greater horseshoe bats (*Rhinolophus ferrumequinum*) do not keep their call frequency constant in flight. They lower it, so that the echo returns at a nearly constant frequency, just above the frequency the bat emits at rest.

**Definition 12.4.1 (Doppler-Shift Compensation)** **Doppler-shift compensation** is the behavior of bats with constant-frequency calls that lower the frequency of their calls in proportion to the Doppler shift caused by their own flight, so that the constant-frequency component of the echo is held within a narrow band around a reference frequency.

Compensation works by feedback: the bat compares the echo with its reference frequency and adjusts the next calls, with a precision of about $0.1$–$0.2\,\%$. It compensates only for upward shifts, which are the ones produced by forward flight. The reason for holding the echo at one frequency lies in the bat's cochlea. In *Rhinolophus*, the frequencies between about the resting call frequency and $3\,\mathrm{kHz}$ above it occupy an unusually long stretch of the basilar membrane, the frequency expansion there being more than forty times that below $70\,\mathrm{kHz}$, and are innervated by about a fifth of all auditory nerve fibers. The tuning curves of these fibers are extremely sharp, and there is a region of markedly reduced sensitivity just below the reference frequency, which protects the bat against its own loud, lowered calls. By analogy with the fovea of the eye, this region is called the *acoustic fovea*. The over-representation continues in the auditory midbrain and cortex, where the CF area and the FM area of horseshoe bats (Schnitzler and Ostwald) and the CF–CF and FM–FM areas of the mustached bat (Suga) analyze the two parts of the call separately.

Doppler-shift compensation puts the echoes of the stationary background, which are the strongest echoes in cluttered vegetation, at the reference frequency, where the bat's hearing is sharpest. An insect beating its wings adds rapid periodic shifts to its echo, and these "acoustic glints" stand out against the constant echo of leaves. Schnitzler and colleagues trained horseshoe bats to distinguish an oscillating from a stationary target and reduced the oscillation until performance dropped: bats detected echoes whose frequency was modulated by only about $\pm 30\,\mathrm{Hz}$ at $83\,\mathrm{kHz}$, a relative deviation below $0.04\,\%$. They cannot compensate for these shifts, which change at each wingbeat, faster than the bat can adjust its voice. Neurons in the cortical CF area respond with one burst per wingbeat to echoes modulated like those of a flying moth but not to pure tones, and the bats lengthen their CF calls when they encounter fluttering targets, giving the auditory system more time to analyze them. Some horseshoe bats can even identify insect species by the pattern of the glints.

### 12.4.5. Other Echolocators

Toothed whales (Odontoceti) evolved echolocation independently of bats. They produce very short broadband clicks with the phonic lips in the nasal passages; in the bottlenose dolphin (*Tursiops truncatus*) a click lasts $40$–$80\,\mu\mathrm{s}$, only a few cycles of a $40$–$150\,\mathrm{kHz}$ carrier, compared with hundreds of cycles in a bat call. The fatty melon of the forehead focuses the clicks into a beam, and echoes reach the inner ear through fat channels in the lower jaw. Because ultrasound is absorbed far less in water than in air, dolphin sonar has a much longer range: a big brown bat can just detect a $2\,\mathrm{cm}$ sphere at $5\,\mathrm{m}$, a bottlenose dolphin a similar object at about $70\,\mathrm{m}$. Like bats, toothed whales click slowly while searching and accelerate into a buzz during capture, and they adjust click intervals so that each echo returns before the next click; sperm whales emit the loudest biological sounds known. Echolocation in two groups that use different sound sources (larynx versus nasal lips), different media and different receivers is a striking example of **convergent evolution**, and the parallel amino acid substitutions in the outer hair cell motor protein prestin of echolocating bats and whales show that the convergence extends to molecules ([7.14.42](/7.14.42#sec-42.6.5)).

Oilbirds (*Steatornis caripensis*) and cave swiftlets (*Aerodramus*) use audible clicks, produced in the syrinx, to fly in the dark of their roosting caves; their resolution is coarse, and they are not known to catch prey by echolocation ([7.17.53](/7.17.53)). Some shrews and tenrecs use faint twittering calls to explore their surroundings at close range. Blind humans can learn to echolocate with tongue clicks, and in expert echolocators echoes activate the visual cortex.

The social life of bats creates a problem that every active sense faces: other bats call at the same frequencies. Many bats forage in groups and roost in colonies of thousands. Some species shift their call frequency away from that of a nearby conspecific, a jamming avoidance response analogous to that of electric fish ([7.18.15](/7.18.15)), and others fall silent and listen to their neighbor's echoes instead. Bats also exploit each other: the feeding buzz of one bat attracts others to a patch of insects, and males of some species emit "sinusoidal frequency-modulated" calls that jam a competitor's buzz just before it reaches an insect (Corcoran and Conner 2014).

## 12.5. How Moths Hear Bats

### 12.5.1. Roeder's Moth Ear

Anyone who watches moths around a street lamp on a summer night will sooner or later see a moth suddenly turn away, loop or drop like a stone toward the ground as a bat swoops past. Kenneth Roeder of Tufts University suspected that the moths heard the bats' ultrasound. Noctuid moths have a **tympanal organ** ([7.17.27](/7.17.27#def-27.2.2)) on each side of the metathorax: a thin tympanum backed by a tracheal air sac, with only two auditory receptor neurons, A1 and A2, attached to it, together with a non-auditory B cell that is probably a stretch receptor. Roeder recorded from the auditory nerve of restrained moths while playing artificial sounds and the calls of bats, and found that this minimal ear has a remarkable set of properties (Roeder 1967):

1. Both receptors respond only to ultrasound, broadly between about $20$ and $60\,\mathrm{kHz}$, the range used by most bats, and are deaf to the low-frequency songs of crickets and frogs.
2. A1 is sensitive and responds to faint calls; A2 begins to fire only at sound levels about $20$–$30\,\mathrm{dB}$ higher.
3. As the sound level rises, A1 fires more spikes per call and with a shorter latency, so its spike count encodes loudness and therefore, roughly, the distance of the bat.
4. A1 responds much more strongly to pulses than to steady ultrasound, which it quickly adapts to, so it is tuned to the pulsed calls of bats rather than to continuous noise.
5. The two receptors have the same frequency tuning; moths are tone-deaf and cannot distinguish a $30\,\mathrm{kHz}$ from an equally loud $50\,\mathrm{kHz}$ call.

The moth's ear does one thing: it detects bats. It also gives the moth some information about where the bat is. A bat on the moth's left stimulates the left A1 cell earlier and more strongly than the right one, which is shielded by the body. A bat above or below the moth produces an A1 discharge that waxes and wanes with each wingbeat, as the wings alternately expose and shield the ear, whereas a bat directly behind produces equal and steady firing in both ears. A1 can respond to the calls of a little brown bat up to about $30\,\mathrm{m}$ away, while the bat itself detects a moth only within a few meters. The asymmetry follows from the physics of sound propagation, because the echo suffers spreading and absorption losses on both legs of its journey ([7.17.27](/7.17.27#sec-27.2.5)).

### 12.5.2. Two Defenses

Roeder and Treat (1961) filmed moths near lights at night while broadcasting pulsed ultrasound, and observed two kinds of response that matched the two receptor cells:

- When the sound was faint, moths turned away from the loudspeaker and flew off: a negative phonotaxis. A moth steers away from the side on which the A1 discharge is stronger until the two sides are balanced, which happens when it faces directly away from the source, a tropotaxis. Since a bat detects moths only at close range and rarely flies straight for long, a moth that moves away from a distant bat has a good chance of never being detected.
- When the sound was loud, as from a bat a few meters away, moths flew in loops, spirals and power dives, or folded their wings and dropped. Such erratic, unpredictable maneuvers are effective against a predator that is about to intercept: a bat closing at several meters per second has less than a second to correct its course. A moth that drops into grass or foliage is hidden among the echoes of the vegetation.

Field observations confirmed that the defenses work: moths that reacted to approaching bats were caught considerably less often than moths that did not, and deafened moths are caught more often than hearing ones. Roeder proposed a simple division of labor: A1 drives the turning response through interneurons in the thoracic ganglia that compare the two sides, and A2, when a bat is close, disrupts the central steering mechanism, so that the flight motor runs erratically.

The division of labor turned out to be too simple. Notodontid moths have only one auditory receptor per ear and still show both responses (Surlykke 1984), and in noctuids the A1 cell alone signals the approach of a bat in great detail, because its firing rises with both the loudness and the rate of the calls. Recordings during simulated attacks (Fullard and colleagues 2003) showed that A1 follows the accelerating calls of the terminal buzz, whereas A2 may stop firing during the last $150\,\mathrm{ms}$ of an attack, probably because the extremely loud and rapid calls of a nearby bat drive it into refractoriness. Interneurons further along the pathway can therefore select the escape tactic from A1 activity alone. The general lesson, which recurs in later chapters, is that behavioral decisions are rarely assigned to a single sensory neuron; they are made by central circuits that read the pattern of activity of a few receptors ([7.18.13](/7.18.13), [7.18.14](/7.18.14)).

The moth's ear illustrates the principle of stimulus filtering, which is developed in the next chapter ([7.18.13](/7.18.13)): the A1 receptor responds only to sound, only to ultrasound, preferably to pulses and without discriminating frequencies. Everything in the acoustic world of the moth except the calls of bats is filtered out at the first stage of processing. A human listener who confuses C with C-sharp loses little; a moth that failed to recognize a bat would be eaten.

### 12.5.3. Ultrasonic Hearing in Other Insects

Ears tuned to ultrasound have evolved independently in many groups of nocturnal insects, and nearly all of them use their ears to evade bats ([7.17.27](/7.17.27#sec-27.2.4)):

- Praying mantises have a single "cyclopean" ear in the ventral midline of the metathorax (Yager and Hoy 1986). A flying mantis that hears ultrasound extends its forelegs and abdomen, which throws it into a steep, spiraling dive. The ear predates bats in mantis phylogeny, so it may first have served another function before being co-opted for bat detection.
- Green lacewings hear ultrasound with an ear at the base of the wing; in response to the terminal buzz they fold their wings and drop.
- Field crickets fly at night during dispersal, and *Teleogryllus oceanicus* turns away from ultrasound in flight. Moiseff, Nolen and Hoy identified a pair of auditory interneurons in the prothoracic ganglion, AN2 (also called int-1), that respond strongly to sounds of $40$–$50\,\mathrm{kHz}$ with more spikes and shorter latency as intensity increases, like the moth's A1 cell. Inactivating AN2 by hyperpolarizing it abolished the steering response of a flying tethered cricket even though the receptors fired normally, and activating it by current injection evoked steering in silence (Nolen and Hoy 1984). AN2 is thus necessary and sufficient for the response. The motor output is simple and fast: the hindleg on the side away from the sound is swung up into the beating hindwing, which slows that wing and yaws the cricket away from the bat; crickets with their hindlegs removed turned about $40\,\mathrm{ms}$ later than intact ones (May 1991). The same cricket ear responds to the $5\,\mathrm{kHz}$ song of conspecifics, but a different interneuron (AN1) carries that information and evokes the opposite response, steering toward the sound ([7.18.16](/7.18.16)): the frequency of a sound decides whether it means "mate" or "predator".
- Some beetles, flies and even hawkmoths hear ultrasound, and nocturnal butterflies (the hedylids) have ultrasound-sensitive ears and evasive responses, whereas diurnal moths in several families have lost their sensitivity.

Comparative evidence shows that hearing is tuned to the local bat fauna. Moths of tropical communities, where bats are diverse and use a wide range of frequencies, hear a broader band than temperate moths (Fullard 1982); moths on islands with few or no bats have reduced auditory sensitivity or have lost their evasive responses, and populations of *Teleogryllus oceanicus* exposed to fewer bats show weaker AN2 responses and less avoidance. Ears and evasive circuits are costly, and they degenerate when the selective pressure that maintains them is relaxed.

## 12.6. Escalation: Tiger Moths and Stealthy Bats

### 12.6.1. Answering Back

Tiger moths (Erebidae: Arctiinae) not only hear bats but answer them. Their metathoracic tymbals ([7.17.27](/7.17.27#sec-27.6.2)) produce trains of ultrasonic clicks when a bat approaches, mostly in the approach phase and the terminal buzz. Three functions of the clicks have been demonstrated, and different species use them in different combinations:

- *Acoustic aposematism.* Many tiger moths sequester toxic compounds from their host plants (pyrrolizidine alkaloids, cardiac glycosides). Naive big brown bats that were offered noxious clicking tiger moths learned within a few nights to reject them, and the clicks were the cue: bats learned faster when the moths clicked than when they were silenced (Hristov and Conner 2005). The clicks are a warning signal in the acoustic channel, the equivalent of the warning colors of day-flying insects (**aposematism**, [7.10.17](/7.10.17)).
- *Acoustic mimicry.* Barber and Conner (2007) presented bats with two species of noxious clicking moths and then with a palatable species that also clicks: the bats rejected the palatable moths too, a case of **Batesian mimicry**, and noxious species that share similar clicks form **Müllerian mimicry** rings, as unpalatable butterflies do with wing patterns.
- *Sonar jamming.* *Bertholdia trigona*, which is palatable, produces clicks at a very high duty cycle, up to about 4,500 clicks per second, during the terminal phase of the attack. Corcoran, Barber and Conner (2009) let big brown bats hunt *B. trigona* in a flight room: bats captured moths whose tymbals had been disabled far more often than intact moths, and their attacks on clicking moths tended to miss, consistent with interference with the bat's estimate of range. The clicks do not act as a startle stimulus, since the bats did not habituate to them; they apparently degrade the bat's processing of echo delay, which requires a clean echo within a narrow time window after each call.

Other moths found other solutions. Some hawkmoths produce ultrasound by rubbing scales on their genitalia, probably as a jamming or startle signal (Barber and Kawahara 2013). Luna moths and other saturniids have long, twisted hindwing tails that spin in flight and produce a strong echo away from the body; bats direct most of their attacks at the tails, and moths whose tails were experimentally removed were captured much more often (Barber and colleagues 2015). Tails evolved several times among silk moths, an acoustic counterpart of the false heads and eyespots of butterflies.

### 12.6.2. The Bats' Reply

If moths escape by hearing bats, bats should be selected to be heard less. Several solutions have been found:

- *Stealth.* The barbastelle (*Barbastella barbastellus*) eats mainly eared moths and hunts them with calls 10 to 100 times weaker than those of other aerial hawking bats, so that moths hear it only from a few meters, when it is too late for a long-range escape (Goerlitz and colleagues 2010; [7.17.27](/7.17.27#sec-27.2.5)).
- *Frequencies outside the moths' hearing.* According to the allotonic frequency hypothesis (Fullard), bats that call at frequencies to which moths are insensitive gain an advantage. The spotted bat (*Euderma maculatum*) calls at about $10\,\mathrm{kHz}$, audible to humans, and feeds largely on moths; at the other extreme, some bats call above $100\,\mathrm{kHz}$. Bats with such calls do eat a higher proportion of eared moths, although low frequencies are also favored for other reasons (longer range, larger prey).
- *Short, high-rate calls* in the terminal phase reduce the time a moth has to react, and gleaning bats that pick insects off leaves often stop calling and listen for the sounds of their prey instead ([7.18.16](/7.18.16)).

### 12.6.3. An Asymmetric Arms Race

The interaction between bats and moths has the hallmarks of an **evolutionary arms race** ([7.10.17](/7.10.17)): reciprocal adaptations and counter-adaptations in two lineages, repeated independently many times. It is, however, strongly asymmetric. Ears evolved independently in at least seven insect orders and more than a dozen times within the Lepidoptera, while bats have changed comparatively little in response to any one prey group. Dawkins and Krebs (1979) explained such asymmetries by the *life–dinner principle*: a moth that fails to escape loses its life, while a bat that fails to catch a moth loses only a meal, so selection on the prey is stronger. A bat, moreover, eats many species of insects, most of them deaf, and its call design is shaped by all of them and by obstacles, clutter and conspecifics; a counter-adaptation that helps against eared moths must not cost too much against the rest. The bat–moth system is therefore better described as *diffuse* coevolution, in which a guild of predators and a guild of prey influence each other, than as a pairwise race.

The system also shows how the tools of neuroethology answer questions about adaptation. The properties of A1 and A2 (thresholds, tuning, adaptation), the behavioral thresholds of turning and diving, the sonar equation and the hunting tactics of bats can all be measured, and they fit together quantitatively: the moth's sensitivity is matched to the loudness of bat calls and to the range at which bats detect moths. Such a match between the properties of a neuron and the ecology of a behavior is what Roeder hoped to find, and it is the central theme of the next four chapters.

- **Echolocation**: discovered by Griffin and Galambos (1938–1941). Range from delay ($17\,\mathrm{cm\,ms^{-1}}$), velocity from Doppler shift (Eq. (12.2)), fluttering from glints. - Moths: two receptor cells, A1 (sensitive) and A2 (about 20–30 dB less sensitive), tuned to pulsed ultrasound, deaf to frequency differences; turning away from distant bats, erratic flight near ones; the A1 cell alone can drive both. ## Questions

1. A biologist finds that planarians accumulate in the darker half of a dish. Describe experiments that would determine whether the accumulation results from an orthokinesis, a klinokinesis or a negative phototaxis.

   **Answer** Track individual worms and measure, as functions of light intensity and of its recent change, the speed, the turning rate and the heading relative to the light. An orthokinesis predicts higher speed in bright light, with headings uniformly distributed, and accumulation proportional to $1/v$ (Eq. (12.1)). A klinokinesis predicts a higher turning rate, and Eq. (12.1) shows that it can produce accumulation only if the turning rate depends on the change of intensity: test this by changing the light intensity uniformly over the whole dish (temporal change without a spatial gradient), which should transiently raise the turning rate. A negative phototaxis predicts headings concentrated away from the light, immediate reorientation when the light is moved, and, if the worm uses paired eyes, circling after one eye is covered. Planarians in fact use both: their eyecups give directional information (taxis) and their turning rate adapts (klinokinesis).

2. Explain why an ant that has been kept in a dark box for two hours walks off in the wrong direction, whereas a honey bee in the same situation does not.

   **Answer** The ant in Santschi's experiment used a light-compass reaction without time compensation: it kept the sun at the same angle to its course as before, but the sun had moved about $30°$ in two hours, so the ant's course was rotated by the same angle. Honey bees (and many ants, including the desert ant *Cataglyphis*) compensate for the movement of the sun using their circadian clock and a learned representation of the sun's course (the solar ephemeris), so they keep a constant geographic direction ([7.18.21](/7.18.21)).

3. Use Example 12.2.1 to explain why a paramecium in an electric field turns its anterior end toward the cathode even if it starts out lying across the field.

   **Answer** When the cell lies at an angle to the field, one flank of the cell is nearer the cathode and is depolarized, the other is nearer the anode and is hyperpolarized (the cytoplasm is isopotential, the outside potential varies with position). On the depolarized side the cilia reverse and beat forward, on the hyperpolarized side they beat backward faster than normal. The two sets of forces act in opposite directions on opposite flanks and produce a torque that rotates the cell until its axis is parallel to the field. Of the two parallel positions, only the one with the anterior end toward the cathode is stable, because only then does the larger hyperpolarized part (the posterior $5/8$) drive the cell forward while the small depolarized anterior zone cannot overcome it; any deviation recreates a torque toward this orientation.

4. A horseshoe bat flies at $6\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ toward a hedge. Its resting CF is $82.0\,\mathrm{kHz}$ and its reference frequency for the echo is $82.2\,\mathrm{kHz}$. What frequency must it emit? Why is the reference frequency slightly above the resting call frequency?

   **Answer** From Eq. (12.2), $f_{\mathrm{call}} = f_{\mathrm{ref}}(c - v)/(c + v) = 82.2 \times 337/349 \approx 79.4\,\mathrm{kHz}$, about $2.8\,\mathrm{kHz}$ below the resting frequency. The reference is slightly above the resting frequency so that the loud emitted call, which is always at or below the resting frequency, falls on the steep low-frequency flank of the acoustic fovea, where hearing is insensitive, while the echo falls on the sharply tuned, sensitive part. This protects the bat against masking by its own call.

5. Why do FM bats shorten their calls as they approach a target, and why do CF–FM bats tolerate overlap between call and echo?

   **Answer** An FM bat measures range from the delay between call and echo, so it needs the call to end before the echo begins; since the echo delay is $2d/c$, the call duration must fall in proportion to the distance (about $5.8\,\mathrm{ms}$ per meter). Overlap would mask the echo and destroy the timing information. A CF–FM bat separates call and echo in frequency instead of time: Doppler-shift compensation shifts the echo above the call, onto the sensitive acoustic fovea, while the call itself lies in an insensitive band. Overlap is therefore harmless, and the long CF call is advantageous because it gives a long observation window for glints from fluttering wings.

6. Roeder concluded that the A2 cell triggers the erratic escape flight of noctuid moths. Which observations argue against this interpretation, and what experiment would test it directly?

   **Answer** Notodontid moths, which have only one auditory receptor per ear, still show both long-range turning and short-range erratic flight (Surlykke 1984); in noctuids, A2 may stop firing during the last part of the terminal buzz, exactly when erratic flight is most needed (Fullard and colleagues 2003); and A1 activity alone changes enough during an attack (in spike rate and pattern) to signal the bat's approach. A direct test would selectively silence A2 (by ablating it or blocking its axon, if it can be separated from A1) and compare the frequency of erratic flight in response to loud ultrasound, or stimulate A1 alone at rates that mimic a close bat while A2 is silent.

7. (Open question) Predict how the auditory system and evasive behavior of moths would evolve on an island colonized by a bat species that hunts with very quiet calls. What data would test your prediction?

   **Answer** Quiet calls shorten the distance at which moths detect the bat, so the long-range turning response loses value and the short-range erratic response becomes the main defense. Selection might lower auditory thresholds (if there is room for improvement given the noise floor of the ear), favor faster and more drastic escape responses, and reduce the threshold for erratic flight, so that moths dive as soon as they detect any bat. If moths cannot compensate, the stealthy bat should take a higher proportion of eared moths, as the barbastelle does. Tests: compare thresholds and escape behavior of moth populations exposed and not exposed to the stealthy bat (common-garden rearing to separate genetic from plastic differences); analyze bat diets by DNA metabarcoding to measure the proportion of eared moths; and use playback experiments with natural and quietened calls to measure detection distances.
