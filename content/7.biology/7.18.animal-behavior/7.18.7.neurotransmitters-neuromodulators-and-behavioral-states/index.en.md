The same stimulus does not always produce the same behavior. A sated animal ignores food that a hungry one devours; a crayfish that has just won a fight attacks where a loser retreats; a male fish courts only in the breeding season. Ethologists explained such variation by internal factors, motivation and **drive** ([7.18.3](/7.18.3)); this chapter treats their chemical basis. Neurons communicate by chemical messengers, and many of these act not by carrying a specific signal from one neuron to the next but by changing how whole circuits respond: they set behavioral states. We begin with the discovery of chemical transmission and the classes of messengers, then follow the main modulatory systems (dopamine, serotonin, norepinephrine, opioid peptides and endocannabinoids) from the viewpoint of behavior and of the organisms that exploit them, and end with neuromodulation as the mechanism of motivational change in invertebrate model systems. The receptors and signaling cascades are treated in [7.16.10](/7.16.10) and [7.16.11](/7.16.11), the computational role of dopamine in reward learning in [7.16.37](/7.16.37) and [7.16.39](/7.16.39); hormones, which act on behavior through the blood over longer times, follow in [7.18.19](/7.18.19).

## 7.1. Chemical Messengers of Behavior

### 7.1.1. The Discovery of Chemical Transmission

In 1921 Otto Loewi stimulated the vagus nerve of an isolated frog heart, which slowed it, collected the fluid that bathed the heart and transferred it to a second heart, whose beat slowed as well. The nerve had released a substance, which Loewi called *Vagusstoff*; Henry Dale identified it as **acetylcholine**, and the two shared the Nobel Prize of 1936. Loewi's experiment established that nerves communicate with their targets chemically, a view that took another thirty years to be accepted for synapses between neurons in the brain.

The two classes of acetylcholine receptor are named after natural products that mimic acetylcholine at one class but not the other: nicotine from tobacco activates the **nicotinic acetylcholine receptor**, a ligand-gated cation channel of muscle and neurons, and muscarine from the fly agaric mushroom (*Amanita muscaria*) activates the muscarinic receptors, G protein-coupled receptors of the heart, smooth muscle, glands and brain. That animal receptors were first characterized with plant and fungal compounds is no accident. Plants cannot run from herbivores, and many defend themselves with alkaloids that act on the nervous systems of animals. Poison hemlock (*Conium maculatum*), the plant used to execute Socrates, contains coniine, which acts on nicotinic receptors at the neuromuscular junction and causes paralysis; nicotine itself protects tobacco leaves against insects.

Herbivores in turn evolve to tolerate these defenses and sometimes to use them. Larvae of the tobacco hornworm (*Manduca sexta*) feed on tobacco, and part of the nicotine they ingest is released through their spiracles, which deters wolf spiders from attacking them (Kumar et al., 2014): the plant's weapon against the herbivore becomes the herbivore's weapon against its predator. Synthetic insecticides that act on insect nicotinic receptors (the neonicotinoids) show that sublethal effects on behavior can matter ecologically: at doses far below those that kill, they impair the homing of honey bee foragers, so that fewer return to the hive (Henry et al., 2012).

### 7.1.2. Transmitters and Modulators

A **neurotransmitter** ([7.14.5](/7.14.5#sec-5.4.3)) is classically accepted as such when it is synthesized in the presynaptic neuron, stored in vesicles, released by stimulation in a $\ce{Ca^2+}$-dependent way, mimicked by applying it to the target, and removed by uptake or enzymatic breakdown. The messengers of the nervous system belong to a few chemical classes:

| Class | Examples | Typical receptors and action |
|---|---|---|
| Amino acids | Glutamate, GABA, glycine | Mainly ionotropic; fast excitation and inhibition |
| Acetylcholine | Acetylcholine | Nicotinic (fast) and muscarinic (slow) |
| Monoamines | Dopamine, norepinephrine, epinephrine (catecholamines); serotonin (an indolamine); histamine; in invertebrates octopamine and tyramine | Almost all metabotropic; modulation |
| Purines | ATP, adenosine | Ionotropic P2X; metabotropic P2Y and adenosine receptors |
| Neuropeptides | Opioid peptides, oxytocin and vasopressin, neuropeptide Y, orexins, substance P and more than a hundred others | Metabotropic; slow, long-range |
| Gases | Nitric oxide | Diffuses through membranes; activates guanylyl cyclase |
| Lipids | Endocannabinoids | Retrograde; presynaptic $\mathrm{CB_1}$ receptors |

The distinction that matters most for behavior is not between molecules but between modes of action ([7.16.11](/7.16.11#sec-11.4)). Fast transmitters acting on ionotropic receptors carry the specific signals of circuits: which receptor was stimulated, which muscle should contract. A **neuromodulator** changes how neurons respond to those signals, acting mostly through metabotropic receptors and often by **volume transmission**, over hundreds of milliseconds to hours. Many neurons release both a fast transmitter and a modulator, and the same molecule can do either: acetylcholine transmits at the end plate and modulates in the cortex.

The importance of slow signaling was recognized by the Nobel Prize of 2000. Arvid Carlsson showed in the late 1950s that dopamine is a transmitter in its own right, not merely the precursor of norepinephrine: a drug that empties the vesicles of monoamines made rabbits immobile, and the dopamine precursor L-DOPA restored their movement, which led to the treatment of Parkinson's disease with L-DOPA. Paul Greengard showed how dopamine and other modulators act through protein phosphorylation, and Eric Kandel how serotonin and phosphorylation store memories in *Aplysia* ([7.18.41](/7.18.41)).

### 7.1.3. The Synthesis of Monoamines

**Definition 7.1.1 (Catecholamine)** A **catecholamine** is a monoamine whose molecule contains a catechol group (a benzene ring with two adjacent hydroxyl groups) and an amine side chain; the catecholamines of animals, dopamine, norepinephrine and epinephrine, are synthesized from the amino acid tyrosine.

The catecholamines and serotonin are **biogenic amines** ([7.1.27](/7.1.27)), and their synthesis follows a short pathway in which each product is the precursor of the next. The rate-limiting step for catecholamines is tyrosine hydroxylase, and a neuron's transmitter is set by which of the later enzymes it expresses: dopaminergic neurons stop at dopamine, noradrenergic neurons add dopamine β-hydroxylase (inside the vesicles), and chromaffin cells of the adrenal medulla add phenylethanolamine *N*-methyltransferase. Serotonin is made from tryptophan by tryptophan hydroxylase and the same decarboxylase; in the pineal gland it is converted further into melatonin, the night hormone of the circadian system ([7.18.18](/7.18.18)). Insects and other invertebrates make tyramine and octopamine from tyrosine by a parallel route, decarboxylation first and β-hydroxylation second; octopamine is the invertebrate counterpart of norepinephrine (Section 7.5.5).

![Synthesis of the monoamines (enzymes beside the arrows). Left: the catecholamines from tyrosine; tyrosine hydroxylase (TH) is rate-limiting, and dopamine β-hydroxylase (DBH) and phenylethanolamine N-methyltransferase (PNMT) convert dopamine into norepinephrine and epinephrine. Middle: serotonin (5-HT) from tryptophan by tryptophan hydroxylase (TPH) and aromatic L-amino acid decarboxylase (AADC), and melatonin in the pineal gland (AANAT, ASMT). Right: tyramine and octopamine in invertebrates, by tyrosine decarboxylase (TDC) and tyramine β-hydroxylase (TβH). The same decarboxylase serves the catecholamine and serotonin pathways.](./plots/monoamine-synthesis.py)

The pathway is also a toolbox for experiments. Genetic deletion of a synthetic enzyme removes one messenger: worms and flies without tryptophan hydroxylase make no serotonin, flies without tyramine β-hydroxylase make no octopamine, and mice without dopamine β-hydroxylase make no norepinephrine. The behavioral defects of such mutants are much of the evidence for the functions discussed below. Drugs that block the vesicular transporter, the breakdown enzymes or the reuptake transporters of the monoamines change behavior and mood, which first drew attention to these systems in psychiatry ([7.16.52](/7.16.52)).

## 7.2. Dopamine and Reward

### 7.2.1. The Mesolimbic System in Behavioral Perspective

The midbrain **dopamine** neurons of the ventral tegmental area (VTA) and their projections to the nucleus accumbens (ventral striatum) and prefrontal cortex form the mesolimbic and mesocortical systems. The classic experiments are treated in [7.16.37](/7.16.37): rats press a lever thousands of times an hour for electrical self-stimulation along these axons (Olds and Milner, 1954), dopamine contributes to "wanting" a **reward** (incentive salience) rather than to "liking" it, and dopamine neurons signal a **reward prediction error**. The self-stimulation sites were long called pleasure centers; the distinction between wanting and liking suggests that what they produce is closer to desire than to pleasure (Berridge and Kringelbach, 2015).

From the viewpoint of behavior, three features of the system stand out.

1. *It is engaged by natural behavior, not only by laboratory rewards.* In singing zebra finches, VTA neurons projecting to the song-learning basal ganglia nucleus respond to the quality of the bird's own song: when playback distorts a syllable, they are suppressed, and when an expected distortion is omitted, they fire more, a prediction error about performance rather than about food (Gadagkar et al., 2016; [7.18.17](/7.18.17)). Dopamine also scales the vigor of foraging and the effort an animal will spend.
2. *It is coupled to homeostasis.* The reward value of food depends on hunger: hypothalamic neurons that signal energy deficit increase the incentive value of food and of cues predicting it, so that the same taste is more rewarding to a hungry animal ([7.16.34](/7.16.34#sec-34.3.3), [7.18.8](/7.18.8)).
3. *The chemistry of reward differs between animal groups, while its logic is conserved.* In insects, dopaminergic neurons innervate the mushroom bodies, the centers of olfactory learning: one cluster (PAM) signals reward and another (PPL1) punishment, each to different compartments, so that dopamine carries both signs of value ([7.16.38](/7.16.38)). In the honey bee, a single identified octopaminergic neuron, VUMmx1, responds to sucrose, and its stimulation can replace sucrose as the reward in olfactory conditioning (Hammer, 1993).

![The mesolimbic reward system in a sagittal section of a rat brain (anterior to the left; positions schematic). Dopamine neurons of the ventral tegmental area (VTA) project to the nucleus accumbens (NAc) and prefrontal cortex (PFC) and receive input from the lateral hypothalamus (LH), which links reward to feeding state, and from cholinergic neurons of the pedunculopontine nucleus (PPT). The NAc also receives glutamatergic input from the PFC, amygdala and hippocampus, which carry information about context and learned cues, and sends output to the ventral pallidum (VP), from which it reaches motor systems.](./plots/reward-circuit.py)

### 7.2.2. Addiction and the Paradox of Drug Reward

**Addiction** ([7.16.37](/7.16.37#sec-37.5)) is the compulsive pursuit of a drug, or of a behavior such as gambling, despite harm. Its neurobiology is treated in [7.16.37](/7.16.37); here we consider two questions of behavioral biology.

The first is an evolutionary paradox. Most addictive substances are, or derive from, plant defenses: alkaloids and other compounds that evolved to deter or poison herbivores. Animals should therefore have evolved to avoid them, yet they act on reward circuits. Sullivan, Hagen and Hammerstein (2008) called this the paradox of drug reward. One answer is that such molecules act on reward circuits by accident, because they resemble messengers that evolved for other purposes; their action does not depend on a prediction and so cannot be "learned away" like the prediction error of a natural reward ([7.16.37](/7.16.37#sec-37.5.1)). Another, not exclusive of the first, is that consuming small amounts of plant toxins sometimes benefits an animal, and that some responses to them are regulated rather than accidental. Observations in other animals show that such benefits exist:

- *Medication of offspring.* Female fruit flies that see parasitoid wasps switch to laying their eggs on food containing ethanol, which their larvae tolerate better than the wasp larvae that develop inside them (Kacsoh et al., 2013).
- *Pollinator manipulation.* Nectar of coffee and citrus flowers contains low doses of caffeine. Honey bees rewarded with caffeinated sucrose were about three times as likely as controls to remember the associated floral scent a day later (Wright et al., 2013): the plant uses a neuroactive compound to make its flowers memorable.
- *Fermented food.* Pen-tailed treeshrews in Malaysia feed nightly on the naturally fermented nectar of the bertam palm without signs of intoxication (Wiens et al., 2008), suggesting that tolerance of dietary ethanol is old among mammals that eat fruit and nectar.

The second question is why only some individuals become addicted. Natural rewards are regulated by the same circuits as drugs: male fruit flies whose courtship has been repeatedly rejected seek out ethanol more than mated males, through a reduction of neuropeptide F, the fly homolog of neuropeptide Y, whose level tracks sexual reward (Shohat-Ophir et al., 2012). Animal models of addiction therefore try to capture compulsion rather than mere use, and find large individual differences: after prolonged access to a self-administered drug, only a minority of rats continue to seek it when it is signaled as unavailable or when seeking is punished (Deroche-Gamonet et al., 2004). These differences, rather than the average effect of a drug, are what models of addiction must explain.

## 7.3. Serotonin, Mood and Animal Models

### 7.3.1. Serotonin as a Signal of Behavioral State

**Serotonin** is one of the oldest signaling molecules; it occurs in plants, and serotonergic neurons are found in nearly all animals with nervous systems. In vertebrates the serotonergic neurons lie in the raphe nuclei of the brainstem and project throughout the brain and spinal cord ([7.16.11](/7.16.11#sec-11.4)). Across animals serotonin appears in a few recurring behavioral contexts:

| Animal | Serotonin-related behavior |
|---|---|
| Leech, *Aplysia*, pond snails | Initiates and sustains feeding; increases the vigor of biting |
| *C. elegans* | Signals food in the gut: increases pharyngeal pumping and egg laying and promotes dwelling on food (Section 7.5.4) |
| Desert locust | Triggers the switch from solitary to gregarious behavior when animals are crowded ([7.18.5](/7.18.5)) |
| Crayfish and lobster | Shifts posture and fighting toward dominance, depending on social status (Section 7.5.3) |
| Mouse | Increases patience: optogenetic activation of dorsal raphe serotonin neurons makes mice wait longer for delayed rewards (Miyazaki et al., 2014) |
| Primates | Low serotonin turnover is associated with impulsive aggression and risk taking |

No single function covers these contexts, but many involve the evaluation of the animal's current situation (food present, crowded, dominant) and a shift between alternative behavioral strategies: exploiting the present patch or leaving, waiting or acting, escalating or retreating. Serotonin is best thought of as a slow signal of state that biases such choices, rather than as a "happiness" molecule.

### 7.3.2. Anxiety and Mood: Animal Models and Their Limits

Mood and anxiety disorders are common, and their biology and treatment are treated in [7.16.52](/7.16.52). Their study in animals relies on behavioral tests, and these tests are a good example of how ethological knowledge improves, or should improve, laboratory paradigms.

Tests of anxiety-like behavior in rodents exploit a natural conflict between exploration and the avoidance of exposed places, where predators are more likely to strike:

- *Open field*: a rat or mouse placed in an empty arena stays near the walls (thigmotaxis) and enters the center less when it is more anxious.
- *Elevated plus maze*: a cross-shaped platform raised above the floor with two walled ("closed") and two open arms; anxiety-like behavior is measured as the proportion of time and entries in the open arms, which anxiolytic drugs increase.
- *Light–dark box* and *novelty-suppressed feeding*: the conflict between a dark refuge and a lit compartment, or between hunger and a novel arena.

Tests of depression-like behavior include the forced swim test (the time a rodent placed in a cylinder of water spends floating immobile; Porsolt, 1977), the tail suspension test, the preference for sucrose solution over water (reduced preference is taken as anhedonia), learned helplessness (animals exposed to uncontrollable shocks later fail to learn to escape controllable ones; Seligman and Maier, 1967) and chronic social defeat, in which a mouse is repeatedly defeated by a larger, aggressive mouse; afterwards some mice avoid social contact (susceptible) while others do not (resilient), a difference with neural correlates in the dopamine system (Krishnan et al., 2007).

![Two tests of anxiety-like behavior, seen from above. A: the elevated plus maze, with two walled (closed) arms and two open arms; an anxious animal stays mostly in the closed arms and the center. B, C: the open field, an empty arena whose center (dashed square) an anxious animal avoids. The tracks are simulated random walks of an animal whose heading is biased toward the nearest wall (thigmotaxis) strongly (B) or not at all (C), with the fraction of time spent in the center below each arena.](./plots/anxiety-tests.py)

Willner (1984) proposed three criteria for an animal model: *face validity* (the behavior resembles the symptoms), *construct validity* (the cause or mechanism is the same) and *predictive validity* (treatments effective in patients change the behavior, and others do not). Most tests were validated by predictive validity alone: they respond to the drugs known in the clinic, which makes them good for finding similar drugs and poor for finding new kinds. The behavioral interpretation is also questionable. Immobility in the forced swim test can be seen as an adaptive switch from active to passive coping that conserves energy in an inescapable situation, rather than as "despair", and it has been argued that the test measures coping strategy and not a depression-like state (Commons et al., 2017). An ethological view of the tests, which asks what the behavior would accomplish in the animal's natural environment, is a better guide to their meaning than the human symptom they were designed to mimic.

## 7.4. Norepinephrine, Opioids and Endocannabinoids

### 7.4.1. Norepinephrine, Arousal and the Response to Threat


**Norepinephrine** is released in the brain mainly by the locus coeruleus, a small nucleus of the pons whose axons reach almost the whole central nervous system, and in the periphery by sympathetic postganglionic neurons; **epinephrine** is released into the blood by the adrenal medulla. Together they prepare the body and brain for action. Walter Cannon called the peripheral response "fight or flight": a threat activates the **sympathetic nervous system** ([7.14.7](/7.14.7#sec-7.4)), heart rate and blood pressure rise, blood is redirected to the muscles, glucose is mobilized and the pupils dilate, within seconds. A slower hormonal response follows through the **hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis**, whose glucocorticoids act over minutes to days ([7.18.19](/7.18.19), [7.14.35](/7.14.35)).

| | Sympathoadrenal response | Hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal response |
|---|---|---|
| Messengers | Norepinephrine (nerves), epinephrine (adrenal medulla) | CRH, ACTH, glucocorticoids (cortisol or corticosterone) |
| Latency | Seconds | Minutes |
| Duration | Minutes | Hours to days |
| Behavioral role | Immediate fight, flight, vigilance | Mobilizing energy, recovery, shaping later behavior and memory |

In the brain, the locus coeruleus regulates arousal and attention. Its neurons fire little in sleep, tonically in waking and in brief bursts to salient or unexpected events. According to the adaptive gain theory of Aston-Jones and Cohen (2005), phasic bursts tied to task events favor focused performance (exploitation of the current task), whereas high tonic firing without phasic responses favors disengagement and the search for alternatives (exploration). The theory connects a neuromodulatory system to the **exploration–exploitation trade-off** of decision theory ([7.16.39](/7.16.39)) and to the foraging decisions of [7.18.22](/7.18.22) and [7.18.23](/7.18.23).

### 7.4.2. Opioid Peptides and the Modulation of Pain

In 1973 Candace Pert and Solomon Snyder showed that the brain contains receptors that bind opiates with high affinity, and in 1975 John Hughes and Hans Kosterlitz isolated the first endogenous ligands, the enkephalins.

**Definition 7.4.1 (Opioid Peptide)** An **opioid peptide** is an endogenous **neuropeptide** that acts on the opioid receptors (the G protein-coupled μ, δ and κ receptors); the main families are the endorphins (from proopiomelanocortin), the enkephalins (from proenkephalin) and the dynorphins (from prodynorphin).

Opioid peptides modulate pain at several levels. In the dorsal horn of the spinal cord, where nociceptors synapse onto projection neurons, activation of opioid receptors on the nociceptor terminals reduces $\ce{Ca^2+}$ entry and transmitter release, and activation of receptors on the postsynaptic neurons opens $\ce{K+}$ channels and hyperpolarizes them; both reduce the transmission of pain signals to the brain. A descending pathway from the periaqueductal gray through the rostral ventromedial medulla controls this gate from above ([7.16.25](/7.16.25)).

The behavioral function of this system is shown by stress-induced analgesia. Rats exposed to a predator, to cues of a previous shock or to a fight show reduced responses to painful stimuli, partly through opioid release and partly through other mechanisms, including endocannabinoids. Bolles and Fanselow (1980) explained it by the priority of defense: an animal under attack should fight or flee and not lick its wounds, so pain is suppressed while fear is high; once the danger has passed, pain returns and promotes recuperative behavior (resting, guarding the injury) that aids healing. Opioid peptides are also involved in social behavior: grooming raises β-endorphin levels in the cerebrospinal fluid of monkeys (Keverne et al., 1989), and opioid signaling modulates the distress calls of young animals separated from their mothers, which suggests a role in maintaining social bonds.

### 7.4.3. Endocannabinoids

**Endocannabinoids** ([7.16.11](/7.16.11#sec-11.6)) are lipid messengers made on demand by postsynaptic neurons; they act backward on presynaptic $\mathrm{CB_1}$ receptors and reduce transmitter release. $\mathrm{CB_1}$ is among the most abundant G protein-coupled receptors in the brain, concentrated in the cortex, hippocampus, basal ganglia, cerebellum and pain pathways, while $\mathrm{CB_2}$ receptors are found mainly on immune cells. The same receptors are the targets of the plant cannabinoids of *Cannabis*. Behavioral functions of the endocannabinoid system include the stimulation of feeding (partly by increasing the sensitivity of olfactory circuits in fasted animals), stress-induced analgesia, the extinction of conditioned fear and the regulation of the stress response.

## 7.5. Neuromodulation and the Flexibility of Behavior

### 7.5.1. Motivation as Modulation of an Input–Output Relation

In an ideal reflex, a construct that probably does not exist in real animals, the stimulus alone determines the response. In real animals the relation between stimulus and response changes: the same female stimulus elicits courtship from a male fish in the breeding season and not outside it. The internal factors that change the relation are motivational, and the structures from which they originate can be regarded as motivational components of the circuit. Motivational change is possible only if the circuit is plastic, and two broad mechanisms have been found.

The first is *structural reorganization*: new neurons and glial cells, changes in dendrites, outgrowth or retraction of axons. In the weakly electric knifefish *Eigenmannia*, males and females produce chirps, brief modulations of the electric organ discharge used in courtship, almost only in the breeding season, which coincides with the tropical rainy season and can be induced in the laboratory by simulating rain (lowering the conductivity of the water and raising its level). Chirps are controlled by neurons of a nucleus in the dorsal thalamus (the central posterior/prepacemaker nucleus) that project to the pacemaker of the discharge ([7.18.15](/7.18.15)). Zupanc and Heiligenberg (1989) labeled these neurons by tracers injected into the pacemaker and reconstructed them: in sexually mature fish their dendrites were about $40\,\%$ longer in total than in immature fish, the difference lying almost entirely in a dorsomedial field of dendrites that receives excitatory input. In mammals, the motor neurons of the spinal nucleus of the bulbocavernosus, which control the penile muscles, shrink and lose dendrites in white-footed mice (*Peromyscus leucopus*) kept on short days (Forger and Breedlove, 1987), and in castrated rats their dendrites shrink and are restored by testosterone (Kurz, Sengelaub and Arnold, 1986). In the hypothalamus, astrocytes that normally separate the cell bodies of oxytocin and vasopressin neurons retract during lactation and dehydration, so that the neurons come into direct contact and fire more synchronously.

The second mechanism is *biochemical switching*: modulators change the properties of the existing neurons and synapses without changing their structure. The two mechanisms suit different kinds of motivational change:

| | Structural reorganization | Biochemical switching |
|---|---|---|
| Speed of change | Slow (days to weeks) | Fast (seconds to hours) |
| Size of change | Often dramatic, all-or-none (breeding versus non-breeding) | Graded, fine adjustments over a wide range |
| Duration | Long (a season) | Short, readily reversed |
| Typical behaviors | Seasonal reproduction, migration | Feeding, aggression, arousal |

Neuropeptides and catecholamines are well suited to biochemical switching because they are often released away from synaptic specializations and act on receptors at a distance from the release site. This "mismatch" between the sites of release and of the receptors means that one modulator can act on several circuits at once, in agreement with the observation that motivational factors that affect one behavior tend to change the probability of others as well.

**Definition 7.5.1 (Polymorphic Network)** A **polymorphic network** is an anatomically defined neural circuit that can operate in several functional configurations, producing different motor patterns or behaviors, according to the modulatory inputs acting on it (Getting and Dekin, 1985).

### 7.5.2. The Stomatogastric Ganglion

The stomatogastric ganglion of decapod crustaceans, about 30 identified neurons that control the chewing of the gastric mill and the filtering movements of the pylorus, is the best-studied polymorphic network; its modulation and a model of it are treated in [7.16.11](/7.16.11#sec-11.5). The ganglion receives about 250 axons through the stomatogastric nerve, mostly from modulatory projection neurons in other ganglia, and these and circulating hormones supply more than twenty modulators. When this input is blocked (by a sucrose solution applied to the nerve), the pyloric rhythm slows or stops and only some neurons keep firing; applying single modulators to the isolated ganglion produces different rhythms from the same neurons: proctolin, crustacean cardioactive peptide, red pigment-concentrating hormone, a tachykinin-related peptide and serotonin all activate the lateral pyloric neuron and speed up the pyloric dilator bursts, each with its own pattern, whereas an FMRFamide-like peptide has only modest effects (Marder and colleagues). In the intact animal, the modulatory projection neurons are recruited by sensory input from the mouth and the stomach, so that the motor patterns of the foregut follow the state of feeding.

### 7.5.3. Serotonin, Aggression and Social Status in Crustaceans

Crayfish and lobsters fight readily over shelters and food, and their fights are easy to quantify: they escalate in a stereotyped sequence from threat displays to pushing and grasping with the claws until one animal retreats, and the loser thereafter avoids the winner. Size usually decides: an animal facing a much larger opponent retreats quickly. Three experiments show that serotonin is part of the mechanism and that its effect depends on social experience.

1. Injected into the hemolymph of lobsters, serotonin produces the flexed, raised posture of dominant animals and octopamine the extended, lowered posture of subordinates (Livingstone, Harris-Warrick and Kravitz, 1980).
2. Serotonin infused into freely moving subordinate crayfish reduced their tendency to retreat from larger opponents, and fights with them lasted longer: the modulator changed the decision to withdraw, that is, the aggressive motivation (Huber et al., 1997).
3. The effect of serotonin on the lateral giant interneuron, which commands the tail-flip escape ([7.18.14](/7.18.14)), depends on social status. In dominant crayfish (and in social isolates) serotonin facilitates the neuron's response to sensory input, in subordinates it inhibits it, and the change develops over days after a dominance relationship is formed and reverses when the relationship changes, apparently through changes in the populations of serotonin receptors (Yeh, Fricke and Edwards, 1996).

The third result shows the modulation of the modulators: social experience changes how a circuit responds to serotonin, not only how much serotonin it receives. Such metamodulation lets an animal's history shape its present state.

### 7.5.4. Behavioral States in Worms and Flies

The nematode *C. elegans* on a lawn of bacteria alternates between two states that last minutes: *roaming*, in which it moves fast and rarely turns, covering ground, and *dwelling*, in which it moves slowly and turns often, staying where it is and feeding. The states correspond to the two choices of a forager ([7.18.22](/7.18.22)): exploit the present patch or search for a better one. Flavell et al. (2013) found that two messengers act in opposite directions. Serotonin, released by the NSM neurons in the pharynx when food is ingested, promotes dwelling through a serotonin-gated chloride channel (MOD-1); the neuropeptide PDF promotes roaming. Mutants without serotonin roam more, and mutants without PDF dwell more. The two messengers act on the lengths of the states rather than producing them directly.

**Example 7.5.1 (A Two-State Model of Roaming and Dwelling)** Model the state of the worm as a Markov chain with two states, roaming (R) and dwelling (D), and switching rates $k_{\mathrm{RD}}$ (from roaming to dwelling) and $k_{\mathrm{DR}}$ (from dwelling to roaming). Bout durations are exponentially distributed with means $1/k_{\mathrm{RD}}$ and $1/k_{\mathrm{DR}}$, and the fraction of time spent roaming is
$$
f_{\mathrm{R}} = \frac{k_{\mathrm{DR}}}{k_{\mathrm{DR}} + k_{\mathrm{RD}}}.
$$
Suppose that serotonin lengthens dwelling (reduces $k_{\mathrm{DR}}$) and PDF lengthens roaming (reduces $k_{\mathrm{RD}}$). With mean bouts of $1\,\mathrm{min}$ of roaming and $4\,\mathrm{min}$ of dwelling, a wild-type worm roams $f_{\mathrm{R}} = 0.25/(0.25 + 1) = 20\,\%$ of the time. Without serotonin, let dwelling bouts shorten to $1.5\,\mathrm{min}$: $f_{\mathrm{R}} = 0.67/(0.67 + 1) = 40\,\%$. Without PDF, let roaming bouts shorten to $0.4\,\mathrm{min}$: $f_{\mathrm{R}} = 0.25/(0.25 + 2.5) \approx 9\,\%$.

:::

The model is a caricature (real bout durations are not exactly exponential, and the rates depend on food density and hunger), but it shows how a modulator can change the time budget of an animal, the quantity that matters for foraging success, by changing only the stability of a state. The same logic, two opposing modulators that set the persistence of alternative states, recurs in other animals. In fruit flies, starvation increases the expression of a neuropeptide receptor (for short neuropeptide F) in a subset of olfactory receptor neurons, which increases the sensitivity of the fly to food odors and its attraction to them (Root et al., 2011): hunger acts at the first synapse of the sensory pathway.

### 7.5.5. Octopamine and Insect Behavioral States

**Definition 7.5.2 (Octopamine)** **Octopamine** is a biogenic amine synthesized from tyrosine through tyramine by tyramine β-hydroxylase; first found in the salivary glands of the octopus (Erspamer, 1948), it is a major neurotransmitter, neuromodulator and neurohormone of insects and other invertebrates, acting on G protein-coupled receptors related to the adrenergic receptors of vertebrates.

Octopamine plays in insects many of the roles that norepinephrine and epinephrine play in vertebrates: it prepares the animal for energetically demanding action. In locusts, octopamine released during flight mobilizes lipid fuel and increases the glycolytic activity of flight muscle, and identified dorsal unpaired median (DUM) neurons release it onto skeletal muscles. The first of these to be studied, DUMETi, innervates the extensor muscle of the hind-leg tibia, the jumping muscle, and modulates the force and relaxation rate of its contractions without causing contraction itself (Evans and O'Shea, 1977). Octopamine also restores the response of habituated visual neurons in the locust and increases the responsiveness of sensory neurons, preparing the animal to respond.

Its role in behavioral states is clearest in social contexts:

- *Aggression in crickets.* A male cricket that loses a fight avoids other males for hours. Flying, which releases octopamine, restores its willingness to fight, and octopamine agonists have the same effect, whereas depleting octopamine suppresses aggression (Stevenson et al., 2005). The experience of winning, and of physical exertion, thus feeds back on motivation through the same modulator.
- *Aggression and flight in fruit flies.* Males without octopamine fight much less (Hoyer et al., 2008) and have difficulty initiating and maintaining flight (Brembs et al., 2007).
- *Division of labor in honey bees.* Octopamine levels in the antennal lobes rise as bees age into foragers, and octopamine treatment causes young bees to start foraging earlier and increases their responsiveness to sucrose, part of the regulation of the age-based division of labor in the colony ([7.18.36](/7.18.36)).

The comparison of crustaceans and insects shows that the effects of an amine are not fixed by its chemistry. In lobsters octopamine is associated with subordinate posture, in crickets with aggression. What is conserved is the use of a small number of widely projecting modulatory neurons to switch whole repertoires of behavior between states.

## Summary

- Chemical transmission was discovered by Loewi (1921); the receptors of acetylcholine are named after the natural products nicotine and muscarine. Plants defend themselves with alkaloids that target animal nervous systems, and herbivores evolve tolerance and sometimes reuse the toxins (the tobacco hornworm).
- Messengers belong to a few classes (amino acids, acetylcholine, monoamines, purines, neuropeptides, gases, lipids). The distinction that matters for behavior is between fast transmission and modulation by a **neuromodulator**, which sets states over seconds to hours.
- Catecholamines (Definition 7.1.1) and serotonin are made by short enzymatic pathways from tyrosine and tryptophan; insects make octopamine from tyrosine through tyramine.
- **Dopamine** signals reward and its prediction errors in natural behavior (song learning) and is coupled to homeostatic state; insects use dopamine for both reward and punishment and octopamine for reward. Addictive substances raise the paradox of drug reward; animals sometimes benefit from neuroactive plant compounds (fly larvae and ethanol, bees and caffeine).
- **Serotonin** biases choices between alternative strategies according to the animal's state. Rodent tests of anxiety-like and depression-like behavior exploit natural conflicts; their validity (face, construct, predictive) is limited, and their interpretation benefits from an ethological view.
- **Norepinephrine** and **epinephrine** mediate the fast sympathoadrenal response and arousal; the **hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis** follows more slowly. Opioid peptides (Definition 7.4.1) gate pain and mediate stress-induced analgesia, which gives defense priority over recuperation; **endocannabinoids** modulate feeding, stress and fear extinction.
- Motivational change uses structural reorganization (slow, dramatic: *Eigenmannia* chirping, spinal motor neurons, hypothalamic glia) or biochemical switching (fast, graded) of polymorphic networks (Definition 7.5.1), such as the stomatogastric ganglion.
- Serotonin shifts crayfish toward dominance, and social status changes the response of the escape circuit to serotonin. Serotonin and PDF set the persistence of dwelling and roaming in *C. elegans* (Example 7.5.1). Octopamine (Definition 7.5.2) prepares insects for flight and fighting and regulates division of labor in bees.

## Questions

1. Why are so many neurotoxins and neuroactive drugs plant or microbial products, and why do some animals deliberately consume them?

   **Answer** Plants and microbes cannot flee or fight their consumers, and the nervous system is a vulnerable target in animals: a few kinds of receptors and channels control all movement, so a molecule that binds one of them can deter or kill at low doses. Such molecules evolved as defenses. Animals consume them deliberately when the benefit outweighs the cost: herbivores that tolerate a toxin gain access to a food others avoid and may reuse it against predators (the hornworm's nicotine), fruit flies lay eggs on ethanol-containing food when parasitoid wasps are present because ethanol harms the parasitoids more than the fly larvae, and bees are attracted to and learn better with caffeinated nectar, a trait the plant exploits. Some responses to such compounds may also be by-products of their resemblance to the animal's own messengers.

2. What do the forced swim test and the elevated plus maze measure? Discuss their validity as models of human depression and anxiety.

   **Answer** The forced swim test measures how long a rodent in an inescapable water cylinder stays immobile; the elevated plus maze measures how much time and how many entries the animal makes into open, unprotected arms. Both have predictive validity: antidepressants reduce immobility, anxiolytics increase open-arm exploration. Face validity is limited (immobility is not sadness, avoiding open arms is a normal antipredator behavior), and construct validity is weak because the tests are acute and do not reproduce the causes of the human disorders. Ethologically, immobility may be an adaptive passive coping strategy that saves energy, and open-arm avoidance reflects the natural conflict between exploration and predation risk. The tests are useful for detecting drugs similar to known ones but poor at revealing new mechanisms; chronic stress paradigms with individual differences (susceptible versus resilient mice) come closer to construct validity.

3. Compare structural reorganization and biochemical switching as mechanisms of motivational change. Which would you expect to underlie seasonal song in a temperate songbird, and which the change in a crab's chewing rhythm after a meal?

   **Answer** Structural reorganization changes the anatomy of circuits (dendrites, synapses, neuron number) and is slow, long-lasting and often all-or-none; biochemical switching changes the properties of existing neurons and synapses through modulators and is fast, graded and reversible. Seasonal song in temperate songbirds is a slow, large and season-long change and would be expected to involve structural reorganization; indeed, the song nuclei of canaries and other seasonal species grow in volume and neuron number in spring under the influence of testosterone ([7.18.17](/7.18.17), [7.18.19](/7.18.19)). The change in the crab's foregut rhythm after a meal is fast and graded and is produced by modulatory projection neurons and peptides acting on the stomatogastric ganglion: biochemical switching.

4. In the two-state model of Example 7.5.1, a mutant has the same fraction of time roaming as the wild type but both bout lengths are doubled. Could the mutant be distinguished from the wild type by measuring the fraction of time roaming? What else would you measure, and why might it matter for foraging?

   **Answer** No: $f_{\mathrm{R}} = k_{\mathrm{DR}}/(k_{\mathrm{DR}} + k_{\mathrm{RD}})$ is unchanged when both rates are halved. The bout-length distributions (means of $2$ and $8\,\mathrm{min}$ instead of $1$ and $4$) or the switching rate would reveal the difference. For foraging it matters because the spatial pattern of search changes: longer roaming bouts carry the worm farther between patches of exploitation, and longer dwelling bouts make it slower to leave a depleting patch, so the two genotypes would sample patchy food differently even with the same time budget.

5. Serotonin facilitates the escape circuit of dominant crayfish and inhibits it in subordinates. Suggest a functional explanation and an experiment that would test it.

   **Answer** A subordinate that has learned that it loses fights may benefit from avoiding the abrupt, conspicuous tail flip, which is costly and may provoke attack, in favor of slower retreat; or it may benefit from suppressing escape so as to remain in the vicinity of resources without triggering further encounters. A dominant animal can afford quick escapes from real threats. An experiment: pair crayfish to form dominance relationships, then expose dominants and subordinates to standardized threatening stimuli (abrupt touches, simulated predator attacks) and to approaching conspecifics, and measure the probability and type of escape (giant-mediated tail flip versus non-giant swimming or walking) and the outcome (injury, retained shelter). Blocking serotonin receptors would test whether the status-dependent difference in escape behavior depends on the serotonergic modulation.
