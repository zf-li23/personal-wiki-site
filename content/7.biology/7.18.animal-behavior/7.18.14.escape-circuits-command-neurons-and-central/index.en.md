The previous chapters followed sensory information inward, from receptors that filter the stimulus to feature detectors that recognize prey, predators and approaching objects ([7.18.12](/7.18.12), [7.18.13](/7.18.13)). This chapter follows it outward, to the circuits that turn a decision into a coordinated movement. Two kinds of motor output have taught neuroethologists most. Escape responses are the fastest behaviors animals perform, and the selection pressure for speed has produced circuits so simple, with giant neurons and electrical synapses, that the whole path from stimulus to muscle can be traced. Rhythmic behaviors such as swimming, walking, flying, breathing and singing are produced by networks that generate patterned output without patterned input: central pattern generators. Between the two lies a question that has accompanied neuroethology since the 1960s: is a behavior "commanded" by particular neurons, as the ethologists' releasing mechanisms implied, or does it emerge from the dynamics of distributed circuits?

The cellular building blocks (action potentials, chemical and electrical synapses, conduction velocity) are treated in [7.14.4](/7.14.4), [7.14.5](/7.14.5) and [7.18.6](/7.18.6); the circuit mechanisms of rhythm generation in [7.14.6](/7.14.6) and the spinal circuits of mammals in [7.16.30](/7.16.30). Here we concentrate on what these circuits do for behavior and how the classic experiments were designed.

## 14.1. The Design of Escape

### 14.1.1. Three Requirements

A prey animal attacked by a predator has a fraction of a second to respond. Its escape must satisfy three requirements, which are partly in conflict:

1. *Speed.* The response must begin before the predator strikes. Latencies are often below $10\,\mathrm{ms}$ from stimulus to movement, close to the physical minimum set by conduction, synaptic delay and muscle activation.
2. *Direction.* The movement must carry the animal away from the threat, or at least not toward it, so the circuit must know where the stimulus came from.
3. *Unpredictability.* A predator that could predict the escape trajectory could anticipate it. Erratic or variable escape paths, which Humphries and Driver (1970) called *protean behavior*, make anticipation impossible.

Speed favors circuits with few synapses, large axons and electrical transmission; direction requires a comparison between the two sides of the body; unpredictability requires variability. The circuits in the following sections show how these requirements have been met, and at what cost. The economic side of the decision, when to flee and how far, belongs to behavioral ecology and is discussed in [7.18.24](/7.18.24).

**Definition 14.1.1 (Escape Response)** An **escape response** is a rapid movement that carries an animal away from a sudden threatening stimulus, such as an attacking predator, a looming object or an abrupt mechanical or acoustic disturbance, typically initiated with minimal latency by a dedicated neural pathway.

### 14.1.2. Giant Fibers

Many escape pathways contain neurons with exceptionally thick axons. Conduction velocity in an unmyelinated axon grows with the square root of its diameter ([7.18.6](/7.18.6#sec-6.3)), so an animal that must send a signal quickly along its body without myelin can do so by making the axon wide.

**Definition 14.1.2 (Giant Fiber)** A **giant fiber** is an exceptionally large-diameter axon of an interneuron or motor neuron, much thicker than those of its neighbors, that conducts signals rapidly over the length of the body and triggers an escape response; examples are the giant axons of squid, the medial and lateral giant fibers of earthworms and crayfish, and the giant fiber of flies.

Giant fibers usually have three further properties: they make electrical synapses, which eliminate the synaptic delay of chemical transmission; they activate many motor neurons at once, so that all the muscles needed for the response contract together; and they fire in an all-or-none manner, often a single spike, so that the response is a stereotyped, maximal movement. Giant fibers have evolved independently in annelids, molluscs, arthropods and vertebrates, an example of **convergent evolution** driven by the same physical constraint ([7.14.4](/7.14.4)).

## 14.2. The Mauthner Cell and the C-Start

### 14.2.1. Anatomy and Behavior

When a goldfish hears a sudden sound or feels a jet of water, it bends its body into a C within about $10$–$15\,\mathrm{ms}$ and then shoots away with a stroke of the tail. This *C-start* consists of two stages: a rapid, unilateral contraction of the trunk muscles that turns the head away from the stimulus (stage 1), followed by a contralateral propulsive stroke (stage 2). The C-start is triggered by a single pair of identified neurons.

**Definition 14.2.1 (Mauthner Cell)** The **Mauthner cells** are a pair of giant reticulospinal neurons in rhombomere 4 of the hindbrain of most fishes and of amphibian larvae, which receive auditory, vestibular, lateral line and visual input and whose single action potential, conducted by a large crossed axon down the spinal cord, excites the motor neurons of the contralateral trunk muscles and initiates the C-start escape.

Each Mauthner cell has two large dendrites: the lateral dendrite receives the eighth-nerve afferents from the saccule and the lateral line, and the ventral dendrite receives visual input from the optic tectum. The auditory afferents terminate as large "club endings" that form mixed synapses, with both a gap-junction (electrical) and a glutamatergic (chemical) component. The axon crosses the midline immediately and descends the length of the spinal cord, where it excites the contralateral primary motor neurons and interneurons. Because the Mauthner axon conducts at up to about $80\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ in large fish and the path from ear to muscle contains only two or three synapses, the first muscle potentials appear on the side opposite the cell that fired within $5$–$10\,\mathrm{ms}$ of the stimulus. The bend is directed away from the stimulated side because the eighth-nerve input is stronger on the side of the sound source.

### 14.2.2. Making the Response Unilateral

A C-start is useful only if one Mauthner cell fires and the other does not: simultaneous firing of both would contract both sides and stiffen the fish. Several inhibitory mechanisms ensure this:

- *Feedback inhibition.* The collaterals of a firing Mauthner axon excite inhibitory interneurons (cranial relay neurons) that inhibit both Mauthner cells. The inhibition arrives within a millisecond, prevents the cell from firing again and prevents the contralateral cell from firing at all.
- *Feed-forward inhibition.* Eighth-nerve afferents excite inhibitory interneurons that inhibit the Mauthner cell with a short delay, so that only a strong, abrupt stimulus can reach threshold before the inhibition arrives; weak or slowly rising stimuli are filtered out.
- *Electrical inhibition.* Some of these interneurons (the passive hyperpolarizing potential neurons) form a dense axon cap around the initial segment of the Mauthner axon, where their synchronous firing produces an extracellular positive field that hyperpolarizes the spike-initiation zone directly, without any transmitter (Furukawa and Furshpan 1963). It is one of the few known examples of inhibition by an electric field effect.
- *Spinal inhibition.* In the spinal cord, the firing Mauthner axon excites commissural glycinergic interneurons that inhibit the contralateral motor neurons, so that the bend on one side is not opposed by the other.

### 14.2.3. Necessary but Not Sufficient

Is the Mauthner cell necessary for escape? Laser ablation of the Mauthner cells in larval zebrafish answered the question (Liu and Fetcho 1999). After ablation, larvae still escaped from abrupt stimuli, but with longer latencies; only when two pairs of segmentally homologous neurons in neighboring rhombomeres (MiD2cm and MiD3cm, small "Mauthner homologs") were ablated as well did escapes to head stimuli disappear. Behavioral analysis confirmed that larvae have two kinds of C-starts: short-latency C-starts ($5$–$10\,\mathrm{ms}$), which require the Mauthner cell, and long-latency C-starts (about $20$–$30\,\mathrm{ms}$), which are slower, more variable in angle and controlled by other reticulospinal neurons (Burgess and Granato 2007). The Mauthner cell is thus sufficient for the fastest escape but not necessary for escape in general: it is the fastest element of a network of escape neurons.

The Mauthner cell is also regulated. Its responses habituate to repeated stimuli, and a weak sound shortly before a strong one reduces the probability of a C-start (prepulse inhibition, a phenomenon also studied in humans as a measure of sensorimotor gating). The probability of a C-start depends on the social context: in cichlid fishes, dominant males escape less readily than subordinates, and serotonin and dopamine modulate the excitability of the Mauthner cell. Visual looming stimuli reach it through the tectum ([7.18.13](/7.18.13#sec-13.3.3)), so that a larva escapes from a dark object approaching on a collision course. Even the fastest escape is thus a decision, weighed against context.

## 14.3. Invertebrate Escape Circuits

### 14.3.1. The Crayfish Tail Flip

A crayfish escapes by flexing its abdomen rapidly under its body, which throws it backward through the water: the tail flip. Two pairs of giant interneurons in the ventral nerve cord trigger tail flips in different directions ([7.17.21](/7.17.21)):

| | Lateral giants (LG) | Medial giants (MG) |
|---|---|---|
| Stimulus | Abrupt touch or water jet on the abdomen | Abrupt touch on the head, sudden visual stimuli in front |
| Structure | A chain of segmental neurons coupled by electrical synapses | One neuron on each side with its cell body in the brain |
| Movement | Flexion of the anterior abdomen only; the crayfish pitches upward and forward, away from the rear | Flexion of the whole abdomen; the crayfish shoots straight backward, away from the front |

In both, a single spike in the giant interneuron triggers the response, and the latency from stimulus to flexion is about $10\,\mathrm{ms}$. In 1959 Furshpan and Potter discovered at the synapse between the LG axon and the giant motor neuron the first electrical synapse to be identified: a rectifying junction that passes current from the interneuron to the motor neuron but not back. The LG circuit was mapped by Krasne, Wine and colleagues: mechanosensory afferents from the tail fan excite the LG both directly through electrical synapses and indirectly through sensory interneurons; the LG then excites the motor giants and other flexor motor neurons of each segment and, through an interneuron, inhibits the extensor muscles and the LG itself (recurrent inhibition), so that the response is a single, uninterrupted flexion.

Three properties of the LG circuit link it to behavior. First, the response habituates rapidly with repeated stimulation; the site of habituation is the chemical synapse between sensory afferents and interneurons, which depresses, while the electrical pathway does not. Second, the circuit receives descending inhibition from the brain when the crayfish is restrained, feeding or otherwise engaged, so that tail flips are suppressed when they would be useless. Third, social status modulates it: in dominant crayfish, serotonin increases the excitability of the LG, whereas in subordinates the same transmitter decreases it, because the two kinds of animals express different serotonin receptors (Yeh, Fricke and Edwards 1996; [7.18.7](/7.18.7)). Crayfish also have non-giant escapes, slower and steerable, which they use when an attack is less sudden.

### 14.3.2. The Fly Giant Fiber System

*Drosophila* escapes from a looming object by jumping into the air and flying away ([7.18.13](/7.18.13#sec-13.3.3)). The fastest takeoff is driven by the giant fiber system. Each of the two giant fibers (GF) has its cell body and dendrites in the brain, where it receives visual input from looming-sensitive projection neurons (LC4, LPLC2) and mechanosensory input from the antennae, and its axon descends to the thoracic ganglion. There it makes two outputs:

- a mixed electrical and cholinergic synapse onto the motor neuron of the tergotrochanteral muscle (TTM), the jump muscle of the middle leg; and
- an electrical synapse onto the peripherally synapsing interneuron (PSI), which excites the motor neurons of the dorsal longitudinal muscles (DLM), the indirect flight muscles that depress the wings.

The electrical synapses are formed by the innexin Shaking-B; flies mutant for *shakB* have defective giant fiber transmission. A single GF spike evokes TTM contraction within about $1\,\mathrm{ms}$ and DLM contraction a few tenths of a millisecond later. The electrical synapse between motor neurons of the same pool also synchronizes their firing, a general principle: electrically coupled motor neurons fire together and produce synchronous movements, as in the inking of *Aplysia* or the jump of a flea.

Flies take off in two ways (von Reyn and colleagues 2014). In a *long-mode* takeoff the fly first raises its wings and then extends its legs, launching itself into stable flight. In a *short-mode* takeoff, the legs extend before the wings have been raised; the jump is faster by several milliseconds, but the fly often tumbles. Short-mode takeoffs are driven by a GF spike; long-mode takeoffs occur without GF activity, through parallel descending neurons. Recordings from the GF of tethered flies watching looming disks showed that the GF fires one or a few spikes, and that the mode of takeoff depends on the timing of that spike: if the GF fires before the long-mode sequence has been completed, it triggers a short-mode takeoff, overriding the slower program; if it fires later or not at all, the fly completes its long-mode takeoff. Rapidly looming stimuli, which drive the GF early, produce mostly short-mode takeoffs; slowly looming ones mostly long-mode takeoffs. Action selection is thus implemented by a race between two pathways, decided by the timing of a single spike, and it trades stability for speed when the threat is most urgent.

### 14.3.3. The Cockroach and the Wind

A cockroach (*Periplaneta americana*) escapes from a toad's tongue or a human foot by sensing the puff of air that precedes the strike. The cerci, two appendages at the rear of the abdomen, carry about 200 filiform hairs each; each hair is hinged so that it can move in only one plane, and its sensory neuron fires for air displacement in one direction. The hair afferents converge in the terminal abdominal ganglion onto seven pairs of giant interneurons, whose large axons ascend to the thoracic ganglia. Camhi and colleagues showed that the cockroach turns away from the wind within a few tens of milliseconds, with the direction of the turn determined by the direction of the wind.

No single giant interneuron encodes the wind direction. Each ventral giant interneuron responds to a broad range of directions with roughly cosine-shaped tuning, and the direction is represented by the relative activity of the population: the wind direction can be recovered as the vector sum of the preferred directions weighted by the firing rates, a population vector of the kind later found for arm movements in the motor cortex of monkeys. Killing individual giant interneurons shifts the escape direction in the way the population vector predicts. The escape itself is unpredictable in detail: cockroaches run away along one of several preferred angles relative to the threat (Domenici and colleagues 2008), choosing among them apparently at random, an example of protean variability built on top of a directed response.

B: wind direction estimated as the population vector of the four responses, each with 20 % multiplicative noise, against the true direction (20 trials per direction; line of identity in gray). ## 14.4. Command Neurons

### 14.4.1. The Concept

In 1964 Wiersma and Ikeda found that stimulating single fibers in the connectives of the crayfish nerve cord evoked complete, coordinated behaviors, such as the rhythmic beating of the swimmerets or a particular posture of the abdomen. They called these fibers *command fibers*: neurons whose activity was sufficient to evoke a whole behavior, the details of which were then generated by circuits below them. The idea was attractive because it seemed to connect the ethologists' releasing mechanisms to identifiable cells, and the term spread rapidly. It also spread loosely: any neuron whose stimulation evoked a behavior was soon called a command neuron.

Kupfermann and Weiss (1978) proposed criteria that made the concept testable.

**Definition 14.4.1 (Command Neuron)** A **command neuron** is a neuron whose activity is both necessary and sufficient for the occurrence of a particular behavior: the neuron fires before and during the behavior in response to its natural stimuli; stimulating the neuron to fire in its natural pattern elicits the behavior (sufficiency); and removing or silencing it abolishes the response to those stimuli (necessity).

Few neurons satisfy all criteria. The crayfish lateral giant does: it fires whenever a tail flip is evoked by abdominal stimuli, a single spike in it evokes the tail flip, and hyperpolarizing it prevents the tail flip. The cricket AN2 interneuron is necessary and sufficient for steering away from ultrasound in flight ([7.18.12](/7.18.12#sec-12.5.3)). In the brain of the field cricket a single pair of descending interneurons is necessary and sufficient for the calling song: they fire throughout singing, depolarizing them evokes singing, and hyperpolarizing them stops it (Hedwig 2000; [7.18.16](/7.18.16)). In adult *Drosophila*, a pair of descending "moonwalker" neurons, when activated optogenetically, makes flies walk backward, and silencing them reduces backward walking when the fly meets an obstacle (Bidaye and colleagues 2014). The Mauthner cell, by contrast, is sufficient for the short-latency C-start but not necessary for escape (Section 14.2.3).

### 14.4.2. From Command Neurons to Command Systems

Most behaviors are not commanded by a single neuron. More often, a group of neurons, none of which is necessary on its own, jointly initiates the behavior, and each neuron contributes to several behaviors. Several observations shaped this view:

- *Roeder's mantis.* Roeder found that a praying mantis whose segmental ganglia are isolated from the brain can still perform vigorous, complete movements when the ganglia are stimulated, and that cutting the connection between the protocerebrum and the rest of the nervous system produces a mantis that walks and grasps simultaneously. A decapitated male mantis performs continuous copulatory movements, which normally are inhibited by the brain; this is why a male whose head has been eaten by the female can still copulate. The brain thus selects among behaviors largely by releasing segmental circuits from tonic inhibition, a principle of disinhibition also found in the basal ganglia of vertebrates.
- *The leech.* When a medicinal leech is touched, it either swims or crawls, with nearly equal probability in an isolated nerve cord. Briggman, Abarbanel and Kristan (2005) imaged the activity of most neurons of a ganglion simultaneously with voltage-sensitive dyes and found a population of neurons whose joint activity diverged toward one or the other behavior a few hundred milliseconds before the choice was expressed, earlier than any single neuron could predict it. One neuron of this group, cell 208, biased the choice: depolarizing it during stimulation favored crawling, hyperpolarizing it favored swimming. The decision was a dynamic process in a network with two attractor states, not a switch in a command neuron.
- *Toads* ([7.18.13](/7.18.13#sec-13.2.4)). Ewert's command releasing systems are combinations of tectal and pretectal neurons whose joint activity releases one element of prey capture.

The modern synthesis distinguishes between "command-like" neurons, which may be identified by optogenetic or electrical stimulation, and the decision itself, which usually depends on many neurons and on the internal state. A neuron that is sufficient for a behavior in an experiment may be one of several parallel routes to it in natural conditions. The command neuron concept remains useful as a hypothesis about how the nervous system is organized hierarchically, and the Kupfermann–Weiss criteria remain the standard for testing it.

## 14.5. Central Pattern Generators

### 14.5.1. Rhythm Without Rhythmic Input

Swimming, walking, flying, chewing, breathing and singing are rhythmic movements in which muscles contract in a fixed sequence and phase relation, cycle after cycle. Two hypotheses about their origin competed in the early twentieth century. According to the chain-reflex hypothesis of Sherrington, each phase of the movement produces sensory feedback that triggers the next phase. According to Thomas Graham Brown (1911), the rhythm is generated within the spinal cord itself: in decerebrate cats whose dorsal roots had been cut, so that no sensory feedback could reach the cord, he still observed alternating rhythmic contractions of the flexor and extensor muscles of the hindlimbs. He proposed that the rhythm was produced by two mutually inhibiting groups of neurons, "half-centers", one driving flexors and the other extensors, each of which fatigues after a period of activity and thereby releases the other.

The decisive experiments came fifty years later. Wilson (1961) removed all the sensory feedback from the wings of locusts and found that the isolated thoracic ganglia, stimulated nonspecifically, still produced the flight motor pattern, with the correct phase relations between elevator and depressor muscles, though at a lower frequency than in intact animals. Similar *fictive* rhythms, recorded from motor nerves in preparations in which the muscles are paralyzed or removed, have since been obtained for swimming in lampreys, leeches and tadpoles, walking in cats and stick insects, breathing in mammals and singing in crickets. The rhythm is generated centrally, by networks now called **central pattern generators** ([7.14.6](/7.14.6#sec-6.4.2)); sensory feedback modulates it rather than creating it. Brown's mechanism of mutual inhibition between two groups of neurons is now known as the **half-center oscillator** ([7.14.6](/7.14.6#sec-6.4.3)).

### 14.5.2. The Swimming Circuit of *Xenopus* Tadpoles

The simplest vertebrate CPG analyzed in cellular detail is that of the hatchling tadpole of the clawed frog *Xenopus laevis*, studied by Alan Roberts and his colleagues in Bristol since the 1970s. A two-day-old tadpole, about $5\,\mathrm{mm}$ long, spends most of its time hanging from a strand of mucus secreted by the cement gland on its head. When touched or when the light is dimmed, it swims away with lateral undulations of the body at $10$–$25\,\mathrm{Hz}$: waves of bending travel from head to tail at about $15\,\mathrm{cm\,s^{-1}}$ and drive the tadpole forward at about $5\,\mathrm{cm\,s^{-1}}$. Swimming stops when the tadpole bumps its head into an object and attaches. Swimming is the tadpole's escape behavior.

The preparation has unusual advantages. The spinal cord contains only about $1\,000$ neurons on each side, of about eight anatomical types; the whole tadpole can be paralyzed with α-bungarotoxin, which blocks the neuromuscular junction, and then fictive swimming can be evoked by a brief touch to the skin and recorded from the ventral roots, with intracellular or whole-cell recordings from identified neurons. Three types of neurons form the core of the CPG in each half of the cord:

| Neuron type | Transmitter and projection | Role in the rhythm |
|---|---|---|
| Descending interneurons (dINs) | Glutamate, ipsilateral, descending; also electrically coupled | Excite all CPG neurons on their side; their NMDA-mediated excitation and electrical coupling sustain a background depolarization |
| Commissural interneurons (cINs) | Glycine, crossing to the other side | Inhibit all CPG neurons of the opposite side |
| Motor neurons | Acetylcholine to muscle, plus central glutamate and acetylcholine | Drive the trunk muscles; electrically coupled, so they fire synchronously |

During fictive swimming, each neuron of a half-center fires a single spike per cycle, and the two sides alternate. The rhythm arises from the interaction of excitation and inhibition. When the neurons on one side fire, their commissural interneurons inhibit the other side for $25$–$40\,\mathrm{ms}$. The inhibited neurons are held depolarized by the sustained NMDA-mediated excitation, and at the end of the inhibitory potential they fire a rebound spike (post-inhibitory rebound), which in turn inhibits the first side. The cycle period, $40$–$100\,\mathrm{ms}$, is therefore approximately the sum of the durations of the inhibitory potentials on the two sides, a quantitative prediction confirmed by experiments. The descending interneurons extend into the hindbrain, where they act as the rhythm's pacemakers: they fire in response to their own excitation and their coupling, and they are the first neurons to fire in each cycle.

How does the wave travel from head to tail? Each segment contains a half-center oscillator, and the oscillators are coupled. The number of excitatory interneurons per segment declines from head to tail, and with it the excitatory drive to the motor neurons, so that the rostral segments reach threshold earlier in each cycle and lead; reducing the gradient pharmacologically (applying an NMDA agonist to the caudal cord) reduces the rostrocaudal delay, and increasing it (applying an NMDA antagonist caudally) increases the delay. The phase lag between successive segments stays a roughly constant fraction of the cycle, so the body always contains about one wavelength, independent of swimming speed.

The tadpole circuit also shows how behavior is started and stopped. A touch to the skin excites Rohon–Beard sensory neurons, which excite sensory pathway interneurons that recruit the descending interneurons in the hindbrain; once started, the activity is self-sustaining. Pressure on the head or cement gland, as when the tadpole bumps into an object, excites trigeminal afferents that activate a group of GABAergic neurons in the hindbrain, which inhibit the descending interneurons and stop swimming. Dimming of the light, through the pineal eye, starts swimming. The circuit for initiating, sustaining and stopping a whole behavior can thus be described neuron by neuron in a vertebrate, which is why Roberts chose the tadpole: following Coghill (1929), he looked for the simplest vertebrate in which a natural behavior could be understood at the cellular level, a clear case of Krogh's principle ([7.18.6](/7.18.6)).

### 14.5.3. Other Pattern Generators

The same building blocks recur in pattern generators throughout the animal kingdom ([7.14.6](/7.14.6#sec-6.5)):

- The escape swimming of the sea slug *Tritonia diomedea*, triggered by contact with a predatory sea star, consists of two to twenty alternating dorsal and ventral flexions of the whole body. It is generated by three classes of interneurons (the dorsal swim interneurons, cerebral neuron 2 and the ventral swim interneurons) whose interplay of excitation, inhibition and delayed excitation produces the alternation, and it is started by the sustained firing of dorsal ramp interneurons (Getting; Frost and Katz).
- The singing of the plainfin midshipman fish, a hum that lasts minutes to an hour, is generated by a hindbrain CPG of three nuclei: a vocal prepacemaker nucleus whose sustained depolarization sets the duration of each call, a vocal pacemaker nucleus that sets the frequency of about $100\,\mathrm{Hz}$, and a vocal motor nucleus whose motor neurons contract the sonic muscles of the swim bladder once per cycle (Bass). The same CPG produces grunts and growls in different temporal patterns, and its output is modulated by steroid hormones ([7.18.19](/7.18.19)).
- The cricket's song is produced by a thoracic and abdominal CPG under the control of the brain's command neurons ([7.18.16](/7.18.16)).
- Walking in insects and mammals is produced by coupled oscillators for each leg or joint, whose coordination changes with speed ([7.16.30](/7.16.30#sec-30.5)).

### 14.5.4. Sensory Feedback and Motor Programs

Central generation does not mean that sensory feedback is unimportant. In intact locusts, stretch receptors at the wing hinge, which fire once per wingbeat, raise the frequency of the flight rhythm to its normal value and help to adjust the timing of depressor activity; removing them lowers the frequency, and stimulating them rhythmically entrains the isolated CPG (Wendler; Pearson). In walking, load receptors in the legs determine when a leg can be lifted, so that the animal does not lift a leg that is still carrying weight. Feedback thus fits the central rhythm to the mechanics of the body and the environment.

The pattern generated by a CPG is an example of a *motor program*: a set of commands, structured in time, that can run to completion without feedback once initiated. Classical ethology's fixed action pattern ([7.18.3](/7.18.3)) has its neural counterpart in such programs. The egg-rolling movement of the greylag goose continues even when the egg is removed, just as fictive swimming continues in a paralyzed tadpole. The difference between the two terms is one of level: the ethologist describes the behavior, the neuroethologist the circuit.

A CPG can also produce more than one behavior. The networks that control feeding in the sea slug *Aplysia* produce either ingestion or egestion depending on the activity of particular neurons and on modulatory inputs that change their properties, and the stomatogastric ganglion of crustaceans produces different rhythms under different neuromodulators ([7.18.7](/7.18.7)). Multifunctional networks, configured by modulation into different patterns, appear to be the rule rather than the exception.

## Summary

- Escape behavior must be fast, directed and unpredictable (protean); escape responses (Definition 14.1.1) are often driven by giant fibers (Definition 14.1.2) with electrical synapses and all-or-none output.

| System | Trigger | Key neurons | Notable mechanism |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fish C-start | Abrupt sound, water jet, looming | Mauthner cells (Definition 14.2.1) | Mixed synapses; feedback, feed-forward and field-effect inhibition ensure one cell fires; short- vs long-latency C-starts |
| Crayfish tail flip | Touch to abdomen (LG) or head (MG) | Lateral and medial giants | First electrical synapse discovered; habituation at chemical synapses; serotonin and social status |

- Command neurons (Definition 14.4.1, Kupfermann–Weiss): necessary and sufficient; met by the crayfish LG, cricket AN2 and calling-song neurons, fly moonwalker neurons; most decisions are made by distributed populations (leech swim versus crawl, cell 208; Roeder's mantis; Ewert's command releasing systems).
- **Central pattern generators** generate rhythm without rhythmic input (Graham Brown 1911; Wilson 1961); the **half-center oscillator** (mutual inhibition plus rebound or adaptation) is the core motif. *Xenopus* tadpole: dINs (glutamate, NMDA, electrical coupling), cINs (glycine), motor neurons; one spike per cycle; period ≈ two IPSP durations; rostrocaudal gradient sets the wave.
- Sensory feedback fits central rhythms to the body (locust wing stretch receptors); motor programs are the neural counterparts of fixed action patterns; CPGs are multifunctional and modulated.

## Questions

1. Why does the Mauthner system use several kinds of inhibition rather than one?

   **Answer** Each solves a different problem. Feedback inhibition ensures that once one Mauthner cell has fired, neither it nor its partner fires again for some milliseconds, which makes the response a single, unilateral bend. Feed-forward inhibition sets a temporal filter: only abrupt, strong stimuli depolarize the cell to threshold before the inhibition arrives, so slowly rising stimuli and weak noise do not trigger escapes. The field-effect inhibition of the axon cap acts at the spike-initiation zone within a fraction of a millisecond, faster than any chemical synapse, which is necessary when the two cells receive nearly simultaneous input. Spinal commissural inhibition ensures that the motor neurons on the side opposite the bend are silent, so the bend is not resisted.

2. Apply the Kupfermann–Weiss criteria to the Mauthner cell and to the crayfish lateral giant. Which is a command neuron?

   **Answer** The crayfish LG fires for every abdominal-stimulus tail flip, its single spike evokes the tail flip, and preventing it from firing (by hyperpolarization) abolishes the tail flip to those stimuli: it is necessary and sufficient and therefore a command neuron for this behavior. The Mauthner cell fires during short-latency C-starts and a spike in it is sufficient to produce a C-bend, but after its ablation larval zebrafish still escape (with longer latency, through other reticulospinal neurons), so it is not necessary for escape; it is a command neuron only for the short-latency form of the C-start.

3. In the tadpole swimming CPG, the cycle period is approximately twice the duration of the reciprocal inhibitory potentials. Explain why, and predict the effect of a drug that prolongs glycinergic inhibition.

   **Answer** Each half-center fires once and then inhibits the other side; the other side fires a rebound spike only when the inhibition ends. One cycle therefore consists of an inhibition of the right side by the left, followed by an inhibition of the left side by the right, and the period is approximately the sum of the two inhibitory durations (plus small synaptic and conduction delays). A drug that prolongs the glycinergic IPSPs (for example by slowing their decay) should lengthen the cycle and lower the swimming frequency; if the inhibition becomes long enough that the depolarizing drive cannot sustain rebound, swimming may stop.

4. Flies take off in short mode or long mode. What are the costs and benefits of each, and why is the choice implemented by spike timing rather than by a separate "decision neuron"?

   **Answer** The short-mode takeoff is faster by several milliseconds, which matters against a predator that is very close, but the fly often tumbles and loses control of its flight; the long-mode takeoff is slower but launches the fly into stable flight, which is better when time allows. Implementing the choice as a race between two pathways means that the faster pathway, the giant fiber, overrides the slower one only when the stimulus is so urgent that it drives the GF to threshold before the slower program is completed. The urgency of the stimulus thus determines the choice without any additional neuron or delay: a separate decision stage would add synaptic delay at exactly the moment when speed matters most.

5. (Open question) Design an experiment to test whether the leech's choice between swimming and crawling is made by a command neuron or by a distributed network.

   **Answer** Record simultaneously from as many neurons as possible during repeated trials of the same stimulus (by voltage-sensitive dye or calcium imaging), and determine when the choice can first be predicted from each neuron and from the population (using a classifier). A command neuron would predict the choice alone, earlier than the others, and its manipulation would determine the outcome; a distributed decision would be predictable earlier from the population than from any single neuron, and manipulating any one neuron would only bias the probability. Then manipulate candidate neurons (depolarize or hyperpolarize them, as with cell 208, or silence them) and measure the change in the probability of each behavior; necessity can be tested by ablation. Briggman, Abarbanel and Kristan's results correspond to the second outcome.
