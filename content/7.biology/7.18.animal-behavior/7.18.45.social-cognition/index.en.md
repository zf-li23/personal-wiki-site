Animals that live in groups are surrounded by the most informative and least predictable part of their environment: other animals. A rival's posture predicts an attack, a companion's gaze reveals a predator or food, and a cache made in view of a competitor may be lost. *Social cognition* is the set of processes by which animals perceive, learn about and predict the behavior of others, and, in its most debated form, attribute to them mental states such as seeing, knowing and believing. This chapter begins with the self, through the much-used mirror test, then treats how animals use the behavior and attention of others, including the remarkable sensitivity of domestic dogs to human gestures, and ends with the question that Premack and Woodruff posed in 1978: does the chimpanzee have a theory of mind? The physical reasoning of [7.18.44](/7.18.44) and the associative learning of [7.18.41](/7.18.41) are the background against which every claim here must be tested; the signals that animals use to manipulate each other are treated in [7.18.37](/7.18.37)–[7.18.40](/7.18.40), and learning from others in [7.18.46](/7.18.46).

## 45.1. Self-Recognition

### 45.1.1. The Mark Test

Most animals react to their reflection in a mirror as if to another individual: fish display at it, birds court or attack it, monkeys threaten it. Gordon Gallup (1970) gave chimpanzees a full-length mirror. During the first days they responded socially, threatening and vocalizing at the image; then social responses waned and self-directed behaviors appeared: they used the mirror to inspect parts of their bodies they could not otherwise see, groomed their teeth and picked food from between them, and made faces. To test whether the change meant that they recognized themselves, Gallup anesthetized the chimpanzees and painted an odorless red dye on an eyebrow ridge and the opposite ear, places invisible without a mirror. After waking, they rarely touched the marks until the mirror was returned, when they touched them repeatedly and inspected and smelled their fingers. Chimpanzees without previous mirror experience did not do so, and rhesus macaques failed the test.

**Definition 45.1.1 (Mirror Self-Recognition)** **Mirror self-recognition** is the ability to recognize one's own reflection as oneself, shown by self-directed behavior in front of a mirror and operationally by the *mark test*: an individual marked in a place it cannot see directly, with a mark that cannot be felt or smelled, touches or inspects the mark more when a mirror is present than when it is absent.

![The mark test and two sets of results that limit its interpretation. A: the procedure of Gallup (1970). After a period of mirror exposure, the animal is marked under anesthesia (or covertly, in children) with an odorless dye; a sham mark (anesthesia without dye, or a transparent mark) is the control. The test compares touches of the mark without and with a mirror. B: the proportion of children aged 35–54 months who touched or removed a sticker on their head after seeing themselves in a mirror differs widely among cultures (Broesch and colleagues 2011). C: in one of the few studies reporting rates, chimpanzees touched their dye marks 2.5 times per 30 min without a mirror and 3.9 times with one (Povinelli and colleagues 1993): a real but modest difference.](./plots/mark-test.py)

Human infants pass the mark test (usually with rouge on the nose) from about 18 to 24 months of age. Among other animals, the evidence is as follows:

- *Great apes.* Chimpanzees, bonobos and orangutans pass, but not all individuals: mark-directed behavior is rare before two and a half years, most common in juveniles and young adults, and less frequent in old chimpanzees (de Veer and colleagues 2003). Gorillas mostly fail; a few individuals raised with humans, such as Koko, have been reported to pass.
- *Monkeys and gibbons.* More than a dozen species of Old and New World monkeys and gibbons failed despite long exposure. Rhesus macaques that were trained to touch light spots projected on their faces while seeing them in a mirror, a visual-somatosensory training, later used the mirror spontaneously to inspect their bodies (Chang and colleagues 2015). Training thus can produce in monkeys what apes do spontaneously.
- *Dolphins and elephants.* Two bottlenose dolphins, marked with black ink and given a mirror, turned to inspect the marked side of their bodies (Reiss and Marino 2001); dolphins cannot touch the mark, so the evidence is inspection rather than touching. One of three Asian elephants tested in a zoo repeatedly touched a mark on her head with her trunk in front of a large mirror (Plotnik, de Waal and Reiss 2006).
- *Birds.* Two of five European magpies (*Pica pica*) tried to remove a colored sticker on the throat feathers when a mirror was present (Prior, Schwarz and Güntürkün 2008); a later attempt to replicate the result, with additional controls, found no mark-directed behavior (Soler and colleagues 2020).
- *Fish.* Cleaner wrasse (*Labroides dimidiatus*), which remove parasites from the bodies of other fish, scraped a brown mark on the throat (resembling a parasite) against the substrate after seeing it in a mirror, but not a transparent mark or a mark without a mirror (Kohda and colleagues 2019). The authors argued that either the fish recognize themselves or the mark test does not measure what it is supposed to; critics replied that the scraping could be a response to an apparent parasite on another fish. Later work reported that cleaner wrasse also recognize photographs of their own faces.

### 45.1.2. What Does the Mirror Test Show?

Gallup argued that passing the mark test shows a *self-concept*, an awareness of oneself as an object of others' attention, and possibly the capacity to attribute mental states to others. The interpretation has been challenged on several grounds.

First, the effect is smaller than often described. Chimpanzees do touch their faces without a mirror; the comparison of rates in the figure above shows a clear but modest increase. Second, experience matters greatly. Robert Epstein, Robert Lanza and B. F. Skinner (1981) trained pigeons to peck blue spots on their own bodies and, separately, to use a mirror to locate a spot of light that had appeared behind them; when a blue dot was then placed on the breast, hidden from direct view by a bib, the pigeons looked in the mirror and pecked at the dot on their bodies. The pigeons had never been trained to use a mirror to locate marks on their bodies, the actual skill tested, but they had been given its components. Human children's performance is also experience-dependent: in a simple version of the test with a sticker placed covertly on the head, 84 % of children aged three to four and a half years from the United States and Canada removed the sticker, but only about half of the children in Peru, Grenada and Saint Lucia, none in Fiji and two of 82 in Kenya, where many children had little experience of mirrors and where it may be unusual to touch one's own head in front of an adult experimenter (Broesch and colleagues 2011).

Third, mirror recognition and self-concept can come apart. People with prosopagnosia, who cannot recognize faces, may fail to recognize themselves in a mirror while their sense of self is intact; conversely, children with autism, who may have difficulty seeing themselves as others see them, usually pass the mark test. In so far as mirror recognition shows anything general, it shows an *own-body concept*, the ability to distinguish one's body from the rest of the world, which nearly all animals must have (an animal that bit its own leg in a fight would not last long). The interesting comparative question then becomes why so few species can map their body onto its reflection, and the answer may lie in how species use vision to identify individuals, and in their experience with reflections, rather than in the depth of their self-awareness.

Finally, the test is visual, whereas many species identify individuals mainly by smell or sound. Domestic dogs spend more time sniffing a sample of their own urine to which another odor has been added than an unaltered sample of their own, and more time sniffing other dogs' urine than their own, a form of olfactory self-recognition (Horowitz 2017). Chimpanzees use live video images of their own arms to guide their hands to hidden targets (Menzel, Savage-Rumbaugh and Lawson 1985), and pigeons recognize themselves in video with delays of several seconds, longer than three-year-old children tolerate (Toda and Watanabe 2008). Tests designed for the sensory world (**Umwelt**, [7.18.9](/7.18.9)) of each species broaden the question beyond mirrors.

## 45.2. Reading the Behavior of Others

### 45.2.1. Demonstrators and Observers

Attending to others gives access to information that an animal could not obtain directly: the location of food, the presence of a predator, the intentions of a rival. Some of this information is offered by **signals** that evolved to influence receivers, such as an alarm call; much of it is in **cues**, by-products of behavior that the sender would rather hide, such as the trail of a fox leading a coyote to its den, exploited by **eavesdropping** ([7.18.37](/7.18.37)). To describe social cognition without assuming the intentions of either party, the individual whose behavior provides information is called the *demonstrator* and the individual that may use it the *observer*, the same terms used for social learning ([7.18.46](/7.18.46)).

Two kinds of explanation are always available for an observer's success. The observer may represent the demonstrator's mental state ("she knows where the food is"), or it may have learned that certain features of the demonstrator's behavior predict events ("food is usually near where a hand points"). The second is called *behavior reading*. Much of the history of the field has been an attempt to devise tests that only mind reading could pass.

### 45.2.2. Dogs, Pointing and Domestication

Domestic dogs became a model of social cognition after Ádám Miklósi and colleagues (1998) used the *object-choice task*. A human stands between two bowls, both smelling of food, only one baited, and points to the baited one; the dog, released from a distance, is allowed to eat if it goes to the correct bowl. All five pet dogs in the first study followed the point, and many also used bowing, nodding and head turning toward the bowl; glancing with the eyes alone was the hardest cue. Brian Hare and colleagues (2002) compared dogs with chimpanzees and with wolves raised by humans, found that dogs performed better than both, and that puppies only a few weeks old already used pointing. They concluded that the domestication of dogs selected for a specialized ability to read human communicative gestures.

Later findings complicated this conclusion. Wolves hand-reared and intensively socialized by people followed momentary distal points as well as pet dogs, and better than dogs living in shelters (Udell, Dorey and Wynne 2008), and shelter dogs could learn to do so with a few dozen trials. Free-ranging dogs in India followed points readily as pups, less as juveniles, when humans become a danger to them, and adults followed more consistently after being rewarded for doing so (Bhattacharjee and colleagues 2017). Since about three-quarters of the world's dogs are village or street dogs rather than pets, pet dogs are not a representative sample of the species. At least 38 species, including dolphins, fur seals, horses, goats, bats and several birds, have followed human points under some conditions, usually when raised or trained by people.

Monique Udell and colleagues (2010) proposed a *two-stage hypothesis*: to follow human gestures, a canid must first accept humans as social companions, through exposure during a sensitive period early in life (socialization), and must then learn, by **operant conditioning**, that following human limbs leads to food or toys. Neither stage requires an understanding of the human's intention to communicate.

![A model of the two-stage hypothesis for point following (model predictions, not data). Stage 1: socialization sets the probability $s$ that an animal attends to a human on a trial. Stage 2: when it attends, it learns the point–food contingency by a delta rule, $w \leftarrow w + \alpha(1 - w)$ after a rewarded follow ($\alpha = 0.08$), and chooses the pointed-at bowl with probability $0.5 + 0.5\,s\,w$. Groups differ in $s$ and in the prior association $w_0$ brought from life with people: pet dogs ($s = 0.95$, $w_0 = 0.75$) follow points from the start; shelter dogs ($s = 0.7$, $w_0 = 0.15$) and hand-reared wolves ($s = 0.85$, $w_0 = 0.1$) start near chance and learn; wolves not socialized to people ($s = 0.1$) hardly learn, because they rarely attend. The model reproduces the qualitative pattern of results with no special communicative ability.](./plots/point-following.py)

**Domestication** did change dogs, but perhaps by changing their development rather than by adding a new cognitive module. In the farm-fox experiment begun by Dmitry Belyaev in Novosibirsk in 1959, silver foxes were selected for generations only for tameness toward humans. The selected foxes developed floppy ears, piebald coats and curled tails, and their sensitive period of socialization ended later than in unselected foxes, so that they could form bonds with people over a longer window (Trut 1999). Kits of the tame line followed human pointing and gaze as well as dog puppies of the same age, whereas kits of the control line did not (Hare and colleagues 2005): selection for reduced fear produced communicative skills as a by-product. In dogs, structural variants in genes of the region deleted in human Williams–Beuren syndrome (*GTF2I*, *GTF2IRD1*), associated with hypersociability in humans, are associated with the prolonged, intense greeting of people that distinguishes dogs from wolves (vonHoldt and colleagues 2017). Dogs are also very sensitive to human behavior in ways that people misread: dogs show the "guilty look" (head and ears lowered, tail low, averted gaze) when their owners scold them, whether or not they have eaten a forbidden treat, and look guiltiest when scolded wrongly (Horowitz 2009); the look is appeasement in response to the owner's behavior, not evidence of guilt. Domestic cats, long assumed to be asocial, follow human points, distinguish their owners' voices and their own names from other words, and form attachments to their owners that resemble those of children and dogs (Vitale, Behnke and Udell 2019): much of the apparent difference between dogs and cats reflects how much each has been studied.

## 45.3. Gaze and Attention

### 45.3.1. Gaze Following

Where another animal looks is a powerful source of information, because eyes orient toward what matters: a predator, food or a rival.

**Definition 45.3.1 (Gaze Following)** **Gaze following** is the orientation of an observer's gaze in the direction in which another individual is looking. In its simplest form the observer turns in the same direction (co-orientation); in *geometric* gaze following the observer moves to look around a barrier at the target of the other's gaze, which requires representing the line of sight as ending at an object.

Gaze following toward distant space has been reported in all great apes, many monkeys, dogs, goats, several corvids and even the red-footed tortoise, which followed the gaze of a conspecific looking up (Wilkinson and colleagues 2010). Geometric gaze following around barriers is rarer: chimpanzees and other apes do it (Tomasello, Hare and Agnetta 1999), and hand-raised ravens repositioned themselves to look behind a barrier when a human looked there, a skill that appeared in the course of development (Bugnyar, Stöwe and Heinrich 2004). Goats in a zoo followed the gaze of other goats but not of humans, with whom they had little contact (Kaminski and colleagues 2005), and dogs, whose vision resolves less detail than ours, follow head and body orientation more readily than eye movements alone. Human-raised jackdaws, which have conspicuous pale irises, used a human's alternating gaze between them and a cup to find food (von Bayern and Emery 2009).

Primates differ in how conspicuous their eyes are. Humans are unusual among primates in having a white sclera that contrasts with the dark iris and a horizontally elongated eye, which makes the direction of gaze visible from a distance. According to the *cooperative eye hypothesis*, this conspicuous morphology evolved because it was advantageous for our ancestors to reveal their gaze to cooperative partners, whereas in most primates, which compete for food, concealing gaze direction is advantageous (Kobayashi and Kohshima 1997; Tomasello and colleagues 2007). Human infants follow eye direction alone, whereas chimpanzees follow head direction.

### 45.3.2. Who Can See Me?

Gaze following does not require any understanding of seeing: following gaze could be a predisposition, or learned because it has led to interesting sights in the past. Tests of perspective taking ask whether animals take into account what others can or cannot see. In the *begging task*, an animal can beg for food from one of two people, one of whom can see it and one who cannot (eyes blindfolded, back turned, a bucket over the head). Daniel Povinelli and Timothy Eddy (1996) found that young chimpanzees begged equally from both, except when one person faced away; with training they gradually learned whom to beg from, but they showed no spontaneous understanding that someone who cannot see cannot respond.

Dogs do better on some versions. They prefer to beg from a person who faces them than from one with her back turned, and from a person whose eyes are open (Gácsi and colleagues 2004); told by their owner not to take food from the floor, they take it more often when the owner's eyes are closed, her back is turned or she is reading (Call and colleagues 2003). But the pattern of successes points to learned cues: in a study comparing pet dogs, shelter dogs and hand-reared wolves, all groups preferred an attentive person over one with her back turned; only pet dogs, which often see people reading, preferred the attentive person over one reading a book; and no group preferred the attentive person over one with a bucket over her head, an unfamiliar but obvious obstruction of vision (Udell, Dorey and Wynne 2011). An animal that understood seeing should avoid the person in the bucket at once.

### 45.3.3. Caching in Front of Observers

Food-storing animals face an acute version of the problem: caches made in view of others are pilfered ([7.18.23](/7.18.23)). Corvids respond to observers in ways that look like perspective taking. Ravens recovered caches more quickly in the presence of a competitor that had watched them cache than in the presence of one that had been behind a curtain (Bugnyar and Heinrich 2005). Western scrub jays (*Aphelocoma californica*) that were watched while caching moved their caches to new sites when they were later alone, but did not if they had cached in private (Emery and Clayton 2001). Most strikingly, only jays that had themselves stolen other jays' caches re-cached after being watched; jays with experience of caching but not of pilfering did not, as if the birds projected their own experience as thieves onto the observers ("experience projection"). Jays also kept track of which individual had watched which cache, re-caching more of the caches seen by a dominant than by a partner, and preferentially cached in shade or far from an observer when they could (Dally, Emery and Clayton 2006); when an observer could hear but not see them, they cached in quiet soil rather than in noisy gravel (Stulp and colleagues 2009).

These results require memory of who saw what ([7.18.42](/7.18.42)), but whether they require attributing knowledge to observers is disputed. A computer model in which jays re-cache simply because the presence of an observer causes stress, and stressed birds manage caches more actively, reproduced many of the findings without any representation of what observers know (van der Vaart, Verbrugge and Hemelrijk 2012). The model did not explain all of them, such as the effect of the jays' own pilfering experience, but it shows how much of the behavior could arise from simple mechanisms.

## 45.4. Theory of Mind

### 45.4.1. The Question

David Premack and Guy Woodruff (1978) asked whether the chimpanzee "has a theory of mind", and the question has organized research on social cognition ever since.

**Definition 45.4.1 (Theory of Mind)** **Theory of mind** is the capacity to attribute mental states, such as perceptions, knowledge, intentions, desires and beliefs, to oneself and to others, and to use these attributions to explain and predict behavior. An animal with a theory of mind infers the mental states of others from their appearance and behavior in various circumstances and treats these states as causes of their behavior (Heyes 1998). The most demanding test is the understanding of *false belief*: predicting that an individual will act on a belief that the observer knows to be false.

The false-belief criterion was proposed by philosophers commenting on Premack and Woodruff's paper: if an animal predicts that another will look for food where the food really is, it may simply be predicting from the state of the world; only if it predicts that the other will look where the other last saw the food, though the food has since been moved, must it be representing the other's belief. Human children pass explicit verbal false-belief tests at about four years of age; infants of one to two years look in anticipation at the location where an agent with a false belief should search (Onishi and Baillargeon 2005; Southgate, Senju and Csibra 2007), although replications of such looking-time and anticipatory-looking studies with infants have often failed, and their interpretation is debated.

### 45.4.2. Deception and Its Interpretation

Some of the earliest evidence came from observations of apparent deception, which would make sense if the deceiver understood that others act on what they believe. Dorothy Cheney and Robert Seyfarth (1990) described a low-ranking male vervet monkey, Kitui, who gave leopard alarm calls when a new male tried to join his group, sending everyone, including the newcomer, into the trees. But Kitui then sometimes came down from his tree and walked across open ground to call closer to the intruder, betraying that he was not afraid of any leopard; a simpler explanation is that he had learned that giving the call kept newcomers away. Richard Byrne and Andrew Whiten collected hundreds of such reports of *tactical deception* in primates, which they argued were most frequent in species with large neocortices, but anecdotes cannot exclude learning and suffer from selective reporting: interesting successes are written down, uninteresting failures are not.

Early experiments with trained chimpanzees had similar problems. In a task in which chimpanzees could indicate the location of food to a cooperative trainer, who shared it, or to a competitive trainer, who kept it, chimpanzees learned to point to the empty container for the competitive trainer, but only after more than 100 trials (Woodruff and Premack 1979); the same discrimination could be learned with two colored lights instead of two people, and nobody would credit a pigeon with a theory of the minds of lights. In the *knower–guesser* paradigm, one trainer baited one of four cups behind a screen while a second trainer was absent; both then pointed to cups. Two of four chimpanzees learned to choose the knower's cup, again slowly; when a new version had the guesser present but with a bag over his head, three of four transferred, but not immediately (Povinelli, Nelson and Boysen 1990). Slow learning of this kind is what associative learning predicts, and it is hard to reconcile with an understanding that seeing leads to knowing.

### 45.4.3. The Competitive Paradigm

Brian Hare, Josep Call and Michael Tomasello argued that these tasks were unnatural, because chimpanzees rarely cooperate over food, and that chimpanzees might show their social knowledge when competing. In their design, a subordinate and a dominant chimpanzee were housed in rooms on opposite sides of a central area in which food was placed; the subordinate was released with a slight head start (figure below).

![The competitive paradigm (Hare, Call and Tomasello 2000, 2001), top views. A subordinate (S) and a dominant (D) chimpanzee face each other across a central area. A: one food item is visible to both, a second is hidden from the dominant by an opaque barrier on the subordinate's side; subordinates went preferentially for the hidden item. B: in later experiments the dominant either watched food being hidden behind a barrier (informed) or its door was closed during baiting (uninformed); subordinates approached the hidden food more often when the dominant was uninformed, and when the dominant that had watched was replaced by another dominant that had not.](./plots/competition.py)

When one piece of food was visible to both animals and a second was hidden from the dominant by a barrier, subordinates preferred the hidden piece (Hare, Call, Agnetta and Tomasello 2000). In a follow-up, the subordinate always saw the food being placed behind a barrier, while the dominant either watched or did not; subordinates went for the food more often when the dominant had not seen it, and when the dominant that had seen it was swapped for one that had not (Hare, Call and Tomasello 2001). Chimpanzees also preferred to approach food by a route that a competitor could not see, and to take food silently when a competitor could hear but not see them (Melis, Call and Tomasello 2006).

After surveying these and other results, Call and Tomasello (2008) concluded that chimpanzees understand others' goals and intentions, and what others can and cannot see and therefore know, but there was no evidence that they understand false beliefs. In a competitive task modeled on the false-belief test, a chimpanzee chose between two cups after a competitor had seen food hidden and then either seen it moved (true belief) or not seen it moved (false belief); the subjects behaved as if they knew what the competitor knew (whether it had seen the hiding) but not what it believed (Kaminski, Call and Tomasello 2008).

### 45.4.4. False Belief and Anticipatory Looking

A new method used eye tracking instead of choices. Christopher Krupenye and colleagues (2016) showed chimpanzees, bonobos and orangutans videos in which a person watched an actor in an ape costume hide an object in one of two locations; while the person was away, the "ape" moved the object to the other location and then removed it. When the person returned to retrieve the object, most apes looked first at the location where the person had last seen the object, where the person should falsely believe it to be, rather than where it had gone. A further experiment used the apes' own experience: apes that had learned that a barrier was opaque or, in another group, that an identical-looking barrier was see-through, then watched videos in which an agent's view was blocked by the barrier; only the apes that had experienced the barrier as opaque anticipated that the agent would act on a false belief (Kano and colleagues 2019). Apes also helped a person open a box differently depending on whether the person had a true or false belief about its contents (Buttelmann and colleagues 2017).

These results suggest that great apes can track others' beliefs, at least implicitly. Skeptics reply that anticipatory looking could reflect a rule about behavior ("agents search where they last saw an object") rather than a representation of belief, the general *logical problem* that any behavioral cue used to infer a mental state could itself be used directly to predict behavior (Povinelli and Vonk 2003). The experience-projection designs of the scrub jays and of Kano and colleagues are the most informative attempt to escape this problem, because the cue (the barrier's appearance) is held constant while only the observer's knowledge of what the barrier allows differs. The debate is no longer whether animals are sensitive to what others perceive and know, but whether that sensitivity is best described as representing mental states, and in which species.

### 45.4.5. Why Theory of Mind?

The **social brain hypothesis** ([7.18.8](/7.18.8)) holds that the cognitive demands of life in complex groups selected for large brains, and theory of mind is often presented as the summit of such social intelligence. The argument that theory of mind is useful in groups and therefore must have evolved in social species is weak: usefulness does not imply existence, and different species may solve the same social problems with different mechanisms. Comparative evidence is more informative: corvids, which converge with apes in physical cognition ([7.18.44](/7.18.44)), also converge in caching strategies that respond to what observers have seen; dogs, selected to live with people, are acutely sensitive to human cues but appear to learn them associatively; and chimpanzees are more sophisticated in competition than in cooperation. Humans differ less in what they can track than in what they share: from early childhood, humans point to show others things, follow and direct attention in *joint attention*, and teach, forms of shared intentionality that Tomasello argues underlie language and culture ([7.18.46](/7.18.46), [7.18.47](/7.18.47)).

## Summary

- Mirror self-recognition (Definition 45.1.1) is tested by the mark test: chimpanzees, bonobos and orangutans pass, monkeys and most gorillas fail, and dolphins, an elephant, magpies (not replicated) and cleaner wrasse have been reported to pass. The effect is modest, experience-dependent (trained pigeons and monkeys; cultural differences in children) and dissociable from self-concept; it shows at most an own-body concept tied to visual identification.
- Observers can use demonstrators' behavior by mind reading or by behavior reading. Dogs follow human pointing in the object-choice task; socialized wolves and many other species also do. The two-stage hypothesis (socialization, then conditioning) explains the pattern; domestication, as in Belyaev's foxes, lengthened the socialization period and increased sociability.
- Gaze following (Definition 45.3.1) is widespread; geometric gaze following around barriers occurs in apes and ravens. The white human sclera may be an adaptation for cooperation. In begging tasks chimpanzees fail and dogs use learned cues (book, but not bucket).
- Corvids re-cache after being watched, track who saw which cache, and, in scrub jays, re-cache only if they have pilfered themselves (experience projection); a stress model explains part of this.
- Theory of mind (Definition 45.4.1): anecdotes of deception and slow learning in cooperative and knower–guesser tasks do not require it; competitive tasks show that chimpanzees know what others can see and know; anticipatory-looking studies suggest implicit tracking of false beliefs in apes, debated because of the logical problem of behavior reading.

## Questions

1. Why is a sham-marked control and a no-mirror baseline essential in the mark test, and what alternative explanation remains even when an animal passes?

   **Answer** Without them, an increase in face touching could result from the anesthesia, from feeling or smelling the dye, or from a general increase in grooming when the mirror is present. The sham mark controls for the procedure, and the comparison with and without a mirror shows that touches are guided by the reflection. Even when the animal passes, it has shown only that it can use the mirror image to locate a spot on its own body; this may reflect experience with mirrors and an own-body concept rather than self-awareness in the human sense, as trained pigeons and monkeys and the cross-cultural differences in children show.
2. According to the two-stage hypothesis, predict the point-following performance of (a) a wolf hand-reared from 10 days of age, (b) a pet dog that has never been rewarded for following a point, (c) a village dog that avoids people.

   **Answer** (a) It should attend to humans (socialized early) and learn the point–food contingency quickly, reaching levels similar to pet dogs after training, as hand-reared wolves do. (b) Probably good from the start, because ordinary life with people provides many occasions on which following a human's arm leads to food or toys, even without formal training; but performance on unusual gestures should be poorer. (c) Poor, because it rarely attends to or approaches people; if it can be habituated and rewarded, it may learn, as free-ranging adult dogs in India did after receiving food.
3. Design a begging-task condition that would distinguish an understanding of seeing from the use of learned cues, and explain the logic.

   **Answer** Use an obstruction of vision that the animals have never encountered but that is obviously effective to a mind reader, such as a bucket over the head or an opaque screen held in front of the eyes, contrasted with a familiar posture and a novel non-obstructing control (a bucket held beside the head). An animal that understands seeing should beg from the seeing person on the first trial; one using learned cues should be indifferent or choose by familiarity. Udell and colleagues' bucket condition, in which no group discriminated, is an example.
4. Scrub jays that had pilfered re-cached after being watched, but jays without pilfering experience did not. Why is this result difficult to explain by a simple rule such as "re-cache when an observer was present"?

   **Answer** Both groups experienced the same stimulus (an observer during caching), so a rule triggered by the observer's presence would predict re-caching in both. The difference depends on the subject's own experience of stealing, which affects behavior in a role (being watched) different from the one in which it was acquired (stealing). This is what would be expected if the birds used their own experience to anticipate what an observer might do, although alternative explanations (for example, pilfering experience increasing general anxiety about caches) are possible and would need their own controls.
5. Explain the false-belief logic of the anticipatory-looking experiment of Krupenye and colleagues, and the logical problem raised by Povinelli and Vonk.

   **Answer** The agent saw the object hidden at location 1 and did not see it moved to location 2. An ape that represents the agent's belief should expect the agent to search at location 1, where the agent falsely believes it to be, and so look there first; an ape that predicts from the real state of the world should look at location 2. Most apes looked at location 1. The logical problem is that the same prediction can be made from a rule about observable behavior, "agents search where they last saw something", without representing belief; whenever the cue to a mental state is observable, the cue itself can be used. Experiments in which the observable situation is identical but the observer's own knowledge of what the agent can see differs (Kano and colleagues) are designed to avoid this.
6. (Open question) Dogs excel at reading human gestures, chimpanzees at competitive perspective taking, and corvids at protecting caches from observers. What do these different profiles suggest about the evolution of social cognition?

   **Answer** They suggest that social cognition is shaped by the specific problems each lineage faces rather than ordered on a single scale. Dogs live with humans, on whom they depend for food, and attention to human cues has been favored by domestication and is refined by individual learning; their skill is cooperative and largely associative. Chimpanzees compete for food with group members, and their sensitivity to what rivals can see is expressed mainly in competition. Food-storing corvids lose caches to observers, and their abilities concern who has seen which cache. A comparative approach should test related species that differ in the relevant ecology (for example, caching and non-caching corvids, socialized and unsocialized canids, cooperative and competitive primates), and should ask which components (gaze following, memory of who saw what, experience projection) evolved together.
