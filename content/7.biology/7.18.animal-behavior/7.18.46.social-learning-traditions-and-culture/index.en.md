An animal that learns everything by itself pays for every lesson: it must sample foods that may be poisonous, approach objects that may be predators, and discover by trial and error how to open a nut. Learning from others can make the same knowledge cheaper, faster and safer, and when the knowledge passes from individual to individual and from generation to generation it creates traditions, behaviors that characterize groups rather than species. This chapter distinguishes the forms of social influence and social learning, asks when copying others pays, examines the experimental evidence for imitation and teaching, and then turns to traditions and culture in animals and to the models of cultural evolution that explain their dynamics. The associative mechanisms underlying much of social learning are those of [7.18.41](/7.18.41); the social cognition that may support copying is in [7.18.45](/7.18.45); song learning, the best-studied case of vocal imitation, is in [7.18.17](/7.18.17); and human cultural evolution, the most extreme case, is treated in [7.10.33](/7.10.33#sec-33.7).

## 46.1. Forms of Social Influence

### 46.1.1. Social Facilitation

When everyone around you suddenly ducks, you duck too. *Social facilitation* is an increase in a behavior caused simply by the presence of others performing it: chickens that have eaten their fill resume eating when a hungry hen is introduced, yawning and laughter are contagious in humans, and male frogs are more likely to join a chorus when a recording of calling males is played than when noise is played (Brooke, Alford and Schwarzkopf 2000). Social facilitation does not require that anything new be learned or that the behavior persist when the others leave. It may nonetheless be adaptive: an elk that bolts when its herd mates bolt, without having seen the predator, benefits from the vigilance of others and from the **dilution effect** of fleeing in a group ([7.18.27](/7.18.27)), and the synchrony of behavior within groups is itself maintained in part by facilitation.

### 46.1.2. Enhancement

Social influences that change what an animal learns about, without its copying any action, are among the most common forms of social learning.

**Definition 46.1.1 (Local Enhancement)** **Local enhancement** is the process by which the presence or activity of a demonstrator at a place draws an observer to that place, where the observer then learns by itself about what the place offers.

**Definition 46.1.2 (Stimulus Enhancement)** **Stimulus enhancement** is the process by which a demonstrator's interaction with an object, or a kind of object, increases the probability that an observer interacts with objects of that kind, so that the observer then learns by itself about their properties.

Konrad Lorenz noted that a duck was more likely to escape from a pen through a hole in the fence if it happened to be near another duck jumping through it, and recognized that the second duck need not be imitating: the first had merely drawn its attention to the hole. A cat that jumps onto the keyboard of a person typing energetically is showing stimulus enhancement, not learning to type. Enhancement is easy to underestimate. Many early claims of imitation turned out to be explicable by it, and it may explain part of dogs' success at following human points: dogs are more accurate when the pointing hand is near the target and remains there than when a brief, distal point is given ([7.18.45](/7.18.45)).

### 46.1.3. Observational Conditioning and Affordance Learning

In *observational conditioning*, the demonstrator's behavior acts as an unconditioned stimulus that becomes associated with a stimulus present at the time. Laboratory-reared rhesus monkeys that watched a video of a wild-born monkey reacting fearfully to a snake acquired a lasting fear of snakes, but not of flowers when the video was edited to show the same reaction to flowers ([7.18.41](/7.18.41#sec-41.5.2)). European blackbirds that heard conspecifics mobbing while they saw a stuffed bird of an unfamiliar species, which they would otherwise ignore, later mobbed that object themselves, and the response was passed along a chain of birds (Curio, Ernst and Vieth 1978; [7.18.27](/7.18.27)). Naive mice that watched another mouse being attacked by biting flies and burying itself in the bedding later showed the same defensive behavior toward flies whose mouthparts had been removed, although they had never been bitten (Kavaliers, Choleris and Colwell 2001).

Some apparent social learning involves no learning about the demonstrator at all. A squirrel that watches another squirrel knock down a bird feeder and later knocks it down itself may have learned only that feeders can fall and release seeds, which it would have learned as well from seeing the wind blow the feeder down. Such *affordance learning*, learning about the properties of objects by seeing them move, is tested by "ghost" controls, in which the object moves by itself (by a hidden mechanism) in the absence of a demonstrator. When observers of a ghost condition learn as well as observers of a demonstrator, the learning was not social in the sense that matters for copying actions.

## 46.2. Social Learning: What It Is and When It Pays

### 46.2.1. Definition and Examples

**Definition 46.2.1 (Social Learning)** **Social learning** is learning that is facilitated by observation of, or interaction with, another individual or its products (Heyes 1994; Hoppitt and Laland 2013); the learned behavior persists when the demonstrator is absent. Learning without such social input is *asocial* (individual) learning.

The products of others include scent marks, feeding remains and trails; the definition covers the enhancement and conditioning processes of Section 46.1 as well as copying in the narrow sense. Its central requirement is a comparison: the observer must learn faster, or learn something different, than it would by itself.

Rodents provide a classic case. Norway rats (*Rattus norvegicus*) eat new foods cautiously and avoid foods that made them ill (**conditioned taste aversion**, [7.18.41](/7.18.41)). Bennett Galef and his colleagues showed that a rat that interacts with a demonstrator that has recently eaten a flavored food later prefers that flavor, even if the demonstrator was anesthetized during the interaction: the observer smells the food on the demonstrator's breath together with carbon disulfide, a component of rat breath, and the combination makes the food acceptable. In mice, the amount of the demonstrator's food that observers later ate increased with the number of times they had sniffed the demonstrator's muzzle (Wrenn and colleagues 2003). Social transmission of food preferences is strong, whereas social transmission of food avoidance is weak: a rat that has eaten poison in nature is usually dead, so information about safe foods is more available and more useful than information about dangerous ones.

Social learning is used for food, predators, routes, mates and songs, and it varies among closely related species in ways that reflect the costs of asocial learning. Nine-spined sticklebacks (*Pungitius pungitius*), which have weak spines and hide in vegetation, watch other fish feeding at two patches and later choose the richer patch, using *public information* about the success of others; three-spined sticklebacks (*Gasterosteus aculeatus*), protected by large spines and armor, sample the patches themselves and ignore what others do (Coolen and colleagues 2003). For the vulnerable species, personal sampling is costly, and copying is cheaper.

### 46.2.2. Rogers' Paradox

If social learning is cheaper than asocial learning, why not always copy? Alan Rogers (1988) answered with a model that has become known as *Rogers' paradox*. Social learners avoid the cost of learning, but their information is only as good as that of the individuals they copy. In a changing environment, copied behavior may be out of date, and if everyone copies, no one is tracking the environment. Social learners therefore do well when rare, because they copy individual learners who have paid for accurate information, and poorly when common. At equilibrium the two strategies have equal fitness, and the population as a whole is no better adapted than a population of individual learners alone (figure below): social learning spreads without improving mean fitness.

![Rogers' paradox and its resolution. The environment changes each generation with probability $u = 0.1$; behavior adapted to the current environment adds $b = 1$ to a baseline fitness of 1. Individual learners always acquire the adaptive behavior at a cost $c$; social learners copy a random adult of the previous generation at no cost, and the copy is adaptive if the model's behavior was and the environment has not changed. With a fraction $p$ of social learners, the proportion of adaptive behavior at equilibrium is $q = (1 - p)/(1 - p(1 - u))$, and the fitness of social learners is $1 + b(1 - u)q$. A ($c = 0.3$): social learners are fitter when rare and less fit when common; at the equilibrium $p^* = 0.74$ both strategies, and therefore the population mean, have the same fitness $1 + b - c$ as a population of individual learners alone. B: mean fitness at equilibrium against $c$. Critical social learners, which copy first and learn individually only if the copied behavior fails, reach fitness $1 + b - cu$ and raise the mean fitness of the population.](./plots/rogers.py)

The paradox is resolved when social learning is used selectively. A *critical social learner* that copies first and then evaluates the copied behavior, learning by itself only if it does not work, outperforms both pure strategies and raises the mean fitness of the population (Enquist, Eriksson and Ghirlanda 2007). More generally, natural selection should favor *social learning strategies*, rules about when to copy and whom to copy (Laland 2004):

- *When to copy*: when asocial learning is costly or dangerous, when the animal is uncertain, when its current behavior is unproductive, and when the environment is stable enough for others' information to remain valid.
- *Whom to copy*: the majority (conformist bias, Section 46.6.1), successful individuals (payoff bias), older or dominant individuals, kin, or individuals of the same sex.

Experiments support several of these rules. Nine-spined sticklebacks copy others more when their own information is unreliable or outdated, and they copy in proportion to the demonstrator's success: given a choice between two feeders, they were more likely to copy when demonstrators fed at a higher rate than they themselves had experienced, a strategy that leads the population toward better options, "hill climbing" (Kendal and colleagues 2009). Vervet monkeys preferentially copy dominant females, and chimpanzees older and higher-ranking individuals.

## 46.3. Imitation and Emulation

### 46.3.1. True Imitation

Copying the form of an action is the most demanding form of social learning, because the observer must translate what it sees (the demonstrator's body from outside) into what it does (its own body from inside), the *correspondence problem*.

**Definition 46.3.1 (Imitation)** **Imitation** (true imitation) is social learning in which an observer reproduces the form of a demonstrator's action, including features of its topography that are not required to obtain the result, as shown when observers of different, equally effective actions on the same object come to perform the action they have seen.

The definition contains its own experimental test. In the *two-action test*, the same apparatus can be operated in two ways, and different observers see demonstrators use different methods. Budgerigars that watched a demonstrator remove a lid from a food dish with the beak or the foot tended to use the method they had seen (Dawson and Foss 1965). Pigeons and Japanese quail that watched a demonstrator press a treadle either by pecking or by stepping on it, although depressing the treadle was all that was required, did more of the action they had observed (Zentall 1996). Quail copied the action more if they had seen the demonstrator rewarded (Akins and Zentall 1998), so imitation in quail is sensitive to the demonstrator's consequences.

In the *bidirectional control* procedure, a demonstrator rat pushed a joystick either to the left or to the right for food, and observers that later operated the joystick pushed it more often in the direction they had seen (Heyes and Dawson 1990). The demonstrators turned out to leave odor traces on the side of the joystick they had pushed, which could guide observers without any imitation (Mitchell and colleagues 1999), but the effect persisted when the joystick was cleaned or rotated (Ray, Gardner and Heyes 2000). Such controls show how hard it is to demonstrate imitation even in simple tasks, and also that action copying is not restricted to primates.

### 46.3.2. Do as I Do and the Model–Rival Method

A different approach trains animals to copy on command. Keith and Catherine Hayes raised the chimpanzee Viki in their home; she learned to imitate novel actions after the command "Do this" (Hayes and Hayes 1952), and later studies confirmed that home-reared chimpanzees reproduce arbitrary human actions (Custance, Whiten and Bard 1995). József Topál and colleagues (2006) trained Philip, an assistance dog, to reproduce nine actions demonstrated by a person on the command "Do it", and then tested him with 16 actions he knew but had never been asked to copy; he reproduced them in about two-thirds of trials, generalizing the copying rule. The method is now used to train dogs (Fugazza and Miklósi 2014). Bottlenose dolphins copy the actions of other dolphins on command, even when blindfolded, perhaps using sound (Jaakkola, Guarino and Rodriguez 2010). An Asian elephant, in contrast, could be trained to perform each action on command but never imitated a new one spontaneously (Dorey 2011).

Irene Pepperberg's *model–rival* method, used to train the African grey parrot Alex ([7.18.43](/7.18.43)), takes advantage of the parrot's attention to social competition. One trainer questions a second person, who serves both as a model of correct answers and as a rival for the trainer's attention and for the object named; errors by the model are corrected. Alex learned labels faster in this way than by conventional operant training. Vocal imitation (**vocal learning**) is a special case of imitation in which the correspondence problem is solved by auditory feedback: the learner hears both the model and itself ([7.18.17](/7.18.17)).

### 46.3.3. Emulation and Overimitation

**Definition 46.3.2 (Emulation)** **Emulation** is social learning in which an observer reproduces the result of a demonstrator's action, the change in the state of an object or the goal achieved, but not necessarily the actions by which the demonstrator produced it.

Victoria Horner and Andrew Whiten (2005) showed young chimpanzees and three- to four-year-old children how to retrieve a reward from a puzzle box, using a sequence that included unnecessary actions: tapping the top of the box with a stick, and pushing a bolt that did nothing. When the box was opaque, so that the causal relevance of the actions could not be seen, both chimpanzees and children copied the whole sequence. When the box was transparent, chimpanzees omitted the irrelevant actions and went straight for the reward, but children continued to copy them. Children's *overimitation*, the faithful copying of actions that are visibly unnecessary, has been confirmed in many studies and cultures; dogs, like chimpanzees, quickly drop irrelevant actions (Johnston, Holden and Santos 2017).

Children's overimitation is not a failure of understanding: children who overimitate know that the actions are unnecessary when asked, and they copy less when the demonstrator is absent or when the actions are presented as accidental. It may reflect the importance in human development of learning conventions, rituals and tools whose causal structure is opaque, for which faithful copying is a better strategy than reconstruction, and the social reward of doing things as others do them. Emulation is efficient for an individual; imitation preserves arbitrary details and so allows the transmission of traditions whose function the learner does not understand. Experiments in which 14-month-old infants copied a model who switched on a lamp by touching it with her forehead when her hands were free, but used their hands when the model's hands had been wrapped in a blanket (Gergely, Bekkering and Király 2002), suggested *rational imitation*, the copying of actions only when they appear to be chosen for a reason. Dogs were reported to do something similar (Range, Viranyi and Huber 2007), but a later study did not replicate the effect (Kaminski and colleagues 2011).

## 46.4. Teaching

In social learning the demonstrator is often unaware of the observer, or even opposed to being copied. In teaching, the demonstrator actively facilitates the learning of others.

**Definition 46.4.1 (Teaching)** An individual (the teacher) **teaches** when (1) it modifies its behavior only in the presence of a naive observer (the pupil), (2) it incurs a cost, or at least obtains no immediate benefit, by doing so, and (3) as a result, the pupil acquires knowledge or skills earlier, faster or more efficiently than it would otherwise, or that it would not otherwise acquire (Caro and Hauser 1992). The noun **teaching** refers to such behavior.

This functional definition makes no assumption about the teacher's understanding of the pupil's ignorance, which allows teaching to be tested in nonhuman animals. It has been satisfied in only a few cases:

- *Meerkats.* Pups of *Suricata suricatta* beg for food from adult helpers, which provision them with prey including scorpions, whose stings are dangerous. Helpers give young pups dead or disabled scorpions (with the sting bitten off), and older pups increasingly intact live ones; they monitor the pups' handling and retrieve and re-present escaping prey. Pups given live, stingless scorpions on three consecutive days handled scorpions more successfully afterward than pups given dead scorpions or egg, and playback of the begging calls of older pups made adults provide more intact prey (Thornton and McAuliffe 2006). The teaching thus uses a simple rule: the adults respond to the age of the pups' calls, not to their skill.
- *Ants.* When a worker of *Temnothorax albipennis* has found food, it leads a naive nestmate to it by *tandem running*: the follower keeps contact by tapping the leader's legs and abdomen with its antennae, and the leader slows down, waits when contact is lost, and continues when tapped. Leaders travel about four times more slowly than they would alone, and followers find the food faster than ants searching alone, learning landmarks on the way (Franks and Richardson 2006).
- *Pied babblers.* Adults of *Turdoides bicolor* give a "purr" call when they feed nestlings, conditioning the nestlings to associate the call with food; after fledging, adults use the call to lead the young to good foraging sites and away from predators (Raihani and Ridley 2008).
- *Chimpanzees.* Over ten years of observation of nut cracking in the Taï Forest, Christophe Boesch (1991) recorded mothers leaving hammers and nuts near anvils, where their young could use them (a behavior not seen in females without offspring), but only two instances of active intervention. In one, a mother whose daughter had failed for eight minutes took the hammer, slowly rotated it into a better position and cracked ten nuts, sharing some with her daughter, who then used the new grip and improved. Chimpanzee mothers in the Goualougo Triangle transfer termite-fishing probes to their offspring on request, at a cost to their own foraging (Musgrave and colleagues 2016).

Why is teaching so rare? In evolutionary terms teaching is a form of helping: the teacher pays a cost for the benefit of the pupil, so it should evolve when the pupil is usually a relative and when the benefit to the pupil, multiplied by relatedness, exceeds the cost (**Hamilton's rule**, [7.10.16](/7.10.16)). Teaching should also be favored where the information cannot easily be acquired by other means, either alone or by less costly social learning (Thornton and Raihani 2008). Meerkats, babblers and ants are cooperative breeders or social insects, in which helpers routinely invest in the young ([7.18.35](/7.18.35)). In humans, teaching, often with language and with explicit attention to what the pupil does not know, is universal and is thought to be one of the foundations of cumulative culture.

## 46.5. Traditions and Culture

### 46.5.1. Definitions

A *tradition* is a behavior pattern shared by members of a group that persists over time because it is socially learned. When groups of the same species differ in such patterns, and the differences cannot be explained by genetic or ecological differences, they are said to have different cultures.

**Definition 46.5.1 (Animal Culture)** **Animal culture** consists of the behavior patterns shared by members of a community that depend on socially learned and transmitted information, so that communities of the same species differ in their behavioral repertoires (Laland and Hoppitt 2003); a stronger definition requires several traditions in different behavioral domains, which together characterize a community.

Culture in this sense is a form of **cultural inheritance** ([7.10.4](/7.10.4)), information transmitted by learning from others rather than by genes. Its study faces the "method of exclusion": a behavioral difference between populations may be due to genes, to the environment or to social learning, and observations alone rarely exclude the first two. The strongest evidence therefore comes from experiments in which traditions are seeded, transplanted or removed.

### 46.5.2. Japanese Macaques and British Tits

In 1952 researchers began to provision a troop of Japanese macaques (*Macaca fuscata*) on the small island of Koshima with sweet potatoes on the beach. In 1953 a young female, Imo, about one and a half years old, was seen washing a sandy sweet potato in a stream before eating it. Her playmates and her mother acquired the habit, then other young monkeys and their mothers, and within ten years most of the troop washed potatoes, later in seawater, apparently also seasoning them with salt; adult males, who interacted little with juveniles, were the last to acquire it. In 1956 Imo invented *wheat placer-mining*: she threw handfuls of wheat mixed with sand into the water, where the sand sank and the grains floated and could be skimmed off. Both habits spread along lines of kinship and companionship, and they were passed to infants by their mothers.

The Koshima observations were long cited as a model case of animal culture and even of imitation. Later analyses were more cautious. Bennett Galef (1992) noted that the habit spread slowly, about one new monkey every two years in the first years, and at a roughly constant rate, whereas copying should accelerate as the number of potential models grows, like an epidemic; the provisioning by people may itself have encouraged washing. Since the processes that produce acquisition curves are now better understood, constant-rate spread is not decisive evidence against social learning, but the case shows how difficult it is to establish transmission from observational records.

In Britain, milk was delivered in bottles left on doorsteps, and from about 1921 blue tits (*Cyanistes caeruleus*) and great tits (*Parus major*) were reported to open the foil or cardboard caps and drink the cream. James Fisher and Robert Hinde (1949) showed that the habit spread from several centers across Britain and in other countries, faster than could be explained by independent invention, and probably by local enhancement around opened bottles. A field experiment on great tits near Oxford later demonstrated traditions directly: two birds trained to open a puzzle box by sliding a door to the left or to the right were released in each of several subpopulations, the technique spread along the birds' social network, each subpopulation adopted the variant of its demonstrators, the local variants persisted over two years, and immigrants switched to the local variant (Aplin and colleagues 2015; [7.18.27](/7.18.27)).

### 46.5.3. Chimpanzee Cultures

Andrew Whiten and colleagues (1999) compiled the behavior recorded during long-term studies at seven chimpanzee sites (later nine) across Africa and identified 39 behavior patterns that were customary or habitual at some sites and absent at others, where the absence could not be explained by ecology: forms of tool use (ant dipping with one or two hands, nut cracking with stone or wooden hammers, leaf sponging), grooming (the *grooming hand-clasp*, in which two chimpanzees raise and clasp hands overhead while grooming each other), courtship (*leaf clipping*, tearing a leaf noisily with the teeth) and others. Each community has a distinctive combination of such traditions, which met the stronger definition of culture. Similar surveys have found cultural variation in orangutans (van Schaik and colleagues 2003).

Critics pointed out that differences among sites might still reflect genetic differences or unmeasured ecological ones. Two kinds of evidence now support the cultural interpretation. First, *diffusion experiments*: Whiten, Horner and de Waal (2005) trained one female in each of two captive groups to retrieve food from an apparatus by a different method (lifting or poking); each method spread through the group in which it was seeded, and most chimpanzees that discovered the other method later reverted to the method of their group. Second, *field experiments*: given the same artificial "honey trap", chimpanzees of the Sonso community of Budongo, which do not use sticks, extracted honey with leaf sponges, whereas chimpanzees of the Kanyawara community in Kibale, which use sticks routinely, used sticks (Gruber and colleagues 2009). In Budongo, the spread of a new technique, moss sponging, between individuals followed the network of observation (Hobaiter and colleagues 2014). Genetic and ecological factors explain part of the variation among chimpanzee populations, but a substantial part is cultural.

### 46.5.4. Fishes, Whales and Songbirds

- *Fishes.* French grunts (*Haemulon flavolineatum*) school by day at traditional sites on coral reefs and migrate along traditional routes to feeding grounds at dusk. Fish transplanted to a new school adopted the residents' routes, whereas when residents were removed, newcomers released there did not use the old routes (Helfman and Schultz 1984). Mating sites of the bluehead wrasse (*Thalassoma bifasciatum*) remained in use for 12 years, about four generations, but when whole populations were replaced, the new fish chose new sites and then maintained them: the sites were traditions, not the best places (Warner 1988). Traditions can also be maladaptive: guppies that followed trained demonstrators along a long route to food continued to use it after the demonstrators were removed, although a short route was available (Laland and Williams 1998).
- *Whales.* All male humpback whales (*Megaptera novaeangliae*) in a population sing the same complex song, which changes continually. In the South Pacific, new song types have repeatedly arisen in the population off eastern Australia and spread eastward from population to population, reaching French Polynesia within about two years, in "cultural waves" of song (Garland and colleagues 2011). Killer whales (*Orcinus orca*) of the northeastern Pacific live in matrilineal groups with group-specific call dialects, and sympatric ecotypes that eat fish or mammals differ in foraging techniques, calls and social structure, differences thought to be maintained by cultural transmission; sperm whales belong to vocal clans defined by their codas. The sponging of bottlenose dolphins is passed from mother to offspring ([7.18.44](/7.18.44)).
- *Songbirds.* Song **dialects** ([7.18.17](/7.18.17)) are traditions maintained by vocal learning, and they evolve culturally. In white-throated sparrows (*Zonotrichia albicollis*), a variant of the song ending in a doublet originating in western Canada spread eastward over two decades, replacing the traditional triplet ending across most of the continent (Otter and colleagues 2020).
- *Migration routes.* Bighorn sheep and moose translocated to unfamiliar ranges did not migrate at first and took decades to learn to track the "green wave" of spring vegetation; migration knowledge accumulates across generations in a population (Jesmer and colleagues 2018). Young cranes and geese learn migration routes by following experienced birds ([7.18.20](/7.18.20)).

### 46.5.5. Imitation, Play and Cultural Behavior in Earlier Classifications

Older textbooks often listed *imitation learning*, *play learning* and the *cultural behavior of animals* as types of learning. The first corresponds to social learning in the broad sense defined here, which includes much that is not imitation; the experiments of Section 46.3 are needed to show that the form of an action is copied. Play ([7.18.41](/7.18.41#sec-41.6.2)) is not itself a mechanism of learning but a context in which young animals practice and discover skills, often in the presence of others, as when young sea otters learn to use anvil stones while playing near their mothers. Lists of cultural behavior in such books often included traditional migration routes, song dialects and the Koshima habits, which are indeed traditions, but also the return of salmon to their natal river, which depends on each fish's own olfactory imprinting ([7.18.21](/7.18.21)) and is not socially transmitted.

## 46.6. Models of Cultural Evolution

### 46.6.1. Conformist Bias

Cultural variants, like alleles, change in frequency in populations, and **cultural evolution** ([7.10.33](/7.10.33#sec-33.7)) can be modeled with the tools of population genetics. Its distinctive feature is that transmission itself can be biased: learners do not copy at random.

**Definition 46.6.1 (Conformist Bias)** **Conformist bias** (conformist transmission) is a disproportionate tendency to adopt the most common variant in a population: a learner adopts the majority variant with a probability greater than its frequency among the models.

Robert Boyd and Peter Richerson (1985) modeled conformity by supposing that each learner samples three models. With unbiased copying the frequency $p$ of a variant is unchanged; with conformity the learner adopts the variant held by the majority of its models more often than its frequency, and the frequency after transmission is

$$
p' = p + D\,p(1 - p)(2p - 1), \tag{46.1}
$$
where $0 \le D \le 1$ is the strength of conformity. The term $(2p - 1)$ is positive when the variant is in the majority and negative when it is in the minority, so conformity drives the majority variant to fixation (the continuous version is in [7.10.33](/7.10.33#sec-33.7)). Its most important consequence concerns groups: conformity maintains differences between groups despite migration, whereas unbiased copying lets migration homogenize them (figure below).

![Conformist transmission maintains differences between groups (Eq. (46.1)). Two groups exchange a fraction $m$ of their members each generation, after which transmission occurs within each group. A: frequencies of a variant in two groups starting at 0.9 and 0.1 with $m = 0.05$: with unbiased copying (dashed) the groups converge to 0.5; with conformity ($D = 0.5$) each group keeps its majority variant. B: the difference between the groups after 500 generations against the migration rate. Conformity of strength $D = 0.3$ maintains differences up to $m \approx 0.06$, and $D = 0.6$ up to $m \approx 0.09$; beyond these thresholds the groups merge.](./plots/conformist.py)

Conformity is adaptive when the environment varies in space: a migrant that adopts the local majority behavior acquires the behavior that is likely to be adapted to its new environment. In the great tit experiment, immigrants switched to the local variant of opening the puzzle box (Aplin and colleagues 2015). In vervet monkeys given dyed maize of two colors, one of which was made distasteful in each group, the preference for the locally favored color persisted after the distasteful taste was removed; infants born later ate the color their mothers ate, and males that migrated between groups switched to the local color (van de Waal, Borgeaud and Whiten 2013). Whether these are cases of conformity in the strict sense (a disproportionate tendency to copy the majority) or of copying in proportion to the majority is debated, and the distinction requires dose–response data on how the probability of adoption depends on the frequency of a variant; such data exist for sticklebacks, which show the sigmoid form expected from conformity (Pike and Laland 2010).

Conformity also has a role in human evolution: by keeping groups internally uniform and different from each other, it makes **group selection** among cultural groups much more effective than group selection on genes, an argument used to explain the scale of human cooperation (Richerson and Boyd 2005; [7.10.16](/7.10.16)).

### 46.6.2. Cumulative Culture

Human culture differs from animal traditions not only in its extent but in its history. Stone tools, boats, mathematics and medicine are the products of many innovations, each building on earlier ones, by individuals who could not have invented the whole by themselves.

**Definition 46.6.2 (Cumulative Culture)** **Cumulative culture** is the accumulation of modifications to a socially transmitted behavior or technology over successive generations, so that its later forms are more complex or more effective than any single individual could invent alone. Michael Tomasello called the process the *ratchet effect*: high-fidelity transmission keeps each improvement from slipping back until the next one is added.

Two conditions are needed: innovations must be made, and they must be transmitted faithfully enough not to be lost. Joseph Henrich (2004) made the role of transmission explicit in a model in which each learner copies the most skilled member of the previous generation, imperfectly: most copies are worse than the model, but a few, by chance, are better. The more learners there are, the more likely it is that one of them exceeds the model, so cumulative improvement depends on population size (figure below). Henrich used the model to explain the loss of technologies, such as bone tools, fishing gear and clothing, in Tasmania after it was isolated from mainland Australia by rising sea levels about 10 000 years ago: a population of a few thousand, cut off from the larger network of the mainland, may have been too small to maintain complex skills. The archaeological interpretation is debated, but laboratory experiments with human groups support the effect of group size and connectedness.

Is cumulative culture unique to humans? The candidates in animals are few and modest. The pandanus tools of New Caledonian crows come in three designs with different regional distributions, and the stepped design may have been derived from simpler forms (Hunt and Gray 2003), but this is inferred from geography rather than observed. Homing pigeons released in pairs, in which one member was replaced by a naive bird every few releases, improved the efficiency of their homing route over "generations" of birds beyond what individuals or fixed pairs achieved (Sasaki and Biro 2017). Humpback whale songs accumulate changes, but they are replaced rather than improved. In a direct comparison, groups of children, chimpanzees and capuchin monkeys were given a puzzle box that could be solved in three successive stages, each giving a better reward; only children reached the higher stages, and they did so with teaching, imitation and prosocial sharing of rewards, behaviors almost absent in the other species (Dean and colleagues 2012). The ratchet in its full form probably depends on the combination of faithful imitation, teaching and language that is characteristic of humans ([7.18.47](/7.18.47)).

## Summary

- Social influence includes social facilitation (no learning), local and stimulus enhancement (Definitions 46.1.1–46.1.2), observational conditioning, and copying; affordance learning, tested with ghost controls, is asocial.
- Social learning (Definition 46.2.1) transmits food preferences in rodents and public information in nine-spined sticklebacks. Rogers' paradox shows that indiscriminate copying does not raise mean fitness; critical social learning and strategies about when and whom to copy (uncertainty, payoff, majority, age) do.
- Imitation (Definition 46.3.1) is shown by two-action and bidirectional tests in birds, rats and primates, and by do-as-I-do training in chimpanzees, dogs and dolphins; emulation (Definition 46.3.2) copies results. Chimpanzees skip visibly irrelevant actions; children overimitate.
- Teaching (Definition 46.4.1) by the Caro–Hauser criteria is found in meerkats, tandem-running ants, pied babblers and, rarely, chimpanzees; it is favored by kin selection when information is hard to acquire otherwise.
- **Animal culture** (Definition 46.5.1) is shown by seeded diffusion and field experiments (great tits, chimpanzees, captive groups), by transplantation (French grunts, bluehead wrasse), by surveys of chimpanzee and orangutan communities, and in whale songs, killer whale dialects, songbird dialects and learned migration routes.
- Conformist bias (Definition 46.6.1, Eq. (46.1)) maintains differences between groups against migration (vervets, great tits, sticklebacks). Cumulative culture (Definition 46.6.2) depends on innovation and faithful transmission, and in Henrich's model on population size; it is weak or absent outside humans.

## Questions

1. A naive pigeon watches a demonstrator obtain food by pushing a sliding door to the left. Later the naive bird opens the door, sometimes to the left and sometimes to the right. What experiment would show whether it learned by local enhancement, affordance learning or imitation?

   **Answer** Use three observer groups. One watches a demonstrator push the door left, a second a demonstrator push it right (two-action test), and a third sees the door slide by itself (a ghost control, operated by a hidden mechanism) to the left. If both demonstrator groups open the door more than naive controls but with no bias in direction, local or stimulus enhancement explains the result. If the ghost group learns as quickly and in the same direction as the demonstrator groups, affordance learning suffices. Imitation is shown if observers preferentially push in the direction they saw the demonstrator push, more strongly than the ghost-control observers.
2. In Rogers' model with $u = 0.1$ and $c = 0.2$, find the equilibrium proportion of social learners. Why is mean fitness unchanged?

   **Answer** At equilibrium the fitness of social learners equals that of individual learners: $b(1 - u)q = b - c$, so $q = (1 - c)/(1 - u) = 0.8/0.9 = 0.889$. Then $(1 - p)/(1 - 0.9p) = 0.889$, so $1 - p = 0.889 - 0.8p$ and $p = 0.111/0.2 = 0.56$. Mean fitness is $p W_S + (1 - p) W_I$, and since $W_S = W_I = 1 + b - c$ at equilibrium, it equals the fitness of a population of individual learners alone: social learning increases in frequency because it pays when rare, but at equilibrium it merely redistributes the same fitness.
3. Why does the transparent version of Horner and Whiten's puzzle box distinguish imitation from emulation, and what does children's overimitation suggest about human cultural learning?

   **Answer** With the opaque box, observers cannot see which actions cause the reward, so copying all of them is reasonable for an emulator too. With the transparent box, the irrelevance of some actions is visible: an emulator focused on the result should skip them, while a faithful imitator copies them. Chimpanzees skipped them; children did not. Overimitation suggests that human children treat demonstrated actions as conventions or as having reasons they cannot see, and that humans are adapted to learn faithfully from others in a world full of artifacts and rituals whose causal structure is opaque; faithful copying preserves such knowledge and supports cumulative culture.
4. Explain how the Caro–Hauser criteria were tested in meerkats and why the meerkats' teaching need not involve understanding the pupils' ignorance.

   **Answer** Criterion 1: adults modify the prey they provide according to the pups' age (dead or disabled scorpions for young pups, intact ones for older), and the playback experiment showed that they respond to the age-specific begging calls. Criterion 2: adults lose time and food by disabling prey and giving it away, and by monitoring and re-presenting it. Criterion 3: pups given live, stingless scorpions handled scorpions better later than control pups given dead scorpions or egg. Because the adults respond to the begging call, a cue correlated with the pups' age, rather than to the pups' observed competence, the behavior can be explained by a simple rule without any representation of what the pups know.
5. Using Eq. (46.1) with $D = 0.5$, compute the frequency after transmission of a variant at $p = 0.6$ and at $p = 0.4$. How does conformity interact with migration?

   **Answer** At $p = 0.6$: $p' = 0.6 + 0.5 \cdot 0.6 \cdot 0.4 \cdot 0.2 = 0.6 + 0.024 = 0.624$. At $p = 0.4$: $p' = 0.4 + 0.5 \cdot 0.4 \cdot 0.6 \cdot (-0.2) = 0.376$. Conformity pushes each group toward its majority variant. Migration brings the frequencies of two groups together; if conformity is strong relative to migration, it restores the majority variant in each group faster than migration erodes it, and the groups remain different (figure in Section 46.6.1); if migration is too strong, the groups merge.
6. In Henrich's model with $\alpha = 1$ and $\beta = 0.25$, what is the expected change in skill per generation for $N = 10$ and $N = 1000$? What happens if the fidelity of copying improves so that $\alpha = 0.5$?

   **Answer** $\Delta\bar z = -1 + 0.25(0.577 + \ln N)$. For $N = 10$: $-1 + 0.25(0.577 + 2.303) = -0.28$, a loss. For $N = 1000$: $-1 + 0.25(0.577 + 6.908) = 0.87$, a gain. With $\alpha = 0.5$, $N^* = \mathrm{e}^{0.5/0.25 - 0.577} = \mathrm{e}^{1.423} \approx 4.2$, so even small populations of more than about four learners accumulate skill: better transmission fidelity, by imitation, teaching or language, reduces the population size needed for cumulative culture.
7. (Open question) Chimpanzee communities differ in dozens of behaviors, but chimpanzee technology shows little cumulative change over thousands of years (nut-cracking stones 4300 years old resemble today's). Discuss which factors might limit cumulative culture in chimpanzees and how they could be tested.

   **Answer** Possible limits: lower transmission fidelity (emulation rather than imitation, so details and improvements are lost); rarity of teaching; small and weakly connected social networks (communities of 20–150 animals with limited migration, mostly of females); conservatism, in that individuals who have mastered a technique rarely switch to a better one (in some experiments chimpanzees did not adopt a more rewarding method); and lack of language to explain causal relations. Tests include transmission-chain experiments measuring how faithfully tool techniques pass along chains of individuals, experiments offering a better variant to groups that already have a working one, network-based diffusion analysis of innovations in the wild, and comparisons of communities of different size and connectivity. The relative weight of these factors is unknown, and some recent work suggests that chimpanzees can acquire behaviors too complex to invent alone when demonstrations are available, so the difference may be one of degree.
