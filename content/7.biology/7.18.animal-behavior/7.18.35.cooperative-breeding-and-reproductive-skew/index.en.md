In about one in ten species of birds, and in a few percent of mammals and a handful of fishes, the young do not all leave home to breed as soon as they can. Some stay with their parents for one or several years and help them rear further broods: they feed nestlings, guard pups, dig burrows and drive off predators and intruders. Others join groups of unrelated breeders and help there. Such *cooperative breeders* include the Florida scrub-jay, the meerkat, the naked mole-rat, the superb fairy-wren and the cichlid *Neolamprologus pulcher*, some of the most intensively studied animals in behavioral ecology. They raise two questions that must be answered separately: why do individuals delay dispersal and independent breeding, and why, having stayed, do they help?

This chapter applies to these questions the tools of the two preceding chapters: **kin selection** and **Hamilton's rule** ([7.18.33](/7.18.33)) and the direct benefits of cooperation, including group augmentation and enforcement ([7.18.34](/7.18.34)). It then turns to the other side of group life, the conflict over who breeds, and to models of how reproduction is shared within groups. The decision to disperse builds on [7.18.26](/7.18.26), the trade-offs of parental care on [7.18.31](/7.18.31) and [7.18.32](/7.18.32); the comparison with the permanently sterile workers of social insects is completed in [7.18.36](/7.18.36).

## 35.1. Forms and Distribution

**Definition 35.1.1 (Cooperative Breeding; Helper)** **Cooperative breeding** is a breeding system in which individuals other than the genetic parents provide care for the young. The nonbreeding individuals that provide care are **helpers** (or alloparents); in most species they are physiologically capable of breeding but do not breed, or breed only occasionally, while they help.

:::

Cooperative breeding occurs in about 9% of bird species (Cockburn 2006), in a few percent of mammal species, mainly carnivores, rodents and primates (Lukas and Clutton-Brock 2012), and in some fishes and insects. It differs from **eusociality** ([7.18.36](/7.18.36)) in that helpers keep the ability to reproduce and usually do so later, so that helping is a phase of life rather than a caste. Groups take two main forms:

- *Family groups* (singular breeders): a single dominant pair breeds, and the helpers are mostly its offspring from previous years that have delayed dispersal. Examples are the Florida scrub-jay (*Aphelocoma coerulescens*), the long-tailed tit, the Seychelles warbler, the meerkat, wolves and the naked mole-rat. In birds, where females usually disperse farther than males, most helpers are sons; in mammals, where males disperse, most helpers are daughters ([7.18.26](/7.18.26)).
- *Joint-nesting and plural-breeding groups*: several females breed in the same group, and the group may include unrelated adults. Acorn woodpeckers (*Melanerpes formicivorus*) store acorns in communal granaries and breed in groups in which several related males and several related females share a nest; groove-billed anis (*Crotophaga sulcirostris*) lay in a common nest; in banded mongooses (*Mungos mungo*) most adult females of a group breed, synchronously. Across cooperatively breeding birds, about 55% of species breed in family groups, 30% in mixed groups of relatives and nonrelatives, and 15% largely with nonrelatives (Riehl 2013).

The two questions, why stay and why help, can have different answers, and sometimes only one arises. Siberian jays (*Perisoreus infaustus*) live in family groups with retained offspring that do not help at the nest at all (Section 35.6.2), while in white-fronted bee-eaters (*Merops bullockoides*) individuals that live in their parents' group may help at a different nest of the same clan.

## 35.2. Why Stay? Constraints on Independent Breeding

### 35.2.1. Habitat Saturation and Ecological Constraints

**Definition 35.2.1 (Ecological Constraints Hypothesis)** The **ecological constraints hypothesis** (Emlen 1982) states that individuals delay dispersal and stay in their natal group when the resources needed for successful independent breeding, typically territories of adequate quality or mates, are limited, so that the expected success of dispersing is low.

:::

Territories are often limiting because all suitable habitat is already occupied (habitat saturation), a common situation in long-lived, sedentary species in which territories rarely fall vacant. In the language of Hamilton's rule, constraints lower the cost $C$ of not breeding: if an individual has little chance of breeding anyway, it loses little by staying, and even a small benefit from helping can tip the balance ([7.18.33](/7.18.33#sec-33.2.2)). The complementary *benefits-of-philopatry hypothesis* (Stacey and Ligon 1987) emphasizes the advantages of staying in a good natal territory: protection, access to food and the chance of inheriting the territory. The two hypotheses are two sides of the same comparison, between the expected payoffs of dispersing and of staying, compared in the figure below.

Staying is favored when territories are scarce, and the threshold moves to scarcer territories when the siblings would be half siblings (r = 1/4) rather than full siblings (r = 1/2), for instance after a parent has been replaced. The Seychelles warbler (*Acrocephalus sechellensis*) provided an experimental test. By the 1960s the species survived only on Cousin Island, about 30 ha, where a conservation program allowed the population to grow from about 26 birds in 1959 to some 300 by the early 1980s. Helping at the nest, which had not been recorded before, appeared in 1973, when all the territories on the island were occupied. Jan Komdeur (1992) then moved 58 birds from Cousin to the neighboring islands of Aride and Cousine, which had no warblers. The vacancies created on Cousin were filled at once, some within hours, by helpers from other territories, and the birds moved to the empty islands all bred independently as pairs, without helpers. Only when the new populations had saturated their islands did helping appear there too (Komdeur and colleagues 1995).

![Habitat saturation and helping, an individual-based simulation loosely modeled on the Seychelles warbler. An island has 110 territories with one breeding pair each; breeders and nonbreeders die at constant annual rates, vacant breeding positions are filled by surviving nonbreeders, each pair raises recruits at a rate that increases slightly with the number of helpers and falls with the total population (food limitation), and recruits that find no vacancy stay on their natal territory as helpers. Helpers appear only when all breeding positions are filled. In year 30 the breeders of 29 territories (58 birds) are removed: the vacancies are filled immediately by helpers, whose number drops almost to zero and then recovers as the island saturates again](./plots/habitat-saturation.py)

Komdeur also found that the decision depends on the quality of the natal territory, measured by its area, vegetation cover and insect food. Young birds on high-quality territories often stayed, survived well and raised their parents' success; those on poor territories, where help made little difference, tended to leave and try to breed elsewhere. Helpers were also more likely to leave when one or both of the parents they had been helping had died and been replaced (Eikenaar and colleagues 2007), so that the young they would help were no longer full siblings, which lowers the indirect benefit of staying (panel A above).

### 35.2.2. Life History, Environment and Mating System

Habitat saturation alone cannot explain why cooperative breeding occurs in some lineages and not in others, since many noncooperative species also live in saturated habitats. Comparative analyses have identified three further correlates.

*Life history.* Across bird families, cooperative breeding is concentrated in lineages with high annual adult survival and sedentary habits (Arnold and Owens 1998): long-lived adults rarely free territories, which creates a surplus of mature nonbreeders, the *life history hypothesis*. Life history may thus predispose lineages to cooperation, with local ecology determining when helping is expressed.

*Harsh and unpredictable environments.* Cooperative breeders are disproportionately common in arid and semiarid regions with low and variable rainfall, such as the African savannas and the Australian interior. Among the 45 species of African starlings, cooperatively breeding species live in environments with more variable rainfall than noncooperative ones (Rubenstein and Lovette 2007), and the association holds across birds worldwide (Jetz and Rubenstein 2011) and across mammals (Lukas and Clutton-Brock 2017). In such environments helpers increase breeding success mainly in poor years. Helpers may also reduce the variance of reproductive success between years, a form of **bet hedging** (Rubenstein 2011), which is favored because long-term fitness depends on the geometric mean of success across years ([7.10.15](/7.10.15)).

*Monogamy.* Indirect benefits are greater when the young that helpers rear are their full siblings, which requires their mother to have mated with one male. Across 267 bird species, cooperative breeding was more common in species with low rates of female promiscuity, and among cooperative breeders helpers were present at fewer nests in more promiscuous species (Cornwallis and colleagues 2010). In mammals, cooperative breeding has evolved only from socially monogamous ancestors (Lukas and Clutton-Brock 2012). This parallels the role of lifetime monogamy in the origin of eusociality ([7.18.36](/7.18.36)).

## 35.3. Why Help? Benefits to Helpers

### 35.3.1. Indirect Benefits

The long-tailed tit (*Aegithalos caudatus*), studied by Ben Hatchwell and colleagues near Sheffield, is the clearest case of helping explained by kin selection. In winter the birds live in flocks of about 16 that include relatives and immigrants. In spring every bird first attempts to breed in a monogamous pair; many nests are destroyed by predators, and some of the failed breeders, instead of trying again, help to feed the young at another nest. Broods with more helpers produced more young that survived to the next year. Of 52 helpers observed, 79% helped close relatives (Russell and Hatchwell 2001), and when a failed breeder lived between two active nests, one of relatives and one of nonrelatives, it went to the relatives in 16 of 17 cases. Helpers recognize kin by learned churr calls ([7.18.33](/7.18.33#sec-33.4.2)). Direct benefits seem unimportant: helpers rarely survive to breed successfully in later seasons, so that individuals gain fitness either by breeding or by helping, rarely both (MacColl and Hatchwell 2004).

White-fronted bee-eaters, which nest in colonies in clay banks in Kenya and live in extended families called clans, show fine kin discrimination. Young birds can breed, help or sit out a season. Among the nests of their clan, helpers choose those of their closest relatives (Emlen and Wrege 1988). Fathers even harass sons that try to breed, disrupting their courtship and nest building until the sons return to help at the parental nest, "redirected helping" (Emlen and Wrege 1992). A young female courted by an older, dominant male with helpers usually pairs and leaves; one offered only a young male without helpers refuses and stays, and helps only if the breeding pair at her natal nest are her own parents (Emlen and colleagues 1995).

**Example 35.3.1 (Inclusive Fitness of Pied Kingfisher Helpers)** Pied kingfishers (*Ceryle rudis*) nest in burrows on the shores of Lake Victoria and Lake Naivasha. Year-old males unable to find a mate adopt one of three tactics (Reyer 1984): *primary helpers* bring fish to their own parents and siblings; *secondary helpers* help unrelated breeding pairs; *delayers* neither help nor breed. Primary helpers work hardest and survive least well into the next year. Reyer's data give, for the first year (indirect fitness, $y\,r$, from the extra young $y$ produced by the helped parents) and the second year (direct fitness, $O\,r\,s\,m$, with $O = 2.5$ offspring per breeding pair, $r = \tfrac12$ to own offspring, survival $s$ and probability $m$ of finding a mate):

| Tactic | Year 1: $y \times r$ | Year 2: $O \times r \times s \times m$ | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| Primary helper | $1.8 \times 0.32 = 0.58$ | $2.5 \times 0.5 \times 0.54 \times 0.60 = 0.41$ | 0.99 |
| Secondary helper | $1.3 \times 0 = 0$ | $2.5 \times 0.5 \times 0.74 \times 0.91 = 0.84$ | 0.84 |
| Delayer | $0.3 \times 0 = 0$ | $2.5 \times 0.5 \times 0.70 \times 0.33 = 0.29$ | 0.29 |

The relatedness of primary helpers to the young they help is $0.32$ rather than $\tfrac12$ because some helped a parent that had remated, so that the young were half siblings. Primary helpers pay a direct cost in the second year, $0.84 - 0.41 = 0.43$ offspring equivalents relative to secondary helpers, and recover it through indirect fitness, $0.58 > 0.43$: Hamilton's rule is satisfied. Secondary helpers help for a direct benefit: 10 of 27 later paired with the female they had helped.

:::

The two kinds of helper in one species thus illustrate the two classes of explanation, indirect and direct. Kin selection also predicts that helpers should help more when they are more closely related to the young, and a meta-analysis of cooperatively breeding birds found that they do so mainly in species in which helping has a large effect on the success of the brood (Griffin and West 2003).

### 35.3.2. Direct Benefits

Helpers gain direct fitness in several ways, which need not exclude indirect benefits.

- *Territory inheritance.* A helper may inherit its natal territory, or a part of it, when a breeder dies. Florida scrub-jay helpers, mostly males, sometimes obtain a territory by "budding" off a part of the family territory, which the group's help in defense allows them to hold. Across 44 cooperatively breeding bird species, helpers provisioned young more in species in which helpers often inherit the breeding position, and where inheritance was unlikely, helpers directed their help mainly to related young (Kingma 2017).
- *Group augmentation.* Helping increases the size of the group, which improves the survival and future breeding prospects of all its members ([7.18.34](/7.18.34#sec-34.3.2)). In meerkats, females, which are more likely than males to stay and breed in their natal group, help more.
- *Pay-to-stay.* Breeders may tolerate subordinates only if they help (Gaston 1978). In the cichlid *Neolamprologus pulcher* of Lake Tanganyika, groups consist of a breeding pair and up to about 20 helpers, many of them unrelated, which clean and defend the eggs and fry and dig out the shelter. Helpers experimentally prevented from helping were attacked by the breeders more often, and after their return they helped more than control helpers, as if paying arrears (Balshine-Earn and colleagues 1998; Bergmüller and Taborsky 2005). Staying in the group gives helpers protection from the abundant predators.
- *Shared parentage.* In many cooperative breeders subordinates obtain some reproduction. In the Seychelles warbler, female helpers often lay eggs in the group's nest and male helpers sometimes father young (Section 35.6.1). In superb starlings (*Lamprotornis superbus*), females breeding for the first time, which have no offspring from previous years to help them, mate with males of their own group that then help feed the young, some of which they fathered (Rubenstein 2007).
- *Experience.* Helpers may learn skills that improve their later success as breeders; this hypothesis has received little consistent support.

### 35.3.3. Benefits to Breeders

The breeders also gain, and their benefit need not appear as larger broods. In many species the presence of helpers allows parents to reduce their own effort, *load lightening*, which improves their survival. Superb fairy-wren mothers with helpers lay smaller eggs and survive better, so that the effect of helpers on chick mass is concealed ([7.18.34](/7.18.34#sec-34.3.2); Russell and colleagues 2007); helpers then gain indirect fitness through their mother's future reproduction rather than through the current brood (Cockburn and colleagues 2008).

## 35.4. The Costs of Helping

Helping is costly. Meerkat babysitters lose body mass while their foraging group mates gain it, and the most active helpers lose up to 11% of their mass during a breeding attempt ([7.18.34](/7.18.34#sec-34.3.2)); meerkats that have helped heavily grow more slowly, and dominant females in the group may reduce their own effort when helpers are numerous. Pied kingfisher primary helpers survived to the next season with probability 0.54, against 0.74 for secondary helpers and 0.70 for delayers. The costs vary with the condition of the helper: in meerkats, individuals in good condition, or given supplementary food, help more, which shows that help is adjusted to what the helper can afford (Clutton-Brock and colleagues 2002). Costs are also paid in delayed breeding: a helper that stays for several years forgoes those years of possible reproduction, and its lifetime success depends on whether it eventually inherits or obtains a breeding position.

## 35.5. Reproductive Conflict

### 35.5.1. Reproductive Suppression

Helpers are not sterile, and their interests in breeding conflict with those of the dominants. Hamilton's rule predicts conflict whenever relatedness is less than 1 ([7.18.33](/7.18.33)).

**Definition 35.5.1 (Reproductive Suppression)** **Reproductive suppression** is the inhibition of breeding in subordinate members of a social group, imposed by dominants through aggression, physiological stress, eviction or the killing of subordinates' offspring, or adopted by subordinates themselves when breeding would provoke such costs (reproductive restraint).

:::

The naked mole-rat (*Heterocephalus glaber*) of the Horn of Africa lives in colonies of about 70 and up to about 300 individuals in large burrow systems, which the workers dig cooperatively in search of tubers. Only one female, the queen, and one to three males breed. Nonbreeding females do not ovulate: their levels of luteinizing hormone are low and their pituitaries respond weakly to gonadotropin-releasing hormone. The suppression is maintained by the queen's behavior, which includes frequent shoving of other colony members, rather than by a pheromone, and it is reversible: when the queen dies or is removed, several females become reproductively active and fight, sometimes to the death, for the position (Faulkes and Abbott 1993; Sherman and colleagues 1991). Because it has a functionally sterile caste maintained by a single breeding female, the naked mole-rat is often called eusocial ([7.18.36](/7.18.36#sec-36.10)).

In meerkats, dominant females become aggressive toward some subordinate females about a month before giving birth and drive them out of the group until the litter is born. The aggression is directed at the subordinates that are pregnant or most likely to become so, which are older and less closely related to the dominant (Young and colleagues 2006). Evicted females live alone or with other evictees, have higher levels of glucocorticoids, abort more often, conceive less often and lose weight. The eviction protects the dominant's litter, since pregnant subordinates that remain in the group often kill and eat the dominant's newborn pups: the survival of a dominant's litter was roughly halved if a subordinate was pregnant when it was born (Young and Clutton-Brock 2006). When subordinates were given contraceptive injections, dominant females attacked them less, evicted them less, gained more weight during pregnancy and produced heavier pups, because more helpers were available and none competed (Bell and colleagues 2014).

Banded mongooses differ: most females of a group give birth on the same day, the pups are raised communally, and reproductive skew among females is low. Dominant females nevertheless limit subordinate breeding by evicting several subordinates at once when groups become large, and by killing litters that are born out of synchrony with their own; females that give birth in synchrony make it impossible for the dominants to recognize and kill their pups (Cant and colleagues 2010, 2014). In common marmosets (*Callithrix jacchus*), subordinate females usually do not ovulate while they live with a dominant breeding female, a suppression that combines the dominant's behavior and cues and the subordinates' restraint. In gray wolves, packs usually have a single breeding pair, but subordinate females are not physiologically suppressed: most are daughters of the breeders, and breeding by subordinates depends mainly on the presence of unrelated males, that is, on inbreeding avoidance (vonHoldt and colleagues 2008). Dominants of acorn woodpeckers and other joint-nesting birds destroy the eggs of the females with which they share a nest until both begin to lay (Mumme and colleagues 1983). Some subordinates restrain themselves: in coral-dwelling gobies, subordinates stay small rather than risk eviction ([7.18.27](/7.18.27#sec-27.5.2)).

### 35.5.2. Reproductive Skew

**Definition 35.5.2 (Reproductive Skew)** **Reproductive skew** is the degree to which reproduction in a social group is unequally distributed among its members of the same sex: skew is high when one individual monopolizes breeding (*singular breeding*) and low when several members breed equally (*plural breeding*).

:::

Skew can be measured, for example, by the effective number of breeders $N_{\mathrm{e}} = 1/\sum_i p_i^2$, where $p_i$ is the share of the group's offspring produced by individual $i$; the index $(N - N_{\mathrm{e}})/(N - 1)$ for a group of $N$ potential breeders is 0 when all breed equally and 1 when one breeds alone. Meerkats are singular breeders with high skew, banded mongooses plural breeders with low skew. Skew affects the kin structure of groups: the more breeders, the less related their offspring are to each other, just as multiple mating by a queen reduces the relatedness of her workers.

*Skew theory* tries to explain variation in skew within and between species by the bargaining between dominants and subordinates. Two families of models make different assumptions about control.

**Formula 35.5.1 (Staying Incentive in the Concession Model)** In a group of a dominant and a subordinate related by $r$, let the dominant breeding alone produce 1 unit of offspring, the subordinate breeding alone $x$, and the group together $k > 1$. If the dominant controls reproduction and concedes a share $p$ of the group's output to the subordinate, the subordinate stays if $pk + r(1 - p)k \geq x + r$, so the smallest share that keeps it in the group is

$$
p_{\mathrm{s}} = \max\!\left(0,\; \frac{x - r(k - 1)}{k(1 - r)}\right).
$$

:::

In *concession* (transactional) models (Vehrencamp 1983; Reeve and Ratnieks 1993), the dominant has complete control and concedes just this "staying incentive". The model predicts that skew is high (the subordinate's share small) when the partners are closely related, since a related subordinate gains indirectly from the dominant's reproduction; when ecological constraints are strong ($x$ small), since the subordinate has little to gain by leaving; and when the group is much more productive than solitary breeders ($k$ large). A share may also be conceded to prevent a fight (a "peace incentive"). In *tug-of-war* models (Reeve, Emlen and Keller 1998), neither party has full control; each invests part of the group's resources in competition for reproduction, which reduces the group's output, and the outcome depends on their relative competitive abilities. These models predict that skew depends mainly on the asymmetry in strength between the partners and that relatedness reduces the wasted competitive effort rather than changing the shares. *Restraint* models (Johnstone and Cant 1999) give control of their own reproduction to subordinates, limited by the dominant's threat of eviction, as in gobies and banded mongooses.

![Two models of reproductive skew. A, B: the concession model (Formula 35.5.1) with group productivity k = 1.5. A: the staying incentive, the share the dominant must concede to keep a subordinate, against relatedness for three values of the subordinate's solitary output x: concessions fall with relatedness and vanish when the subordinate's indirect gain from staying exceeds what it could get alone. B: the staying incentive against x for unrelated and related partners: the weaker the subordinate's options outside the group (stronger ecological constraints), the higher the skew. C: a tug-of-war model in which each party invests a fraction y of the group's output in competition and the subordinate's share is y_s/(a y_d + y_s), a being the dominant's competitive advantage. At the Nash equilibrium the subordinate's share (solid) depends mainly on a, while the fraction of output wasted in competition (dashed) falls steeply with relatedness](./plots/skew-models.py)

Tests of skew models have given mixed results (Nonacs and Hager 2011). Some predictions of concession models hold in some species, for example lower skew when subordinates are less related in some social wasps, but many studies find no relation between skew and relatedness or ecological constraints, and the assumption of complete control by dominants is often unrealistic. The models are most useful as a framework for asking who controls reproduction and what each party would gain by leaving or fighting.

### 35.5.3. Extra-pair Paternity and Social Structure

Mating outside the social pair changes both conflict and kin structure. In the Seychelles warbler, about 40% of offspring are sired by males from other groups (Richardson and colleagues 2002), so that helpers are usually half siblings of the young they rear. Promiscuity lowers the indirect benefits of helping and, across birds, is associated with less cooperation (Section 35.2.2). Conversely, the structure of a group affects mating: subordinate males in many cooperative breeders gain matings within the group, and their help may be partly paternal care.

### 35.5.4. Competition among Females and Female Ornaments

When breeding positions are few and valuable, females compete for them as intensely as males, and traits that improve competitive ability are favored in both sexes. Among African starlings, cooperatively breeding species have less sexual dimorphism in plumage and body size than noncooperative species, with females more ornamented and with relatively longer wings (Rubenstein and Lovette 2009); the variance in wing length, a measure of the intensity of selection, was similar between the sexes in cooperative species and greater in females of noncooperative ones. Across nearly all passerine birds, females of cooperatively breeding species are more colorful (Dale and colleagues 2015). In meerkats, the traits that make a female more competitive, such as body mass and age, have a stronger effect on her breeding success than the equivalent traits have in males (Clutton-Brock and colleagues 2006). Such cases extend **sexual selection** ([7.18.28](/7.18.28)) to competition among females over breeding opportunities rather than mates.

## 35.6. Case Studies

### 35.6.1. The Seychelles Warbler

Long-term studies can reverse earlier conclusions. Early work on the Seychelles warbler suggested a simple story: helpers were offspring of the territory owners, assumed to be helping full siblings ($r = \tfrac12$), and habitat saturation lowered the cost of helping. Genetic analysis with microsatellites changed it (Richardson and colleagues 2002). Because of extra-group paternity, the average relatedness between helpers and the young they fed was only about 0.13; female helpers laid eggs in the group nest, producing on average 0.46 offspring per year, and male helpers fathered 0.14:

| | Female helper | Male helper |
|---|---|---|
| Direct fitness (own offspring per year) | 0.46 | 0.14 |
| Indirect fitness (through the breeders' young) | 0.07 | 0.04 |

Direct benefits therefore far exceed indirect ones. Yet kin selection still shapes helping: female subordinates fed young at higher rates the more closely they were related to them, while males did not, and the cue used by females appears to be whether the breeding female is the one that raised them, a reliable cue of maternal relatedness, whereas the presence of the male that raised them is not used, appropriately, given the high rate of extra-pair paternity (Richardson and colleagues 2003). Breeding females in turn adjust the sex of their eggs: on high-quality territories, where daughters, the helping sex, increase the parents' success, they produce mostly daughters, and on poor territories mostly sons ([7.10.13](/7.10.13#sec-13.3.3)).

### 35.6.2. The Siberian Jay

Siberian jays of the boreal forests of Fennoscandia live in groups of a breeding pair and one to four retained offspring and immigrants, yet no bird other than the parents feeds the nestlings. Retained offspring benefit from staying: parents tolerate them at food and protect them, and they survive better than offspring that disperse early. The parents' alarm calls show the nepotism. When Griesser and Ekman (2004) presented a model hawk, breeding females called more often when they were accompanied by their own retained offspring than by unrelated immigrants, while breeding males called indiscriminately; playback showed that the calls inform the listeners of danger and make them escape. When a predator such as an owl is found in the territory, group members mob it with calls and swoops; in groups of relatives, males gave many more mobbing calls than in groups of nonrelatives (Griesser and Ekman 2005). Family living in this species thus brings indirect benefits through the protection of kin, without any helping at the nest, and shows that delayed dispersal can evolve before, or without, cooperative care of the young.

## Summary

- Cooperative breeding (Definition 35.1.1) occurs in about 9% of birds and a few percent of mammals; most groups are families of a breeding pair and its retained offspring (singular breeders), others plural-breeding groups that include nonrelatives.
- Delayed dispersal is explained by ecological constraints (Definition 35.2.1), habitat saturation and the benefits of philopatry: Seychelles warbler helpers bred at once when vacancies were created or empty islands offered. Cooperative breeders tend to be long-lived, to live in variable, harsh environments and to be monogamous.

| Benefit of helping | Mechanism | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Indirect | Raising relatives' young | Long-tailed tits (79% help kin), bee-eaters, pied kingfisher primary helpers (Example 35.3.1) |
| Territory inheritance | Breeding position later | Florida scrub-jays (budding); comparative data across 44 species |
| Group augmentation | Larger, safer groups | Meerkats |
| Pay-to-stay | Help as the price of tolerance | *Neolamprologus pulcher* |
| Shared parentage | Breeding within the group | Seychelles warbler females (0.46 offspring per year), superb starlings |
| Load lightening (breeders) | Parents reduce effort, survive better | Superb fairy-wrens |

- Helping is costly in body mass, survival and delayed breeding, and help is adjusted to condition.
- Dominants suppress subordinate breeding (Definition 35.5.1) by aggression and stress (naked mole-rats, marmosets), eviction and infanticide (meerkats, banded mongooses) or egg destruction (acorn woodpeckers); in wolves, breeding is limited mainly by inbreeding avoidance.
- Reproductive skew (Definition 35.5.2) is explained by concession models (dominant control, staying incentive of Formula 35.5.1), tug-of-war models (incomplete control) and restraint models; empirical support is mixed.
- Extra-pair paternity lowers indirect benefits; competition for breeding positions favors ornaments and competitive traits in females of cooperative species.
- Long-term studies (Seychelles warbler) show that direct and indirect benefits combine, and Siberian jays show that family living can be favored without helping at the nest.

## Questions

1. Using Formula 33.2.1, explain why a helper whose mother has been replaced by a stepmother should be more likely to leave, and test the prediction with the model in panel A of the first figure.

   **Answer** If her mother has died and her father has remated, the young are half siblings ($r = \tfrac14$) instead of full siblings ($r = \tfrac12$); each extra sibling is worth half as much in indirect fitness, so a helper needs twice as many extra young per own offspring forgone ($B/C > 2$ rather than $> 1$). In the model, the inclusive fitness of staying falls from $0.5 \times 0.8 + 0.3 = 0.70$ to $0.25 \times 0.8 + 0.3 = 0.50$, and dispersing becomes better when the probability of finding a territory exceeds about 0.36 rather than 0.50. Thus at intermediate availability of territories, a helper with a stepparent should leave while one with both parents should stay, which is what Seychelles warbler helpers do.

2. Komdeur's translocation experiment showed that helpers become breeders when vacancies appear. Why does this not by itself show that helping, as opposed to staying, is adaptive?

   **Answer** The experiment shows that young birds stay because they cannot obtain territories (the ecological constraint on dispersal), and that they prefer to breed when they can. It says nothing about why, having stayed, they feed the young: they might stay and help because helping raises their indirect fitness, because it is the price of staying or improves their chance of inheritance, or they could stay without helping, as Siberian jays do. To test the adaptive value of helping itself, one must measure its effect on the success of the breeders (for example by removing helpers), the relatedness of helpers to the young, and any direct benefits such as parentage or inheritance.

3. In the concession model with $k = 1.5$ and $x = 0.2$, calculate the staying incentive for an unrelated subordinate and for a full sibling. At what relatedness does the incentive vanish?

   **Answer** For $r = 0$: $p_{\mathrm{s}} = 0.2/1.5 \approx 0.13$, so the dominant must concede about 13% of the group's output. For $r = \tfrac12$: the numerator is $0.2 - 0.5 \times 0.5 = -0.05 < 0$, so $p_{\mathrm{s}} = 0$: a full sibling gains more indirectly from staying than it could get breeding alone, and the dominant need concede nothing. The incentive vanishes when $x = r(k - 1)$, i.e. $r = 0.2/0.5 = 0.4$. The model thus predicts complete skew in groups of close relatives when solitary breeding is poor, and some sharing in groups of nonrelatives.

4. Why do dominant female meerkats evict pregnant subordinates, and why is eviction directed at older, less related females?

   **Answer** A pregnant subordinate in the group threatens the dominant's own litter, since subordinates often kill the dominant's newborn pups, and her pups would compete with the dominant's for the care of helpers. Evicting her before the dominant gives birth removes both threats; it also raises the subordinate's stress hormones and makes her abort or fail to conceive. Older females are more likely to conceive, and less related females gain less indirect fitness from the dominant's pups, so they are both more likely to breed and more likely to harm the dominant's litter; the dominant also loses less indirect fitness by harming a less related female's reproduction. Eviction is costly to the dominant too (fewer helpers while the subordinates are away), so it is targeted at the most threatening individuals and lasts only around the time of birth.

5. Compare the reasons why naked mole-rats and gray wolves have only one breeding female per group.

   **Answer** In naked mole-rats, subordinate females are physiologically suppressed: they do not ovulate, because the queen's behavior keeps their reproductive hormones low, and they start to breed only when the queen is gone; the restriction is imposed by the dominant. In gray wolves, subordinate females are not physiologically suppressed, but most are daughters of the breeding pair and would have to mate with their father or brothers; they breed when unrelated males join the pack, so that single breeding mainly reflects inbreeding avoidance, with some aggression by the dominant female. The same pattern of reproductive skew can therefore result from dominant control in one species and from the lack of suitable mates in another.
