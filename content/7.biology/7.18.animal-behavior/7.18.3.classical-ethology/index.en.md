The ethologists of the 1930s to 1950s, above all Konrad Lorenz and Niko Tinbergen, built a set of concepts for the analysis of behavior that is still in use. They noticed that much of the behavior of animals in nature consists of stereotyped, species-typical movements that appear in full form without practice, are triggered by a few simple features of the environment, and are performed more or less readily depending on the animal's internal state. From these observations they derived the concepts of the fixed action pattern, the sign stimulus and the releasing mechanism, and a theory of motivation. They tested their ideas with dummies, models that reproduce or exaggerate single features of a natural stimulus, an experimental method that remains one of the most powerful in the study of behavior.

This chapter presents these concepts, the experiments behind them and the criticisms that modified them. It follows the historical introduction of [7.18.1](/7.18.1) and the methods of [7.18.2](/7.18.2). The concepts of this chapter are the behavioral side of problems that later chapters treat mechanistically: the sensory filters that implement releasing mechanisms ([7.18.9](/7.18.9)–[7.18.13](/7.18.13)), the neural circuits that generate stereotyped motor patterns ([7.18.14](/7.18.14)), the neural and hormonal basis of motivation ([7.18.7](/7.18.7), [7.18.8](/7.18.8), [7.18.19](/7.18.19)), and the evolution of displays into signals ([7.18.39](/7.18.39)). The last section, on the opposition between instinct and learning, leads to the chapters on genes and development ([7.18.4](/7.18.4), [7.18.5](/7.18.5)).

## 3.1. Reflexes and Fixed Action Patterns

### 3.1.1. Reflexes

The simplest stereotyped responses are reflexes: rapid, involuntary responses of a part of the body to a specific stimulus, such as the knee jerk, the withdrawal of a hand from a hot object, blinking, sneezing, coughing and the constriction of the pupil in bright light. Their anatomical basis is the **reflex arc** ([7.14.7](/7.14.7)), a chain from receptor through afferent neurons, one or more synapses in the central nervous system and efferent neurons to the effector. The strength of a reflex response is graded with the strength of the stimulus, and reflexes can be modified by experience, as in the conditioned reflexes studied by Pavlov ([7.18.41](/7.18.41)).

Early behaviorists and some physiologists tried to explain all behavior as chains of reflexes, each movement providing the stimulus for the next. The ethologists rejected this view for two reasons. First, many behavior patterns continue in the absence of the sensory feedback that a reflex chain would need. Second, the readiness to perform a behavior varies with the internal state of the animal, so that the same stimulus may elicit a vigorous response at one time and none at another. Simple orientation responses of whole animals, such as kineses and taxes, are treated in [7.18.12](/7.18.12).

### 3.1.2. Fixed Action Patterns

**Definition 3.1.1 (Fixed Action Pattern)** A **fixed action pattern** is a stereotyped, species-typical sequence of movements that is triggered by a specific stimulus and, once triggered, tends to run to completion without further sensory guidance.

Ethologists attributed several properties to fixed action patterns: they are performed in nearly the same form by all members of a species (or of one sex), often without prior practice; their form is independent of the strength of the triggering stimulus, although their probability and intensity are not; and they continue even if the stimulus is removed after they start. Because of their constancy, Lorenz and others used fixed action patterns as taxonomic characters, comparing them among species like anatomical structures; the courtship displays of ducks were a celebrated example.

The classic example is the egg-retrieval behavior of the greylag goose (*Anser anser*), analyzed by Lorenz and Tinbergen (1938). When an incubating goose sees an egg outside its nest, it stretches its neck toward the egg, places the underside of its bill against the far side of the egg and rolls it back into the nest with a backward movement of the neck. During the roll, small side-to-side movements of the bill keep the egg, which tends to wobble, from slipping away. If the experimenter removes the egg while the goose is rolling it, the backward movement of the neck continues to the nest as though the egg were still there, but the side-to-side movements stop. Lorenz and Tinbergen concluded that the behavior combines two components: a fixed action pattern (the backward rolling movement), which once released runs to completion, and a taxis (the lateral corrections), which depends continuously on sensory feedback from the egg. The goose will also retrieve almost any rounded object near the nest, such as a beer bottle or a toy, and will retrieve a giant egg in preference to its own ([§3.2.2](#sec-3.2.2)).

A second example, made famous by Jean-Henri Fabre in the nineteenth century, is the provisioning behavior of the digger wasp *Sphex*. A female stings a cricket, paralyzing it, drags it to her burrow, leaves it at the entrance, enters the burrow to inspect it, then returns and drags the cricket inside, where she lays an egg on it. Fabre found that if he moved the cricket a few centimeters away while the wasp was inside, the wasp, on emerging, dragged it back to the entrance and again entered alone to inspect the burrow; he repeated the displacement about 40 times with one wasp, which inspected the burrow each time. The example is often cited to illustrate the rigidity of instinct. Later field studies of *Sphex ichneumoneus* by Jane Brockmann showed that the behavior is less rigid than the story suggests: many wasps stop reinspecting after a few repetitions, and individuals differ. The stereotypy is a property of a particular decision rule, not of all insect behavior.

Other examples include the prey-catching tongue flick of toads and frogs, which, once aimed, is completed even if the prey moves ([7.18.13](/7.18.13)); the yawn; and many courtship displays. Careful measurement has shown that even fixed action patterns vary somewhat in form, duration and orientation between performances and individuals, so many authors now prefer the term *modal action pattern* (George Barlow, 1968), emphasizing that the pattern has a typical form around which it varies. The neural basis of such patterns, central pattern generators that produce rhythmic or sequential motor output without patterned sensory input, is described in [7.18.14](/7.18.14) (**central pattern generator**, [7.14.6](/7.14.6)).

## 3.2. Sign Stimuli and Releasing Mechanisms

### 3.2.1. Sign Stimuli

Sense organs deliver to the brain a vast amount of information, of which only a small part influences any particular behavior. The ethologists found that a behavior is often triggered by just a few features of a complex object, while other features that seem conspicuous to us are ignored. Jakob von Uexküll had already emphasized that each animal lives in a perceptual world, its Umwelt, made of the features its sense organs and brain extract ([7.18.9](/7.18.9)).

**Definition 3.2.1 (Sign Stimulus and Releaser)** A **sign stimulus** is the component of a complex stimulus situation that triggers a particular behavior. A sign stimulus produced by another animal, in particular a structure or behavior that has evolved to serve as a signal in communication, is called a **releaser**. The hypothetical mechanism in the nervous system that detects the sign stimulus and triggers the response is the **releasing mechanism**; when it was thought to function without prior experience, it was called the **innate releasing mechanism**.

The method for identifying sign stimuli is the dummy experiment. In the late 1930s David Lack studied the territorial behavior of European robins (*Erithacus rubecula*). Adult robins of both sexes have an orange-red breast; juveniles are brown. A territorial robin attacks an adult intruder but not a juvenile. Lack placed models in robins' territories: a stuffed juvenile, a stuffed adult with the red breast painted brown, and a bunch of red breast feathers on a wire. The territory owners attacked the bunch of feathers almost as vigorously as a complete stuffed adult, but largely ignored the stuffed juvenile and the brown-breasted adult, although these looked far more like robins to a human observer. The red breast is the sign stimulus for territorial attack.

Tinbergen obtained similar results with the three-spined stickleback. In the breeding season, a territorial male attacks other males, which have a red throat and belly. Tinbergen presented models: a realistic model of a stickleback without red, and a series of crude shapes (a cigar, a disk) painted red on the underside. The crude red models were attacked much more than the realistic model without red. Males also responded to the orientation of the model: a model held head-down, in the posture of a male digging his nest or threatening, was attacked more than one held horizontally. Conversely, the sign stimuli of a receptive female for courtship are her swollen silvery abdomen and her head-up posture.

The most thorough analysis was Tinbergen and Albert Perdeck's (1950) study of the begging of herring gull chicks (*Larus argentatus*). A chick pecks at the tip of its parent's bill, and the parent then regurgitates food. The adult herring gull has a yellow bill with a red spot near the tip of the lower mandible. Presenting flat cardboard heads to naive chicks, Tinbergen and Perdeck varied one feature at a time and counted the chicks' pecks. The color of the head had no effect; the color of the bill had some effect; and the red spot had a large effect: a model with no spot received only about a quarter as many pecks as one with a red spot, and spots of other colors were intermediate, according to their contrast with the bill. The chicks also pecked more at a low, moving, elongated bill held pointing downward. The most effective model of all was not a gull's head but a thin red rod with three white bands near its tip, which received more pecks than a realistic model.

Sign stimuli also work in other modalities. Wolfgang Schleidt found that incubating turkey hens attack any small moving object near the nest, including their own chicks, unless it emits the chick's contact call; hens that had been deafened killed their chicks. The call of the chick is the sign stimulus that inhibits attack. Male mosquitoes are attracted to sound at the frequency of the female's wingbeat, which their antennae are tuned to detect (**Johnston's organ**, [7.17.27](/7.17.27)), and will fly to a tuning fork of the right pitch.

The concept of the releasing mechanism raised two questions that occupied neuroethology for decades. Where in the nervous system is the stimulus filtered, in the sense organs or centrally? The male mosquito's ear is tuned peripherally; the toad's recognition of prey by its shape and movement is computed centrally ([7.18.13](/7.18.13)). And is the mechanism really innate? Many sign stimuli are effective at first encounter, but experience can sharpen or alter responses. Jack Hailman (1967) showed that the pecking of laughing gull chicks becomes more accurate and more specific to the features of the parent's head over the first days after hatching, through practice and reinforcement by food. A releasing mechanism provides a starting point that learning then refines.

### 3.2.2. Supernormal Stimuli

**Definition 3.2.2 (Supernormal Stimulus)** A **supernormal stimulus** is an artificial stimulus that elicits a stronger response than the natural stimulus.

The herring gull chick's preference for a striped red rod is one example. Incubating birds of several species prefer to brood eggs larger than their own. Tinbergen found that oystercatchers (*Haematopus ostralegus*), which lay three eggs, preferred a clutch of five to a normal clutch, and given a choice between their own egg and an egg of giant size, tried to incubate the giant egg although they could barely sit on it. Ringed plovers prefer eggs with a higher contrast of dark spots on a pale background than their own.

Why should natural stimuli not be optimal? The response function of the receiver may simply increase monotonically with some feature of the stimulus, such as size or contrast, while the natural stimulus is held at a moderate value by other selective pressures: an egg larger than normal would cost the female more to produce and would not fit the nest. The receiver's preference is open-ended because exaggerated stimuli rarely occur in nature, so there has been no selection against responding to them. This open-endedness has consequences. Brood parasites exploit it: the gape and begging of a common cuckoo chick act as a supernormal stimulus for its hosts, which feed it in preference to their own young ([7.18.32](/7.18.32)). And signalers can be selected to exaggerate their signals to exploit receivers' biases, one route to the evolution of elaborate ornaments (**sensory bias**, [7.10.13](/7.10.13); [7.18.39](/7.18.39)).

### 3.2.3. Heterogeneous Summation and the Gestalt Principle

A stimulus situation usually contains several sign stimuli. How do they combine? Alfred Seitz (1940), a student of Lorenz, proposed the *law of heterogeneous summation*: independent, heterogeneous features of a stimulus add their effects on behavior, so that a model combining several features elicits a response equal to the sum of the responses to models with each feature alone.

**Definition 3.2.3 (Heterogeneous Summation)** **Heterogeneous summation** is the additive combination of the effects of independent stimulus features on a behavioral response: $R(A + B) = R(A) + R(B)$, where $R(S)$ is the response (relative to a baseline) to a model with feature set $S$.

A quantitative test was made by Daisy Leong (1969) with the cichlid fish *Astatotilapia burtoni* (formerly *Haplochromis burtoni*), from Lake Tanganyika. Territorial males are aggressive toward other fish and attack young fish in their tank at a rate that increases when they see a rival male. Leong separated a test male, which shared its compartment with a group of young fish, from a second compartment in which she presented dummies, and measured the change in the rate at which the test male attacked the young. Two features of male coloration were tested: a black eye bar, and orange spots on the flanks and fins. A dummy with the eye bar alone increased the attack rate by 2.81 attacks per minute; a dummy with the orange spots alone decreased it by 1.77 attacks per minute (the orange spots appear to inhibit aggression); and a dummy with both features increased it by 1.12 attacks per minute. The sum of the separate effects, $2.81 - 1.77 = 1.04$ attacks per minute, agrees closely with the observed effect of the combined dummy.

![Heterogeneous summation in the cichlid fish *Astatotilapia burtoni* (data of Leong 1969). Change in the attack rate of a territorial male toward young fish when a dummy was presented: a dummy with a black eye bar alone, a dummy with orange spots alone, and a dummy with both features, compared with the sum of the effects of the single features. The observed combined effect (1.12 attacks per minute) is close to the additive prediction (1.04)](./plots/heterogeneous-summation.py)

Summation does not always hold. Sometimes a configuration of features is effective only as a whole, and its components presented separately are much less effective, or ineffective, a pattern ethologists described with the *Gestalt principle* from the psychology of perception: the whole is more than the sum of its parts. Configural perception is common in vertebrates, which recognize faces, shapes and patterns of movement as wholes. Whether summation or configural processing applies is an empirical question about how a nervous system combines features, and can differ between ages and species; in infants, for example, T. G. R. Bower (1966) reported that responses to a compound visual stimulus were close to the sum of the responses to its parts at 8 weeks of age but greater than the sum at 20 weeks. Neurobiologically, the two cases correspond to linear and nonlinear combination of feature-detector outputs ([7.18.13](/7.18.13)).

### 3.2.4. Releasers and Communication

Many sign stimuli are produced by other animals: the red breast of the robin, the red belly of the stickleback, the spot on the gull's bill. When such a feature has evolved because it influences the behavior of others, it is a signal, and the ethologists' releasers were the first systematic study of animal signals. They noticed that releasers are typically conspicuous, simple and exaggerated beyond any other function they might have, and proposed that many displays evolved from movements that originally served other purposes, through a process they called ritualization. Communication is treated systematically in [7.18.37](/7.18.37)–[7.18.40](/7.18.40), where the distinction between signals and cues (features that convey information but did not evolve to do so) and the problem of honesty are central.

## 3.3. The State of the Receiver

### 3.3.1. Thresholds and Motivation

The same stimulus does not always produce the same response. A male stickleback attacks a red model vigorously early in the breeding season but hardly at all when he is caring for eggs; a hungry animal responds to food that a satiated one ignores. The internal factors that determine the readiness to perform a behavior are summarized by the concept of motivation.

**Definition 3.3.1 (Motivation)** **Motivation** is the set of internal factors (physiological state, hormonal state, circadian and seasonal state, recent experience) that, together with external stimuli, determine whether and how strongly an animal performs a particular behavior at a given time.

Motivation is not observed directly but inferred from changes in responsiveness to constant stimuli, and an explanation in terms of motivation must eventually be replaced by an account of the physiological mechanisms (the hypothalamic control of feeding, for instance, [7.18.8](/7.18.8)). Several observations show its effects.

- *Threshold changes.* The longer a behavior has not been performed, the weaker the stimulus needed to elicit it. A male jumping spider (*Epiblemum scenicum*) shown a crude dummy, a black disk with a pair of legs, may court it or try to catch it as prey. Oskar Drees (1952) found that satiated males courted even crude dummies, but as males were deprived of food, an increasing proportion of their responses to the same dummies were attempts at prey capture. The same stimulus is classified differently depending on the animal's needs.
- *Vacuum activity.* In the extreme, a behavior may be performed in the absence of any stimulus.

**Definition 3.3.2 (Vacuum Activity)** A **vacuum activity** is the performance of a behavior pattern in the absence of the stimuli that normally release it, typically after a long period in which it could not be performed.

Lorenz described a hand-reared starling that, never having caught an insect, would fly up from a perch, snap at the air, return to its perch, perform the beating and swallowing movements used to kill an insect, and shiver as birds do after eating, although no insect was present. Captive weaverbirds deprived of nest material perform nest-building movements with nothing in the bill. Vacuum activities were evidence for the ethologists that the motivation to perform a particular behavior accumulates when it is not performed.

- *Specific fatigue.* Conversely, repeated performance or repeated stimulation reduces responsiveness. Ethologists distinguished stimulus-specific fatigue, a decline in response to one stimulus that leaves the response to other stimuli intact (equivalent to **habituation**, [7.14.46](/7.14.46)), from response-specific fatigue, in which the behavior cannot be elicited by any stimulus for a while.
- *Long-term effects of releasers.* Releasers can also change motivation over a longer time. Walter Heiligenberg and Ursula Kramer (1972) presented a dummy of a rival male *A. burtoni* to a territorial male for 30 s every 15 min throughout the day for ten days. The test male's rate of attack on the young fish in his tank rose steadily over the days and, when the presentations stopped, declined only slowly over the following days. The releaser had not only triggered attacks but raised the underlying aggressive motivation, an effect that later work linked to hormonal and neural changes in social status ([7.18.19](/7.18.19)).

### 3.3.2. Dual Quantification

If both stimulus strength and internal state determine the response, then the same response should be produced by a strong stimulus with weak motivation or a weak stimulus with strong motivation. Gerard Baerends and colleagues (1955) tested this with male guppies (*Poecilia reticulata*). The male's sexual motivation could be estimated from his color pattern, which changes with his internal state, and the strength of the stimulus by the size of the female he was shown. The intensity of courtship (from no courtship through orientation to the full sigmoid display) depended on both: males in a high motivational state performed the full display to small females, males in a low state only to large ones. Plotting the combinations of stimulus strength and motivation that produce the same response gives curves called *motivational isoclines*. Their shape (approximately hyperbolic in Baerends's data) indicates how the two factors combine: hyperbolic isoclines mean that the factors multiply, so that neither stimulus nor motivation alone produces a response when the other is absent. Tinbergen called this the rule of *dual quantification*.

### 3.3.3. Appetitive and Consummatory Behavior

Wallace Craig (1918) observed that much of the behavior of doves consists of variable searching that ends when a particular goal situation is reached, at which point a stereotyped act is performed and the searching stops. Hungry animals search, sexually motivated animals seek mates, birds in spring look for nest sites.

**Definition 3.3.3 (Appetitive and Consummatory Behavior)** **Appetitive behavior** is the variable, often exploratory and learned behavior that brings an animal into the situation in which a goal can be attained. **Consummatory behavior** is the relatively stereotyped behavior performed in the goal situation, whose performance reduces the motivation for the sequence.

A hungry hawk patrols its hunting ground, a flexible appetitive phase in which it may use learned routes and perches; on sighting prey it stoops, strikes, kills and eats, a stereotyped sequence. Tinbergen's view of instinct as a hierarchy linked these ideas: a "major instinct" such as reproduction activates appetitive behavior (searching for a territory) until a sign stimulus releases the next level (territorial fighting, nest building, courtship), each with its own appetitive behavior, down to the consummatory fixed action patterns at the bottom. The distinction remains useful, and it has a neural counterpart in the distinction between "wanting" and "liking", the appetitive and consummatory components of reward in the brain's dopamine system ([7.18.7](/7.18.7)). But the satiating effect of the consummatory act need not come from the act itself: what reduces motivation may be its consequences (food in the stomach, a rise in blood glucose) rather than the performance of the movements.

## 3.4. Models of Motivation

### 3.4.1. Lorenz's Psychohydraulic Model

Lorenz (1950) summarized the ethological theory of motivation in a mechanical analogy.

**Definition 3.4.1 (Psychohydraulic Model)** In Lorenz's **psychohydraulic model**, the motivation for a particular behavior is represented by water ("action-specific energy") flowing at a constant rate from a tap into a reservoir. The outlet of the reservoir is closed by a valve held shut by a spring, which represents the inhibition by higher centers. The valve is opened by the pressure of the water in the reservoir together with weights placed in a scale pan, which represent the strength of the sign stimulus. Water flowing out of the valve falls into a trough with holes at different heights, which represent the components of the behavior: a weak flow reaches only the lowest hole (a low-intensity form of the behavior), a strong flow reaches higher ones as well.

![Lorenz's psychohydraulic model of motivation (1950), redrawn schematically. The tap T supplies "action-specific energy" continuously to the reservoir R. The valve V is held closed by the spring S (inhibition) and opened by the pressure of the accumulated energy together with weights in the scale pan Sp (the strength of the sign stimulus). The outflow runs into the trough Tr, whose openings at different heights correspond to responses of increasing intensity](./plots/hydraulic-model.py)

The model accounts for the main observations of §3.3. Thresholds fall with time since the behavior was last performed, because water accumulates. With enough water, the valve opens without any weight, which is vacuum activity. The intensity of the response depends on both the stimulus and the accumulated energy, as dual quantification requires. Performing the behavior drains the reservoir, which explains specific exhaustion.

The model was influential but was abandoned in the 1960s, for several reasons summarized by Robert Hinde (1960) and others. Its "energy" corresponds to no physical quantity in the nervous system, and the analogy can be adjusted to fit almost any observation, so it predicts little. Behavior often stops not because some energy is exhausted but because of negative feedback from its consequences. A dog with an esophageal fistula, so that water it drinks drains out of the neck instead of reaching the stomach, drinks far more than a normal dog, although it performs the consummatory act of drinking many times; drinking stops normally when water reaches the stomach and blood. And responsiveness can fall for reasons other than exhaustion, such as habituation or the switch to a different, higher-priority behavior. The modern view treats motivation in terms of homeostatic control systems with set points and feedback ([7.14.1](/7.14.1)), together with the incentive value of stimuli and the circadian and hormonal modulation of neural circuits.

### 3.4.2. Measuring Motivation

Motivation can be measured by how much an animal will do, or endure, to perform a behavior or reach a resource. Classic methods include:

- *The obstruction method.* In the Columbia obstruction box (Warden, 1931), a rat had to cross an electrified grid to reach food, water or a mate; the number of crossings in a fixed time measured the strength of each motivation as a function of deprivation.
- *Adulteration.* The amount of quinine, which tastes bitter, that an animal will tolerate in its food or water.
- *Force and speed.* The force with which an animal pulls against a harness to reach a goal, or its running speed in a runway.
- *Operant tasks.* The rate of lever pressing for a reward, and the progressive ratio schedule, in which the number of presses required for each successive reward increases until the animal stops responding; the last ratio completed (the breakpoint) measures the value of the reward.

The same logic underlies modern methods for assessing the welfare of animals. Marian Dawkins proposed measuring how much animals "want" resources by borrowing the concept of elasticity of demand from economics: if the amount of work an animal does to obtain a resource falls steeply as the price rises (elastic demand), the resource is a luxury; if the animal maintains its consumption at high prices (inelastic demand), it is a necessity. Hens work hard for access to a nest box before egg laying and for a dust-bathing substrate, results that have informed the design of housing for laying hens ([7.18.47](/7.18.47)).

### 3.4.3. Conflict Behavior

Animals often face situations that activate incompatible tendencies at once: to attack and to flee from a rival at a territorial boundary, to approach and to avoid a strange object, to court and to attack a partner. The resulting behavior was one of the most fruitful topics of classical ethology, because many displays appear to have evolved from conflict behavior.

- *Ambivalent behavior.* The animal shows elements of both tendencies, alternately or together. Gulls in a boundary dispute show the "upright threat posture", which combines elements of attack (raised bill, ready to peck) and escape (sleeked plumage, neck stretched upward); intention movements, the incomplete initial movements of a behavior such as crouching before take-off, are common in conflict.
Two other kinds of conflict behavior are especially common.

**Definition 3.4.2 (Displacement and Redirected Behavior)** A **displacement activity** is a behavior that appears out of context, irrelevant to the tendencies in conflict, when an animal is torn between incompatible tendencies or thwarted in performing a motivated behavior. **Redirected behavior** is a behavior directed at an object other than the one that elicited it, typically aggression directed away from a dominant or dangerous opponent toward a substitute.

Herring gulls in a territorial dispute may suddenly pull up tufts of grass, a movement of nest building; male sticklebacks meeting at a territorial boundary dig in the sand, which resembles nest digging; fighting cocks peck at the ground as though feeding; many birds preen, and rats groom, in conflict situations. Redirected aggression is familiar: a gull that loses a dispute may attack a smaller neighbor or peck violently at the ground, and a baboon threatened by a dominant male may chase a subordinate.

Two explanations of displacement activities were proposed. Tinbergen and others supposed that the blocked motivation "sparked over" into another behavior system. The *disinhibition hypothesis* (van Iersel and Bol, 1958) proposed instead that two conflicting tendencies inhibit each other, releasing from inhibition a third behavior of lower priority whose causal factors are present anyway: a gull disputing a boundary is also near its nest, a stickleback near the sand. In support, the form of a displacement activity is influenced by the stimuli present (terns preen more in wet weather), as the disinhibition hypothesis predicts. Displacement activities have also been used as indicators of stress and anxiety, since self-directed behaviors such as scratching and self-grooming increase in primates in tense social situations and decrease after anxiolytic drugs.

### 3.4.4. Decisions and Behavioral Sequences

An animal can do only one thing at a time with its main effector systems; behavior proceeds through a final common path, and the animal must continually decide what to do next. David McFarland and Richard Sibly developed a quantitative framework for these decisions. The motivational state of the animal is represented as a point in a space whose axes are the relevant internal variables (deficits of food and water, for example) together with the strength of external cues. The space is divided into regions in which different behaviors are performed, and the boundaries between regions are the decision rules. Performing a behavior moves the state through the space (eating reduces the food deficit and, because dry food increases thirst, raises the water deficit), so a sequence of behaviors is a trajectory through the space.

This framework makes predictions that can be tested by perturbing the state. McFarland (1974) found that when a dove alternates between feeding and drinking, interrupting the dominant behavior sometimes caused the animal to switch to the other activity immediately and sometimes did not. He interpreted this as time-sharing: a dominant behavior (say drinking) can allow, or disinhibit, a subdominant one (feeding) during its pauses, and the subdominant behavior appears when the dominant one is interrupted. The framework connects classical motivation theory with optimal decision theory: if the costs of the deficits and of switching between activities are known, the optimal sequence of behaviors can be computed by dynamic programming (**dynamic programming**, [4.1.13](/4.1.13); [7.18.23](/7.18.23)), and the observed sequence compared with it.

## 3.5. Instinct and Learning

The classical ethologists divided behavior into instinctive (innate) and learned components. Lorenz regarded fixed action patterns and innate releasing mechanisms as the products of the genome, developing without the influence of experience, and the deprivation experiment, rearing an animal in isolation from the relevant experience, as the way to identify them: if a behavior appears in normal form in an animal deprived of the experience, it is innate.

**Definition 3.5.1 (Instinct)** **Instinct** (innate behavior) traditionally refers to behavior that appears in functional form the first time it is performed, without prior opportunity to learn it from the relevant experience, and is typical of the species.

Daniel Lehrman's critique (1953) argued that this dichotomy is misleading. Rearing in isolation removes some experiences but not all: an isolated animal still experiences its own body, its own movements and the general environment of the cage, and these can be essential for the development of a behavior. Lehrman's example was the pecking of chicks, which might be influenced by the movements of the head that the embryo makes in the egg in time with its heartbeat. More fundamentally, every trait develops through the interaction of genes and environment, so that the question "is this behavior innate or learned?" cannot be answered for a behavior as a whole. A valid question is narrower: does a particular difference in behavior between individuals arise from a difference in their genes or from a difference in their experience? This is the interactionist view that underlies the modern study of development ([7.18.4](/7.18.4), [7.18.5](/7.18.5)).

The word "innate" itself conflates several properties that need not go together: being present at birth, developing without a particular experience, being invariant within a species, being an adaptation, being heritable, and being controlled by a specific developmental program (Bateson and Mameli, 2007). A behavior can be heritable without being invariant (it varies among genotypes) and invariant without being independent of experience (all members of a species may learn it from the same universal experience).

The development of nut opening in red squirrels (*Sciurus vulgaris*), studied by Irenäus Eibl-Eibesfeldt, illustrates the interaction. Squirrels reared in isolation, without ever having seen nuts, performed the gnawing and prying movements used to open nuts the first time they were given one, but their first attempts were inefficient: they gnawed at random over the surface. Only after experience did they learn to gnaw a furrow along the grain of the shell and crack it open with a twist of the lower incisors, the efficient technique of experienced squirrels. The movements were available without learning; their effective use was learned.

The modern synthesis is that learning itself is guided by evolved predispositions: animals have "instincts to learn" particular things at particular times (Peter Marler's phrase). Songbirds learn the songs of their own species more readily than others ([7.18.17](/7.18.17)); goslings follow the first moving object they see ([7.18.5](/7.18.5)); rats associate taste with illness but not with shock ([7.18.41](/7.18.41)). The classical concepts of the sign stimulus and the fixed action pattern survive as descriptions of how behavior is organized, while the questions of how such organization develops and how it is implemented in the nervous system have become the subject matter of developmental biology and neuroethology.

## Summary

| Concept | Meaning | Classic example |
|---|---|---|
| Reflex | Rapid, graded response of part of the body to a stimulus, via a reflex arc | Knee jerk, blink |
| Fixed action pattern (Definition 3.1.1) | Stereotyped, species-typical motor sequence that runs to completion once triggered | Egg retrieval in the greylag goose; burrow inspection by *Sphex* |
| Sign stimulus, releaser (Definition 3.2.1) | Feature that triggers a behavior; releaser if it is a signal from another animal | Red breast of robins; red belly of sticklebacks; red spot on the gull's bill |
| Releasing mechanism | Neural filter that detects the sign stimulus | Tuned antennae of male mosquitoes; prey recognition in toads |
| Supernormal stimulus (Definition 3.2.2) | Artificial stimulus more effective than the natural one | Giant eggs for oystercatchers; striped rod for gull chicks |
| Heterogeneous summation (Definition 3.2.3) | Independent features add their effects | Eye bar and orange spots in *A. burtoni*: $2.81 - 1.77 = 1.04 \approx 1.12$ |
| Motivation (Definition 3.3.1) | Internal factors setting responsiveness | Hunger changes how jumping spiders classify a dummy |
| Vacuum activity (Definition 3.3.2) | Behavior without its releasing stimulus | Starling catching nonexistent insects |
| Appetitive and consummatory behavior (Definition 3.3.3) | Variable search versus stereotyped goal act | Hawk patrolling versus stooping and eating |
| Displacement activity, redirected behavior (Definition 3.4.2) | Out-of-context behavior in conflict; aggression toward a substitute | Grass pulling by gulls; attacking a smaller neighbor |

- Response intensity depends on both stimulus strength and motivation (dual quantification); isoclines of equal response are approximately hyperbolic.
- Lorenz's psychohydraulic model (Definition 3.4.1) explained threshold lowering, vacuum activity and exhaustion, but its "energy" is unmeasurable, and behavior often stops through feedback from its consequences rather than exhaustion; motivation is now analyzed as homeostatic control, incentive and neural modulation.
- Motivation is measured by obstruction, adulteration, force and operant demand (progressive ratio, elasticity of demand), methods now used to assess animal welfare.
- Animals in conflict show ambivalent, displacement and redirected behavior; decisions can be analyzed as trajectories in a motivational state space.
- The instinct–learning dichotomy has been replaced by an interactionist view: all behavior develops through genes and environment, learning is guided by evolved predispositions, and "innate" conflates several distinct properties.

## Questions

1. In the egg-retrieval experiment, the goose continued the backward movement of the neck after the egg was removed, but the lateral movements stopped. What does this tell us about the control of the two components? How might you test the same idea with modern methods?

   **Answer** The backward rolling movement is executed open-loop: once triggered, it does not depend on continuing sensory input from the egg, as expected of a centrally generated motor pattern. The lateral movements are closed-loop corrections that depend on tactile feedback from the wobbling egg (a taxis). A modern test could record muscle activity (electromyography) during retrieval of real eggs and during "retrievals" after the egg is removed, or replace the egg with a motorized object that wobbles in controlled ways, to show that the lateral muscle activity tracks the egg's movements while the neck flexion follows a fixed time course; one could also anesthetize the bill locally and test whether rolling persists without lateral corrections.
2. A herring gull chick pecks more at a thin red rod with white bands than at a realistic model of its parent's head. Does this mean the chick's releasing mechanism is "badly designed"? Explain.

   **Answer** No. The releasing mechanism need only discriminate reliably between the parent's bill and other objects that occur in the chick's natural environment; it is tuned to a few features (elongation, a low and downward-pointing position, contrast near the tip, movement) that suffice for this. Natural selection has no opportunity to penalize a strong response to objects that never occur, such as striped red rods, so the response function can be open-ended and an artificial stimulus that exaggerates the relevant features can be more effective than the natural one. The mechanism is efficient for its natural task; supernormal responses are a by-product. Experience after hatching further refines pecking toward the actual parent.
3. In Leong's experiment, suppose the dummy with both features had increased the attack rate by 3.5 attacks per minute. How would you interpret this, and what further experiment would distinguish possible explanations?

   **Answer** An effect of 3.5 attacks per minute exceeds the additive prediction of 1.04, so the features would interact: the combination would be more effective than the sum of its parts, a configural (Gestalt) effect; for instance, the orange spots might inhibit attack when seen alone (resembling a non-rival) but enhance it in combination with the eye bar (identifying a rival in full territorial coloration). To test this, one would vary the intensity of each feature factorially (for example three levels of eye-bar darkness crossed with three levels of spot size), fit a model with main effects and an interaction, and test whether the interaction term is significant; dummies with the features in abnormal spatial arrangements would show whether the configuration matters.
4. Explain why the hydraulic model predicts vacuum activities, and why the observation of drinking in dogs with an esophageal fistula is evidence against it.

   **Answer** In the model, "action-specific energy" accumulates in the reservoir at a constant rate when the behavior is not performed; eventually its pressure alone opens the valve, even with no weights (stimuli) in the scale pan, so the behavior occurs without its releasing stimulus. The model also implies that performing the consummatory act drains the reservoir and so reduces motivation. A dog with an esophageal fistula performs the act of drinking repeatedly, but drinks far more than a normal dog and does not become satiated, because the water does not reach its stomach and blood. Satiation therefore depends on feedback from the consequences of the behavior (water in the stomach, restoration of blood volume and osmolarity), not on the performance of the motor act draining an energy store, contrary to the model.
5. A territorial male bird is presented with a stuffed intruder at the edge of its territory and alternates between approaching and retreating, then suddenly preens. Classify these behaviors, and propose how you would test the disinhibition hypothesis for the preening.

   **Answer** The alternating approach and retreat is ambivalent behavior, reflecting simultaneous tendencies to attack and flee; the preening is a displacement activity. The disinhibition hypothesis predicts that preening appears when the attack and escape tendencies are roughly balanced and inhibit each other, and that its frequency and form depend on the normal causal factors for preening, which are present anyway (for example, plumage condition). Tests: (1) wet the bird's plumage slightly or dust it, which should increase displacement preening in the conflict situation if disinhibition is correct, but not if the activity arises from "sparking over" of aggressive motivation; (2) vary the position of the model to change the balance of attack and escape tendencies and test whether preening peaks where approach and retreat are equally frequent; (3) record latencies to show that preening occurs at moments when the conflicting behaviors stop.
6. Why is the question "Is the song of this bird species innate or learned?" poorly posed? Reformulate it as a question that can be answered experimentally.

   **Answer** Song develops through an interaction between genes (which build the brain's song system, its predispositions to learn conspecific song, and the vocal apparatus) and experience (hearing adult song, hearing one's own voice, social interactions). Every song therefore depends on both, and the question has no answer for the song as a whole. Answerable questions concern differences: do differences in song between two populations or species persist when birds are reared in identical acoustic environments (indicating genetic differences), and do individuals of the same genotype raised hearing different tutor songs sing differently (indicating effects of experience)? Experiments include cross-fostering, rearing in acoustic isolation, deafening, and tutoring with taped songs of different species ([7.18.17](/7.18.17)).
7. (Open question) Using the state-space framework of §3.4.4, describe how a dove that is both hungry and thirsty should organize its feeding and drinking if switching between food and water dishes costs time. What would you measure to test your prediction?

   **Answer** Represent the state as $(h, w)$, the food and water deficits. Eating reduces $h$ and raises $w$ (dry food increases thirst); drinking reduces $w$. Without switching costs, the dove would alternate rapidly, keeping the larger deficit in check. With a cost per switch, the optimal policy has hysteresis: the dove should continue an activity until the other deficit exceeds the current one by a margin that grows with the switching cost, producing long bouts of each activity. Predictions: bout lengths increase when the dishes are placed farther apart; the deficit difference at which switches occur increases with the distance; and the sequence can be compared with the policy computed by dynamic programming from measured rates of intake and deficit change. One would record the time course of feeding and drinking bouts under different deprivation levels and dish separations.
