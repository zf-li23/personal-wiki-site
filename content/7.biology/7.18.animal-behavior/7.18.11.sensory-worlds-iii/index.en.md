Mechanical stimuli are the most varied in the sensory world. The same physical event, a movement of matter, reaches animals as touch when they contact an object, as vibration when it travels through the ground or a plant stem, as flow when water or air moves past the body, and as sound when it propagates as a pressure wave. Animals have built from a few kinds of mechanoreceptor cells a remarkable range of organs to sense them: the touch-sensitive star of a mole, the lateral line of fishes, the vibration receptors in the legs of insects and spiders, and ears that locate a mouse in darkness to within a degree.

This chapter follows mechanical stimuli from contact to distance. It starts with the shared machinery of mechanoreception and the physics of near-field and far-field sound, which decides what kind of receptor can detect a stimulus; turns to touch, substrate vibration and the lateral line; and then to hearing, where the physical constraints of body size shape both the frequencies animals use and the way they locate sounds. The central example is the barn owl, whose sound localization was analyzed from behavior down to neurons and whose auditory map is calibrated by vision during development; the smallest ears, of crickets and of the fly *Ormia*, show how animals too small to use the cues of large ears have solved the same problem. The cellular physiology of hair cells, the cochlea and the auditory pathway is treated in [7.14.42](/7.14.42), the computations of sound localization, including the Jeffress model and the mammalian alternative, in [7.16.24](/7.16.24), and the insect mechanoreceptors and ears in [7.17.27](/7.17.27#sec-27.2). The principles of sensation and the idea of the Umwelt were introduced in [7.18.9](/7.18.9). Echolocation and the hearing of moths that listen for bats follow in [7.18.12](/7.18.12), the acoustic communication of insects and frogs in [7.18.16](/7.18.16), and the production and transmission of sound signals in [7.18.38](/7.18.38).

## 11.1. Mechanoreception

### 11.1.1. A Shared Machinery

All mechanoreceptors convert a deformation into the opening of mechanically gated ion channels. They differ in the structure that delivers force to the channels, and this accessory structure determines what the receptor detects.

- In vertebrates, the **hair cell** carries a bundle of stereocilia graded in height; deflection of the bundle toward the tallest stereocilia stretches the tip links between them and opens transduction channels within microseconds; deflection in the opposite direction closes them ([7.14.42](/7.14.42#sec-42.3.3)). Hair cells are therefore directional, and organs that contain hair cells of different orientations (the otolith organs, the lateral line) can measure the direction of a stimulus. They serve hearing, balance and the lateral line.
- In insects and crustaceans, the **chordotonal organ** consists of scolopidia stretched between two points of the body; their ciliated dendrites respond to stretch and vibration ([7.17.27](/7.17.27#sec-27.2.3)). Chordotonal organs are proprioceptors, vibration receptors and, combined with a thin membrane over an air sac, the ears of insects (**tympanal organs**). **Johnston's organ** in the antennal pedicel detects the movements of the antenna.
- Hairs and setae on the body surface of arthropods, and the hairs, whiskers and skin receptors of mammals, transmit touch and flow to sensory endings at their base.

Organs built from these cells are often called by their function, but the same cell type can serve several functions in one animal. The Johnston's organ of *Drosophila* contains separate groups of neurons that detect gravity, wind and the male's courtship song, and the same genes are needed for all three ([7.17.27](/7.17.27#sec-27.2.3)).

### 11.1.2. Near Field and Far Field

A vibrating source moves the surrounding medium in two ways. Close to the source, the medium flows back and forth with it as an incompressible tide: this is the near field, in which the motion of particles of the medium dominates. Farther away, the motion propagates as a compression wave, in which pressure varies strongly while the particle displacement is minute: this is the far field. The transition lies at roughly a wavelength divided by $2\pi$, so the near field is large for low frequencies and in water, where sound travels about 4.4 times faster than in air and wavelengths are correspondingly longer (Bradbury and Vehrencamp 2011).

The two components require different detectors, and this physical distinction explains much of the diversity of ears.

- Particle motion is detected by structures that are moved by the medium relative to the rest of the body: light hairs dragged by the flow, or dense masses that lag behind because of their inertia. The filiform hairs on the cerci of crickets and cockroaches, the trichobothria on the legs of spiders, the hairs of caterpillars that detect the wingbeats of an approaching wasp at up to $70\,\mathrm{cm}$, the plumed antennae of male mosquitoes tuned to the wingbeat of females, and the arista and Johnston's organ of fruit flies that hear the courtship song of a male a few millimeters away are all near-field detectors. The otolith organs of fish, the **statocysts** of crustaceans and cephalopods, and the lateral line also respond to particle motion or acceleration.
- Pressure is detected by a thin membrane, the tympanum, backed by an enclosed volume of air that can be compressed: the eardrum of terrestrial vertebrates, the tympanal organs of insects, and in fish the gas of the **swim bladder** (Section 11.3.3).

Near-field signals are private: their amplitude falls with the cube or a higher power of distance, so they are useful only within a few body lengths of the source. Courting fruit flies, which communicate at a distance of a few millimeters, can therefore use low-frequency songs that a small animal could never broadcast to a distant receiver, and elephants within a herd may exchange low rumbles partly through the near field.

## 11.2. Touch, Vibration and Flow

### 11.2.1. The Star-Nosed Mole

The star-nosed mole (*Condylura cristata*) of North American wetlands hunts small invertebrates in dark tunnels and underwater, with eyes that are tiny and nearly useless. Its nose is surrounded by 22 fleshy appendages, eleven on each side, covered by about $25\,000$ minute touch organs (Eimer's organs), each containing several types of mechanoreceptor endings; the star is innervated by more than $100\,000$ nerve fibers, several times the number of touch fibers in the human hand (Catania 2011). The mole touches its surroundings with the star about 13 times per second. When an appendage contacts a potential prey item, the mole moves the star in a fast saccade so that the two smallest appendages, the ventral appendages 11 just above the mouth, explore the object (Catania and Kaas 1997).

The appendages 11 are a tactile fovea, the touch equivalent of the fovea of the eye. They carry about 7 % of the Eimer's organs of the star, but are innervated by about 11 % of its nerve fibers and represented by about 25 % of the area of somatosensory cortex devoted to the star (figure below). The disproportionate representation of sensory surfaces that matter, measured by the **cortical magnification factor**, is a general property of mammalian sensory maps ([7.16.18](/7.16.18#sec-18.1.2)), and its pattern differs among species in a way that matches their sensory ecology: in the cortex of the star-nosed mole the star and the forelimbs dominate, in the eastern mole and in shrews the vibrissae around the nose, and in primates the hands and lips (Catania 2000).

B: the share of the two appendages 11 among the appendages of the star (2 of 22), the Eimer's organs (about 7 %), the afferent nerve fibers (about 11 %) and the area of somatosensory cortex that represents the star (about 25 %). The tactile fovea is part of a foraging strategy. Star-nosed moles identify and eat a prey item in as little as $120\,\mathrm{ms}$, on average about $230\,\mathrm{ms}$, among the shortest handling times known for any mammal (Catania and Remple 2005). Such speed allows them to profit from prey so small that it would not be worth the time of a slower forager, a prediction of the prey model of optimal foraging ([7.18.22](/7.18.22)). The handling time approaches the limit set by the conduction and processing times of the nervous system.

### 11.2.2. Substrate Vibration

Many small animals communicate and hunt through the substrate on which they sit: plant stems, leaves, spider webs, the surface of water, sand. Bending waves on a plant stem travel at only tens of meters per second for the frequencies that insects use, much more slowly than sound in air, and they lose little energy, so a small insect can signal effectively to a partner on the same plant. Cocroft and Rodríguez (2005) estimated that about 195 000 described species of insects communicate by substrate vibration, more than by airborne sound.

- Treehoppers and leafhoppers court with vibrational songs produced by vibrating the abdomen against the plant; in the treehopper *Umbonia crassicornis*, nymphs on a stem produce a synchronized vibrational signal when a predator attacks, and the mother responds by defending them (Cocroft 1996).
- Web-building spiders detect prey and the courtship signals of males through the vibrations of the web; males of many species pluck the web in species-specific rhythms that prevent the female from treating them as prey.
- Water striders and fishing spiders signal and locate prey by ripples on the water surface.
- Some vertebrates sense the ground: the Namib golden mole (*Eremitalpa granti*) appears to locate the grass hummocks in which termites live from their vibration in the sand (Narins and colleagues 1997), and elephants respond to seismic components of the rumbles of other elephants, perhaps over several kilometers (O'Connell-Rodwell and colleagues 2006).

Vibration receptors are proprioceptive organs in a new role: the subgenual organs in the legs of insects, the slit sensilla in the legs of spiders, Pacinian corpuscles in the feet of mammals ([7.17.27](/7.17.27#sec-27.2.3), [7.14.39](/7.14.39#sec-39.5.3)). Their use in communication is described in [7.18.16](/7.18.16) and [7.18.38](/7.18.38).

### 11.2.3. The Lateral Line and Hydrodynamic Sensing

The **lateral line** of fishes and aquatic amphibians ([7.14.42](/7.14.42#def-42.6.1)) consists of **neuromasts**, groups of hair cells whose bundles project into a gelatinous cupula. Superficial neuromasts on the skin respond to the velocity of water flow, including steady flow; canal neuromasts, enclosed in canals that open to the water through pores, respond to the pressure differences along the canal, that is, to the acceleration of the water, and filter out the slow flows that the fish's own swimming produces (Bradbury and Vehrencamp 2011). A moving prey or predator creates a flow field that decays steeply with distance, so the lateral line is a short-range sense, with a range of one to two body lengths, but it works in darkness and turbid water. Its behavioral functions include

- orienting to currents (rheotaxis), which in many fish depends on superficial neuromasts;
- detecting and striking prey: surface-feeding topminnows locate insects struggling on the water surface from the ripples that reach the neuromasts of the head, and some predatory fish strike at a vibrating sphere in darkness;
- schooling: saithe deprived of vision by opaque blinkers still keep their place in a school, whereas those whose lateral line nerves are cut swim abnormally close to their neighbors (Partridge and Pitcher 1980);
- mapping the surroundings: the blind cavefish *Astyanax mexicanus*, which lost its eyes in caves, swims faster when placed in a new environment or when objects are moved, which increases the flow distortions caused by obstacles, and learns the layout of its tank from them (Teyke 1985).

Animals without a lateral line can use similar information through other structures. Harbor seals track the hydrodynamic trail left by a fish (or, in the laboratory, by a miniature submarine) with their vibrissae: blindfolded seals followed trails seconds old over long distances, and lost them when the vibrissae were covered with a stocking mask (Dehnhardt and colleagues 2001). The wavy surface of seal vibrissae reduces the vibrations induced by the seal's own swimming, which would otherwise mask the faint signal of the trail.

## 11.3. Hearing

### 11.3.1. Two Kinds of Ear

Far-field sound in air is a pressure wave, and every terrestrial ear uses a membrane that is moved by a difference of pressure between its two faces. The ears of animals come in two designs, distinguished by what lies behind the membrane.

**Definition 11.3.1 (Pressure Receiver, Pressure-Difference Receiver)** An ear in which the tympanic membrane separates the outside air from a closed cavity, so that it is moved by the outside sound pressure alone, is a **pressure receiver**. An ear in which sound also reaches the inner face of the membrane, through air spaces or tubes that open at another point of the body (such as the other ear or a spiracle), so that the membrane is moved by the difference between the pressures on its two faces, is a **pressure-difference receiver**.

A pressure receiver responds equally to sound from every direction; it gives directional information only by comparison with the other ear, or through the shadowing of the head and the shape of the outer ear. Mammals have pressure receivers: the middle ear cavities are closed (the Eustachian tube opens only to equalize pressure), and the two ears are acoustically independent. Moths, mantises and other insects that only detect bats also have pressure receivers.

A pressure-difference receiver is directional by itself. Because the sound that reaches the inner face has traveled a different path, its amplitude and phase depend on the direction of the source, and the net force on the membrane depends strongly on direction even when the outside pressures at the two openings are almost the same. Crickets and bush crickets have tympana on their forelegs whose inner faces receive sound through a tracheal tube from the spiracles; grasshoppers, cicadas, frogs, lizards and birds have tympana whose inner faces are connected through air sacs, an interaural canal or the mouth cavity (Bradbury and Vehrencamp 2011).

![Pressure receivers and pressure-difference receivers. A: each tympanum (red) closes a separate air cavity and is driven by the outside pressure alone. B: an air passage links the inner faces of the two tympana, so each tympanum is driven by the difference between the outside pressure and the pressure arriving from the other ear. C: model of the drive of the right tympanum as a function of the direction of a 5 kHz tone, for ears 1 cm apart (maximum external time difference 29 µs), internal transmission g = 0.8 and three internal delays. **Example 11.3.1** Why does an internal delay make the ear directional? Let the tone have angular frequency $\omega$ and reach the right ear with phase $+\omega\Delta/2$ and the left ear with phase $-\omega\Delta/2$, where $\Delta = (d/c)\sin\theta$. The inside of the right tympanum receives the left ear's sound with gain $g$ and an extra delay $\tau$, so the drive is
$$
p_{\mathrm{net}} = \mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\omega\Delta/2} - g\,\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\omega(\Delta/2 + \tau)}, \qquad \lvert p_{\mathrm{net}}\rvert^2 = 1 + g^2 - 2g\cos\bigl(\omega(\Delta + \tau)\bigr). \tag{11.1}
$$
For sound from the left, $\Delta = -d/c$; if $\tau = d/c$ the two contributions arrive in phase and nearly cancel, $\lvert p_{\mathrm{net}}\rvert = 1 - g = 0.2$. For sound from the right, $\Delta + \tau = 2d/c$; with $d = 1\,\mathrm{cm}$ and $f = 5\,\mathrm{kHz}$, $\omega\cdot 2d/c = 0.92\,\mathrm{rad}$ and $\lvert p_{\mathrm{net}}\rvert = \sqrt{1.64 - 1.6\cos 0.92} \approx 1.43$. The ratio of $7.2$ is a level difference of $17\,\mathrm{dB}$ between ears whose external level difference is negligible. The internal path converts a time difference too small to be measured by neurons into a large difference of amplitude.

### 11.3.2. Body Size and the Frequencies of Hearing

One of the most robust generalizations in bioacoustics is that small animals use high frequencies and large animals low ones. Three constraints act together.

- Production: an animal radiates sound efficiently only at wavelengths up to about twice its body size; the songs of insects rise in frequency as body size falls (Bradbury and Vehrencamp 2011).
- Propagation: high frequencies are absorbed and scattered more strongly by air and vegetation, which favors low frequencies for long-distance signals ([7.18.38](/7.18.38)).
- Reception and localization: an ear of a pressure-receiver design can compare levels at the two ears only for wavelengths smaller than the head, which casts an acoustic shadow. Small mammals therefore need high frequencies to localize sounds, and among mammals the highest audible frequency falls as the distance between the ears rises (Heffner and Heffner 1992): mice hear up to about $90\,\mathrm{kHz}$, humans to $20\,\mathrm{kHz}$, elephants to only about $10\,\mathrm{kHz}$, while elephants hear infrasound below $20\,\mathrm{Hz}$ ([7.14.42](/7.14.42#sec-42.6.6)).

Fletcher (2004) combined the three constraints in a model that predicts an optimal communication frequency proportional to about the $-0.4$ power of body mass, in good agreement with birds and mammals from small songbirds to elephants. Exceptions confirm the logic: burrowing rodents such as mole rats, which communicate through the ground over short distances, have lost high-frequency hearing; fruit flies, which court in the near field, use songs of a few hundred hertz.

Within these limits, the tuning of an ear is matched to the sounds that matter to its owner, a property that ethologists call a matched filter. The two auditory receptor neurons of noctuid moths respond broadly to the ultrasound of bats and not to lower frequencies ([7.18.12](/7.18.12)). Female *Ormia* flies, which find hosts by their song, are most sensitive near $5\,\mathrm{kHz}$, the frequency of the cricket song, whereas male flies, which do not hunt crickets, are not (Robert and colleagues 1992). The tuning can change with reproductive state: female plainfin midshipman fish, which find nesting males by their humming at night, become more sensitive to the higher harmonics of the hum in the breeding season, an effect mediated by seasonal changes of steroid hormones (Sisneros and Bass 2003; [7.18.19](/7.18.19)).

### 11.3.3. Hearing in Water

In water the problem is reversed. The density of the body of a fish is close to that of water, so a far-field sound passes through the fish, which moves with the water and senses nothing unless some part moves differently. Fish detect particle motion with their otolith organs, whose dense otoliths lag behind the rest of the head. To sense pressure they need a compressible gas volume: the **swim bladder** pulsates in a sound field and converts pressure into motion, and in otophysans (carps, catfishes and their relatives) the **Weberian apparatus**, a chain of small bones, transmits this motion to the inner ear, which extends their hearing to several kilohertz ([7.14.42](/7.14.42#sec-42.6.2)). Fish without such a connection hear only low frequencies at short range, where particle motion is large. Since the otolith organs contain hair cells of different orientations, a fish can estimate the direction of a sound from the axis of particle motion, which points toward the source, even with a single ear; cod localize sources to within 12–20° in this way.

## 11.4. Sound Localization

### 11.4.1. The Cues and Their Limits

An animal with two ears can use three cues to locate a sound: the difference in arrival time (**interaural time difference**, ITD), the difference in sound level (**interaural level difference**, ILD), both defined in [7.14.42](/7.14.42#def-42.4.1), and the changes of the spectrum caused by the head, the pinnae or the feathers around the ears, which can be read with one ear if the spectrum of the sound is known. The availability of the binaural cues depends on body size.

The largest possible ITD is roughly the distance between the ears divided by the speed of sound, so it scales directly with head size (figure below): about $3\,\mathrm{ms}$ for an elephant, $0.66\,\mathrm{ms}$ for a human, $0.2\,\mathrm{ms}$ for a barn owl, $30\,\mathrm{\mu s}$ for a cricket, and $1.5\,\mathrm{\mu s}$ for the fly *Ormia*, whose tympana are half a millimeter apart. Humans resolve ITDs of about $10\,\mathrm{\mu s}$; for an animal whose maximal ITD is below this, timing alone gives little information. ILDs, in turn, require wavelengths shorter than the head, that is, high frequencies for small heads. A small animal therefore has three options: hear very high frequencies (small mammals), use a directional ear (pressure-difference receivers of insects, frogs and small birds), or move its receivers apart (spiders and insects that compare the vibrations reaching their legs).

![Head size limits the interaural time difference. The maximum ITD, reached for a source on the axis through the two ears, is about d/c (black line, speed of sound c = 343 m/s), where d is the effective acoustic distance between the ears. Points: approximate maximum ITDs of the fly Ormia ochracea (about 1.5 µs), a field cricket (about 30 µs), the Mongolian gerbil (about 135 µs), the barn owl (about 200 µs), humans (about 660 µs) and the African elephant (about 3 ms). Dashed lines: the smallest ITDs discriminated behaviorally by humans (about 10 µs) and by Ormia (about 50 ns, Mason and colleagues 2001), a hundred times below its largest external ITD.](./plots/itd-scaling.py)

Localization accuracy varies accordingly. Crickets and bush crickets, with pressure-difference ears, locate a singing male to within about 15–20° in azimuth; frogs to 15–30°; small songbirds to 20–30°; hawks to 2–12°; owls to 1–7°, and barn owls to 1–2° in both azimuth and elevation; humans to 1–5° in azimuth near the midline. Larger animals are generally more accurate, but the best accuracy is found in species whose way of life depends on it (Bradbury and Vehrencamp 2011).

### 11.4.2. The Barn Owl: From Behavior to Mechanism

The barn owl (*Tyto alba*) hunts small rodents at night, often in complete darkness and in grass or snow where the prey moves under cover. Its sound localization was analyzed first by behavioral experiments, which defined the problem, and then by recordings that found the neurons solving it; it is a textbook example of the neuroethological approach ([7.18.6](/7.18.6)).

The first experiments were done by Roger Payne (1971) at Cornell University. Owls trained to catch mice released on a floor covered with dry leaves in a light-tight room struck successfully in total darkness; in one series of 16 trials an owl struck every time at mice at least $4\,\mathrm{m}$ away and missed by no more than $5\,\mathrm{cm}$. To exclude other senses, Payne dragged a mouse-sized wad of paper through the leaves; the owl struck the paper, which excluded smell and infrared radiation (the paper had the temperature of the leaves). When the floor was covered with sand, on which a mouse walks silently, and the mouse towed a rustling leaf behind it, the owl struck the leaf and not the mouse. The owl locates prey by sound alone.

For precise measurements, Masakazu Konishi and Eric Knudsen used the owl's natural reaction to a sound: a rapid turn of the head toward the source. Owls were trained to stay on a perch and turn toward sounds from a movable loudspeaker, and a search coil mounted on the head, inside a magnetic field, measured the direction of the head to a fraction of a degree (Knudsen and colleagues 1979). The results established four facts.

- Accuracy and speed. Owls locate broadband noise to within about $1^\circ$–$2^\circ$ in both azimuth and elevation. The head turn starts about $60\,\mathrm{ms}$ after the onset of the sound and is equally accurate when the sound stops before the head starts to move: the owl computes the direction in advance and executes the turn without auditory feedback (open-loop control), although it may correct its course in flight if the prey continues to make noise.
- Frequencies. Accurate localization requires frequencies of about $5$–$9\,\mathrm{kHz}$, and about half of the owl's basilar papilla is devoted to the range of $5$–$10\,\mathrm{kHz}$ (Köppl and colleagues 1993), an acoustic fovea comparable to that of the horseshoe bat ([7.14.42](/7.14.42#sec-42.6.5)).
- Ear plugs. Partly blocking one ear produced errors mainly in elevation: with the left ear plugged, the owl turned its head above and slightly to the right of the source, with the right ear plugged below and to the left, and the tighter the plug the larger the error (Knudsen and Konishi 1979). Because a plug changes the level but not the timing of the sound, level differences must code elevation. The reason is anatomical. The owl's ear openings are asymmetric, the left one higher on the head and directed downward, the right one lower and directed upward, and the facial ruff of stiff feathers funnels high-frequency sound into them, so the right ear is more sensitive to sounds from above and the left to sounds from below.
- Earphones. Presenting sounds through earphones in the ear canals separates the two cues. A time difference alone made the owl turn its head mainly horizontally, toward the leading ear, and the turn grew with the difference; a level difference alone made it turn mainly vertically; combinations made it turn to the corresponding point in space (Moiseff and Konishi 1981). The owl uses ITD for azimuth and ILD for elevation, two independent coordinates.

**Example 11.4.1** An owl on a perch $5\,\mathrm{m}$ above the ground locates a mouse with an azimuthal error of $1^\circ$. How large is the error on the ground? In the simplest geometry the error is $x = h\tan\alpha = 5\,\mathrm{m}\times\tan 1^\circ \approx 9\,\mathrm{cm}$, comparable to the length of a mouse. Since the owl approaches its prey from above, closes in during the strike, and spreads its talons over several centimeters, this accuracy is sufficient for a catch, as Payne's misses of at most $5\,\mathrm{cm}$ showed.

:::

These behavioral results guided the search for the neural mechanism, which is described in [7.16.24](/7.16.24#sec-24.5.1). In brief: the auditory nerve encodes both the timing and the level of each frequency component; the two are separated into parallel pathways in the cochlear nuclei; ITDs are computed in the nucleus laminaris by coincidence detectors that receive inputs from the two ears through axons acting as delay lines, as the **Jeffress model** predicts ([7.16.24](/7.16.24#def-24.5.1)); ILDs are computed in a nucleus of the lateral lemniscus; the two pathways converge, ambiguities are removed by combining frequency channels, and in the external nucleus of the inferior colliculus neurons respond only to sounds from a small region of space, forming a map of auditory space (Knudsen and Konishi 1978). The map projects to the optic tectum, where it is aligned with the visual map of space, and the tectum drives the head turn: electrical stimulation of a tectal site turns the head toward the point in space represented there.

### 11.4.3. Vision Calibrates the Auditory Map

The relation between ITD and ILD on one side and a location in space on the other is not fixed by genes: it depends on the size and shape of the head and the facial ruff, which change during growth. How does the owl learn which cue values correspond to which direction? Knudsen and his colleagues answered the question by changing the relation between sight and sound.

In the first experiments, young owls wore an earplug in one ear for weeks. At first they made errors, but within weeks they localized accurately again; when the plug was removed, they erred in the opposite direction and readjusted slowly, provided they were young (Knudsen and colleagues 1984). The brain had adjusted the interpretation of the cues. To find what instructed the adjustment, Knudsen and Brainard (1991) fitted young owls with spectacles containing prisms that displaced the visual field by $23^\circ$ to the right, leaving the acoustic cues untouched. Over the following weeks, the auditory maps of the owls shifted to match the displaced visual world: the best ITDs of tectal neurons changed so that each site again responded to sounds coming from the place where its visual receptive field now looked (figure below), and the owls' head turns to sounds shifted by the same amount. The site of plasticity is the external nucleus of the inferior colliculus, where the auditory map is first formed, and the instructive signal comes from the optic tectum.

![Vision calibrates the barn owl's auditory space map (schematic after Knudsen and Brainard 1991 and Brainard and Knudsen 1998; illustrative data). A: each point is a site in the optic tectum, plotted by the azimuth of its visual receptive field (seen through the prisms) and its best ITD. In normal owls the best ITD changes by about 2.2 µs per degree of azimuth. The capacity to adjust is limited to a sensitive period. Owls fitted with prisms after about 200 days of age adjust little, whereas owls that adjusted when young retain the ability to switch between the normal and the shifted map as adults, as if the early experience had laid down an alternative set of connections that can be reactivated (Knudsen 1998). Adult plasticity can be enhanced in two ways: by shifting the visual field in small steps rather than all at once (Linkenhoker and Knudsen 2002), and by letting the owls hunt live prey rather than eat dead mice, which increased the adjustment about fivefold (Bergan and colleagues 2005). Behavioral relevance and attention gate learning in adults; the same principles of sensitive periods, instructive signals and experience-dependent maps appear in the development of song ([7.18.17](/7.18.17)) and in imprinting ([7.18.5](/7.18.5)).

### 11.4.4. Small Animals: Directional Ears and Coupled Tympana

Small insects face the localization problem in its most severe form. A female field cricket walks toward a singing male (phonotaxis, [7.18.16](/7.18.16)) using pressure-difference ears on her forelegs: the inner face of each tympanum receives sound through a tracheal tube from the spiracles on both sides of the thorax, and the delays of the internal paths are tuned so that each ear is strongly directional at the frequency of the male's song, near $5\,\mathrm{kHz}$. At other frequencies the directionality breaks down, which is one reason why females respond best to songs of the right frequency.

The most extreme solution is found in the parasitoid fly *Ormia ochracea*. The gravid female locates singing male crickets at night by their song and deposits larvae on and around them, which burrow into the cricket and eat it from inside within a week. Her two tympana, on the front of the thorax, are only about $0.5\,\mathrm{mm}$ apart, which gives a maximum external ITD of $1.5\,\mathrm{\mu s}$ and no measurable ILD at $5\,\mathrm{kHz}$, where the wavelength ($7\,\mathrm{cm}$) is a hundred times larger than the distance between the ears. Yet the fly turns toward a sound source with an accuracy of about $2^\circ$, comparable to humans, and discriminates sources whose external time difference is only about $50\,\mathrm{ns}$ (Mason and colleagues 2001).

Miles, Robert and Hoy (1995) discovered the mechanism. The two tympana are joined by a flexible cuticular bridge, so that they cannot move independently: their motion is the sum of two modes, a rocking mode in which they move in antiphase like a seesaw and a bending mode in which they move in phase. The mean of the two pressures drives the bending mode, and their tiny difference drives the rocking mode. At the frequency of the cricket song the rocking mode is close to its resonance and the bending mode is not, so the small difference is amplified relative to the mean. The tympana then vibrate with a time difference of tens of microseconds and a level difference of several decibels, cues large enough for the fly's receptor neurons, which in addition fire with exceptionally precise timing.

The model's numbers are illustrative, but the principle has been confirmed by laser measurements of the tympana and has been copied by engineers in miniature directional microphones for hearing aids. It shows a recurring theme of sensory ecology: when the physics of the stimulus does not provide a usable cue, the accessory structure of the sense organ can create one. A related solution, internal coupling of the two ears through the mouth cavity or an interaural canal, gives directional hearing to many frogs, lizards and small birds.

## Summary

- Mechanoreceptors gate ion channels by deformation; the accessory structure sets what they detect. Vertebrate **hair cells** and arthropod **chordotonal organs** serve hearing, balance, vibration and flow; **Johnston's organ** detects antennal movement.
- Near to a source, sound is particle motion, detected by light hairs and inertial otoliths; far from it, sound is pressure, detected by a tympanum over air. Near-field signals are private and allow low frequencies at short range.
- The star-nosed mole has a tactile fovea (appendages 11) that is magnified at each stage of the somatosensory pathway (7 % of receptors, 25 % of cortex) and supports handling times of about 230 ms.
- Many insects communicate by substrate vibration; the **lateral line** detects water flow and acceleration within one to two body lengths and supports rheotaxis, prey detection, schooling and mapping by blind cavefish; seals track hydrodynamic trails with their vibrissae.
- Pressure receivers are omnidirectional; pressure-difference receivers (Definition 11.3.1) are directional by themselves (Example 11.3.1: a 17 dB difference from an internal delay). Small animals use high frequencies, large ones low frequencies (optimal frequency proportional to about the −0.4 power of mass); ears are matched filters, sometimes seasonally tuned. Fish need a swim bladder to hear pressure.
- ITDs scale with head size (from 3 ms in elephants to 1.5 µs in *Ormia*); localization accuracy ranges from 1–2° in barn owls to 20–30° in small birds.
- Barn owl experiments (Payne; Konishi; Knudsen): prey are located by sound alone, within 1–2°, by an open-loop head turn; plugs and earphones show that ITD codes azimuth and ILD elevation (asymmetric ears); the neural map follows the **Jeffress model** and is aligned with vision in the tectum. Prism rearing shows that vision calibrates the auditory map during a sensitive period; adult plasticity is enhanced by small steps and hunting.
- *Ormia* amplifies a 1.5 µs external time difference about twentyfold with mechanically coupled tympana (rocking and bending modes) and resolves about 50 ns.

## Questions

1. Why can a fruit fly use a courtship song of a few hundred hertz, whereas a cricket of similar size calling to distant females must sing at several kilohertz?

   **Answer** The fly courts at a distance of a few millimeters, inside the near field of its wing vibration, and the female detects the particle motion with her arista and Johnston's organ, which are moved directly by the oscillating air; near-field signals need no efficient radiation of a pressure wave. The cricket must broadcast a far-field pressure wave to females meters away; an animal can radiate sound efficiently only at wavelengths not much larger than about twice its body size, and a small sound-producing structure is a poor radiator at low frequencies. High frequencies also allow females with small, pressure-difference ears to localize the male.

2. (Calculation) Using Eq. (11.1), show that a pressure-difference ear with internal delay $\tau = 0$ is not directional with respect to left and right, and find its response to sound from straight ahead.

   **Answer** With $\tau = 0$, $\lvert p_{\mathrm{net}}\rvert^2 = 1 + g^2 - 2g\cos(\omega\Delta)$. For a source on the right, $\Delta = d/c$; on the left, $\Delta = -d/c$; since cosine is even, the two give the same value, so the ear cannot tell left from right. From straight ahead, $\Delta = 0$ and $\lvert p_{\mathrm{net}}\rvert = 1 - g = 0.2$ for $g = 0.8$: the two faces receive nearly the same pressure and the membrane hardly moves. The figure-eight pattern of the dashed curve results. Directionality between left and right requires an internal delay (or a different internal gain at the two sides) that breaks the symmetry.

3. In Payne's experiments, the owl struck a paper wad dragged through leaves and a leaf towed by a mouse on sand. What alternative hypotheses did each experiment exclude, and what further control would you add?

   **Answer** The paper wad excluded smell (the paper did not smell of mouse), vision (complete darkness) and infrared radiation (the paper was at the temperature of the leaves). The leaf towed on sand dissociated the sound source from the mouse: the owl struck the source of the rustle, not the warm, smelly body a few centimeters away, which shows that sound alone determines the target. Further controls: test the owl with one ear plugged to show that binaural hearing is needed; vary the spectrum of the sound (low-pass filtered rustles) to show which frequencies are needed; and verify that the room was truly dark with infrared cameras.

4. Why was it important that the prisms in Knudsen and Brainard's experiment displaced the visual field without changing the acoustic cues, and what does the result say about which sense "teaches" the other?

   **Answer** Earplugs change the acoustic cues, so an adjustment after plugging could reflect auditory learning alone (for example, recalibration through comparison with other auditory cues or motor feedback). Prisms leave the acoustic cues untouched and change only vision; if the auditory map shifts, the shift must be instructed by vision. The result shows that during development vision acts as the teacher: the auditory space map, which depends on head geometry that changes with growth, is adjusted to agree with the visual map. This makes sense because the retina provides a direct, geometrically precise map of space, whereas auditory locations must be computed from cue values whose relation to space changes as the head grows.

5. (Open question) Female *Ormia* resolve sound directions with ears only 0.5 mm apart. Propose how you would test whether the mechanical coupling of the tympana is necessary for the fly's directional behavior.

   **Answer** First, measure the vibration of the two tympana with laser vibrometry while playing tones from different directions, to quantify the mechanical time and level differences in intact flies. Then disrupt the coupling, for example by cutting or stiffening the cuticular bridge (or by loading it with a small mass to change the rocking resonance), confirm with vibrometry that the mechanical differences have decreased, and test phonotaxis on a spherical treadmill toward speakers at different azimuths. If directional turning is lost or much less accurate after decoupling, while responses to sound per se persist (the fly still walks toward a single sound but cannot choose the side), coupling is necessary. Shifting the song frequency away from the rocking resonance should also degrade directionality, as the model predicts.
