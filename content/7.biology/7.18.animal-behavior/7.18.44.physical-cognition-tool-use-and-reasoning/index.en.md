To reason is to go beyond what is immediately given: to use a stick to reach food that cannot be reached, to infer that an object will fall where a tube leads rather than straight down, or to conclude that if A dominates B and B dominates C, then A will dominate C. This chapter treats *physical cognition*, the ways animals represent and use objects, forces and relations, and the closely related reasoning about orders and exchanges. Many of these abilities were long held to be uniquely human; the last decades have shown them, in some form, in apes and monkeys, corvids and parrots, and even in fish and insects. But the same decades have shown how often a performance that looks like reasoning can be produced by the associative learning of [7.18.41](/7.18.41), applied to a history of experience that the experimenter does not know. The central theme of the chapter is therefore methodological: how to tell understanding from learned rules. The concepts and relations on which reasoning builds are in [7.18.43](/7.18.43), reasoning about the minds of others in [7.18.45](/7.18.45), and learning from others in [7.18.46](/7.18.46).

## 44.1. Tool Use

### 44.1.1. What Counts as a Tool

Tool use was once offered as a definition of humanity ("man the tool-maker"). When Jane Goodall reported in 1960 that chimpanzees at Gombe strip twigs and poke them into termite mounds to fish out termites, Louis Leakey replied that we must now redefine "tool", redefine "man", or accept chimpanzees as humans. Biologists chose the first option, and the definition matters, because many animals manipulate objects in ways that resemble tool use.

**Definition 44.1.1 (Tool Use)** **Tool use** is the use of an external, unattached (or manipulable attached) object to alter more efficiently the form, position or condition of another object, another organism or the user itself, where the user holds or directly manipulates the object during or just before use and is responsible for its proper orientation (Beck 1980; Shumaker, Walkup and Beck 2011). *Tool manufacture* is any modification of an object that makes it serve better as a tool: detaching it, removing parts, reshaping it or combining several objects.

The definition excludes several familiar behaviors. A gull that drops a clam onto rocks, and a song thrush (*Turdus philomelos*) that smashes a snail against a stone "anvil", use an object, but the rock is not held or oriented; these are usually classed as proto-tool use. A bird's nest is a structure, not a tool, and building material is not a tool any more than wool is a tool in knitting. In contrast, an Egyptian vulture (*Neophron percnopterus*) that picks up a stone in its beak and throws it at an ostrich egg until the shell cracks uses a tool, and so does a sea otter (*Enhydra lutris*) that floats on its back and hammers mussels on a stone held on its chest, carrying the stone under its arm between dives.

The definition is about the mechanics of an act, not about the cognition behind it, and tool use spans a wide range of flexibility. Larval antlions (Neuroptera) flick sand at prey slipping down the walls of their pit; the archerfish (*Toxotes*) knocks insects off overhanging leaves with a jet of water, a behavior that some definitions count as tool use and others do not. These behaviors are performed by all members of the species, with little variation, and need not involve any representation of the relation between tool and goal. Other definitions therefore add criteria of flexibility: an animal that selects among objects, transports a tool to where it will be needed, or modifies it for the task is more likely to represent what the tool does. The question of what the animal understands is taken up in Section 44.2.

### 44.1.2. Tool Use in Primates

Chimpanzees (*Pan troglodytes*) are the most versatile tool users after humans. Across the African populations studied, they use tools in some 40 different ways, and the repertoires of different populations differ in ways that are largely cultural ([7.18.46](/7.18.46)).

- *Fishing and dipping.* At Gombe and elsewhere chimpanzees select grass stems or twigs, strip them of leaves, insert them into the exit holes of termite mounds and withdraw them with termites clinging to them. In the Goualougo Triangle of the Republic of Congo, they use *tool sets*: a stout stick to puncture an underground termite nest, followed by a thin probe whose end they fray with their teeth into a brush that collects more termites (Sanz, Call and Morgan 2009). Driver ants are harvested with long wands ("ant dipping"), with techniques that differ between populations.
- *Nut cracking.* In the Taï Forest of Côte d'Ivoire and at Bossou in Guinea, chimpanzees crack hard-shelled nuts with a wooden or stone hammer on a root or stone anvil, carrying hammers up to several hundred meters to nut trees; suitable stones are scarce. Young chimpanzees take years to master the skill, learning mainly between three and five years of age, and individuals who have not learned by about seven rarely do (Inoue-Nakamura and Matsuzawa 1997). Stone hammers with starch residues of the same nut species, excavated in Côte d'Ivoire, show that chimpanzees have cracked nuts there for at least 4300 years (Mercader and colleagues 2007): there is a chimpanzee archaeology.
- *Hunting.* At Fongoli in Senegal, chimpanzees, mostly females and juveniles, sharpen branches with their teeth and jab them into tree hollows in which bushbabies sleep (Pruetz and Bertolani 2007).

Stone tool use has evolved independently in several monkey lineages. Bearded capuchin monkeys (*Sapajus libidinosus*) in the dry forests of Brazil crack palm nuts by lifting stones as heavy as themselves and dropping them onto nuts placed in pits on a stone anvil (Fragaszy and colleagues 2004); excavations at Serra da Capivara have uncovered capuchin stone tools spanning some 3000 years, with changes in tool size over time (Falótico and colleagues 2019). Long-tailed macaques (*Macaca fascicularis*) on islands off Thailand pound oysters and snails with stones selected for size, using a different grip for different prey (Gumert, Kluck and Malaivijitnond 2009). In both cases, as in chimpanzees, tool use occurs in only some populations of a widespread species, and it depends on the availability of hard foods and stones and on time spent on the ground.

### 44.1.3. Tool Use in Birds and Other Animals

The New Caledonian crow (*Corvus moneduloides*), a corvid endemic to the islands of New Caledonia, makes and uses the most complex tools known outside primates. Gavin Hunt (1996) described two kinds. *Hooked stick tools* are made from forked twigs: the crow breaks off a twig just below a side branch, trims it and sculpts the remnant of the branch into a hook, which it uses to pull insect larvae and other prey from holes. *Pandanus tools* are cut from the barbed edges of the leaves of *Pandanus* trees; the crows tear them into narrow, wide or stepped shapes, and the designs differ between regions of the island in a pattern that suggests cumulative cultural change ([7.18.46](/7.18.46)). In "larva fishing", a crow inserts a straight stick into a borehole until the beetle larva bites it and is pulled out (Bluff and colleagues 2010). Juveniles develop basic tool use without seeing adults use tools, but they spend a long period with their parents, during which they pick up discarded tools and become more proficient; adults use tools much more efficiently than young birds. The crows have a straight bill and unusually large binocular overlap, which allows them to see the tip of a tool held in the beak, a morphological adaptation for tool use (Troscianko and colleagues 2012).

Tool use in birds is not confined to corvids. The woodpecker finch (*Camarhynchus pallidus*) of the Galápagos probes for insect larvae with cactus spines and twigs, which it breaks to the right length. Sabine Tebbich and colleagues (2001) showed that young finches develop the behavior whether or not they see adults using tools, and that adult finches of other species do not learn it by watching tool users: in this species tool use develops through a predisposition to manipulate twigs combined with individual learning, not by social learning. Its frequency is highest in the arid zone, where prey are hard to reach in the dry season. The Hawaiian crow (*Corvus hawaiiensis*), now extinct in the wild, turned out to be a skilled tool user when the whole remaining population was tested in captivity (Rutz and colleagues 2016). Goffin's cockatoos (*Cacatua goffiniana*) made tools in captivity: a male named Figaro cut splinters from a wooden beam and used them as rakes (Auersperg and colleagues 2012), and wild cockatoos were later found to use sets of up to three tools to extract seeds from a fruit (O'Hara and colleagues 2021).

Among invertebrates, veined octopuses (*Amphioctopus marginatus*) in Indonesia dig up discarded coconut shell halves, clean them and carry them stacked under the body, walking awkwardly on stiffened arms ("stilt-walking"), and later assemble them into a shelter (Finn, Tregenza and Norman 2009). The tool is carried for future use, at a present cost. Hermit crabs place sea anemones on their shells, which protect them against octopuses; funnel ants (*Aphaenogaster*) drop bits of leaf and soil into liquid food and carry the soaked debris back to the nest, and they select materials that absorb well; thread-waisted wasps (*Ammophila*) hold a pebble in the mandibles and use it to tamp the soil closing the burrow in which an egg and paralyzed caterpillar lie. Bottlenose dolphins (*Tursiops* sp.) in Shark Bay, Western Australia, break off marine sponges and wear them over the rostrum while probing the sandy bottom for fish; *sponging* is performed mostly by females, it is learned from the mother, and almost all spongers belong to one matriline (Krützen and colleagues 2005).

### 44.1.4. The Distribution and Evolution of Tool Use

Tool use is phylogenetically scattered: it occurs in a few species of many orders, and in only some populations within species (figure below). It has evolved by **convergent evolution** many times, and its comparative study poses two questions: what ecological conditions favor it, and what cognitive capacities it requires.

![Tool use has evolved many times. A cladogram (topology only) of a selection of tool-using animals, with the best-documented tool behavior of each; bars mark tool use and stars tool manufacture. The tool users are separated by many lineages of non-tool-using relatives (not shown): tool use is not a shared ancestral trait of birds, mammals or invertebrates, but has arisen independently in each lineage.](./plots/tool-phylogeny.py)

The ecological correlates are fairly consistent. Tool use is most common in *extractive foragers*, animals that eat embedded foods, such as insect larvae in wood, nuts in shells and seeds in hard fruits, and it often appears where competitors that extract such foods anatomically are absent: there are no woodpeckers on New Caledonia or the Galápagos, and a crow or a finch with a twig fills the vacant niche. Time on the ground, the availability of raw materials and the profitability of the hidden food add to the picture.

The cognitive correlates are more contested. Across birds, the frequency of reported tool use and of feeding innovations in general increases with the relative size of the forebrain, after phylogeny and research effort are controlled (Lefebvre, Nicolakakis and Boire 2002); across primates, innovation, tool use and social learning are correlated with each other and with the size of the neocortex and striatum (Reader and Laland 2002). Brain size, however, predicts the flexibility of tool use rather than its occurrence: archerfish, antlions and woodpecker finches show that elaborate tool use can be specialized and largely predisposed. Tool use is therefore not by itself evidence of intelligence; what matters is how flexibly the tool is selected, made and applied to new problems.

## 44.2. Understanding or Association?

### 44.2.1. The Trap-Tube Problem

Does a chimpanzee using a stick understand how sticks move food, or has it learned by trial and error which actions are rewarded? Elisabetta Visalberghi and Luca Limongelli (1994) devised a test. A transparent horizontal tube contains a treat near one end and a trap, a hole in the floor, in the middle. A monkey given a stick of the right diameter can push the treat out of the tube, but if it pushes from the wrong end the treat falls into the trap. Of four capuchin monkeys, only one learned within 140 trials to insert the stick at the end farther from the treat. When the tube was then rotated so that the trap pointed upward and was harmless, the monkey continued to avoid it: it had learned the rule "push from the end farther from the food" without representing the trap's effect. Two of five chimpanzees solved the task, and one of them, Sheba, passed a version in which the trap was moved off center, so that the rule failed (Limongelli, Boysen and Visalberghi 1995).

Later studies refined the design so that rules based on the arrangement of the apparatus could be distinguished from an understanding of its causal properties. Rooks (*Corvus frugilegus*), which do not use tools in the wild, were tested with a tube with two traps, of which only one was functional (its bottom was open or closed), so that they had to attend to the features of the traps; seven of eight birds learned it, and one transferred immediately to new configurations (Seed and colleagues 2006). New Caledonian crows that had learned a trap-tube task transferred to a *trap-table*, a visually different apparatus with the same causal structure, in which a reward had to be raked past a hole rather than pushed (Taylor and colleagues 2009). Transfer between perceptually different problems that share causal structure is the best available evidence that something more abstract than a stimulus–response rule has been learned.

### 44.2.2. Strings, Tubes and Meta-Tools

Other paradigms ask whether animals represent connections, gravity and the functions of objects:

- *String pulling.* Food hangs from one of several strings or lies at the end of one of several strings on a table. When the strings are parallel, pulling the string directly above or in front of the food works; when they cross, or when one string is broken, the animal must follow the connection. Bernd Heinrich (1995) found that some naive ravens (*Corvus corax*) pulled up meat hung on a string from a perch by a coordinated sequence of pulling with the beak and stepping on the loop, on the first trial. Corvids solve crossed-string problems, with large individual differences; New Caledonian crows, however, fail when they cannot see the food move as they pull, which suggests that they use visual feedback from each pull rather than a model of connectivity (Taylor and colleagues 2010). Domestic cats failed even with two parallel strings, and dogs prefer the string end closest to the food regardless of its connection (Osthaus, Lea and Slater 2005; Whitt and colleagues 2009).
- *Gravity bias.* Cotton-top tamarins watched an object dropped into an opaque tube that led to one of three cups, never the one directly below. They searched directly below the point of entry, as two-year-old children do, rather than at the end of the tube (Hood and colleagues 1999); domestic dogs behave the same way (Osthaus, Slater and Lea 2003). The expectation that things fall straight down is a powerful default that is hard to override.
- *Meta-tools.* Alex Taylor and colleagues (2007) presented New Caledonian crows with food that could be reached only with a long stick, which was itself locked in a cage and could be extracted only with a short stick; the crows used the short stick to get the long stick and the long stick to get the food, many on the first trial, and later studies extended the sequence to three tools. Crows also combine elements into compound tools (von Bayern and colleagues 2018).
- *Context-dependent use.* Crows that never used a stick to take food only 1 cm behind bars did so when the food lay next to a novel object, and especially next to a model snake: they used the tool to keep their distance from potential danger (Taylor, Hunt and Gray 2012).

None of these tests settles whether animals have a "folk physics" (Daniel Povinelli's term) of weight, connection and support; the more modest conclusion is that some species learn causal regularities quickly and transfer them across problems, while others learn arbitrary rules that fail when the problem changes.

## 44.3. Insight

### 44.3.1. Köhler's Chimpanzees

Wolfgang Köhler directed the Prussian Academy's anthropoid research station on Tenerife, where he was stranded during the First World War, and set his chimpanzees problems that required detours, tools and the combination of objects. In the most famous, bananas hung from the roof out of reach and the enclosure contained wooden boxes. After vainly jumping, the chimpanzee Sultan paced up and down, suddenly stopped in front of a box, dragged it toward the bananas, climbed on it and seized them; other chimpanzees later stacked two or three boxes. In another problem Sultan had two hollow bamboo sticks, each too short to reach fruit outside the bars. After an hour of unsuccessful attempts he gave up and played with the sticks, and when he happened to fit the thinner into the thicker he ran to the bars and raked in the fruit with the joined stick.

**Definition 44.3.1 (Insight)** **Insight** is the solution of a novel problem by a sudden reorganization of behavior, not by a gradual acquisition through trial and error during the test: the animal combines previously separate experiences or behaviors into a new sequence appropriate to the problem, often after a pause, and the solution is then repeated without errors.

Köhler emphasized the abrupt transition from failure to success and argued that the apes saw new relations among the elements of the problem as a whole, in the spirit of the Gestalt psychology he helped to found. Critics noted that little was recorded of the apes' previous experience: they had played for months with boxes and sticks, and Köhler himself had once put his finger into a bamboo tube in Sultan's presence. Trial and error may have occurred earlier, unobserved. Sixty years later, Donald Premack and Guy Woodruff (1978) showed the chimpanzee Sarah videotapes of a person failing to reach bananas, for example ones hung from the ceiling with a box nearby, and offered her photographs of possible solutions; she chose the photograph of the person standing on the box, or of the appropriate tool, on 21 of 24 tests. The result shows that Sarah recognized solutions, but again not how she came to.

### 44.3.2. Epstein's Pigeons

Robert Epstein and colleagues (1984) asked what experience would produce Sultan's performance in an animal not credited with insight. Pigeons were trained separately (1) to climb on a small box and peck a toy banana hung overhead, and (2) to push a box toward a green spot placed at random positions on the floor; flying or jumping at the banana was not rewarded. When a trained pigeon was put in a chamber with the banana overhead, the box at a distance and no spot, it appeared confused for a minute, stretching toward the banana and looking from banana to box, and then pushed the box under the banana, climbed on it and pecked. Pigeons that had learned only to climb and peck, or that had learned to push the box but not toward a target, failed. The "insightful" solution emerged from the interconnection of separately learned repertoires, through the generalization of pushing toward a target from the spot to the banana.

The experiment is sometimes dismissed as showing only that pigeons can be trained to mimic insight. Its real point is different: for once, the animals' complete history with the elements of the problem was known, and knowing it removed the apparent mystery. Insight, on this view, is not an alternative to learning but a description of how previously learned behaviors combine in a new situation; the cognitive question is how far an animal can generalize from its experience, and how much experience it needs.

### 44.3.3. Bending Wire and the Aesop's Fable Paradigm

In 2002 a New Caledonian crow named Betty, in a test of whether crows preferred hooked or straight wire tools for lifting a small bucket from a vertical tube, was left with only a straight wire after her partner flew off with the hook. She wedged one end of the wire and bent it into a hook, and used it to lift the bucket; given only straight wire in later trials, she bent it in nine of ten (Weir, Chappell and Kacelnik 2002). Wire is not found in nature, and the observation was widely taken as evidence of insightful tool manufacture. Later work showed that wild crows bend twigs as part of their normal manufacture of hooked tools, so the behavior may draw on a natural motor routine; Betty's flexibility in applying it to a new material is nonetheless remarkable.

Aesop's fable of the thirsty crow that dropped pebbles into a pitcher until the water rose within reach inspired a family of experiments. Christopher Bird and Nathan Emery (2009) gave four captive rooks a transparent tube with a wax worm floating on water out of reach and a pile of stones; all four dropped stones into the tube until they could reach the worm, and they used larger stones when they were available. New Caledonian crows learned the task more slowly, after stones had first fallen into the tube accidentally (Taylor and colleagues 2011), but then discriminated among the options in ways that respect Archimedes' principle: they dropped sinking rather than floating objects, solid rather than hollow ones, and chose a tube of water over a tube of sand (Jelbert and colleagues 2014). Eurasian jays and great-tailed grackles have also been tested; children pass similar tests from about seven years of age.

![The Aesop's fable paradigm and the physics behind it. A: a floating food item lies on water at the bottom of a transparent tube, below the reach of the beak; dropping stones raises the water. B: water level against the number of objects dropped, from Archimedes' principle: each sinking object of volume $v$ raises the level in a tube of radius $r$ by $v/(\pi r^2)$, and a floating object of relative density $\rho$ displaces only $\rho v$. With objects of 6 cm³, an initial level of 4 cm and food reachable at 10 cm, 8 stones suffice in a narrow tube (radius 1.5 cm) but 20 are needed in a wide one (radius 2.5 cm), and foam (density 0.05) is almost useless.](./plots/aesop.py)

The model in the figure defines what a bird with causal understanding should prefer. But the birds' choices could also be learned during the experiment: each stone dropped either brings the food closer or not, which is exactly the feedback that **operant conditioning** uses. Stefano Ghirlanda and Johan Lind (2017) reanalyzed the published data and found that birds generally chose randomly on the first trials and improved with experience, the pattern expected from learning by trial and error rather than from prior understanding. The crows also failed a U-tube version in which dropping stones into one arm raised the water in a connected arm, a counterintuitive mechanism for which no amount of folk physics would suffice. The paradigm is now used less to demonstrate insight than to measure how quickly different species learn causal regularities.

## 44.4. Reasoning about Relations

### 44.4.1. Analogy

To recognize that "dog is to puppy as cat is to kitten" is to see that the relation within one pair is the same as the relation within another, a relation between relations. Douglas Gillan, Premack and Woodruff (1981) tested Sarah, who had been taught to use plastic tokens as words, including a token meaning "same". She was shown two objects, the "same" token and a third object, and had to choose the fourth object that completed the analogy. With geometric forms she completed analogies of size, color and shape; with household objects she answered "padlock is to key as tin can is to ...?" with a can opener rather than a paintbrush, and, given paper and pencil as the first pair, chose the paintbrush for the can. She was correct on 15 of 18 such trials.

The laboratory version of the problem is *relational matching to sample*: given a sample pair of identical items (AA), the subject must choose the identical pair (BB) rather than the different pair (CD) among new items. It is the relational counterpart of the **same–different concept** ([7.18.43](/7.18.43)), and it was long thought to require language or token training. Hooded crows (*Corvus cornix*) trained only on ordinary identity matching chose relationally matching pairs spontaneously on their first trials (Smirnova and colleagues 2015), and baboons and pigeons have learned relational matching with extensive training.

### 44.4.2. Transitive Inference

If Mary is taller than Susan and Susan is taller than Jane, then Mary is taller than Jane: the relation "taller than" is a **transitive relation** ([1.2.2](/1.2.2)), and an agent who knows this can infer the relation between items never compared.

**Definition 44.4.1 (Transitive Inference)** **Transitive inference** is the ability to infer the relation between two items that have never been presented together, from their relations to other items in a series: having learned A > B, B > C, C > D and D > E, the subject chooses B over D at the first presentation of that pair.

Brendan McGonigle and Margaret Chalmers (1977) devised the nonverbal test. Squirrel monkeys were trained on four overlapping pairs of colored containers, a peanut under the rewarded member of each pair: A+B−, B+C−, C+D−, D+E−, at first separately and then intermixed. The critical test is the novel pair BD: B and D were each rewarded in one pair and unrewarded in another, so their history of reward is equal, whereas pairs including A (always rewarded) or E (never rewarded) can be solved without inference. Five items are the minimum for an informative test. The monkeys preferred B. Pigeons (von Fersen and colleagues 1991), rats (Davis 1992), several corvids, cichlid fish and paper wasps (Tibbetts and colleagues 2019) have since passed the test; honeybees so far have not (Benard and Giurfa 2004). Performance typically shows two further regularities: accuracy is higher for pairs farther apart in the series (the *symbolic distance effect*: BE is easier than BD), and, among the trained pairs, the end pairs AB and DE are learned better than the inner ones.

Why should pigeons, rats and wasps share an ability once credited only to children above a certain age? The ecological answer is social: an animal in a group with a dominance hierarchy that can infer ranks from a few observed encounters avoids many costly fights. With 12 group members there are 66 pairs; observing the 11 encounters between neighbors in rank is enough for an animal that can infer transitively. Alan Bond, Alan Kamil and Russell Balda (2003) compared two corvids: highly social pinyon jays (*Gymnorhinus cyanocephalus*), which live in flocks of hundreds, learned a seven-item series faster and more accurately than the less social western scrub jays. Pinyon jays that watched a stranger defeat a bird that dominated them were more deferential to the stranger than jays that saw it defeat a bird they themselves dominated (Paz-y-Miño and colleagues 2004). Males of the cichlid *Astatotilapia burtoni*, which compete for territories, watched fights between pairs of other males arranged so that A beat B, B beat C and so on; in later choice tests they spent more time near the weaker of two males they had never seen fight each other, including the critical pair (Grosenick, Clement and Fernald 2007). *Polistes* paper wasps, whose queens form dominance hierarchies, also learned a five-item series and chose correctly in untrained pairs, whereas honeybees, whose workers do not compete for rank, did not (Tibbetts and colleagues 2019).

The mechanism is less clear than the function. A choice of B over D could rest on an ordered mental representation of the series, a "mental line" on which positions are compared; or it could arise from differences in associative value acquired during training. Associative explanations work because the experiences of the items are not symmetric even when their programmed reinforcement is. In the AB pair, A is always rewarded and the animal soon chooses it almost always, so B is rarely chosen and rarely unrewarded; in the CD pair, C has only intermediate value, D is chosen more often, and D accumulates more nonreward than B. Models in which only the chosen item's value is updated, or in which value transfers from the rewarded item to its partner, predict a preference for B over D and the symbolic distance effect (von Fersen and colleagues 1991; Wynne 1995).

![Two models of transitive inference in the five-term series (A+B−, B+C−, C+D−, D+E−, intermixed). Associative model: each stimulus has a value $V$; the animal chooses between two stimuli by a **softmax function** of their values (inverse temperature $\beta = 15$), and only the chosen stimulus is updated, $V \leftarrow V + \alpha(r - V)$ with $\alpha = 0.05$ and $r = 1$ for a correct choice (4000 training trials, mean of 200 simulated subjects). Both models predict a preference for B in the critical pair BD and higher accuracy for more distant pairs; the associative model also predicts that the inner trained pairs, especially CD, are learned worst, whereas the ordinal model predicts equal accuracy for all adjacent pairs.](./plots/transitive.py)

The two kinds of model differ in other predictions. Associative values depend on the reinforcement history of each item, so altering the reinforcement of one item after training (for example, extra reward for D) should change BD choices, whereas an ordinal representation should be insensitive to it. Studies with pigeons generally support value-based accounts, whereas results with monkeys and humans are better described by ordinal representations, and animals may use both. Neurons in the lateral prefrontal cortex of monkeys encode the serial position of items in learned sequences, consistent with a representation of order (Ninokura, Mushiake and Tanji 2004).

### 44.4.3. Linear Ordering

A related task gives very different results. Herbert Terrace (1993) trained pigeons to peck five colors, displayed simultaneously at random positions among eight keys, in a fixed order (A → B → C → D → E); errors ended the trial. The pigeons needed about 120 sessions to master the five-item chain. When they were then tested on pairs of items from the list, they were accurate on any pair containing the first item (A) or the last (E), but near chance on interior pairs such as BC, CD and BD, even those adjacent in training. Capuchin monkeys learned the same task faster and were accurate on all pairs, including BD (D'Amato and Colombo 1988).

Terrace proposed that the pigeons learned a chain of associations: "when the lights come on, peck A", "after A, peck B", "after B, peck C" and so on, with E being pecked last because nothing else remains. Rules that begin with A cannot be activated when A is absent, which explains the failure on interior pairs. The monkeys appear to have represented the position of each item in the list, which can be accessed without running through the chain. Linear ordering and transitive inference thus show the same contrast as the trap tube: similar performances under training can rest on different representations, which only transfer tests reveal.

## 44.5. Fairness and Inequity Aversion

In human societies people care not only about what they get but also about what others get compared with them. Do other animals?

**Definition 44.5.1 (Inequity Aversion)** **Inequity aversion** is a negative reaction to an unequal distribution of rewards. *Disadvantageous* inequity aversion is a reaction to receiving less than a partner for the same effort, such as refusing a reward that would otherwise be accepted; *advantageous* inequity aversion is a reaction to receiving more than a partner.

Sarah Brosnan and Frans de Waal (2003) trained brown capuchin monkeys to exchange a token with an experimenter for a piece of cucumber, which they readily accepted, or a grape, which they preferred. Two monkeys in adjacent cages exchanged in turn. When both received cucumber, they almost always accepted it; when the partner received a grape for the same token, the subject more often refused the cucumber, sometimes throwing it out of the cage; and when the partner received a grape without having to exchange, refusals were most frequent. In a control condition the grape was visible but given to no one; refusals increased less.

The study prompted much debate. Critics pointed out that seeing a grape, even one that no one else eats, could raise the subject's expectation of a better reward and make cucumber less acceptable (Roma and colleagues 2006): the refusal could be an effect of frustrated expectation, a familiar phenomenon of learning (successive negative contrast), rather than of social comparison. Later experiments that controlled for expectation found a social effect in capuchins and chimpanzees under some conditions but not others, and the effect was absent in males in the original study. Domestic dogs stopped giving the paw on command when a partner dog was rewarded and they were not, but did not react to differences in reward quality (Range and colleagues 2009); in another test, dogs preferred a trainer who rewarded another dog generously over one who rewarded fairly (Horowitz 2012). Carrion crows and ravens reduced exchanges when a partner received better food (Wascher and Bugnyar 2013). Brosnan and de Waal (2014) concluded that reactions to inequity are most likely in species that cooperate with unrelated individuals, in which comparing payoffs helps to choose and keep partners ([7.18.34](/7.18.34)); disadvantageous inequity aversion has been reported far more often than the advantageous kind, which is known mainly in humans and, inconsistently, in chimpanzees.

Economists formalized inequity aversion with a simple utility function.

**Formula 44.5.1 (Fehr–Schmidt Utility)** For two individuals with payoffs $x_i$ and $x_j$, the utility of $i$ is

$$
U_i = x_i - a\,\max(x_j - x_i,\, 0) - b\,\max(x_i - x_j,\, 0), \qquad a \ge b \ge 0,
$$
where $a$ measures aversion to disadvantageous and $b$ aversion to advantageous inequity (Fehr and Schmidt 1999).

The formula makes clear why the capuchins' refusals are puzzling. In the exchange task the partner keeps its grape whatever the subject does, so refusing the cucumber lowers $x_i$ and increases $x_j - x_i$: for any $a \ge 0$ it reduces utility. Refusal pays only when it reduces the partner's payoff as well, as in the *ultimatum game*, in which one player proposes a division of a sum and the other can accept it or reject it, in which case neither gets anything. Humans typically reject offers below about 20–30 % of the sum, as $U_i$ with $a > 0$ predicts. Chimpanzees tested in a mechanical version of the game accepted almost any nonzero offer (Jensen, Call and Tomasello 2007), behaving as "rational maximizers". In animals, the refusals of the exchange paradigm may function, if they function at all, as a signal in a repeated relationship (to an experimenter, a partner or a group member), or they may be the frustration of an unmet expectation. The question is open, and its answer depends on careful controls for expectation, effort and the presence of the partner.

## 44.6. Interpreting Animal Reasoning

The studies of this chapter show a recurring pattern. A striking observation in the field or a first experiment suggests understanding; controlled experiments find that much or all of the performance can be explained by associative learning, applied to a history of experience; and in the best cases, transfer tests reveal that some species do represent more than the trained contingencies. **Morgan's canon** ([7.18.1](/7.18.1)) and the lesson of Clever Hans apply throughout: before attributing reasoning, the simpler explanations must be excluded, and unintended cues from experimenters must be prevented. Morgan's canon is not a claim that animals never reason; it is a rule about the order in which hypotheses are tested.

Two further cautions concern the pattern of results across species. First, there is no simple ranking of intelligence. Pigeons can solve Köhler's box problem, given the right history; rats form transitive inferences; and corvids, with brains organized very differently from those of primates (the avian pallium, [7.18.8](/7.18.8)), match apes in many physical-cognition tasks, an example of convergent cognitive evolution (Emery and Clayton 2004). Second, the most adaptive problems are not always solved. Dorothy Cheney and Robert Seyfarth (1990) placed a stuffed gazelle carcass in a tree near the sleeping site of vervet monkeys; leopards cache their kills in trees, and the monkeys certainly fear leopards and give specific **alarm calls** to them ([7.18.40](/7.18.40)). Yet the monkeys showed no alarm and no increased vigilance, and they ignored fresh python tracks in the same way. A large-brained primate with a sophisticated communication system failed to draw an inference of obvious importance, while a pigeon could be brought to a performance that in a chimpanzee is called insight. Cognition is shaped by the problems that a species' ancestors actually had to solve, and by the information they actually used, not by a general scale of reasoning power.

Novel behavior can itself be studied. Bottlenose dolphins trained to produce, on the cue "vary", a behavior they had not performed earlier in the session, produced entirely new behaviors in a fifth to a half of their responses (Kuczaj and Eskelinen 2014), extending a classic study in which a rough-toothed dolphin was reinforced only for novel responses (Pryor, Haag and O'Reilly 1969). Innovation and creativity, like insight, are ultimately questions about how animals generate new combinations of behavior and select those that work.

## Summary

- Tool use (Definition 44.1.1) is the use of an unattached object, held and oriented by the user, to act on another object or organism; tool manufacture modifies the object. Chimpanzees fish for termites, use tool sets and crack nuts (an archaeology of at least 4300 years); capuchins and long-tailed macaques use stones; New Caledonian crows make hooked and pandanus tools; woodpecker finches, cockatoos, sea otters, dolphins, octopuses, ants and wasps use tools.
- Tool use evolved convergently many times; it is favored in extractive foragers lacking anatomical specialists, and its flexibility (not its occurrence) correlates with relative brain size. Some tool use is predisposed and inflexible.
- Trap-tube, trap-table, string-pulling, gravity and meta-tool tests distinguish learned rules from causal representations; transfer to perceptually different problems with the same causal structure is the strongest evidence.
- Insight (Definition 44.3.1): Köhler's chimpanzees stacked boxes and joined sticks; Epstein's pigeons combined two separately learned behaviors into the same solution. Betty bent wire into hooks; rooks and crows drop stones to raise water, choosing sinking and solid objects, but reanalysis shows that much of the performance is learned during testing.
- Sarah solved analogies; hooded crows match relations spontaneously. Transitive inference (Definition 44.4.1) is shown by the BD test in monkeys, pigeons, rats, corvids, cichlids and paper wasps; it is linked to dominance hierarchies (pinyon jays, cichlids, wasps) and can be explained by associative values or ordinal representations, which make different predictions. In linear ordering, pigeons learn associative chains, monkeys positional representations.
- Inequity aversion (Definition 44.5.1) in capuchins, dogs and corvids is debated because of expectation effects; the Fehr–Schmidt utility (Formula 44.5.1) shows that refusal in the exchange task lowers utility, and chimpanzees accept unfair offers in the ultimatum game.
- **Morgan's canon** guides interpretation; cognitive abilities are not ranked on a single scale, and even primates fail at ecologically obvious inferences.

## Questions

1. Under Definition 44.1.1, which of the following are tool use: (a) a gull dropping a whelk on rocks; (b) an Egyptian vulture throwing a stone at an ostrich egg; (c) a bird building a nest; (d) a chimpanzee leaf-sponging water from a tree hollow? Explain.

   **Answer** (a) is not tool use, because the rock is not held or oriented by the bird (it is a substrate; the gull moves the prey). (b) is tool use: the stone is unattached, held and directed at the egg to change its condition. (c) is not, because the nest material becomes a structure and is not used to act on another object. (d) is tool use, and the chewing of leaves to make them absorbent is tool manufacture.
2. Why was the inverted trap tube an important control in Visalberghi and Limongelli's study, and what result would have indicated causal understanding?

   **Answer** With the trap facing upward, it can no longer capture the treat, so pushing from either end works. A monkey that understood why the trap was dangerous should stop avoiding it; one that had learned a rule based on the position of the treat ("push from the far end") would continue to follow it. The capuchin that had learned the task continued to avoid the harmless trap, revealing a rule. Causal understanding would be indicated by indifference to the end used when the trap is inverted, and by immediate success when the trap is moved off center.
3. In Epstein's experiment, which control groups were necessary to show that both trained behaviors were required for the "insightful" solution, and why does this experiment not show that chimpanzees lack insight?

   **Answer** Pigeons trained only to climb and peck, and pigeons trained to push the box but not toward a target, both failed, which shows that each component, including directed pushing, was needed. The experiment shows that the behavior of solving the box problem can arise from the combination of learned components; it does not show how chimpanzees solved it. It shows that claims of insight require knowledge of the animal's prior experience, which Köhler lacked, and it reframes insight as the novel combination of learned behaviors.
4. Using the model in the Aesop's fable figure, calculate how many 4 cm³ stones a crow needs to raise water from 3 cm to 9 cm in a tube of radius 2 cm. If a crow chose randomly between a narrow and a wide tube on the first trial but later preferred the narrow one, what would this suggest?

   **Answer** Each stone raises the level by $4/(\pi \cdot 2^2) \approx 0.32$ cm, so a rise of 6 cm requires $6/0.32 \approx 18.8$, that is 19 stones. Random choice at first and a later preference suggest that the crow learned from the consequences of its own drops (the food rose faster in one tube) rather than knowing beforehand that a given volume raises the level more in a narrow tube; it is evidence of efficient learning of a causal regularity, not of prior understanding.
5. In the five-term transitive inference task, why is the BD test critical, and how can an associative model predict a preference for B even though B and D were each rewarded in one training pair and unrewarded in another?

   **Answer** Pairs including A or E can be solved by the reinforcement history of the end items alone (A always rewarded, E never). B and D have the same programmed reinforcement, so a preference must come from their relation to the rest of the series. In an associative model in which only chosen items are updated, B is rarely chosen against the strong A and so rarely experiences nonreward, while D is chosen more often against the weaker C and accumulates more nonreward; B therefore acquires a higher value than D, and the animal prefers B (figure in Section 44.4.2).
6. A capuchin refuses cucumber after seeing its partner receive a grape. Using the Fehr–Schmidt utility, show that refusal reduces its utility, and propose an experiment that distinguishes social comparison from frustrated expectation.

   **Answer** Let cucumber be worth 1 and grape 3. Accepting gives $U = 1 - a(3 - 1) = 1 - 2a$; refusing gives $U = 0 - a(3 - 0) = -3a$, which is lower for any $a \ge 0$. Refusal therefore cannot maximize this utility unless it affects the partner's payoff or future interactions. To separate social comparison from expectation, keep the subject's experience of grapes identical across conditions (for example, grapes shown and then placed in an empty cage, given to a partner, or eaten by a partner), with the partner present or absent; if refusals depend on whether another individual eats the grape, beyond the effect of seeing it, the effect is social. Measuring refusal rates over sessions controls for learned expectation.
7. (Open question) New Caledonian crows and chimpanzees both make tools, but their brains differ in structure. Discuss what kinds of evidence would show that their tool cognition is convergent, and what would show that it rests on different mechanisms.

   **Answer** Convergence would be supported if both species pass the same transfer tests (trap-tube to trap-table, meta-tools, selection of tools by length and rigidity), show similar developmental trajectories (a sensitive period, a long period of practice, use of discarded tools) and recruit functionally analogous brain regions (an associative pallial area in crows, prefrontal and parietal cortex in apes) whose damage or inactivation impairs flexible tool use. Different mechanisms would be indicated if one species relies on visual feedback and trial and error during each problem (as crows do in string pulling), while the other solves new problems on the first trial, or if the tool behavior of one species is strongly predisposed (narrow developmental canalization, as in woodpecker finches) and that of the other is acquired socially and varies across populations. Comparing species of each group that do not use tools in the wild (rooks, bonobos, other corvids) helps to separate general physical cognition from adaptations for tool use.
