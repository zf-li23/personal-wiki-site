A female burying beetle buries a mouse carcass, strips it of fur, shapes it into a ball and feeds regurgitated carrion to her larvae; a male three-spined stickleback builds a nest, fans the eggs and guards the fry; a pair of great tits may make several hundred feeding trips to their nest in a day. Parental care is costly, and in most animals it does not occur at all: most invertebrates and most fishes leave their eggs to chance. This chapter asks when parental care evolves, which sex provides it, and how much a parent should invest in each offspring. The answers combine the theory of **life history** ([7.10.15](/7.10.15#def-15.1.1)), in particular the **cost of reproduction** and the trade-off between offspring number and quality, with the sex differences in mating opportunities of [7.18.28](/7.18.28).

Parental care is also the arena of three conflicts within families: between the two parents over who should care and how much, between siblings over who gets the care, and between parents and offspring over how much is given. The first two sections of the next chapter ([7.18.32](/7.18.32)) treat these conflicts; this chapter takes the parent's point of view. The neural and hormonal basis of parental behavior (prolactin, oxytocin, the medial preoptic area) is described in [7.18.19](/7.18.19) and [7.16.36](/7.16.36); here we deal with its evolution and ecology. The last section treats the sex ratio of offspring as a decision about how to invest.

## 31.1. Parental Care

### 31.1.1. Definition and Forms

**Definition 31.1.1 (Parental Care)** **Parental care** is any behavior or physiological provision of a parent that increases the survival or growth of its offspring: the preparation of nests and burrows, the provisioning of eggs with yolk, the feeding and protection of eggs and young before and after birth or hatching, and the carrying, warming and cleaning of the young (after Clutton-Brock 1991).

The definition is broad: by it, a yolky egg is already a form of care, and so are gestation and lactation. Care can be given before fertilization (nest building, provisioning eggs), during development (incubation, guarding, gestation) or after birth (feeding, defense, carrying). Some forms of care, such as a nest or the defense of a clutch, benefit all the offspring at once; others, such as a meal, must be given to one offspring at a time, a distinction that matters for the conflicts of the next chapter.

### 31.1.2. Patterns of Care

The distribution of care across animals is striking (Kokko and Jennions 2008):

| Taxon | Pattern |
|---|---|
| Invertebrates | Care uncommon; when it occurs, mainly by the female; biparental care uncommon, male-only care rarer still |
| Teleost fishes | Most families without care; among genera with care, male-only : biparental : female-only about 9 : 3 : 1 |
| Amphibians | Female-only and male-only care about equally common; biparental care rare |
| Reptiles | Care by the female alone or by both parents; mostly limited to guarding eggs (crocodilians also guard young) |
| Birds | About 90 percent of species biparental (including about 9 percent with helpers); females care alone in most of the rest; male-only care rare |
| Mammals | Females care in all species; in about 95 percent the female cares alone and in about 5 percent the male helps; no male-only care |

Among invertebrates, care tends to occur where few young are produced and they can be protected from a hostile physical or biotic environment: in earwigs, which guard their eggs in burrows; in the subsocial treehoppers, in which females guard eggs and nymphs against predators, a behavior that a phylogenetic analysis of the subfamily Membracinae suggested evolved about three times (Lin and colleagues 2004); in the burying beetles; and in many spiders, scorpions and crustaceans. Care also evolves where the young can exploit a rich but ephemeral resource only with help, such as the carcasses of burying beetles or the dung balls of dung beetles. To explain the patterns, we need two kinds of factor: physiological and life-history constraints, which may predispose one sex to care, and ecological conditions and mating opportunities, which determine the costs and benefits of care for each sex.

## 31.2. Which Sex Cares?

### 31.2.1. Birds and Mammals

Reproductive success in birds is often limited by the rate at which food can be brought to the nest. If two parents can feed about twice as many young as one, both gain by staying together: a deserter would roughly halve the output of the current brood and would still have to find a new mate and nest. Biparental care and monogamy are then easy to understand ([7.18.30](/7.18.30#sec-30.5.1)). When one parent can raise the young almost as well as two, as in many seed and fruit eaters with seasonally abundant food or in species with precocial young, one parent deserts, and it is usually the male. Two factors make it the male. First, with internal fertilization he has the opportunity to desert first, and the female is left, literally, holding the babies. Second, he usually gains more by deserting, because his success depends more on the number of his matings ([7.18.28](/7.18.28#sec-28.1.2)).

In mammals, females are even more predisposed to care. The young develop inside the female for a long gestation, during which a male can do little directly, and after birth they are fed on milk, which only females produce. Male care occurs mainly where males can help by feeding the young (some canids), carrying them (marmosets, tamarins, siamangs) or defending them, and it is associated with monogamy ([7.18.30](/7.18.30#sec-30.5.2)). Why male mammals have not evolved lactation, which would seem the obvious way to help in such species, is an open question (Daly 1979): male lactation has been recorded only in two fruit bats, and in small amounts.

### 31.2.2. Fishes and the Mode of Fertilization

In bony fishes about 79 percent of families show no care (Gross and Sargent 1985), and where there is care, it is usually by one parent alone and consists of guarding and fanning eggs, which one parent can do as well as two. Surprisingly, male care is commoner than female care. Mart Gross and Richard Shine (1981) counted the families of teleosts with male and with female care and found that the sex that cares depends on the mode of fertilization: with internal fertilization, almost all families with uniparental care have female care; with external fertilization, most have male care.

![Parental care in teleost fishes by mode of fertilization: number of families with male care, female care and no care (data of Gross and Shine 1981). With internal fertilization, 14 of the 16 families with uniparental care have female care; with external fertilization, 61 of 85 have male care. The predominance of male care in fishes reflects the predominance of external fertilization](./plots/fish-care.py)

Three hypotheses have been proposed for the association.

1. *Paternity certainty* (Trivers 1972). With internal fertilization, sperm of several males may compete inside the female, so a male is less certain of paternity than with external fertilization, where he releases sperm onto the eggs as they are laid. The argument is sound in theory (Queller 1997; [7.18.28](/7.18.28#sec-28.1.5)), but it is not known that paternity is generally more certain with external fertilization: in sunfishes, for instance, cuckolders fertilize eggs at the moment of spawning ([7.18.28](/7.18.28#sec-28.3.4)).
2. *Order of gamete release* (Dawkins and Carlisle 1976). With internal fertilization the male can desert first. With external fertilization, the argument went, the male must wait until the eggs are laid before releasing sperm, which would otherwise drift away, so the female can desert first. The hypothesis fails on two counts. In most external fertilizers the sexes release gametes simultaneously, and yet in 36 of 46 species with simultaneous release and uniparental care, the male cares. And in the armored catfishes (Callichthyidae) and the gouramis and their relatives (Osphronemidae, which include the Siamese fighting fish), the male builds a bubble nest and releases sperm before the female lays, so he could desert first, but it is still the male that cares.
3. *Association* (Williams 1975). The sex most closely associated with the embryos is preadapted to care for them. With internal fertilization this is the female, which may proceed to retain the embryos and give birth to live young. With external fertilization, eggs are often laid in a male's territory, and his defense of the territory, which he holds to attract further females, incidentally defends the eggs. Male care then has a low opportunity cost: a male guarding eggs can still attract more females, and females of some species even prefer males that already have eggs in their nests. Male care is indeed more common in territorial species, and male territoriality is especially common with external fertilization. This hypothesis fits the data best.

### 31.2.3. Why Do Males Care at All?

The association hypothesis identifies a general condition: males care when the cost of care in lost matings is low and the benefit to offspring is high, and when they are likely to be the fathers. Among birds, males care where female care alone cannot raise a brood. Among insects, male-only care is rare but instructive. In the giant water bugs (Belostomatidae), the female glues her eggs onto the male's back, and he carries and aerates them until they hatch. Males mate repeatedly with a female during egg laying, insisting on copulating before each few eggs are added, which assures their paternity of the eggs they carry (Smith 1979). In the large eggs of these bugs, gas exchange is a problem in water and desiccation on land, and male brooding solves it; it has evolved, perhaps, because large eggs could be produced only if someone carried them.

Paternity certainty does affect male care within species. Bryan Neff (2003) exposed nesting male bluegill sunfish to the presence of potential cuckolders, placed in clear containers near the nest during spawning, and later measured how vigorously the males defended their broods against an egg predator. Males exposed to sneakers defended their eggs less vigorously than control males. In a second experiment, some of a male's newly hatched fry were replaced by unrelated fry, and the males then defended their fry less, apparently assessing paternity from the odor of the fry: males reduced their care when they had good reason to believe that some of the young were not theirs. In dunnocks, as we saw, males feed broods in proportion to their share of the matings ([7.18.30](/7.18.30#sec-30.6.1)). Not all studies have found such adjustments, perhaps because males in many species cannot assess paternity, or because reducing care would harm their own young as much as the young of rivals.

## 31.3. Parental Investment and the Parent's Optimum

### 31.3.1. Parental Investment

Robert Trivers (1972) gave the costs of care a precise form.

**Definition 31.3.1 (Parental Investment)** **Parental investment** is any investment by a parent in an individual offspring that increases the offspring's chance of surviving (and hence reproducing) at the cost of the parent's ability to invest in other offspring, current or future (Trivers 1972).

The definition measures the cost of care not in energy or time but in lost reproduction, the currency of fitness. A parent's lifetime parental investment is limited, and what matters is how it is divided. Two trade-offs are involved: between the number and the quality of offspring within a brood, recognized by David Lack, and between current and future broods, recognized by George Williams (1966).

**Proposition 31.3.1 (The Parent's Optimum)** Let an offspring that receives investment $x$ gain benefit $B(x)$ (its expected contribution to the parent's fitness), with $B$ increasing and concave, and let the cost to the parent's other (current and future) offspring be $C(x)$, increasing and convex. The investment that maximizes the parent's fitness from this offspring, $B(x) - C(x)$, is the value $x^*$ at which

$$
B'(x^*) = C'(x^*).
$$
It decreases when the marginal cost increases, for example when the parent's future reproductive prospects are better.

**Proof** $B - C$ is concave, so its maximum is where its derivative vanishes, $B'(x^*) - C'(x^*) = 0$. If the cost is $kC_0(x)$ with $k$ larger, the condition $B'(x^*) = kC_0'(x^*)$ requires a larger $B'$, which, since $B'$ is decreasing, means a smaller $x^*$. $\square$

A: the benefit to an offspring (black) shows diminishing returns as its needs are met, and the cost to the parent's other offspring (dashed) keeps increasing. The net value B − C (solid colored curves) is maximal where the slopes of benefit and cost are equal (dots), at a lower investment when the cost is high (red) than when it is low (blue). B: the optimal investment decreases as the cost of investment, for example the value of future reproduction, increases](./plots/investment-optimum.py)

### 31.3.2. Lack's Clutch Size

Lack (1947) proposed that birds lay the clutch that produces the most surviving young, because parents cannot feed large broods adequately and survival per chick declines with brood size.

**Definition 31.3.2 (Lack Clutch Size)** The **Lack clutch size** is the clutch (or brood) size that maximizes the number of offspring from a single breeding attempt that survive to independence, taking into account the decline of offspring survival with brood size but not the costs of the breeding attempt to the parents' own survival and future reproduction.

**Example 31.3.1 (Lack and Williams)** If each of $n$ chicks survives with probability $s(n) = 1 - n/10$, the number of survivors $n s(n) = n - n^2/10$ is maximal at the Lack clutch size $n = 5$. Suppose that raising $n$ chicks also reduces the parents' expected future reproduction to $V(n) = V_0(1 - cn)$. Lifetime success is $n - n^2/10 + V_0(1 - cn)$, maximal where $1 - n/5 - V_0 c = 0$, that is at

$$
n^* = 5\,(1 - V_0 c) .
$$
With $V_0 = 4$ young and $c = 0.06$, $n^* = 3.8$: the cost of reproduction makes the optimal clutch smaller than Lack's, and the more a parent can expect in the future ($V_0$ larger), the smaller its optimal clutch.

![Lack's clutch size and Williams's trade-off (Example 31.3.1). A: survival per chick (dashed, right axis) falls with brood size, and the number of surviving young per brood (solid, left axis) is maximal at the Lack clutch size, n = 5. B: when raising a larger brood also reduces the parent's expected future reproduction (dotted), lifetime success (black) is maximal at a smaller brood size, n* = 3.8, than the Lack clutch size](./plots/lack-williams.py)

:::

Brood-size manipulations in many birds have confirmed that survival per chick falls with brood size, and that the clutch with the most surviving young is usually somewhat larger than the one actually laid, as the cost of reproduction predicts. In the Dutch great tits analyzed in [7.10.15](/7.10.15#sec-15.3.3), the clutch size estimated to maximize fitness, including the parents' future success, was close to the observed mean. Clutches may also be smaller than Lack's optimum because the best clutch varies from year to year and among parents, which should lay the clutch that is best for their own condition and territory: in the experiments of Pettifor and colleagues with great tits, each female's clutch was close to her individual optimum, so that enlarging or reducing it lowered her lifetime success (the *individual optimization hypothesis*).

### 31.3.3. Current Against Future Reproduction

Experiments that increase the current reproductive effort of parents show the cost of reproduction directly; the form of the cost varies.

- *Survival.* Gravid female side-blotched lizards (*Uta stansburiana*) carry a heavy clutch that hinders their legs. Females from which half the eggs had been removed surgically ran better on a treadmill and were more likely to survive to produce another clutch, probably because they escaped predators more easily (Miles and colleagues 2000).
- *Future fecundity in fishes.* Male common gobies (*Pomatoschistus microps*) fan the eggs in their nests to aerate them. When the oxygen content of the water in their aquaria was lowered, males fanned more, lost more mass and were more likely to abandon their next clutch (Jones and Reynolds 1999).
- *Future fecundity in burying beetles.* Male and female burying beetles (*Nicrophorus vespilloides*) feed their larvae on a carcass they have buried. When pairs were given a large brood to rear in their first breeding attempt, both sexes produced fewer larvae in later broods than pairs that had reared a small first brood (Ward and colleagues 2009).
- *Future fecundity in birds.* Collared flycatchers (*Ficedula albicollis*) given enlarged broods fed them more, survived as well as controls, but laid smaller clutches the following year (Gustafsson and Sutherland 1988).

Why should increased effort sometimes reduce future fecundity rather than survival? One answer is that reproduction and self-maintenance compete for resources, including those of the immune system: parents working harder may be less able to resist infections, and their condition at the next breeding attempt is poorer (Sheldon and Verhulst 1996).

## 31.4. Adjusting Care to Costs and Benefits

If parents allocate care optimally, they should vary it with its costs and benefits. Comparative and experimental studies show that they do.

### 31.4.1. Life History and Risk-Taking

Songbirds of the north temperate zone tend to lay large clutches (four to six eggs) and have low annual adult survival (about 50 percent or less); related species in the tropics of South America lay small clutches (two to three eggs) and have high adult survival (about 75 percent or more). The current brood is therefore a larger part of a northern bird's lifetime reproduction, and a smaller part of a tropical bird's. Cameron Ghalambor and Thomas Martin (2001) predicted that northern parents should take more risks for their nestlings and tropical parents more care of themselves. Because parental visits to the nest attract predators, a parent can reduce the risk to its nestlings or to itself by visiting less. In five pairs of species, one North American and one South American species each from the flycatchers, thrushes, wrens, buntings and warblers, they played the calls of a nest predator (a jay) or presented a mounted hawk, a predator of adults, and measured the reduction in feeding visits. North American parents reduced their visits more in response to the nest predator, South American parents more in response to the predator of adults, as predicted.

Prospects can change within a season. Hihi (*Notiomystis cincta*) nestlings beg with a red mouth, and when broods were given extra carotenoids their mouths became redder and their parents fed them more. But on territories where the adults were also given carotenoid-rich food, many pairs went on to raise a second brood, and these pairs did not respond to the redder mouths of their first brood (Thorogood and colleagues 2011): parents with better future prospects invested less in their current young.

### 31.4.2. Flexible Care in a Mouthbrooder

The St Peter's fish (*Sarotherodon galilaeus*) is a cichlid in which a pair digs a shallow pit, the female lays batches of eggs into it and the male fertilizes them, and then the female, the male or both take the eggs into their mouths and brood them and the fry for about two weeks; the pair bond dissolves once brooding begins. Balshine-Earn and Earn (1998) varied the opportunity for further matings by keeping fish in tanks with even sex ratios (two males, two females), male-biased (three males, one female) or female-biased (one male, three females) ratios. Males deserted more often when extra females were available, and females deserted more often when extra males were available. They also varied the benefit of male care: small males, whose small mouths hold fewer eggs, deserted more often. Both the costs of care (lost matings) and its benefits influence who cares.

### 31.4.3. Filial Cannibalism

**Definition 31.4.1 (Filial Cannibalism)** **Filial cannibalism** is the eating of one's own offspring (eggs or young) by a parent, either of part of a brood (*partial* filial cannibalism) or of the whole brood (*total* filial cannibalism).

Long regarded as abnormal, filial cannibalism is common in fishes with paternal care, and Rohwer (1978) suggested that it can be adaptive: eating part of a brood may fuel the care of the rest, and eating a whole brood that is too small to be worth caring for frees a male to start a new breeding cycle. Andrea Manica (2002, 2004) tested these ideas in the scissortail sergeant (*Abudefduf sexfasciatus*), a coral-reef damselfish. Males defend territories and alternate between a mating phase of two to three days, in which they are golden and court females, and a parental phase of four to five days, in which they lose their color and guard the eggs until they hatch. When Manica removed 75 percent of a male's eggs on the first day of the parental phase, males were more likely than disturbed controls to eat the rest and return to the mating phase; the same reduction on the third day had no effect, because by then only a short period of care remained. Males given supplementary food ate fewer of their eggs, which suggests that partial cannibalism supplies the energy for care. In other fishes, males cannibalize more eggs when the risk of cuckoldry is high (Gray and colleagues 2007), reducing their investment in broods of doubtful paternity, as the bluegill fathers above reduced their defense.

### 31.4.4. Differential Allocation

A parent should invest more when the value of the current brood is high, for example when its mate is of high quality (Burley 1986). Nancy Burley (1988) found that male zebra finches (*Taeniopygia guttata*) wearing red leg bands were more attractive to females than males with green bands, and that females paired to red-banded males fed their chicks more and raised more young. Female mallards lay larger eggs after mating with attractive males, and peahens lay more eggs after mating with males with more elaborate trains (Petrie and Williams 1993). Such *differential allocation* complicates tests of good genes ([7.18.29](/7.18.29#sec-29.3.2)), because the offspring of attractive males may do well because their mothers invested more in them.

### 31.4.5. Recognizing One's Own Offspring

Investment in an unrelated offspring is lost to the parent's own young, so parents should recognize their offspring when there is a real risk of caring for others. Where this risk is low, they need not. Bank swallows (*Riparia riparia*) nest in dense colonies, and their fledglings may land at the wrong burrow; parents learn the distinctive calls of their own young by the time the young leave the nest, at about two to three weeks of age, and then reject strange young. Northern rough-winged swallows (*Stelgidopteryx serripennis*), which nest solitarily, do not discriminate, and their chicks have less distinctive calls (Beecher 1982). Mexican free-tailed bat (*Tadarida brasiliensis*) mothers, which leave their pups in crèches of millions in caves, find their own pup most of the time, using its location, calls and odor (McCracken 1984).

Adoption of unrelated young nevertheless occurs. In ring-billed gulls (*Larus delawarensis*), chicks that have been poorly fed by their own parents sometimes leave their nest and beg confidently from other adults; fewer than 10 percent of adults adopt a stranger in a given year, and adopters lose on average about half a chick of their own. A rule that accepts any chick that begs confidently in the nest may persist because learning to recognize one's own chicks carries the opposite risk of rejecting or killing them by mistake, and because adoption is rare. The extreme case of misdirected care, the raising of brood parasites such as cuckoos, is the subject of [7.18.32](/7.18.32).

## 31.5. Sex Allocation

### 31.5.1. The Sex Ratio as a Parental Decision

Investment in an offspring includes the decision of whether it is a son or a daughter.

**Definition 31.5.1 (Sex Allocation)** **Sex allocation** is the allocation of reproductive resources between male and female function: in species with separate sexes, between sons and daughters (the sex ratio of offspring and the investment per son and per daughter); in hermaphrodites, between sperm and eggs (or pollen and ovules).

The baseline is **Fisher's principle** ([Theorem 13.3.1](/7.10.13#thm-13.3.1)): because every offspring has one mother and one father, the rarer sex has the higher reproductive value, and parents should invest equally in the two sexes. The exceptions arise when the value of a son and of a daughter to a particular parent differs: when the condition of the parent affects sons and daughters differently, when offspring of one sex compete with or help their parents or each other, or when brothers compete for mates (**local mate competition**). Sex allocation is a good test of adaptive theory because the quantity measured, the proportion of sons, is directly comparable with precise quantitative predictions (Charnov 1982; West 2009). A limitation is mechanistic: in species with chromosomal sex determination, a parent may be unable to control the sex of each offspring precisely, whereas haplodiploid insects can, by fertilizing an egg or not ([7.4.6](/7.4.6)).

### 31.5.2. The Trivers–Willard Hypothesis

**Trivers–Willard hypothesis** (Trivers and Willard 1973; [7.10.13](/7.10.13#sec-13.3.3)): if the condition of a mother affects the reproductive success of her sons more than that of her daughters, mothers in good condition should produce sons and mothers in poor condition daughters. The argument rests on three assumptions: mothers in good condition produce offspring in better condition; offspring condition persists into adulthood; and adult condition increases the reproductive success of males more than of females, as in polygynous species where large males win harems.

![Conditional sex allocation. A: the Trivers–Willard argument. The expected fitness of a son increases more steeply with his mother's condition than that of a daughter; a mother should produce daughters if her condition is below the crossing point (shaded red) and sons if it is above (shaded blue). B: Seychelles warblers adjust the sex of their single egg to the quality of their territory: pairs on low-quality territories produce mostly sons, pairs on high-quality territories mostly daughters, which stay and help; pairs moved from low- to high-quality territories switched from sons to daughters (approximate proportions from Komdeur and colleagues 1997)](./plots/sex-allocation.py)

In red deer on the Isle of Rum, a hind's condition is closely related to her rank in the dominance hierarchy. Clutton-Brock and colleagues (1984) found that the proportion of sons increased from 47 percent among low-ranking hinds to 61 percent among high-ranking hinds, and they confirmed the three assumptions: high-ranking mothers produced heavier calves, heavy calves became large adults, and adult size increased the lifetime success of sons much more than that of daughters. In a later period, as the population density on Rum increased, the relationship between rank and sex ratio disappeared (Kruuk and colleagues 1999), and support for the hypothesis in other ungulates has been mixed. A meta-analysis found that the effect is most consistent when condition is measured around the time of conception (Sheldon and West 2004), which suggests that mothers can adjust the sex ratio only at that stage, by mechanisms still unclear. The same logic applies to the quality of a mate: female blue tits paired with males with brighter ultraviolet crowns produced more sons (Sheldon and colleagues 1999).

### 31.5.3. Competition and Cooperation Between Relatives

When offspring of one sex stay near their parents, they may compete with them and with each other, or help them.

- *Local resource competition.* Anne Clark (1978) found that the greater galago (*Otolemur crassicaudatus*) produces a male-biased sex ratio. Daughters, as in most mammals, stay near their mother and compete with her and each other for food trees in her range, while sons disperse. Producing fewer daughters reduces competition among relatives, so sons are worth more to the mother than daughters.
- *Local resource enhancement.* Where offspring of one sex stay and help, they repay part of their cost. Ido Pen and Franjo Weissing (2000) showed that this need not bias the population sex ratio toward the helping sex in general, but that groups with few helpers should produce the helping sex and groups with many helpers the other sex. In African wild dogs (*Lycaon pictus*), in which males stay and help, packs with few helpers produced litters with 63 percent sons and packs with many helpers litters with 64 percent daughters (Creel and colleagues 1998).
- *The Seychelles warbler.* The most precise adjustment known in a vertebrate is that of the Seychelles warbler (*Acrocephalus sechellensis*), in which pairs hold territories for up to nine years and lay one egg a year, and daughters often stay and help their parents ([7.18.35](/7.18.35)). On high-quality territories, with plenty of insect food, a helper increases the parents' success (enhancement); on low-quality territories, a helper competes with them for food (competition). Komdeur and colleagues (1997) found that pairs on high-quality territories produced about 90 percent daughters, while pairs on poor territories produced about 80 percent sons. When pairs were moved from poor to good territories on a new island, they switched from about 90 percent sons to about 85 percent daughters; and pairs on good territories that already had two or more helpers produced sons, since further helpers would compete.

### 31.5.4. Local Mate Competition in Parasitoid Wasps

The most extreme sex ratio biases occur when brothers compete with each other for mates. Hamilton (1967) showed that if $N$ mothers lay eggs in a patch where their offspring mate before the daughters disperse, the evolutionarily stable proportion of sons is $(N - 1)/2N$ ([Theorem 13.3.2](/7.10.13#thm-13.3.2)): a lone mother should produce just enough sons to mate with her daughters, and the sex ratio should rise toward one-half as more mothers share the patch. Parasitoid wasps, which can control the sex of each egg, are ideal for testing the prediction. Females of *Nasonia vitripennis*, which lay their eggs in fly pupae, produce a less female-biased sex ratio when more females lay eggs in the same patch (Werren 1983), and they adjust their sex ratio also when they lay eggs in a host already parasitized by another female, producing more sons when their own offspring will be a smaller fraction of the brood, as the theory of local mate competition predicts quantitatively. The viviparous mite *Acarophenax tribolii* takes the logic to its limit: a female produces one son and up to about 20 daughters, and the son mates with his sisters inside the mother and dies before birth.

## Summary

- Parental care (Definition 31.1.1) is absent in most invertebrates and fishes; females care in mammals, both parents in most birds, males in most fishes with care. Constraints (gestation, lactation, internal fertilization) and the costs of care in lost matings determine which sex cares.
- In teleosts, female care goes with internal and male care with external fertilization (Gross and Shine). Of three hypotheses, paternity certainty is unproven, order of gamete release is refuted (simultaneous release, bubble nests), and association with the eggs in a male's territory fits best. Males reduce care when paternity is doubtful (bluegills) and care when it costs few matings.
- Parental investment (Definition 31.3.1) is measured by the cost to other offspring; the parent's optimum equates marginal benefit and cost (Proposition 31.3.1). Lack's clutch size (Definition 31.3.2) maximizes survivors per brood; costs to the parent's future make the optimum smaller (Example 31.3.1), as experiments on lizards, gobies, burying beetles and flycatchers show.
- Parents adjust care to costs and benefits: North American songbirds protect broods, South American ones themselves; St Peter's fish desert when remating is likely; sergeant fish eat broods too small to be worth guarding (filial cannibalism, Definition 31.4.1); parents invest more with attractive mates. Recognition of own young evolves where mixing is likely (bank swallows).
- Sex allocation (Definition 31.5.1) deviates from Fisher's 1 : 1 when sons and daughters differ in value: Trivers–Willard (red deer, blue tits), local resource competition (galagos), enhancement (wild dogs, Seychelles warblers) and local mate competition (*Nasonia*).

## Questions

1. Using Proposition 31.3.1, predict whether parents should invest more or less in each offspring (a) as they grow older and their future reproduction declines; (b) when food is abundant and the cost of each unit of investment falls; (c) when the offspring is unlikely to survive whatever the investment.

   **Answer** (a) The cost of investment is the loss of future reproduction; as this declines with age, the marginal cost falls and the optimal investment per offspring rises (terminal investment), as observed in many birds and mammals in which old parents defend broods more fiercely. (b) A lower cost per unit of investment also raises the optimum: parents invest more per offspring when food is abundant (or have larger broods). (c) If the benefit curve is low and flat (a sick or very small offspring), $B'$ is small everywhere and the optimum may be zero: the parent should withdraw investment (brood reduction, filial cannibalism, abandonment).
2. In Example 31.3.1, show how the optimal brood size depends on the parent's expected future reproduction $V_0$, and explain how this relates to the comparison of North and South American songbirds.

   **Answer** $n^* = 5(1 - V_0 c)$, which decreases as $V_0$ increases: the more a parent can expect from future breeding, the more it stands to lose by investing heavily now, and the smaller its optimal current brood. Tropical songbirds with high adult survival have a high $V_0$ and lay small clutches; temperate species with low survival have a low $V_0$ and lay large clutches. The same logic predicts their responses to predators: tropical parents protect themselves (their high future value), temperate parents protect the current brood.
3. Explain why the order-of-gamete-release hypothesis fails to explain male care in fishes, and what observations support the association hypothesis.

   **Answer** The hypothesis predicts that the sex releasing its gametes last should care, because the other can desert first. But most external fertilizers release gametes simultaneously, so neither sex can desert first, yet males care in 36 of 46 such species with uniparental care; and in bubble-nest builders the male releases sperm before the female lays, so he could desert first, but he still cares. The association hypothesis is supported because eggs are usually laid in territories defended by males, so guarding eggs costs a male little (he can still attract females, and females sometimes prefer males that already have eggs), and male care is more common in territorial species.
4. A biologist finds that in a species of seal, high-ranking mothers produce more sons. List the assumptions of the Trivers–Willard hypothesis that would have to be checked, and an alternative explanation for the correlation.

   **Answer** Assumptions: (1) maternal rank or condition affects the condition of the offspring (weight at weaning); (2) differences in offspring condition persist to adulthood; (3) adult condition increases the lifetime reproductive success of sons more than that of daughters (in a polygynous seal, likely). One must also show that the fitness return to the mother through sons versus daughters actually depends on her rank. Alternatives: differential mortality of male embryos or pups in poor mothers (males are often more vulnerable), which would produce the correlation without any adaptive adjustment; or a correlation of both rank and sex ratio with a third variable such as age or the time of conception. The timing (sex ratio at conception versus at birth) helps to distinguish adjustment from differential mortality.
5. Using Hamilton's result for local mate competition, predict the proportion of sons produced by a *Nasonia* female who is alone on a patch, one of 2 females, and one of 10 females; and explain why the sex ratio is female-biased even though Fisher's argument predicts 1 : 1.

   **Answer** $(N - 1)/2N$ gives 0 for $N = 1$ (in practice the minimum number of sons needed to mate all daughters, a few percent), 0.25 for $N = 2$ and 0.45 for $N = 10$. Fisher's argument assumes that sons compete for mates with sons of other mothers in a large population; when sons compete with their own brothers for their sisters, an extra son adds little to the mother's fitness, because he mostly takes matings from his brothers, while an extra daughter adds a whole new mated female. As more mothers share the patch, a son competes more with unrelated males and the predicted ratio approaches one-half.
6. (Open question) Male mammals almost never lactate. Propose hypotheses for this, and say how you might test them.

   **Answer** Hypotheses: (1) paternity uncertainty: males whose paternity is uncertain gain less from care, so selection for an expensive form of care is weak; (2) opportunity costs: lactation would prevent males from competing for further matings, which in most mammals bring more fitness than care; (3) developmental and physiological constraints: lactation depends on endocrine control (prolactin, oxytocin, mammary development under ovarian hormones) that would have to be reorganized in males, and the necessary genetic variation may be lacking; (4) lack of benefit: in many species females alone can supply enough milk. Tests: compare mammals with high paternity certainty and male care (monogamous canids, marmosets, the fruit bats in which male lactation has been recorded) with others; examine whether males of these species show partial mammary development or prolactin responses; model the fitness gain of male lactation under different paternity and mating opportunities.
