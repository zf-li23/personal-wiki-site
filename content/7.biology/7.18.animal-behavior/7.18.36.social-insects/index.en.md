A colony of the African driver ant *Dorylus wilverthi* may contain some 22 million workers weighing about 20 kg in all, every one of them the daughter of a single queen and none of them ever reproducing. Darwin regarded the sterile castes of insects as the one special difficulty that "at first appeared to me insuperable, and actually fatal to my whole theory", for how can natural selection produce individuals that leave no offspring, and how can it shape their specialized bodies and behavior? The two preceding chapters provide the tools to answer him: **kin selection** and **Hamilton's rule** ([7.18.33](/7.18.33)) and the enforcement of cooperation ([7.18.34](/7.18.34)). The social insects are also where those tools have had their most precise successes. Kin selection predicts not only cooperation but conflict, and the conflicts between queens and workers over the sex ratio and over who produces males have been predicted quantitatively from relatedness and then found in nature.

The natural history of the social insects, the major groups of termites, ants, bees and wasps, their castes, nests, communication and social parasites, is described in [7.17.38](/7.17.38); this chapter builds on it and does not repeat it. It first asks how eusociality evolved, then how conflicts within colonies are expressed and resolved, and finally how colonies of thousands of individuals organize their work and make decisions without central control, continuing the analysis of collective behavior begun in [7.18.27](/7.18.27).

## 36.1. What Makes a Society Eusocial

**Eusociality**, as defined in [7.10.16](/7.10.16#def-16.3.3), combines cooperative care of the young, overlapping generations and a reproductive division of labor (Wilson 1971). Crespi and Yanega (1995) argued that this definition is too broad, since it includes many cooperative breeders, and proposed to restrict it to societies with castes: groups of individuals that become irreversibly different in behavior before reproductive maturity, one of them reproducing more and helped by the others. Boomsma (2009) drew a further line between facultative and obligate eusociality. In *facultatively* eusocial species, such as the **primitively eusocial** paper wasps and sweat bees, at least some workers can still mate and breed if a vacancy arises; in *obligately* eusocial species, the *advanced eusocial* ants, honey bees, stingless bees, vespine wasps and higher termites ([Definition 38.1.1](/7.17.38#def-38.1.1) in 7.17.38), the castes are fixed during development and neither can live without the other. Obligate eusociality is a point of no return: the queen depends on the workers and the workers on the queen, as the germ line and the soma of a multicellular organism depend on each other.

How many times eusociality has evolved depends on the definition. In the Hymenoptera, social behavior of some kind has arisen perhaps a dozen times, but obligate eusociality only a few times: once in the ants, once or twice in the bees, once in the vespine wasps and perhaps once in the polistine wasps (Boomsma 2009). Termites, which are social cockroaches, are all eusocial and arose once. Eusocial or nearly eusocial societies with soldier castes have also evolved in gall-forming aphids and thrips, in the ambrosia beetle *Austroplatypus incompertus*, several times in the sponge-dwelling snapping shrimps *Synalpheus*, and in two African mole-rats, the naked mole-rat and the Damaraland mole-rat (Section 36.10). Only the eusocial Hymenoptera and the termites have radiated into thousands of species; ants alone include more than 14,000 described species and dominate the animal biomass of many tropical forests.

## 36.2. The Life of a Colony

The black garden ant *Lasius niger*, common in European gardens and fields, illustrates the life cycle of a temperate ant. In July or August, winged males and young queens leave their colonies in mass nuptial flights and mate in the air. A queen stores the sperm in her spermatheca and uses it for the rest of her life, which may last nearly 29 years in captivity. She sheds her wings, digs a small chamber in the soil and seals herself in. Without feeding, she lays eggs and rears the first larvae on trophic eggs made from her fat reserves and the proteins of her histolyzed flight muscles. The first workers, a few to about 20 small individuals, emerge the following spring, open the nest, forage and take over brood care. The colony grows for several years, to thousands of workers, before it begins to produce winged reproductives each summer; when the queen dies, no new eggs are laid and the colony dwindles.

The females of the different castes usually do not differ genetically; caste is determined during larval development by the environment, above all by nutrition. In honey bees, larvae in the large queen cells are fed abundant **royal jelly** throughout their development and become queens ([7.17.38](/7.17.38#sec-38.2.3)), while the queen inhibits the rearing of new queens by her pheromones. In ants, the amount and quality of food, temperature, the season and the age of the queen influence whether a female larva becomes a worker or a queen, and in species with polymorphic workers, whether it becomes a minor or a major. Exceptions show that caste can also have a genetic component. In some populations of harvester ants (*Pogonomyrmex*), queens and workers come from different genetic lineages; in leafcutter ants, some patrilines contribute disproportionately to the queens a colony produces (Hughes and Boomsma 2008); and in stingless bees of the genus *Melipona*, a genetic predisposition interacting with nutrition allows female larvae a large say in their own fate (Section 36.8.3).

Comparative genomics has not found a single "eusociality gene". Caste differences arise mainly through differences in gene regulation, with many genes and pathways shared with solitary relatives (the insulin/TOR nutrient-sensing pathway, juvenile hormone and vitellogenin, which regulates the transition from nursing to foraging in honey bee workers; [7.18.19](/7.18.19)) and some lineage-specific innovations, such as the expansion of odorant receptor genes in ants, which may be related to their chemical communication (Kapheim and colleagues 2015).

## 36.3. The Economics of Eusociality

Kin selection frames the question of how eusociality evolved. A female that helps rather than breeding independently is favored if $rB > C$: if the benefit $B$ of her help to the reproduction of relatives, weighted by her relatedness $r$ to the young she rears, exceeds the cost $C$ in her own lost offspring. Explanations of eusociality must therefore identify the factors that made relatedness high enough and the benefit-to-cost ratio favorable enough. Hamilton's rule is not the only possible accounting; but kin selection is the only explanation of sterile workers that has generated tested predictions (Section 36.11).

Two routes to eusociality have been proposed ([7.17.38](/7.17.38#sec-38.1.1)). In the subsocial route, offspring stay in their natal nest and help their mother; in the parasocial route, females of the same generation, often sisters, found a nest together, and one comes to dominate the others. Mapped onto phylogenies, all the evidence favors the subsocial route: in the Hymenoptera there is no documented case in which parasocial nesting preceded the evolution of obligately sterile castes, and in termites the workers and soldiers are specialized juveniles of the founding pair, as the subsocial route implies (Bourke and Franks 1995; Boomsma 2009). Eusocial colonies are families.

## 36.4. The Haplodiploidy Hypothesis

The eusocial Hymenoptera make up only a small fraction of insect species, yet eusociality has evolved more often in this order than in all other insects. Hamilton (1964, 1972) suggested a reason in their genetic system, **haplodiploidy**: males develop from unfertilized eggs and are haploid, females from fertilized eggs and are diploid. A haploid male produces genetically identical sperm, so his daughters share all of their paternal genes, and full sisters are related by $r = \tfrac12 \cdot 1 + \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac12 = \tfrac34$ ([Example 16.3.1](/7.10.16#ex-16.3.1) in 7.10.16), whereas a female is related to her own offspring by $\tfrac12$.

**Definition 36.4.1 (Haplodiploidy Hypothesis)** The **haplodiploidy hypothesis** is the proposal (Hamilton 1964) that the high relatedness of full sisters in haplodiploid species ($r = \tfrac34$, against $\tfrac12$ to own daughters) predisposed the Hymenoptera to the evolution of sterile female workers, because rearing sisters gives a female a greater genetic return than rearing daughters.

:::

By Formula 33.2.1 ([7.18.33](/7.18.33#sec-33.2.2)), a female that forgoes daughters to rear sisters is favored if $B/C > \tfrac12/\tfrac34 = \tfrac23$: helping could pay even if she were less efficient at rearing sisters than at rearing her own daughters. Trivers and Hare (1976) pointed out the flaw. A worker rears brothers as well as sisters, and she is related to a brother by only $\tfrac14$, since he carries none of her father's genes. If the colony invests equally in the two sexes, as **Fisher's principle** predicts when the queen controls allocation ([7.10.13](/7.10.13)), her average relatedness to the brood is $\tfrac12(\tfrac34 + \tfrac14) = \tfrac12$, the same as to her own offspring, and the advantage disappears.

The hypothesis could be rescued if workers reared more sisters than brothers. With a 3:1 ratio of females to males, a worker's average relatedness to the brood would be $\tfrac34 \cdot \tfrac34 + \tfrac14 \cdot \tfrac14 = \tfrac58$, and the critical ratio would fall to $\tfrac12 / \tfrac58 = \tfrac45$. But a female bias in the whole population changes the value of the sexes. When females outnumber males three to one, each male mates on average with three females and contributes three times as much to future generations: the **reproductive value** of a male is three times that of a female.

**Example 36.4.1 (Relatedness Weighted by Reproductive Value)** With a population sex ratio of 3:1 and males worth three times as much as females, the genetic value of a unit of brood is (number of each sex × value × relatedness). For a worker rearing siblings at the population ratio:

$$
\tfrac34 \times 1 \times \tfrac34 + \tfrac14 \times 3 \times \tfrac14 = \tfrac{12}{16};
$$

for a female rearing her own offspring at the same ratio:

$$
\tfrac34 \times 1 \times \tfrac12 + \tfrac14 \times 3 \times \tfrac12 = \tfrac{12}{16}.
$$

The critical ratio is $B/C > 1$, exactly as in a diploid species (Trivers and Hare 1976; Craig 1979). If the solitary female instead produces only sons, which are the more valuable sex in a female-biased population, her offspring are worth $1 \times 3 \times \tfrac12 = \tfrac32$, and helping requires $B/C > \tfrac32 / \tfrac{12}{16} = 2$, twice the diploid value.

:::

The increased relatedness to sisters is thus exactly balanced by the increased value of brothers. Haplodiploidy would give helping an advantage only if female bias were concentrated in the nests where helping occurs while the population as a whole was not biased, a situation of "split sex ratios" (Seger 1983; Grafen 1986); but such variation also disfavors helping in the male-biased nests, and an independently breeding female in a female-biased population would do best by producing sons, which makes helping harder (Gardner, Alpedrinha and West 2012). Several further facts weigh against the hypothesis: termites, shrimps and mole-rats are diploid and eusocial; most haplodiploid lineages, including many groups of solitary wasps, are not eusocial; and in many eusocial Hymenoptera the queen mates with several males, which lowers the relatedness of sisters below $\tfrac34$. The haplodiploidy hypothesis now looks like a red herring. It is important not to confuse it with kin selection: kin selection explains eusociality whether or not haplodiploidy helped, and the hypothesis was only a suggestion as to why eusociality might be especially easy to evolve in the Hymenoptera.

## 36.5. Lifetime Monogamy

**Definition 36.5.1 (Monogamy Hypothesis)** The **monogamy hypothesis** (Boomsma 2007, 2009) states that strict lifetime monogamy of the mother was a necessary condition for the origin of obligate eusociality, because only then is a potential helper exactly as related to the siblings she would rear as to her own offspring ($r = \tfrac12$ on average), so that any consistent efficiency advantage of rearing siblings ($B/C > 1$, however slight) favors helping.

:::

If the mother has mated with several males, her daughters are on average more related to their own offspring than to their siblings, and helping requires a substantial efficiency advantage, $B/C \gg 1$, which is unlikely before group living has produced specialized cooperative behavior: feeding a sibling is not much more productive than feeding one's own young. With lifetime monogamy, the argument holds equally for haplodiploids and diploids, which explains why it applies to termites, whose king and queen pair for life, as well as to Hymenoptera.

Hughes and colleagues (2008) tested the hypothesis by reconstructing the mating systems of the ancestors of eusocial lineages. They compiled data on the mating frequency of queens in 267 species of eusocial bees, wasps and ants and mapped them onto phylogenies. In every independent origin of eusociality, the ancestral queen mated once. Multiple mating, which reaches 10–20 males in honey bees and occurs also in leafcutter ants, army ants and *Vespula* wasps, arose only in lineages that already had obligately sterile workers. The same pattern holds in termites and in the eusocial shrimps (Duffy and Macdonald 2010). Once workers have lost the ability to mate and colonies depend on specialized castes, the lower relatedness caused by multiple mating no longer leads to the loss of eusociality, because the efficiency of the colony gives a large $B/C$; multiple mating may then even be favored, for example because genetically diverse colonies are more resistant to disease and better at regulating their nests ([7.17.38](/7.17.38#sec-38.4.1)).

## 36.6. The Ecological Benefits of Helping

Monogamy makes relatedness to siblings equal to that to offspring; ecology must then make rearing siblings more efficient than rearing offspring, $B/C > 1$. Three advantages have been documented.

### 36.6.1. Life Insurance

In ants, bees and wasps, the young need extended care, and a solitary mother who dies before her brood is independent loses the whole brood. In a group, the brood survives the death of any one carer, because others complete the care; a female in a group receives "assured fitness returns" for the care she has already given (Queller 1989, 1994). In the paper wasp *Ropalidia marginata* in India, development from egg to adult takes on average 62 days, and a female has only a 0.12 probability of surviving that long; Gadagkar (1991) estimated that by nesting in a group a female could increase her expected success 3.6-fold. Field and colleagues (2000) tested the effect experimentally in the hover wasp *Liostenogaster flavolineata* of Malaysia by removing helpers from some nests: the number of small larvae reared to a large size decreased with the removal, and groups with more females after removal still reared more, so that helping increased the number of offspring raised about 2.4-fold relative to solitary breeding.

A: if a female dies on a given day, the care she has given is lost if she nests alone, but in a group it is still realized if one of the other females survives to complete the brood (lines for groups of 2 and 5): she has assured fitness returns. ### 36.6.2. Fortress Defense

A second advantage is the defense of a valuable site that provides both food and shelter: the wood galleries of termites, the galls of aphids and thrips, the tunnels of ambrosia beetles and the sponges of snapping shrimps. Staying in the natal "fortress" avoids the risks of dispersal and founding, and since food is available locally, little care is needed and the first specialized caste is usually soldiers. In gall-forming aphids such as *Pemphigus spyrothecae*, first-instar soldiers attack predators that enter the gall. Foster (1990) set up galls with ten soldiers or ten nonsoldiers and introduced a single predator: in galls with soldiers the predator was usually killed, at the cost of a few soldiers, while in galls without soldiers the predator survived and most aphids were eaten. Clonal reproduction of aphids makes relatedness to siblings and to offspring equal ($r = 1$), so that, as with monogamy, a small advantage suffices. Snapping shrimps of the genus *Synalpheus* live inside sponges, which they eat and defend against conspecifics; eusocial species, with a single breeding female and up to several hundred nonbreeding workers, warn intruders by snapping their large claws in unison. Across species in Belize, eusocial species were more abundant, occupied more sponges and used a broader range of host sponges than their less social relatives (Duffy and Macdonald 2010).

Queller and Strassmann (1998) divided the social insects into two types according to the main ecological advantage of group living:

| Characteristic | Fortress defenders | Life insurers |
|---|---|---|
| Taxa | Aphids, thrips, ambrosia beetles, lower termites, snapping shrimps | Ants, bees, wasps |
| Main advantage | Valuable, defensible resource | Overlapping adult lives provide extended care |
| Food | Inside the nest or protected site | Outside the nest |
| Juveniles | Active; feed themselves and may work | Helpless; must be fed |
| First specialized caste | Soldiers | Foragers |
| Colony size and success | Usually small, ecologically limited | Often large, ecologically dominant |

### 36.6.3. Food Distribution

The African mole-rats include solitary species, social species with groups of up to about 15 and the two eusocial species with colonies of up to about 300 (naked mole-rat) and 40 (Damaraland mole-rat). All live underground and feed on roots and tubers. The *aridity food-distribution hypothesis* (Jarvis and colleagues 1994) holds that in arid regions with low, unpredictable rainfall, tubers are large but sparse and patchily distributed, and digging in hard soil is expensive, so that a lone animal risks starving before it finds food, while a group digging cooperatively finds enough food to supply it for a long time. Across 12 species of mole-rats, group size increased with lower densities of food plants and greater variability of rainfall (Faulkes and colleagues 1997), and in Damaraland mole-rats dispersal from colonies takes place mainly after rains, when the soil is soft.

## 36.7. Conflict over Sex Allocation

Because relatedness within colonies is high but less than 1, kin selection predicts conflict wherever the inclusive fitness interests of colony members differ. The asymmetries of haplodiploidy make these conflicts sharp and predictable.

### 36.7.1. Queens and Workers

A queen is equally related ($\tfrac12$) to her sons and daughters, so her evolutionarily stable allocation of resources to reproductive offspring is equal investment in the two sexes (Fisher's principle). The workers, which rear the brood, are related to their sisters by $\tfrac34$ (if the queen mated once) and to their brothers by $\tfrac14$, and favor investment in females.

**Formula 36.7.1 (Workers' Favored Investment Ratio)** If workers are related to the colony's female reproductives by $r_{\mathrm{f}}$ and to its males by $r_{\mathrm{m}}$, their evolutionarily stable ratio of investment in females to males is $r_{\mathrm{f}} : r_{\mathrm{m}}$; the favored proportion of investment in females is $r_{\mathrm{f}} / (r_{\mathrm{f}} + r_{\mathrm{m}})$. With a singly mated queen, $\tfrac34 : \tfrac14 = 3 : 1$; with a queen mated to $k$ males contributing equally, $r_{\mathrm{f}} = \tfrac14 + \tfrac1{2k}$ and $r_{\mathrm{m}} = \tfrac14$.

:::

At a 3:1 investment ratio a male's expected reproductive success is three times a female's, which exactly compensates the workers for being three times less related to brothers. The queen favors 1:1, so there is a direct conflict (Trivers and Hare 1976).

![Relatedness asymmetries in colonies of social Hymenoptera against the number k of males with which the queen mated (equal paternity assumed). A: the workers' favored proportion of investment in females (Formula 36.7.1) falls from 3/4 with a singly mated queen toward the queen's optimum 1/2 as k increases. B: a worker's relatedness to her own sons (1/2), her brothers (1/4) and the average son of another worker (her nephew, r_f/2): with k > 2 nephews are worth less than brothers, and workers are favored to destroy other workers' eggs (worker policing). The square marks the honey bee, whose queens mate with about 12 males](./plots/relatedness-asymmetry.py)

Trivers and Hare tested the prediction with data on 21 ant species believed to have single, singly mated queens. Using the ratio of dry weights of all males and females produced as a measure of investment, they found ratios scattered but on average much closer to 3:1 than to 1:1: the workers appeared to win. As a control they examined slave-making ants, whose brood is reared by workers of another species captured as pupae; these workers are unrelated to the brood and have no interest in its sex ratio, and the investment ratio of slave-makers was close to 1:1, as expected if the queen's interest then prevails. The analysis was criticized: dry weight overestimates the cost of the larger females, since metabolic costs scale less than proportionally with mass, and corrected ratios are closer to 2:1 (Boomsma 1989); and female bias can have other causes, such as **local mate competition** ([7.10.13](/7.10.13#sec-13.3.2)) or competition among daughters for nest sites near the parental colony. Comparisons across species could not settle the question. Much stronger evidence came from variation among colonies within species.

### 36.7.2. Split Sex Ratios

**Definition 36.7.1 (Split Sex Ratio)** A **split sex ratio** is a bimodal distribution of the sex ratio of reproductives among colonies of a population, some colonies producing mainly females and others mainly males, predicted when workers control allocation and colonies differ in the workers' relatedness asymmetry (Boomsma and Grafen 1990, 1991).

:::

Suppose that some queens in a population mated once and others several times. Workers in colonies of singly mated queens are much more related to sisters than to brothers; in colonies of multiply mated queens the asymmetry is smaller. At the population level, the female bias favored in the first kind of colony makes males more valuable, which favors investment in males in the second kind, and the reduced investment in females by the second kind makes females more valuable, which favors more females in the first. The stable outcome is that colonies with singly mated queens specialize in females and colonies with multiply mated queens in males.

Sundström (1994) studied the wood ant *Formica truncorum* on islands off the coast of Finland, determined with genetic markers how many males each queen had mated and measured the sex ratio of the reproductives produced. Colonies with singly mated queens produced mainly females and colonies with multiply mated queens mainly males, as predicted; the mating frequency of the queen explained about two thirds of the variation among colonies. How could workers know their queen's mating frequency? In *F. truncorum*, the variation among workers in the cuticular hydrocarbons that give ants their colony odor was greater in colonies of multiply mated queens, and among such colonies the proportion of females in the brood decreased as the variation increased (Boomsma and colleagues 2003). Workers erred where expected: when a queen had mated with two males whose hydrocarbon profiles were similar, the colony's workers behaved as if she had mated once and produced a female-biased brood. How could they change the sex ratio, when the queen lays the eggs? In *Formica exsecta*, the proportion of males was the same among eggs and among pupae in colonies of multiply mated queens, but fell between the egg and the pupal stage in colonies of singly mated queens: workers selectively eliminated male brood (Sundström, Chapuisat and Keller 1996). In *Leptothorax acervorum*, workers instead change the proportion of female larvae reared as queens rather than workers (Hammond and colleagues 2002).

Workers do not always win. In the fire ant *Solenopsis invicta*, colonies also specialize in producing males or females, but when Passera and colleagues (2001) exchanged queens between male-producing and female-producing colonies, the colonies' production after the exchange followed the queen's former colony, not the workers'. The queens control allocation by the proportion of eggs they fertilize: by laying almost no male eggs, a queen forces the workers to rear females, and by laying few female eggs, she forces them to rear those few as workers and the males as reproductives. Sex allocation in social Hymenoptera is a tug of war whose outcome depends on which party has the means of control in each species (Ratnieks, Foster and Wenseleers 2006).

## 36.8. Conflict over Reproduction

### 36.8.1. Male Production and Worker Policing

In many ants, bees and wasps, workers cannot mate but have functional ovaries and can lay unfertilized eggs that develop into males. Who should produce the colony's males? With a queen mated to many males, as in honey bees, the relatedness values give three predictions (Woyciechowski and Łomnicki 1987; Ratnieks 1988):

1. The queen prefers her own sons ($r = \tfrac12$) to the workers' sons, her grandsons ($\tfrac14$).
2. A laying worker prefers her own sons ($\tfrac12$) to her brothers ($\tfrac14$).
3. Other workers prefer their brothers ($\tfrac14$) to their nephews, which are mostly half nephews ($\tfrac18$).

Workers should therefore destroy eggs laid by other workers, **worker policing**. If the queen mated once, the third prediction is reversed: a worker is related to the sons of her full sisters by $\tfrac38$, more than to her brothers, and policing is not favored. The threshold lies at an effective mating frequency of two (panel B above). Ratnieks and Visscher (1989) introduced male eggs laid by queens and by workers into honey bee colonies, in combs surrounded by a wire mesh that excluded the queen. Within a few hours almost all the worker-laid eggs had been removed and eaten, while most of the queen-laid eggs remained. The prediction was made before the behavior was known: kin selection theory led to the discovery. Policing explains why, although about one honey bee worker in ten thousand has active ovaries, fewer than 0.1% of the males in a colony are workers' sons. Workers probably recognize queen-laid eggs by a chemical cue on their surface.

Across species, policing is more frequent where workers are relatively more related to the queen's sons than to other workers' sons: in a comparative study of 50 species of ants, bees and wasps, the proportion of males produced by workers decreased, and policing increased, as the relatedness advantage of brothers over nephews increased (Wenseleers and Ratnieks 2006a). Relatedness is not the only factor. Policing occurs in some species whose queens mate once, where workers would gain from nephews; in these species it may be favored because worker reproduction reduces the colony's productivity, since laying workers work less, an efficiency argument emphasized by Hammond and Keller (2004).

### 36.8.2. Enforced Altruism

If policing is effective, a worker that lays eggs gains nothing, and the cost of not reproducing ($C$) is close to zero: she might as well help. Policing should therefore reduce the proportion of workers that try to reproduce. Across ten species of bees and wasps in which the proportion of worker-laid eggs removed had been measured, the proportion of workers with active ovaries was lower where policing was more effective, from about 30% in a weakly policed *Polistes* to almost none in the honey bee and in *Vespula* wasps whose queens mate multiply (Wenseleers and Ratnieks 2006b). Policing, an enforcement mechanism ([7.18.34](/7.18.34#sec-34.5)), thus maintains altruism; it does not replace kin selection as its explanation, because the workers' sterility still pays only because they rear relatives. The history of the Hymenoptera suggests an order of events: eusociality arose with monogamous queens, where policing is not expected; multiple mating arose later in some lineages, and with it policing, which then helped to maintain workers' sterility despite the lower relatedness.

### 36.8.3. Conflict over Caste Fate and Rank

A female larva may also be in conflict with the colony over whether she becomes a queen or a worker. In most species workers control larval nutrition and hence caste. In stingless bees of the genus *Melipona*, however, queens and workers are reared in cells of the same size, sealed with the same amount of food, so that females have a large influence over their own development. Many more queens are produced than are needed (up to about a fifth of the females in some species), and the excess young queens are killed by workers soon after they emerge. Wenseleers and Ratnieks (2004) predicted that the degree of queen overproduction should depend on the cost to a larva of leaving the workforce: when workers produce many of the colony's males, a female that becomes a queen deprives her sisters of help in rearing their sons, her nephews ($r = \tfrac38$ in these singly mated species), and so harms relatives. As predicted, among four *Melipona* species, fewer females developed as queens in species in which workers produced a larger share of the males.

In ants in which the queen caste has been lost and a mated worker, the gamergate, reproduces, rank is settled by aggression and policing. In *Dinoponera quadriceps*, the gamergate smears a high-ranking worker that challenges her with a secretion from her sting, after which low-ranking workers seize and immobilize the challenger for days (Monnin and colleagues 2002). In *Harpegnathos saltator*, workers hold nestmates whose ovaries have begun to develop in a firm grip for days, which arrests their ovarian development (Liebig, Peeters and Hölldobler 1999).

## 36.9. The Colony as an Integrated Unit

### 36.9.1. Superorganisms

When conflicts are absent or suppressed, a colony behaves as if it were a single organism, a **superorganism** ([Definition 38.5.1](/7.17.38#def-38.5.1) in 7.17.38): the queen is its germ line, the workers its soma, and foraging, defense and nest maintenance can be understood as maximizing colony productivity. This sounds like the "good of the group" reasoning that was rejected as a general principle of evolution ([7.10.3](/7.10.3)). Gardner and Grafen (2009) reconciled the two views by asking when selection on individuals produces behavior that maximizes the fitness of the group. It does so in two limiting cases: when relatedness within the group is very high, as in clonal groups and in colonies of a singly mated queen, or when policing is so effective that individuals cannot gain by selfish behavior. Both conditions are approached in many obligately eusocial species, so that treating a honey bee colony as a superorganism is justified; but the general principle remains that individuals maximize their inclusive fitness, and group-level adaptation is the special case in which within-group conflict has been eliminated.

### 36.9.2. Division of Labor

A colony allocates thousands of workers among foraging, brood care, defense, nest building and thermoregulation without any individual knowing the needs of the whole. In honey bees, workers change tasks with age (age polyethism), from cell cleaning and nursing to building, guarding and foraging ([7.17.38](/7.17.38#sec-38.4.1)), and in many ants workers differ in size and shape. The allocation is nevertheless flexible. In the ant *Pheidole*, with minor and major workers, Wilson (1984) removed most of the minors: the majors, which normally specialize in defense and seed milling, took over brood care and other tasks of the minors and performed them nearly as well, once the ratio of minors to majors had fallen below about 1:1.

The response threshold model (Bonabeau, Theraulaz and Deneubourg 1996; [7.17.38](/7.17.38#sec-38.4.1)) explains such flexibility by two ingredients: each task has a stimulus that rises when the task is neglected (hungry larvae, a warm nest), and workers differ in their thresholds for responding to it. Low-threshold workers respond first and keep the stimulus low, so they become specialists; high-threshold workers join only if the stimulus rises, as it does when the specialists are removed.

Threshold variation can be genetic, as between the patrilines of a multiply mated honey bee queen, or arise from age, size and experience: in some models, performing a task lowers an individual's threshold for it and not performing it raises it, so that specialization is self-reinforcing. Not every worker is busy. In many colonies a large fraction of workers is inactive at any moment; these "reserves" may allow rapid responses to sudden needs. The colony's organization emerges from local interactions, an example of **self-organization** ([7.5.12](/7.5.12)) like the collective motion of animal groups ([7.18.27](/7.18.27#sec-27.6)).

### 36.9.3. Collective Decisions

When a honey bee colony outgrows its nest in spring, the old queen leaves with about two thirds of the workers, some 10,000 bees, and the swarm settles in a cluster on a branch while a few hundred scouts search the surroundings for a cavity. The process by which the swarm chooses among the sites found, studied for decades by Thomas Seeley and colleagues, was summarized in [7.18.27](/7.18.27#sec-27.7.2): each scout that has inspected a site advertises it by a **waggle dance** on the surface of the cluster, dancing for longer and with more waggle runs for a better site; recruits inspect the site and may dance for it in turn; each scout's dancing declines with each return and eventually stops, so that support for poor sites dies out; and the decision is made by a **quorum response** at the site itself. When about 15 scouts are present at a site at the same time, they return to the swarm and produce piping signals that prepare the bees for flight, and the swarm flies to the site (Seeley and Visscher 2004). The colony usually chooses the best of the available sites, even when no single scout has visited more than one.

A further element prevents deadlock between sites of similar quality. Scouts committed to one site deliver *stop signals*, short vibrations given while head-butting, to scouts dancing for other sites, which then stop dancing sooner (Seeley and colleagues 2012). This cross-inhibition has the same structure as the mutual inhibition between populations of neurons that encode alternative choices in the brain during a perceptual decision ([7.16.47](/7.16.47#sec-47.4)). Models of swarms and of brains show the same behavior (Marshall and colleagues 2009; Pais and colleagues 2013): recruitment amplifies small differences in support, abandonment allows the colony to forget poor options, and cross-inhibition turns a symmetric deadlock into a choice, while a quorum threshold sets the trade-off between speed and accuracy in the way that the bound of the **drift-diffusion model** does in individual decisions ([7.16.47](/7.16.47#sec-47.2)).

Ant colonies solve other problems by similar means. When the Argentine ant *Linepithema humile* was offered two bridges of different lengths to a food source, the colony soon used the shorter one, because ants returning by it laid more pheromone per unit time and the trail **pheromone** was amplified by positive feedback (Goss and colleagues 1989), a process that has inspired optimization algorithms in computer science. Rock ants (*Temnothorax albipennis*) choosing a new nest also use a quorum: scouts recruit nestmates slowly by tandem running until the number of ants at the new site exceeds a threshold, and then switch to carrying nestmates rapidly (Pratt and colleagues 2002).

## 36.10. Eusocial Vertebrates and Insects Compared

The naked mole-rat (*Heterocephalus glaber*) and the Damaraland mole-rat (*Fukomys damarensis*) are the vertebrates closest to eusociality. A naked mole-rat colony has a single breeding queen and one to three breeding males; the other colony members dig, carry food and defend the burrow, and the largest individuals, which may defend the colony against snakes and intruding mole-rats, form something like a soldier class. Unlike insect workers, nonbreeding mole-rats are not sterile; they are held in a reversible reproductive suppression by the queen ([7.18.35](/7.18.35#sec-35.5.1)) and some become breeders when the queen dies. Colonies are highly inbred, because most matings take place within the colony, although a rare dispersing morph, fatter and more inclined to leave, may found new colonies with unrelated partners (O'Riain, Jarvis and Faulkes 1996).

Cooperatively breeding birds and mammals resemble the social insects in several ways: in both, helping is most common in family groups formed by the subsocial route, helpers defend the nest and provision the young, and promiscuity reduces cooperation (Cornwallis and colleagues 2010; [7.18.35](/7.18.35#sec-35.2.2)). Why, then, have vertebrates, with the possible exception of the mole-rats, not evolved sterile castes? Three differences seem decisive (Davies, Krebs and West 2012). First, strict lifetime monogamy is rare in vertebrate populations, so that potential helpers are usually more related to their own offspring than to their siblings, and a large, consistent efficiency advantage would be needed to start the path to sterility. Second, the insurance benefits of helping are smaller in vertebrates, whose adults rarely die during the period needed to rear a brood. Third, fortress defense is rarely available, since vertebrate cooperative breeders such as meerkats and Seychelles warblers do not live in protected sites that also provide their food. The mole-rats, which live in sealed burrow systems and feed on underground tubers, are the exception that suggests the rule.

## 36.11. Kin Selection and the Social Insects

In 2010 Nowak, Tarnita and Wilson argued that inclusive fitness theory had not explained eusociality and proposed a model in which eusociality arises from "eusocial alleles" that make offspring stay in the nest, with relatedness a consequence rather than a cause. The response by more than 130 researchers (Abbot and colleagues 2011) emphasized the empirical record reviewed in this chapter ([7.18.33](/7.18.33#sec-33.8)). It is perhaps ironic that the clearest quantitative support for kin selection comes not from the explanation of altruism but from its predictions of conflict: the sex ratios of colonies according to the mating frequency of their queens, the policing of workers' eggs where queens mate multiply, and the reluctance of *Melipona* larvae to become queens where workers produce many males. These predictions can be made from relatedness alone, without measuring the benefits and costs that are so hard to estimate in studies of altruism, and they were confirmed in species and situations chosen in advance.

## Summary

- **Eusociality** in the strict sense requires castes; obligately eusocial societies, in which castes are fixed and mutually dependent, arose only a few times in Hymenoptera and once in termites, always by the subsocial route.
- Caste is mostly determined by larval nutrition (**royal jelly**), with genetic components in some ants and *Melipona*; caste differences arise mainly from gene regulation.
- The haplodiploidy hypothesis (Definition 36.4.1) fails because a worker's high relatedness to sisters is offset by low relatedness to brothers, and a female-biased sex ratio raises the **reproductive value** of males exactly enough to cancel any gain (Example 36.4.1).
- Lifetime monogamy (Definition 36.5.1) was ancestral in every origin of eusociality examined; multiple mating evolved later, in lineages with sterile workers.
- Ecological benefits made $B/C > 1$: life insurance (assured fitness returns) in ants, bees and wasps; fortress defense in aphids, thrips, termites and shrimps; patchy food in mole-rats.

| Conflict | Prediction from relatedness | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Sex allocation | Queen 1:1; workers $r_{\mathrm{f}} : r_{\mathrm{m}}$ = 3:1 with a singly mated queen (Formula 36.7.1) | Female-biased ratios in ants, 1:1 in slave-makers; split sex ratios (Definition 36.7.1) in *Formica*; queen control in fire ants |
| Male production | Policing when effective mating frequency exceeds 2 | Honey bee workers eat worker-laid eggs; comparative data across 50 species |
| Sterility | Effective policing makes worker reproduction futile | Fewer laying workers where policing is effective |
| Caste fate | Fewer self-made queens where workers produce many males | *Melipona* species |

- Colonies approach **superorganisms** when relatedness is very high or conflict is policed (Gardner and Grafen 2009).
- Division of labor emerges from response thresholds; honey bee swarms choose nest sites by dancing, abandonment, cross-inhibiting stop signals and a quorum, a mechanism parallel to neural decision circuits.
- Mole-rats approach eusociality; other vertebrate cooperative breeders lack lifetime monogamy, insurance benefits and fortress resources, and have no sterile castes.

## Questions

1. Calculate the relatedness of a worker to the son of her full sister and to the son of her half sister, and show that the average relatedness of a worker to other workers' sons equals that to her brothers when the queen has mated with two males.

   **Answer** A worker is related to a full sister by $\tfrac34$ and to a half sister by $\tfrac14$; a mother passes half her genes to her son, so the worker is related to a full nephew by $\tfrac34 \times \tfrac12 = \tfrac38$ and to a half nephew by $\tfrac14 \times \tfrac12 = \tfrac18$. With $k$ fathers contributing equally, another worker is a full sister with probability $1/k$, so the average relatedness to a nephew is $\tfrac12(\tfrac14 + \tfrac{1}{2k})$. For $k = 2$ this is $\tfrac12 \times \tfrac12 = \tfrac14$, equal to the relatedness to a brother. For $k > 2$ nephews are worth less than brothers and workers are favored to police each other's eggs; for $k < 2$ they would prefer nephews.

2. Explain why Trivers and Hare used slave-making ants as a control, and what result would have contradicted their interpretation.

   **Answer** In slave-making ants the brood is reared by workers of another species captured as pupae. These workers are unrelated to the brood and gain nothing from biasing its sex ratio, so the queen's interest, equal investment, should prevail: the predicted investment ratio is 1:1. In free-living species the workers are the queen's daughters and should bias investment toward females. Finding female-biased ratios in free-living species but 1:1 in slave-makers supports the view that the bias is caused by workers' relatedness interests, not by some other factor common to ants. Female-biased ratios in slave-makers as well would have suggested a cause unrelated to workers' kinship, such as local mate competition.

3. In a population of an ant in which some queens mate once and others twice, predict which colonies should produce mainly males, and describe two mechanisms by which workers could achieve this.

   **Answer** Workers of singly mated queens have the larger relatedness asymmetry ($\tfrac34$ to sisters against $\tfrac14$ to brothers); those of doubly mated queens a smaller one ($\tfrac12$ against $\tfrac14$). With worker control, colonies with singly mated queens should specialize in females and colonies with doubly mated queens in males, as in *Formica truncorum*. Workers must first assess their queen's mating frequency, for example by the variation in cuticular hydrocarbons among nestmates. They can then eliminate male eggs or larvae in colonies that should produce females (as in *F. exsecta*), or change the caste of female larvae, rearing more of them as workers rather than queens in colonies that should produce males (as in *Leptothorax acervorum*).

4. Why does life insurance favor helping in wasps more than in birds?

   **Answer** Insurance pays when there is a high probability that a lone parent dies before her brood becomes independent, because then her investment is lost, whereas in a group others complete it. In tropical wasps, development takes about two months and a foundress has only about a 0.12 probability of surviving that long, so groups greatly increase the probability that a brood is completed (2.4- to 3.6-fold estimates). Adult birds have much higher survival over the few weeks needed to raise a brood, so the probability that care is interrupted by death, and hence the insurance benefit of helpers, is small.

5. In a model of nest-site choice, what would happen without the abandonment of dancing, and why are stop signals needed when two sites are equally good?

   **Answer** Without abandonment, scouts committed early to a poor site would keep recruiting, and positive feedback could lock the swarm into whichever site was discovered first, regardless of quality; abandonment at a rate that is higher for poorer sites lets support for them decay, so that the better site, whose dancers recruit longer, comes to dominate. With two equal sites, recruitment alone leads to a symmetric state with half the scouts committed to each, which is stable, and the swarm would never reach a quorum at either. Stop signals make support for one site suppress support for the other; above a critical strength, the symmetric state becomes unstable, so that small random differences grow and the swarm commits to one site, which is better than not deciding at all.
