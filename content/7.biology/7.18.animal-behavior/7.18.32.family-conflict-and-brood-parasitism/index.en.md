A family looks like the model of cooperation, but it contains three conflicts of interest. The two parents both gain from the survival of their young, but each gains more if the other does the work. Siblings compete for the food their parents bring. And parents and offspring disagree over how much should be given: an offspring is related to itself by 1, but its parent is related equally to all its offspring, present and future. This chapter treats each conflict in turn, first in theory and then in the field and the laboratory; the parent's own optimum, the starting point for all three, was derived in [7.18.31](/7.18.31#sec-31.3). The conflicts build on two concepts from evolutionary biology: **parent–offspring conflict** ([7.10.16](/7.10.16#def-16.3.2)), and the game theory of evolutionarily stable strategies ([7.18.25](/7.18.25)).

The last part of the chapter deals with the most extreme exploitation of parental care, by brood parasites that lay their eggs in the nests of other species and leave all the care to the hosts. Unrelated to their nestmates and foster parents, parasitic chicks behave with a selfishness that ordinary offspring cannot afford, and the arms race between cuckoos and their hosts is one of the best-studied cases of coevolution.

## 32.1. Three Conflicts in the Family

Parker, Royle and Hartley (2002) summarized the conflicts in a family as a triangle:

| Conflict | Between | Over | Section |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sexual conflict | Female and male parents | Who cares and how much | 32.2–32.3 |
| Sibling rivalry | Offspring | The share of parental investment each receives | 32.4–32.5 |
| Parent–offspring conflict | Parents and offspring | The total amount of investment given | 32.4, 32.6 |

The three are linked. Sibling rivalry is the cause of parent–offspring conflict: if a parent had only one offspring in its life, there would be nothing for the parent and offspring to disagree about. And the amount each parent invests depends on how much its partner invests and on how hard the young demand.

## 32.2. Who Should Care?

### 32.2.1. Maynard Smith's Model

Each parent's best decision depends on what the other does: if the female cares, it may pay the male to desert, but if she deserts, it may pay him to care. John Maynard Smith (1977) was the first to treat the problem as a game between the sexes, in which each parent chooses independently, in a "blind bid", whether to care or desert.

**Theorem 32.2.1 (ESSs of Care and Desertion)** Let eggs survive with probability $P_0$, $P_1$ or $P_2$ when cared for by no parent, one or two parents ($P_2 > P_1 > P_0$). A male who deserts has a chance $p$ of mating again; a female who deserts lays $W$ eggs and a female who cares lays $w < W$ eggs, because caring females have fewer resources for egg production. The payoffs are

| | Male cares | Male deserts |
|---|---|---|
| Female cares | female $wP_2$; male $wP_2$ | female $wP_1$; male $wP_1(1 + p)$ |
| Female deserts | female $WP_1$; male $WP_1$ | female $WP_0$; male $WP_0(1 + p)$ |

and a pair of strategies is evolutionarily stable if neither parent can gain by switching while the other keeps its strategy. The four possible ESSs and their conditions are:

1. both desert: $WP_0 > wP_1$ and $P_0(1 + p) > P_1$;
2. female deserts, male cares: $WP_1 > wP_2$ and $P_1 > P_0(1 + p)$;
3. female cares, male deserts: $wP_1 > WP_0$ and $P_1(1 + p) > P_2$;
4. both care: $wP_2 > WP_1$ and $P_2 > P_1(1 + p)$.

**Proof** In each cell of the table, compare the payoff of each parent with the payoff it would receive by switching while its partner keeps the same strategy. For example, (female deserts, male cares) is stable for the female if $WP_1$, her payoff, exceeds $wP_2$, the payoff of caring with a caring male; and for the male if $WP_1$ exceeds $WP_0(1 + p)$, the payoff of deserting a deserting female, that is $P_1 > P_0(1 + p)$. The other three cells are treated in the same way. $\square$

The conditions can hold for more than one cell at the same time, so the model often has alternative ESSs: "both desert" and "both care" can be alternatives, and so can "only the male cares" and "only the female cares". Which one a population reaches then depends on its history.

![Evolutionarily stable patterns of parental care in Maynard Smith's model (Theorem 32.2.1), as functions of the benefit of a second parent, P₂/P₁, and the gain in female fecundity from deserting, W/w, with P₁/P₀ = 3. A: when deserting males rarely find another mate (p = 0.3), biparental care is stable wherever a second parent adds enough (green), male-only care where females gain much by deserting (blue), and female-only care where neither parent adds much (red). B: when deserting males often remate (p = 1.0), female-only care becomes stable over a much wider range. Hatched areas have two alternative ESSs, female-only or male-only care, and history decides between them. With these parameters a male always gains by caring for a brood that the female has deserted, so "both desert" is never stable](./plots/who-cares.py)

The model fits the broad patterns of the previous chapter. ESS 2, male-only care, is likely when a female gains many more eggs by not caring ($W \gg w$), when one parent is much better than none ($P_1 \gg P_0$) but two are not much better than one ($P_2 \approx P_1$): this is the situation of many fishes, in which one parent can guard eggs as well as two ([7.18.31](/7.18.31#sec-31.2.2)). ESS 3, female-only care, is likely when a deserting male has a good chance of mating again, as in many mammals and birds with precocial young. ESS 4, biparental care, is likely when two parents raise many more young than one ($P_2 \gg P_1$) or deserting parents seldom remate, as in many songbirds.

### 32.2.2. A Flaw and the Need for Sequential Models

Maynard Smith's payoff matrix contains a flaw that remained unnoticed for 25 years (Kokko and Jennions 2003): deserting males gain extra offspring by mating with females that appear from nowhere, females who are not accounted for anywhere in the female payoffs. Total paternity then exceeds total maternity, which is impossible, since every offspring has one mother and one father. Wade and Shuster (2002) corrected the model by specifying where the extra matings come from, for instance from females that have themselves deserted their mates. The central insight of the model survives the correction: the best decision of one parent depends on the decision of the other, and on the operational sex ratio, which determines the chance of remating ([7.18.28](/7.18.28#sec-28.1.3)).

The assumption of simultaneous, independent decisions is also unrealistic. Parents usually decide in sequence, and the one that deserts first can force the other to care, since a parent left with a brood often does better by caring than by deserting too. In penduline tits (*Remiz pendulinus*), either parent may desert, leaving the other to raise the brood alone; but in about a third of nests both parents desert and the clutch is lost (Persson and Öhrström 1989; Szentirmai and colleagues 2007), a sign of a conflict over who deserts first, in which each parent may try to time its desertion so that the other is left holding the clutch. In St Peter's fish, by contrast, independent decisions would sometimes lead both parents to desert, but at least one parent always cares (Balshine-Earn 1995). Models in which desertion decisions are sequential and parents can respond to each other are needed for such cases.

## 32.3. How Much Should Each Parent Care?

### 32.3.1. Best Responses

When both parents care, they still disagree over how hard each should work. A simple experiment reveals the conflict: when one parent of a pair of birds is removed temporarily, the other often increases its feeding rate, which shows that it had spare capacity. Each parent could be tempted to do less than its share, relying on its partner to make up the difference. Houston and Davies (1985) analyzed the conflict as a game in which each parent chooses a level of effort.

**Theorem 32.3.1 (Best Responses and Partial Compensation)** Let the success of a brood be $B(x + y)$, an increasing, concave function of the total effort of the female ($x$) and the male ($y$), and let each parent pay a cost of its own effort, $K_{\mathrm{f}}(x)$ and $K_{\mathrm{m}}(y)$, increasing and convex. The female's best response $\hat x(y)$ to a male effort $y$ satisfies $B'(\hat x + y) = K_{\mathrm{f}}'(\hat x)$, and its slope is

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}\hat x}{\mathrm{d}y} = \frac{B''}{K_{\mathrm{f}}'' - B''} \in (-1, 0),
$$
and similarly for the male. Hence each parent responds to a reduction in its partner's effort by increasing its own effort by a smaller amount (*partial compensation*), and the intersection of the two best-response curves, the ESS of biparental care, is stable: successive responses converge to it.

**Proof** Differentiating $B'(\hat x + y) = K_{\mathrm{f}}'(\hat x)$ with respect to $y$ gives $B''(\hat x' + 1) = K_{\mathrm{f}}''\hat x'$, so $\hat x' = B''/(K_{\mathrm{f}}'' - B'')$. With $B'' < 0$ and $K_{\mathrm{f}}'' \geq 0$, the numerator is negative and the denominator positive and larger in absolute value than the numerator, so $-1 < \hat x' < 0$. If the female's and male's best responses have slopes $a$ and $b$ in $(-1, 0)$, a deviation $\delta$ in the female's effort produces a male response $b\delta$, a female reply $ab\delta$, and so on; since $\lvert ab\rvert < 1$, the deviations shrink geometrically and the efforts converge to the intersection, as in the stability analysis of a fixed point of an iterated map. $\square$

If the brood's benefit were accelerating over some range (for example, if a minimum total effort is needed for any chick to survive), the best responses could have slopes steeper than $-1$; a parent would then more than compensate for a reduction in its partner's effort, the intersection would be unstable, and the ESS would be uniparental care, by whichever parent was left with the brood.

![Best-response curves for parental effort (Theorem 32.3.1). A: with diminishing returns of total effort to the brood and increasing costs to each parent, each parent's best response to its partner's effort has a slope between 0 and −1 (partial compensation); the curves cross at a stable equilibrium of biparental care, which successive best responses (dashed staircase) approach from any starting effort. B: if each parent overcompensated for a change in its partner's effort (slopes steeper than −1, here linear), the crossing point would be unstable: successive responses diverge until one parent does all the work](./plots/negotiation.py)

**Definition 32.3.1 (Partial Compensation)** **Partial compensation** is a response of a parent to a change in its partner's parental effort in the opposite direction but of smaller magnitude, so that total effort changes in the same direction as the partner's effort; in *full compensation* total effort is unchanged, in *overcompensation* it changes in the opposite direction, and in a *matching* response the parent changes its effort in the same direction as its partner.

### 32.3.2. Negotiation

In the Houston–Davies model, effort levels are fixed strategies and the equilibrium is reached over evolutionary time. Real parents adjust their efforts to each other during a breeding attempt.

**Definition 32.3.2 (Negotiation Model)** A **negotiation model** of parental care is a game in which each parent's strategy is a response rule specifying how its effort depends on its partner's effort, observed during repeated interactions in a breeding attempt; the efforts converge on a negotiated outcome within the breeding attempt, and it is the response rules, not the effort levels, that evolve (McNamara, Gasson and Houston 1999).

The negotiation models differ in detail from the blind-bid models (in particular, the negotiated effort is lower than the blind-bid effort, because each parent's responsiveness can be exploited by its partner), but they also predict partial compensation, now as a behavioral response. The prediction has been tested in many experiments with birds in which one parent's effort was reduced by temporary removal, clipping of flight feathers, attaching weights to the tail or implanting testosterone, which makes males sing and patrol more and feed less. A meta-analysis of such studies found that, on average, the partner increased its effort but by less than the reduction, partial compensation as predicted, although individual studies varied from no response to full compensation (Harrison and colleagues 2009). The theory also predicts that a partner who reduces its effort suffers, because its young are less well fed.

A study by Camilla Hinde (2006) produced a surprising result. She placed a small loudspeaker in nest boxes of great tits and played extra begging calls, so that the brood seemed hungrier than it was. When only one parent was exposed to the playback, because it was played only when that parent visited, the exposed parent increased its provisioning, as expected; but so did its partner, which had not heard the extra calls.

| Treatment | Female feeds/h | Male feeds/h | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| Control (no playback) | 15 | 18 | 33 |
| Playback to female | 22 | 25 | 47 |
| Playback to male | 19 | 27 | 46 |

The partner matched the increase rather than compensating for it. The most likely explanation is that the basic model omits information. A parent's effort affects its partner in two ways: it changes the marginal benefit of the partner's effort, which favors compensation, and it may carry information about the needs of the brood, if one parent knows them better, which favors matching (Johnstone and Hinde 2006). When the partner's work rate is a cue to brood need, a parent that sees its partner working harder should work harder too.

## 32.4. Sibling Rivalry and Parent–Offspring Conflict: Theory

Robert Trivers (1974) pointed out that the optimal investment in an offspring differs from the offspring's point of view and from the parent's. Two forms of conflict can be distinguished. In *intrabrood* conflict, siblings in the same brood compete for the food a parent brings: a parent related by $\tfrac12$ to each has no genetic reason to favor one, but each is related to itself by 1 and to a full sibling by $\tfrac12$, so each should try to take more than its share. In *interbrood* conflict, an offspring competes with siblings yet to be born: there comes a time when a parent gains by ending care for its current offspring and saving its resources for the next, but the current offspring gains by demanding more.

**Proposition 32.4.1 (The Zone of Parent–Offspring Conflict)** Let an investment $x$ in the current offspring give it a benefit $B(x)$ (increasing, concave) and cost its siblings, current or future, a total $C(x)$ (increasing, convex), both measured in offspring-equivalents of survival. A parent equally related to all its offspring maximizes $B(x) - C(x)$; an offspring related by $r$ to the siblings that bear the cost maximizes $B(x) - rC(x)$. The parent's optimum $x_{\mathrm{p}}$ satisfies $B'(x_{\mathrm{p}}) = C'(x_{\mathrm{p}})$, the offspring's optimum $x_{\mathrm{o}}$ satisfies $B'(x_{\mathrm{o}}) = rC'(x_{\mathrm{o}})$, and $x_{\mathrm{o}} > x_{\mathrm{p}}$ whenever $r < 1$. In the interval $(x_{\mathrm{p}}, x_{\mathrm{o}})$, the offspring is selected to demand more investment and the parent to refuse it.

**Proof** Both objective functions are concave, so their optima are where the derivatives vanish. At $x_{\mathrm{p}}$, $B' - rC' = (1 - r)C'(x_{\mathrm{p}}) > 0$, so the offspring's objective is still increasing at $x_{\mathrm{p}}$, and its maximum lies to the right. $\square$

The proposition is Hamilton's rule seen from both sides ([Theorem 16.3.1](/7.10.16#thm-16.3.1)). With $C$ linear, the parent should continue investing while $B'/C' > 1$, and the offspring wants investment to continue while $B'/C' > r$, that is $\tfrac12$ for full siblings and $\tfrac14$ for half siblings ([Definition 16.3.2](/7.10.16#def-16.3.2)). Beyond $x_{\mathrm{o}}$, parent and offspring agree that investment should stop. The theory predicts that the conflict is greater when siblings are less related: in species with multiple paternity, offspring should demand more.

![Parent–offspring conflict over investment per offspring (Proposition 32.4.1). A: with a diminishing benefit to the offspring and a linear cost to its siblings, the parent's fitness (purple) is maximal at a lower investment than the offspring's (red, for full siblings); between the two optima (shaded) the offspring is selected to demand more and the parent to refuse. B: the parent's optimum does not depend on the relatedness among its offspring, but the offspring's optimum increases as relatedness falls; the zone of conflict (shaded) is wider for half siblings (r = 1/4) than for full siblings (r = 1/2) and vanishes only for r = 1](./plots/conflict-zone.py)

## 32.5. Sibling Rivalry: Evidence

### 32.5.1. Siblicide

Siblings compete for parental resources everywhere, and the competition is most dramatic when they kill each other.

**Definition 32.5.1 (Siblicide)** **Siblicide** is the killing of an individual by its sibling, directly by attack or indirectly by monopolizing parental food. It is *facultative* when it occurs only under some conditions, typically when food is scarce, and *obligate* when the older sibling kills the younger in nearly every brood regardless of conditions.

Siblicide often arises because parents produce an "optimistic" number of offspring when food supply is unpredictable, which are then reduced if conditions turn out poor.

- *Galápagos fur seals* (*Arctocephalus galapagoensis*) raise one pup at a time. When fish are abundant, a mother weans her pup at about 18 months, but in poor years pups grow slowly and suckle for two or three years. As a result up to 23 percent of pups are born while an older sibling is still being nursed. The two then compete for milk, and the younger pup usually dies within a month, of starvation or from attacks by the older pup, which may grab it and toss it about; mothers sometimes intervene, which can lead to a fatal tug-of-war (Trillmich and Wolf 2008). This is interbrood conflict.
- *Blue-footed boobies* (*Sula nebouxii*) lay two eggs and start incubating with the first, so the first chick hatches about four days before the second. The older chick, taller, intercepts the fish regurgitated by the parent, and the younger is fed only when the older is satiated. When the older chick's weight falls to about 20–25 percent below normal, it pecks the younger, which cowers, stops begging and starves. When Drummond and Chavelas (1989) deprived both chicks of food for a day, in a year when food was abundant, the older chicks increased their attacks, which declined again once feeding resumed: siblicide is facultative and depends on the hunger of the older chick. The parents never intervene.
- *Obligate siblicide* occurs in some eagles, pelicans and boobies. The Nazca booby (*Sula granti*) lays two eggs, but the first chick to hatch nearly always pushes its sibling out of the nest scrape, where it dies. Why lay a second egg? It is an insurance against failure of the first egg or early death of the first chick: second eggs often produce surviving young when the first egg fails (Anderson 1990), and the experimental removal of second eggs reduced the reproductive success of Nazca boobies and of American white pelicans (*Pelecanus erythrorhynchos*).

The great egrets (*Ardea alba*) studied by Douglas Mock drop small fish in front of their broods and let the chicks fight for them; dominant chicks sometimes beat a sibling to death or push it from the nest, while the parents look on. Great blue herons (*Ardea herodias*) usually bring larger prey, which chicks cannot monopolize, and their chicks fight less; when heron broods were experimentally fed small fish, aggression among the chicks increased (Mock 1985): the way food is delivered determines whether monopolizing it, and so fighting, pays.

### 32.5.2. Relatedness and Begging

In passerine birds, nestlings compete by "charm" rather than by force: they beg with loud calls and brightly colored gapes, and parents feed the chicks that beg most. The theory predicts more vigorous begging when nestmates are less related. In a comparison of passerine species, nestlings begged more loudly in species with more extra-pair paternity, in which nestmates are more often half siblings; the species with the loudest calls was the brown-headed cowbird, a brood parasite unrelated to its nestmates (Briskie and colleagues 1994). Nestlings also had redder mouths in species with more extra-pair paternity, though only in species with open, well-lit nests, where mouth color is visible (Kilner 1999).

The same competition explains ornaments of chicks. In the rail family (Rallidae), 36 of 97 species studied have ornamented chicks with brightly colored bills, bare skin or plumes, and the ornaments have evolved several times, in association with larger broods and multiple parentage (Krebs and Putland 2004). Newly hatched American coots (*Fulica americana*) have long, orange-tipped plumes on their black bodies. When Bruce Lyon and colleagues (1994) trimmed the plumes of all the chicks in a brood, parents fed them and they grew as well as normal broods: the plumes have no direct effect on survival. But in broods in which half the chicks were trimmed, parents preferred the orange chicks, and the black chicks grew more slowly. Parental preference is relative, a key condition for the evolution of exaggerated signals, as in mate choice ([7.18.29](/7.18.29)).

### 32.5.3. Brood Reduction as a Parental Strategy

Not every case of sibling rivalry is against the parents' interests. David Lack (1947) proposed that parents begin incubation before completing their clutch so that the chicks hatch asynchronously, creating a size hierarchy that makes brood reduction efficient when food is short: the smallest chick dies quickly, rather than all chicks being weakened. Robert Magrath (1989) tested this in European blackbirds (*Turdus merula*), whose earthworm food becomes scarce in dry weather. He created synchronous broods of four chicks of similar size and asynchronous broods with a natural size hierarchy by swapping newly hatched young between nests, and provided some pairs with extra food.

| Broods | Good food supply | Poor food supply |
|---|---|---|
| Synchronous hatching | 2.9 young (8 broods) | 1.3 young (21 broods) |
| Asynchronous hatching | 2.3 young (13 broods) | 2.1 young (25 broods) |

(Mean number of young surviving to two weeks after fledging.) Under poor conditions asynchronous broods produced more surviving young, because the smallest chicks died quickly and the survivors could be raised well; under good conditions synchronous broods tended to do better. Hatching asynchrony, and the sibling rivalry it allows, can be a parental adaptation, and observing rivalry is not by itself evidence of parent–offspring conflict.

## 32.6. Parent–Offspring Conflict: Evidence

### 32.6.1. Battlegrounds

Weaning tantrums in mammals and the escalating begging of fledglings look like conflicts, and Trivers (1974) suggested that they reflect attempts by offspring to extract more than parents want to give. But behavioral squabbles do not demonstrate an evolutionary conflict: they may be part of normal development, or a way in which parents gain information about their young (Bateson 1994). Godfray (1995) distinguished "battleground" models, which define the zone of conflict (Proposition 32.4.1), from "resolution" models, which predict how the conflict is resolved. Evidence for a battleground requires showing that the optima of parents and offspring differ, or that traits of parent and offspring act against each other. Two cases are convincing.

- *Sex ratios in social insects.* In a colony of ants, bees or wasps headed by a single, singly mated queen, the queen is related equally to her sons and daughters ($r = \tfrac12$) and favors equal investment in reproductive males and females, but the workers, related to their sisters by $\tfrac34$ and their brothers by $\tfrac14$ because of **haplodiploidy**, favor a $3 : 1$ investment ratio in favor of females (Trivers and Hare 1976; [7.10.13](/7.10.13#sec-13.3.3)). Workers can bias the ratio by selectively killing male larvae, and in some species the investment ratio approaches their optimum; in others it is closer to the queen's, or intermediate, so either party can win depending on circumstances (Ratnieks and colleagues 2006). The details are given in [7.18.36](/7.18.36).
- *Genomic imprinting.* In mammals in which females mate with several males, a gene in an offspring inherited from its father is less likely to be present in its mother's other offspring than a gene inherited from its mother. Paternally derived genes are therefore expected to extract more maternal resources than maternally derived genes, and **genomic imprinting**, the expression of a gene from only one parental copy, can evolve as part of this conflict (Moore and Haig 1991; [7.10.16](/7.10.16#sec-16.3.2)). In mice, *Igf2*, which encodes a growth factor that promotes fetal growth, is expressed only from the paternal allele; inactivating it reduces birth weight to about 60 percent of normal. *Igf2r*, which encodes a receptor that removes the growth factor, is expressed only from the maternal allele, and inactivating it increases birth weight by about 20 percent: a tug-of-war between genes of different parental origin within the offspring.

### 32.6.2. Resolution by Costly Begging

If offspring are selected to demand more than parents should give, parents should respond only to signals that honestly reveal need. Godfray (1991, 1995) showed that an evolutionarily stable resolution is possible if begging is costly to the offspring.

**Definition 32.6.1 (Begging)** **Begging** is the solicitation of food or other care by dependent young from their parents (or other caregivers), by calls, postures, gaping and color displays, whose intensity typically increases with the young's hunger or need.

In Godfray's model, the need of an offspring (its condition) is known to itself but not to the parent; the offspring chooses a begging intensity, and the parent chooses how much to provide in response to it. If begging is costly, and the cost of a given level of begging is relatively smaller for needier offspring (whose marginal benefit from food is larger), then there is a signaling equilibrium in which begging intensity increases with need, and the parent, by responding to begging, gives each offspring close to what is optimal from the parent's point of view. The logic is that of the condition-dependent handicap ([7.18.29](/7.18.29#sec-29.3.2)): the cost keeps the signal honest. At such an equilibrium, part of the resource that the conflict is about is wasted on begging.

The predictions have been tested in canaries (*Serinus canaria*). Hungry nestlings begged more, and parents provided more food as begging increased (Kilner 1995). Begging was costly: when pairs of siblings were given the same amount of food by hand, but one had to beg for 10 seconds and the other for 60 seconds before each meal (both within the natural range), the one that begged longer gained less mass (Kilner 2001). Resolution models also predict that offspring demand and parental supply become coadapted. Mathias Kölliker and colleagues (2000) cross-fostered great tit nestlings, so that the begging of each chick could be measured independently of the parents that raised it, and measured each pair's response to playbacks of begging calls. Nestlings from the same genetic brood had similar begging intensities, and chicks whose genetic mother was more generous begged more. The same positive correlation between offspring demand and maternal provisioning has been found in burying beetles, whose larvae beg from their parents (Lock and colleagues 2004). In canaries, the correlation is produced by a maternal effect: females given better food before laying deposited more androgens in their eggs, provisioned more and had nestlings that begged more (Hinde and colleagues 2009). When canary broods were cross-fostered so that they begged more or less than their foster parents' own young would have, they grew best when their begging matched what the parents expected: broods that under-demanded were fed too little, and broods that over-demanded were fed but did not compensate for the energy spent begging (Hinde and colleagues 2010). The conflict is resolved, and concealed, by coadaptation.

## 32.7. Brood Parasitism

### 32.7.1. Parasites of Parental Care

Some birds, fishes and insects exploit the parental care of others. **Brood parasitism** ([7.17.53](/7.17.53)) is the laying of eggs in the nests of other individuals, which then raise the young. It is *interspecific* when the hosts are of another species, *intraspecific* when they are of the same species, and *obligate* when the parasite never raises its own young. Among birds, about 1 percent of species are obligate brood parasites, in five lineages: Old World cuckoos, honeyguides, the cowbirds of the New World, the African indigobirds and whydahs with the cuckoo finch, and the black-headed duck of South America. Within the cuckoo family (Cuculidae), about 40 percent of the roughly 140 species are brood parasites, and the habit has evolved three times independently (Sorenson and Payne 2005).

Because they are unrelated to their hosts and nestmates, parasitic young are predicted to be extremely selfish, and they are.

- *Cuckoos.* A common cuckoo (*Cuculus canorus*) chick, hatching earlier than the host's eggs after an unusually short incubation, balances the host eggs, and any newly hatched host chicks, on its back one by one within a few hours of hatching and heaves them over the rim of the nest. It then has the hosts' full care, and reed warbler hosts go on feeding it until it weighs about seven times as much as they do.
- *Honeyguides.* Nestlings of the greater honeyguide (*Indicator indicator*) have sharp hooks on the tips of their bills, which they use to stab the host nestlings to death in the dark of their tree-hole or burrow nests within days of hatching; the hooks are lost before fledging (Spottiswoode and Koorevaar 2012).
- *Cowbirds.* The brown-headed cowbird (*Molothrus ater*) does not eliminate its nestmates, and it grows best when it shares the nest with about two host young: across host species, cowbird chicks grew fastest in nests that fledged about two host young, and in experiments with eastern phoebe hosts (*Sayornis phoebe*), cowbird chicks raised with two host chicks grew faster than chicks raised alone (Kilner and colleagues 2004). The host chicks' begging raises the total food brought by the parents, of which the larger cowbird takes the lion's share.
- *Social parasites of ants.* Caterpillars of large blue butterflies (*Phengaris*, formerly *Maculinea*) produce cuticular hydrocarbons that mimic those of *Myrmica* ant larvae, so that worker ants carry them into the nest, where they either eat the ant brood or are fed by the workers like ant larvae. Caterpillars of *P. rebeli* even mimic the sounds of queen ants, and workers then treat them like royalty, feeding them in preference to their own brood when food is short (Barbero and colleagues 2009).

The cuckoo's tactics show how a parasite must "tune in" to its host's provisioning rules. Reed warbler parents adjust the food they bring to two cues: the total area of gapes in the nest, which reflects the number and age of chicks, and the rate of begging calls, which reflects their hunger. A single cuckoo chick needs as much food as a brood of four reed warblers, but its gape is smaller than the combined gapes of a brood. It compensates with an extraordinarily rapid begging call, which sounds like a whole brood of hungry host chicks (Davies and colleagues 1998; Kilner and colleagues 1999). The northern hawk-cuckoo of Japan (*Hierococcyx hyperythrus*, formerly treated as Horsfield's hawk-cuckoo, *Cuculus fugax*) manipulates the visual cue instead: while begging, it raises its wings to expose bare yellow patches the color of its gape, and hosts sometimes try to place food in a wing patch; blackening the patches experimentally reduced provisioning (Tanaka and Ueda 2005).

### 32.7.2. The Arms Race at the Egg Stage

Brood parasitism is costly for hosts, which lose their whole brood when a cuckoo chick hatches, so hosts are selected to defend themselves and cuckoos to overcome the defenses: an **evolutionary arms race** ([7.10.17](/7.10.17)). Field experiments by Nick Davies and Michael Brooke with model eggs placed in host nests uncovered the stages of the race.

- *Cuckoo tactics are adapted to host defenses.* A female common cuckoo watches hosts building their nests, then parasitizes a nest during the hosts' laying period, in the afternoon, by gliding down, removing one host egg, laying her own and flying off, often within ten seconds. Reed warblers were more likely to reject a model egg if it matched their own eggs poorly, if it was placed before they had begun to lay, if it was placed at dawn (when hosts themselves lay), or if they had seen a mounted cuckoo on their nest. Mimetic eggs, the timing of laying and the speed of laying all reduce rejection.
- *Cuckoo egg mimicry evolves in response to host discrimination.* The common cuckoo consists of several host races (*gentes*), each specializing on one host species and laying eggs that match, to varying degrees, those of its host; the egg type is inherited through the female line. Measured through a bird's eye, using the four cone types of avian color vision, cuckoo eggs match host eggs more closely in host races whose hosts reject non-mimetic eggs more strongly (Stoddard and Stevens 2011). The dunnock, whose cuckoo race lays non-mimetic eggs, rejects hardly any foreign eggs: either the dunnock is a recent host that has not yet evolved defenses, or rejection does not pay it.
- *Host defenses evolve in response to parasitism.* Species that are unsuitable as cuckoo hosts, because they feed their young on seeds or nest in holes inaccessible to cuckoos, show little rejection of odd eggs and little aggression toward adult cuckoos, whereas suitable hosts do (Davies and Brooke 1989). Hosts also evolve egg "signatures": species parasitized by cuckoos have less variation in egg appearance within a clutch and more between the clutches of different females than unparasitized species (Stokke and colleagues 2002), which makes foreign eggs easier to spot and harder to forge. The tawny-flanked prinia (*Prinia subflava*), parasitized by the cuckoo finch (*Anomalospiza imberbis*) in Africa, has eggs that vary independently in background color and in the size, shape and dispersion of markings; the cuckoo finch lays its varied eggs at random with respect to host signatures and suffers high rejection (Spottiswoode and Stevens 2010).

When mimicry becomes good, rejection becomes risky, because a host may reject one of its own eggs by mistake, and it may then be better to accept. Hosts face a problem of **signal detection theory** ([7.16.47](/7.16.47)): the appearance of their own eggs and that of cuckoo eggs overlap, and the best rejection threshold depends on the costs of the two errors and on the probability of parasitism. Reed warblers behave as this predicts: they are more likely to reject a moderately mimetic egg when they have seen a cuckoo near their nest, a cue that parasitism is likely (Davies and colleagues 1996).

### 32.7.3. The Chick Stage

Why do hosts that reject odd eggs accept a cuckoo chick that soon grows far larger than themselves and has a differently colored gape? Arnon Lotem (1993) suggested that the answer lies in how hosts learn. Experiments show that hosts learn the appearance of their own eggs in their first breeding attempt and later reject eggs that differ from them. Learning at the egg stage is safe enough, because a host's first clutch usually contains several of its own eggs. But a host parasitized in its first breeding attempt sees only a cuckoo chick in its nest, because the cuckoo ejects the host eggs before they hatch; if it learned the appearance of its chicks from that brood, it would reject its own chicks in all later, unparasitized broods. The cost of such misimprinting exceeds the benefit of rejecting cuckoo chicks, and chick rejection does not evolve, at least against chick-ejecting cuckoos. The argument does not apply where the parasite does not eject the host young, so that hosts can compare: superb fairy-wrens (*Malurus cyaneus*) in Australia abandon about 40 percent of the chicks of Horsfield's bronze-cuckoo (*Chrysococcyx basalis*) (Langmore and colleagues 2003), and in response the chicks of several Australian bronze-cuckoos have evolved to resemble the chicks of their hosts in appearance and call.

Hosts can also be coerced. Brown-headed cowbirds return to nests they have parasitized, and in an experiment with prothonotary warblers (*Protonotaria citrea*), nests from which the cowbird eggs were removed experimentally were depredated much more often than nests in which the cowbird eggs were left, apparently by the cowbirds themselves (Hoover and Robinson 2007). This "mafia" behavior makes acceptance the hosts' best option.

### 32.7.4. Intraspecific Brood Parasitism

Brood parasitism within species is more widespread than interspecific parasitism, reported in more than 230 bird species, especially in waterfowl, and in fishes and insects. Females that cannot obtain a nest of their own, or that lose their nest during laying, lay some eggs in the nests of neighbors; others lay part of their clutch parasitically and raise the rest themselves, spreading their eggs over several nests. In American coots, about 40 percent of nests in a British Columbia population received parasitic eggs, and host females recognized many parasitic eggs and pushed them to the edge of the nest, where they hatched less well; hosts that accepted parasitic eggs reduced the number of their own eggs, which suggests that they counted their eggs and used only their own as a cue for the end of laying (Lyon 2003). Intraspecific parasitism is one possible route to obligate interspecific parasitism, though the evidence for this route is mixed.

### 32.7.5. Why Are Not All Birds Brood Parasites?

If brood parasitism is so profitable, why do only about 1 percent of bird species practice it? Several factors limit it.

1. *Suitable hosts must exist.* A parasite needs hosts whose eggs it can match, whose incubation period it can beat and whose diet and care suit its chicks; seed-eating hosts cannot raise a cuckoo, and precocial chicks, which feed themselves, offer less care to exploit. Parasites are concentrated among species with altricial young parasitizing smaller, altricial hosts.
2. *Many adaptations are needed at once.* An obligate parasite must find nests at the right stage, lay quickly, produce eggs that resist host defenses (thick shells, mimicry), hatch early, and have chicks that compete with or eliminate host young and stimulate host care. A lineage that starts parasitizing without these traits does poorly, so the transition is difficult.
3. *Hosts fight back.* Defenses such as rejection, nest guarding and mobbing reduce the success of parasites, and if parasites become common, hosts evolve stronger defenses or abandon parasitized nests: the success of parasitism is frequency-dependent, and parasites can be common only where hosts are abundant and poorly defended.
4. *Parental care has its own benefits.* A parent that raises its young can adjust care to their needs, while a parasitic chick receives care tuned to another species; and in many species, defended nest sites, territories and pair bonds bring benefits that a parasite gives up.

## Summary

- A family contains three linked conflicts: between parents (who cares and how much), among siblings (shares of investment) and between parents and offspring (total investment); sibling rivalry is the cause of parent–offspring conflict.
- Maynard Smith's model (Theorem 32.2.1) gives four possible ESSs of care and desertion, often with alternatives; it predicts male care in fishes, female care where deserting males remate, and biparental care where two parents raise many more young. Its payoffs violate the parity of paternity and maternity (Wade and Shuster), and real decisions are sequential (penduline tits).
- Diminishing returns and increasing costs make each parent's best response a partial compensation for its partner's effort, which stabilizes biparental care (Theorem 32.3.1, Definition 32.3.1); negotiation models (Definition 32.3.2) predict the same behaviorally, and experiments confirm partial compensation on average. Matching responses (great tits) show that a partner's effort can carry information about brood need.
- An offspring's optimal investment exceeds its parent's whenever siblings are related by $r < 1$ (Proposition 32.4.1).
- Siblicide (Definition 32.5.1) is facultative in fur seals and blue-footed boobies, obligate in Nazca boobies (second eggs as insurance). Begging is louder and mouths redder where nestmates are less related; coot parents prefer ornamented chicks. Hatching asynchrony can be a parental adaptation (blackbirds).
- Battlegrounds of parent–offspring conflict: sex ratio conflict in social Hymenoptera, imprinted *Igf2* and *Igf2r* in mice. Costly begging (Definition 32.6.1) can resolve the conflict (Godfray); canaries, great tits and burying beetles show coadaptation of offspring demand and parental supply.
- Brood parasites (cuckoos, honeyguides, cowbirds, large blue butterflies) exploit host provisioning rules; egg mimicry, host rejection and egg signatures form an arms race in which, once mimicry is good, acceptance may be optimal. Chick rejection is rare because of misimprinting, except where hosts can compare (fairy-wrens). Intraspecific parasitism is common; obligate parasitism is limited by host suitability, the many adaptations needed, host defenses and the benefits of caring.

## Questions

1. Using Theorem 32.2.1, explain why male-only care is common in fishes and female-only care in mammals, identifying the parameters that differ.

   **Answer** In many fishes, care consists of guarding eggs, which one parent does nearly as well as two ($P_2 \approx P_1$), while care greatly improves on no care ($P_1 \gg P_0$); a female that does not care can put her resources into many more eggs ($W \gg w$), and a guarding male can still attract other females (care costs him few matings, which in the model lowers his effective $p$). These conditions favor ESS 2, male cares and female deserts. In mammals, the female must gestate and lactate, so female care is obligatory ($w$ is the only option, and $P_0 = 0$ for a deserting female); males can do little to increase survival ($P_2 \approx P_1$), and a deserting male has a good chance of mating again ($p$ high), conditions for ESS 3, female cares and male deserts.
2. Derive the slope of a parent's best response in Theorem 32.3.1 for $B(T) = 1 - \mathrm{e}^{-T}$ and $K(x) = kx^2/2$, and explain why it implies partial compensation.

   **Answer** The best response satisfies $\mathrm{e}^{-(x + y)} = kx$. Differentiating, $-\mathrm{e}^{-T}(x' + 1) = kx'$, so $x' = -\mathrm{e}^{-T}/(\mathrm{e}^{-T} + k)$, which lies between $-1$ and 0 because $k > 0$. A reduction $\delta$ in the partner's effort raises the parent's effort by $\lvert x'\rvert\delta < \delta$, so total effort falls: partial compensation. This follows because the brood gains less from each extra unit of total effort (diminishing returns) while the parent's own cost rises, so making up the whole loss is not worth it.
3. In Hinde's great tit experiment, the parent that did not hear the extra begging calls also increased its feeding rate. Explain why this contradicts the simple best-response model, and design an experiment to test the information hypothesis.

   **Answer** In the best-response model, an increase in one parent's effort lowers the marginal benefit of the other's effort, so the partner should reduce its effort (partial compensation), not increase it. Under the information hypothesis, a parent uses its partner's work rate as a cue to the brood's needs. Test: manipulate the information available to the unmanipulated parent independently, for example by letting it hear the extra begging directly (if it already knows brood need, its partner's effort carries no new information, and it should compensate) or by preventing it from observing its partner's visits (then matching should disappear). Alternatively, manipulate one parent's effort without changing brood need (for example a handicap such as a tail weight) and compare with manipulations that increase its effort through apparent brood need (playback).
4. Explain why the blue-footed booby is said to show facultative siblicide and the Nazca booby obligate siblicide. Why do Nazca boobies lay a second egg?

   **Answer** In blue-footed boobies, the older chick attacks its sibling only when it is underfed (below about 20–25 percent of its expected weight), and both chicks can survive in good years; experimental food deprivation increased attacks, which declined when feeding resumed, so siblicide depends on conditions. In Nazca boobies, the older chick ejects the younger in nearly every brood regardless of food. The second egg is insurance: if the first egg fails to hatch or the first chick dies early, the second provides a surviving chick; experiments removing the second egg reduced reproductive success, so the cost of producing an egg that usually dies is outweighed by the insurance value.
5. Why do reed warblers reject non-mimetic cuckoo eggs but accept a cuckoo chick? What evidence supports this explanation, and where would you expect hosts to reject parasitic chicks?

   **Answer** Hosts learn the appearance of their own eggs during their first clutch, which normally contains several own eggs, so learning is reliable. For chicks, a host parasitized in its first attempt would see only the cuckoo chick (the host eggs having been ejected) and would learn to accept cuckoo chicks and reject its own in future broods; the cost of such misimprinting outweighs the benefit of rejecting cuckoo chicks (Lotem 1993). Evidence: experiments show that hosts learn egg appearance in their first breeding attempt; and chick rejection occurs where the parasite chick is not alone, so that hosts can compare chicks, or where hosts can use cues other than learning, as in superb fairy-wrens, which abandon bronze-cuckoo chicks (and the chicks have evolved mimicry of host chicks in response). Chick rejection is expected where parasites do not eliminate host young and hosts can compare chicks, or where parasite chicks differ from host chicks in a fixed, innate way.
6. (Open question) Why have not all bird species evolved brood parasitism? Discuss the constraints, and suggest how a comparative analysis could test one of them.

   **Answer** Constraints include: the need for suitable hosts (with compatible diet, incubation period and altricial young; seed-eating or hole-nesting species are unsuitable); the many adaptations required simultaneously (finding nests, fast laying, mimetic and robust eggs, short incubation, competitive chicks that tune into host provisioning rules), which make the transition from parental care difficult; frequency-dependent costs, since host defenses escalate as parasitism becomes common; and the benefits of caring for one's own young, which a parasite forgoes. A comparative test: on a phylogeny of birds, examine whether obligate parasitism has evolved preferentially in lineages with traits that make the transition easier, such as an altricial mode of development with small eggs relative to body size (allowing the parasite to match smaller hosts' eggs), a generalist insectivorous diet of the young, or a history of intraspecific brood parasitism, using phylogenetic comparative methods to test whether these traits precede the origins of parasitism.
