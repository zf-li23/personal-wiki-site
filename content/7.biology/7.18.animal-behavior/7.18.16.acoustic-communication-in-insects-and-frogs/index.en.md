Acoustic communication requires agreement between two nervous systems. The sender must produce a signal with a structure that the receiver can detect, recognize and localize, and the receiver must respond to the signal and not to the many other sounds of a nocturnal meadow or pond. For a neuroethologist this agreement is an opportunity: the features of the signal tell the experimenter what to look for in the receiver's brain, and the receiver's preferences reveal the features that the sender's motor system must control. Crickets and frogs, whose choruses fill warm nights across the world, became the classic model systems. In crickets, the pioneering work of Franz Huber and his school, continued by Ronald Hoy, Berthold Hedwig and many others, traced the communication system from the motor neurons that drive the wings of the singing male to the interneurons in the brain of the female that recognize his song. In frogs, Robert Capranica, Michael Ryan, Carl Gerhardt and colleagues related the structure of calls to the tuning of the inner ear and to the preferences of females.

This chapter combines several themes of the preceding chapters: sound reception and the **tympanal organ** ([7.17.27](/7.17.27#sec-27.2.4), [7.18.11](/7.18.11)), feature detection ([7.18.13](/7.18.13)), command neurons and central pattern generators ([7.18.14](/7.18.14)) and the cancellation of self-generated signals ([7.18.15](/7.18.15#sec-15.7)). The evolution of signals under **sexual selection** and the general theory of animal signals are treated in [7.10.13](/7.10.13) and [7.18.37](/7.18.37)–[7.18.40](/7.18.40).

## 16.1. Cricket Song

### 16.1.1. Three Songs

Male field crickets (Gryllidae) sing three kinds of song, each in a different behavioral context:

- The *calling song*, produced at the entrance to the male's burrow, attracts sexually receptive females from a distance, often many meters.
- The *courtship song*, softer and with a different temporal pattern, is produced when a female has arrived and touched the male with her antennae; it induces her to mount the male, who then transfers a spermatophore.
- The *aggressive* (rivalry) *song* is produced during encounters between males, which recognize each other's sex by antennal contact, and accompanies fights for burrows.

The calling song carries the information needed for long-distance mate attraction. Its structure is hierarchical. The basic unit is the *syllable* (or pulse), an uninterrupted train of sound waves produced by one closing stroke of the wings; syllables are grouped into *chirps*, separated by silences; and the sound within each syllable is an almost pure tone, the *carrier frequency*. In the European field crickets *Gryllus campestris* and *G. bimaculatus*, syllables last about $15$–$20\,\mathrm{ms}$ and follow each other at intervals of about $35\,\mathrm{ms}$ (a syllable rate of about $30\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$), three to five syllables form a chirp, and chirps are repeated two to four times per second; the carrier frequency is $4$–$5\,\mathrm{kHz}$. These parameters are highly stereotyped within a species and differ between species, mainly in the temporal pattern: the number and rate of syllables and the arrangement of chirps or trills. The carrier frequency varies less between species.

### 16.1.2. Producing the Song

Crickets sing by stridulation ([7.17.27](/7.17.27#sec-27.6.1)): during the closing stroke of the forewings the scraper on the edge of the left forewing is drawn across a file of teeth on the underside of the right one. Each tooth impact excites a thin, resonant area of the wing, the harp, which vibrates at its natural frequency. The harp in turn controls the rate at which teeth are struck, as the escapement of a clock controls its gears, so that the tooth-strike rate equals the resonant frequency and the song is an almost pure tone. The opening stroke is silent. One syllable is therefore one closing stroke, and the syllable rate is the rate of the wing movements. Radiating sound efficiently from such a small source is a problem; crickets solve it with resonant wings and, in some species, with baffles and horns ([7.17.27](/7.17.27#sec-27.6.1)).

The wing movements are driven by the same muscles of the mesothorax that move the wings in flight, each innervated by one to a few motor neurons. When all sensory nerves to the thoracic ganglia are cut, the ganglia still produce the motor pattern of the calling song: the song is generated by a **central pattern generator**. Recordings and lesions located the pattern generator for the syllables and chirps not in the mesothoracic ganglion, which contains the wing motor neurons, but mainly in the metathoracic ganglion and the anterior abdominal ganglia, which send the pattern forward (Schöneich and Hedwig 2011).

The pattern generator is switched on by the brain. In the late 1950s Huber showed that electrical stimulation of particular regions of the cricket brain elicited singing of particular song types. Hedwig (2000) recorded from a single pair of descending interneurons in the brain of *G. bimaculatus* whose activity was tonically elevated throughout calling song. Depolarizing one of these neurons elicited normal calling song, with the species-specific pattern, which stopped when the current was turned off; the stronger the depolarization, the more chirps per second. The neuron sets the motivation to sing but not the pattern, which is produced by the thoracic and abdominal circuits. Its efficacy depends on the state of the animal: it elicits song more readily in a male that is in a singing mood. By the criteria of Kupfermann and Weiss, it is a command neuron ([7.18.14](/7.18.14#def-14.4.1)).

### 16.1.3. Hearing While Singing

A singing cricket produces sound of about $100\,\mathrm{dB}$ at a few centimeters, and its own ears are only a few centimeters away from its wings. How does it avoid being deafened by its own song, and how can it hear other males while it sings? Poulet and Hedwig (2006) found a single identified interneuron, the corollary discharge interneuron, which is driven by the singing pattern generator and fires a burst during each closing stroke of the wings. It inhibits the primary auditory afferents and the first-order auditory interneurons of the prothoracic ganglion exactly during the syllables. The cricket's hearing is thus suppressed while it produces each syllable and restored between syllables, when it can hear the songs of other males. The neuron provides an **efference copy** of the motor command, as in the electric fish ([7.18.15](/7.18.15#sec-15.7)), and it was the first corollary discharge circuit identified at the level of a single neuron.

## 16.2. Phonotaxis

### 16.2.1. Measuring the Female's Response

**Definition 16.2.1 (Phonotaxis)** **Phonotaxis** is an orienting movement toward (positive phonotaxis) or away from (negative phonotaxis) a source of sound.

A receptive female cricket that hears a calling male walks or flies toward him. In 1913 Johann Regen, a Viennese schoolteacher, placed a singing male in front of a telephone and a female near the receiver in another room: the female walked to the receiver. Sound alone guides the female. Since then, phonotaxis has been the main tool for asking which features of the song matter. Kramer (1976) built a *locomotion compensator*, a sphere about $50\,\mathrm{cm}$ in diameter on which the cricket walks freely. An infrared camera tracks a reflective marker on the cricket's back, and motors rotate the sphere to compensate every step, keeping the cricket at the top. The cricket walks toward a loudspeaker for hours without getting any closer, and its speed and direction are recorded continuously. On a smaller treadmill on which the cricket's movements are measured but not compensated, an "open-loop" arrangement, Hedwig and Poulet (2004) found that females steer toward each syllable within about $55$–$60\,\mathrm{ms}$ of its onset, faster than the duration of a chirp: steering is a rapid reflex-like response to each sound pulse, and recognition of the song pattern, which takes longer, regulates whether the steering responses are expressed.

### 16.2.2. What the Female Recognizes

Playback of synthetic songs in which one parameter at a time was varied showed that three features determine the response of female *G. campestris* and *G. bimaculatus*:

- *Syllable rate.* Females walk toward songs with syllable rates near the natural value of about $30\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$ (syllable periods of about $30$–$40\,\mathrm{ms}$) and respond little to substantially faster or slower rates. The preference is a band-pass function of the syllable period. Other parameters matter less: the number of syllables per chirp is unimportant as long as there are at least three, and a continuous trill with the right syllable rate is also attractive.
- *Carrier frequency.* The most effective carrier is the natural $4$–$5\,\mathrm{kHz}$, to which the ear is most sensitive.
- *Intensity.* Louder songs are preferred, within limits, which favors larger or closer males.

Females do not walk straight to the speaker but in a zigzag, which suggests that they compare the sound levels at their two ears (tropotaxis, [7.18.12](/7.18.12#sec-12.1.3)) and correct their course repeatedly.

## 16.3. The Cricket Ear and Brain

### 16.3.1. A Four-Input Pressure-Difference Receiver

The cricket's ears lie in the tibiae of the forelegs. Each ear has two tympana, of which the larger posterior one is acoustically important; behind it lies the tracheal system, a branched air tube that connects the two ears through the prothorax and opens to the outside through a spiracle on each side of the prothorax. A group of about 60 receptor neurons (scolopidia of a **chordotonal organ**) lies on the trachea behind the tympanum.

Because sound reaches each tympanum both from outside and through the tracheae from the spiracles and the opposite ear, the cricket's ear is a **pressure-difference receiver** ([7.18.11](/7.18.11)): the tympanum moves in response to the difference between the pressures on its two faces. Laser vibrometry of the tympanum (Michelsen and colleagues) showed that the internal pathway is about half a wavelength longer than the external one at the carrier frequency of the song, so that a pressure peak arriving at the outer face coincides with a trough at the inner face when the sound comes from the side of that ear; the tympanum is then pushed and pulled in the same direction, and its vibration is large. For sound from the opposite side, the internal and external inputs partly cancel. The directionality of the ear is therefore strongly frequency-dependent and best at the carrier frequency of the species' own song: the ear is a matched filter not only in its sensitivity but also in its directional properties.

### 16.3.2. Auditory Interneurons

The receptor axons end in the prothoracic ganglion, where a small set of identified interneurons processes the auditory information:

| Neuron | Properties | Role |
|---|---|---|
| ON1 (omega neuron) | Excited by the ear on its own side; its axon crosses the midline and inhibits the auditory neurons of the other side; the two ON1 neurons inhibit each other | Enhances the difference between the two sides, sharpening directional information |
| AN1 | Tuned to $4$–$5\,\mathrm{kHz}$; copies the temporal pattern of the song faithfully, syllable by syllable; ascends to the brain | Carries song information to the brain; necessary for phonotaxis |
| AN2 (int-1) | Tuned to high frequencies, especially ultrasound | Triggers avoidance steering away from bats in flight ([7.18.12](/7.18.12#sec-12.5.3)) |

All these neurons copy the temporal pattern of the song; none of them shows a preference for the natural syllable rate. The recognition of the pattern takes place in the brain.

### 16.3.3. Recognizing the Rhythm

In the 1980s Schildberger recorded from neurons in the cricket's brain that receive input from AN1 and found three types: some responded best to syllable rates at and above the natural rate (high-pass), some at and below it (low-pass), and a subset responded only to rates near the natural one (band-pass), with a tuning curve resembling the female's phonotactic preference. He proposed that band-pass neurons combine the outputs of high-pass and low-pass neurons by a logical AND.

A more recent analysis identified the brain circuit in detail (Kostarakos and Hedwig 2012; Schöneich, Kostarakos and Hedwig 2015). AN1 projects to the anterior protocerebrum, where it excites a local interneuron (LN2) that inhibits another (LN5). LN5 responds to the end of each sound pulse with a post-inhibitory rebound, delayed by about $20\,\mathrm{ms}$. A third neuron, LN3, receives both the direct excitation from AN1 and the delayed excitation from LN5, and fires strongly only when the two coincide, that is, when the next pulse begins at the moment the rebound from the previous pulse arrives. This happens only when the pulse period equals the sum of the pulse duration and the rebound delay. A fourth neuron, LN4, which receives excitation from LN3 and inhibition from LN2, responds to the natural pulse pattern with the band-pass selectivity of the female's behavior: it is a feature detector for the species' song ([7.18.13](/7.18.13#def-13.1.2)). The circuit is a delay line combined with **coincidence detection**, the principle of the **Jeffress model** of sound localization ([7.16.24](/7.16.24#sec-24.5.2)), here applied to recognize a temporal pattern instead of a time difference between the ears.

Through LN2 inhibition, LN5 produces a rebound (orange) a fixed delay after the end of each pulse. C: LN3 output per chirp against pulse period: a band-pass tuning peaking near 32 ms, the shape of the female's phonotactic preference.](./plots/coincidence.py)

The rebound delay is an intrinsic property of LN5, which sets the preferred pulse period. Changing this property during evolution would shift the preference, and differences in the songs of closely related species could be matched by small changes of one neuron's membrane properties: a hypothesis that comparative studies of the circuit in other species can test.

## 16.4. Temperature Coupling

Crickets and most other singing insects are ectotherms, and the speed of their neural and muscular processes depends on temperature. The song of a male is faster on a warm night than on a cool one: in the North American field cricket *Gryllus firmus*, chirp and syllable rates nearly double between $15$ and $30\,°\mathrm{C}$, and the syllables become shorter, while the carrier frequency, set by the mechanics of the wing, barely changes (Pires and Hoy 1992). If the female's preference were fixed, she would reject the songs of her own species at some temperatures and might accept those of other species at others. Three solutions are possible: the receiver could accept a broad range of signals; it could use features of the signal that do not depend on temperature (such as ratios of durations); or its preferences could change with temperature in parallel with the signal.

**Definition 16.4.1 (Temperature Coupling)** **Temperature coupling** is the parallel change, with ambient temperature, of the temporal properties of a signal produced by the sender and of the response criteria of the receiver, so that the receiver prefers at each temperature the signal produced by its conspecifics at that temperature.

Pires and Hoy synthesized model songs with the temporal parameters of songs recorded in the field at $15$, $21$ and $30\,°\mathrm{C}$ and tested laboratory-reared females on a Kramer compensator at each of these temperatures. At every temperature, the females walked most strongly and most directly toward the song corresponding to their own temperature, both when the songs were presented one at a time and when two were presented simultaneously from opposite loudspeakers. Temperature coupling has also been demonstrated in other crickets, katydids, grasshoppers and treefrogs. In the gray treefrogs *Hyla chrysoscelis* and *H. versicolor*, two cryptic species that differ mainly in the pulse rate of their calls, females discriminate the species by pulse rate, and their preferred pulse rate changes with temperature as the males' call does (Gerhardt 1978).

How is the coupling achieved? The simplest explanation is that the song pattern generator in the male's thorax and the recognizer in the female's brain have similar temperature dependences, because both depend on time constants of neurons. A shared mechanism is also possible in principle, since in some insects both sexes possess the song circuits. Bauer and von Helversen (1987) answered the question for a grasshopper by warming different parts of the body with small heating devices: warming a female's head shifted her preference, whereas warming her thorax did not, and warming a male's thorax changed his song. The recognizer lies in the brain, the pattern generator in the thorax, and the coupling arises because the two separate circuits, built from similar components, change in parallel with temperature.

## 16.5. Eavesdroppers: Parasitoid Flies and Silent Crickets

A signal designed to be detected by females is also available to other receivers. The tachinid fly *Ormia ochracea*, a **parasitoid** of crickets, finds singing males by phonotaxis: gravid female flies are attracted to loudspeakers broadcasting cricket song, deposit larvae on or near the male, and the larvae burrow into the cricket and kill it within about a week as they develop. The female fly's ear, a mechanically coupled pair of tympana on the prothorax, is tuned to the carrier frequency of cricket song and localizes sources with a precision similar to that of humans, despite the tiny distance between its ears ([7.18.11](/7.18.11)). Flies of the related genus *Therobia* parasitize bush crickets and prefer hosts that sing with many syllables per chirp ([7.18.13](/7.18.13#sec-13.1.1)).

*Ormia* has been established on the Hawaiian islands since at least the late 1980s, where it parasitizes the introduced Pacific field cricket *Teleogryllus oceanicus*. On Kauai, where parasitism rates were high, Zuk, Rotenberry and Tinghitella (2006) found in the early 2000s that the singing cricket population had almost disappeared, replaced by males with a mutation, *flatwing*, which changes the venation of the male forewing so that the file and the harp are lost or reduced: flatwing males cannot sing. Within about 20 generations more than 90 percent of the males on Kauai were silent. The mutation is inherited at a single X-linked locus (Tinghitella 2008), and an independent flatwing mutation with a different genetic basis spread in a population on Oahu (Pascoal and colleagues 2014). It is one of the fastest documented cases of evolution in a wild animal population, and it shows the trade-off at the heart of **sexual selection** ([7.10.13](/7.10.13)): a song that attracts mates also attracts parasitoids, and when the parasitoid pressure is strong enough, silence wins.

How do silent males mate? They behave as satellites: they gather near the few remaining calling males and intercept the females attracted by them. The behavior of females has changed as well. In populations with many flatwing males, females are less selective and more readily accept males without having heard them, and their phonotactic responses are more permissive (Tinghitella and Zuk 2009). Males of some populations have since evolved new, quieter songs, such as a broadband "purring", which are less attractive to the fly (Tinghitella and colleagues 2018).

## 16.6. Frogs

### 16.6.1. Calling

Most frogs and toads communicate by sound, and in many species males gather in choruses at breeding ponds and call for hours each night. A frog calls by forcing air from its lungs over the vocal cords of the larynx into the buccal cavity and, in many species, into one or a pair of inflatable vocal sacs. The sac radiates the sound, adds a visual signal, and recycles the air back into the lungs, so that the frog can call many times without breathing. The muscles that compress the trunk to drive the air are the main engine of calling. In the small North American treefrogs they make up as much as 15 percent of body mass, consist mainly of oxidative fibers rich in mitochondria and fuel calling largely with lipids. Calling is among the most expensive activities of any ectotherm: the oxygen consumption of calling spring peepers and gray treefrogs rises to some 10 to 20 times the resting rate (Taigen and Wells 1985), and males lose weight during the breeding season. A male's energy reserves therefore limit how many nights he can spend in the chorus, and chorus tenure is often the best predictor of mating success.

Calls are of several types. The *advertisement call* attracts females and announces the male's position to other males; *aggressive calls*, often with a different temporal pattern, are used in disputes over calling sites; and *release calls*, together with vibrations of the body, are given by males or unreceptive females clasped by a male. Choruses have their own dynamics: neighboring males avoid overlapping their calls, alternating or timing them in the gaps between neighbors' calls, because overlapping calls mask each other and because females often prefer the leading call of an overlapping pair. In some katydids and frogs, males call in synchrony; models of coupled oscillators that reset their phase when they hear a neighbor show that such synchrony can arise as a by-product of competition to call first (Greenfield and Roizen 1993).

### 16.6.2. Two Ears in One

The frog's inner ear has two auditory end organs, both covered by tectorial membranes and both containing **hair cells**:

- The *amphibian papilla*, unique to amphibians, is sensitive to low and middle frequencies, from about $100\,\mathrm{Hz}$ to about $1\,200\,\mathrm{Hz}$, and is tonotopically organized.
- The *basilar papilla* responds to higher frequencies, typically above $1\,500\,\mathrm{Hz}$; it is not tonotopic, and all its fibers have nearly the same best frequency, which differs between species and often between the sexes.

Capranica (1965) discovered that male bullfrogs respond to playbacks of synthetic calls with their own calls (an evoked vocal response) only if the stimulus contains energy both near $200\,\mathrm{Hz}$, which stimulates the amphibian papilla, and near $1\,400\,\mathrm{Hz}$, which stimulates the basilar papilla, and that energy near $500\,\mathrm{Hz}$ suppresses the response. The two spectral peaks of the bullfrog's call match the best frequencies of the two papillae. This *matched filter* principle, that the receiver's peripheral tuning matches the spectrum of the species' call, has been confirmed in many frogs. In the cricket frog *Acris crepitans*, the call frequency varies geographically, and so does the tuning of the females' basilar papilla, although the match is imperfect: the papilla is often tuned somewhat below the average call frequency, which makes females prefer calls of lower frequency, typically from larger males.

### 16.6.3. Female Choice

Female frogs choose among males by their calls, as shown by two-speaker playback experiments in which a female placed between loudspeakers moves to one of them. Females of many species prefer calls with a lower dominant frequency (larger males), higher call rates and longer calls, which are energetically costly to produce, and more complex calls. In the gray treefrog *Hyla versicolor*, females prefer long calls, and in a split-clutch experiment the offspring of long-calling males grew faster and survived better than half-siblings fathered by short-calling males (Welch, Semlitsch and Gerhardt 1998): a test of the **good genes hypothesis** ([7.10.13](/7.10.13)).

### 16.6.4. The Túngara Frog

The túngara frog (*Physalaemus pustulosus*) of Central America shows how the sensory system of the receiver, the costs of signaling and eavesdropping predators interact in shaping a signal. Its advertisement call consists of a *whine*, a downward frequency sweep from about $900$ to $400\,\mathrm{Hz}$ lasting a third of a second, to which males may add one to several *chucks*, short broadband sounds with most of their energy near $2.5\,\mathrm{kHz}$. The whine alone is necessary and sufficient to attract females; adding chucks makes the call much more attractive, and females prefer a call with chucks over one without by about five to one. The whine stimulates mainly the amphibian papilla and the chucks the basilar papilla, which in females is tuned to about $2.1$–$2.2\,\mathrm{kHz}$, slightly below the dominant frequency of the chuck; calls of larger males, whose chucks are lower in frequency, therefore stimulate the papilla better.

Why do females prefer chucks? Ryan and colleagues (1990) compared the túngara frog with a closely related species, *P. coloradorum*, whose males produce a whine but never a chuck. Females of *P. coloradorum* nevertheless preferred a whine of their own species with an artificial chuck added, and their basilar papilla is tuned similarly. The preference for chucks therefore existed before chucks evolved, and the chuck exploited a pre-existing **sensory bias** ([7.10.13](/7.10.13)), a hypothesis of signal evolution discussed in [7.18.39](/7.18.39).

If chucks are so attractive, why do males not always chuck? The answer lies in other listeners. The fringe-lipped bat (*Trachops cirrhosus*) hunts frogs by listening for their calls, and in playback experiments bats approached speakers broadcasting whines with chucks more often than those broadcasting whines alone, because the chuck is easier to localize (Tuttle and Ryan 1981); blood-sucking midges (*Corethrella*) also find frogs by their calls. Males call with chucks mainly in large choruses, where competition for females is intense and the risk of being singled out by a predator is diluted, and they stop calling when they see a dark shape overhead. The structure of the call is a compromise between attracting females and escaping eavesdroppers, the same compromise that silenced the crickets of Kauai.

## Summary

| Topic | Crickets | Frogs |
|---|---|---|
| Signal | Calling, courtship and aggressive songs; syllables (one wing closure, about 30 per second), chirps, carrier $4$–$5\,\mathrm{kHz}$ | Advertisement, aggressive and release calls; whines, chucks, pulsed calls |
| Production | Stridulation; harp resonance sets carrier and tooth rate; thoracic–abdominal CPG; brain command neuron (Hedwig) | Lungs, larynx and vocal sac; trunk muscles; very high metabolic cost |
| Hearing | Tibial ear as a four-input **pressure-difference receiver**, directional at the song frequency; ON1, AN1, AN2 | Amphibian papilla (low, tonotopic) and basilar papilla (high, single best frequency); matched filters (Capranica) |
| Recognition | Band-pass for syllable rate; brain delay line (LN5 rebound) plus coincidence detector (LN3) and feature detector (LN4) | Spectral and temporal preferences; preference for low frequency, long and complex calls |
| Self and other | Corollary discharge interneuron suppresses hearing during each syllable | Call timing in choruses avoids masking |

- Phonotaxis (Definition 16.2.1) is measured on locomotion compensators and open-loop treadmills; steering follows each syllable, recognition gates it.
- Temperature coupling (Definition 16.4.1): sender and receiver change in parallel (*Gryllus firmus*, gray treefrogs); separate circuits in head and thorax change in step (grasshoppers).
- Eavesdroppers: *Ormia* flies drove the spread of silent flatwing crickets on Kauai within about 20 generations; fringe-lipped bats favor chucks; the túngara chuck exploits a pre-existing **sensory bias**.

## Questions

1. Why does the carrier frequency of cricket song change little with temperature, whereas the syllable rate nearly doubles between $15$ and $30\,°\mathrm{C}$?

   **Answer** The carrier frequency is the resonant frequency of the harp, a mechanical property of the wing that depends on its stiffness and mass, which change little with temperature; the escapement mechanism makes the tooth-strike rate follow this resonance. The syllable rate is the rate of wing closures, set by the central pattern generator, whose neurons and synapses have rate constants with temperature coefficients well above 1, so the pattern runs faster when warm. The temperature-dependent part of the signal is therefore the temporal pattern, which is exactly the feature that females use to recognize the song, hence the need for temperature coupling.

2. The coincidence-detector model of song recognition predicts that the preferred pulse period equals the pulse duration plus the rebound delay of LN5. How would the female's preference change if (a) the pulses were half as long at the same period, and (b) LN5's rebound delay were lengthened by a drug?

   **Answer** (a) With shorter pulses, the rebound from each pulse occurs earlier relative to the next pulse onset (the rebound is timed from the pulse end), so it would arrive before the next pulse begins; coincidence would be best at a shorter period. The model therefore predicts that the preference depends on pulse duration as well as period, a prediction that can be tested with songs of varied duty cycle; behaviorally, crickets show such interactions only partially, suggesting that the circuit has additional elements. (b) A longer rebound delay would shift the coincidence to longer periods, and the female should prefer slower songs; this is a direct test of the model's mechanism.

3. Explain how the cricket ear achieves directionality although the two ears are only a few centimeters apart.

   **Answer** The difference in arrival time between two points a few centimeters apart is only about a hundred microseconds, and the body produces little sound shadow at a wavelength of about $7\,\mathrm{cm}$, so separate pressure receivers would be nearly non-directional. The cricket's tympanum is acted on from outside and, through the tracheal tubes connecting the spiracles and the other ear, from inside. The internal path is about half a wavelength longer at the song frequency, so for sound from the ipsilateral side the external and internal pressures act in the same direction on the membrane and add, while for sound from the contralateral side they partly cancel. The ear responds to the pressure difference, which depends strongly on the direction of the sound; ON1's reciprocal inhibition then enhances the difference between the two sides.

4. On Kauai, flatwing males cannot sing. Why did the mutation spread rather than disappear, and what changes in female behavior would you predict in a population dominated by silent males?

   **Answer** Singing males were heavily parasitized by *Ormia*, so silent males survived much better; they still obtained matings by acting as satellites near the remaining callers and by being encountered by females directly, so their reduced mating success did not outweigh their survival advantage (net positive $s$). In a population of mostly silent males, females that require song before mating would rarely mate; selection should therefore favor females that are less choosy, respond to weaker or less typical acoustic cues, accept males on contact, and move around more in search of males. Tinghitella and Zuk found such increased permissiveness in females from flatwing-dominated populations.

5. (Open question) A female túngara frog prefers calls with chucks, which increase her mate's risk of predation and may expose her to bats as she approaches. Discuss why such a preference persists.

   **Answer** Points to discuss: the preference is a by-product of the tuning of the basilar papilla, which existed before chucks evolved (sensory bias, shown by the *P. coloradorum* comparison), so it may not need a direct benefit to persist; chucks make calls easier to localize, which may reduce a female's search time and her own exposure to predators; chucks of larger males are lower in frequency and better matched to the papilla, so the preference may correlate with male size, which may bring direct or indirect benefits (fertilization success in amplexus, possibly good genes); and the cost of predation is borne mainly by males, whose calling behavior adjusts (chucking mainly in large choruses, stopping when a predator is detected), which reduces the cost of the preference to females. Experiments could test whether preference strength varies with predation risk and whether chucking males sire higher-quality offspring.
