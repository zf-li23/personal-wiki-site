Every animal acts on a small, filtered version of the world. A moth flying through a dark wood ignores almost everything except a few molecules of the female's pheromone and the ultrasound of a bat; a tick waiting on a twig needs nothing more than the smell of butyric acid and the warmth of skin. Which signals an animal can receive, how far away it can detect them, and how its brain classifies them set the stage on which all its behavior is played: foraging, avoiding predators, choosing mates, finding home. This is the subject of *sensory ecology*, and the next three chapters take it up sense by sense.

This chapter first introduces the idea of an animal's own perceptual world and the general principles shared by all senses: how stimuli are transduced, amplified and coded, and how we can find out what another animal perceives. It then turns to the chemical senses, the oldest of all, from bacteria and yeast to the pheromones of moths and mice and the taste receptors whose genes record each lineage's diet. The cellular physiology of smell and taste is treated in animal physiology ([7.14.43](/7.14.43)) and its neural computation in computational neuroscience ([7.16.23](/7.16.23)); the insect sense organs are described in zoology ([7.17.27](/7.17.27)). Here we ask what the senses do for the animal and how they shape behavior. Vision follows in [7.18.10](/7.18.10) and mechanical senses and hearing in [7.18.11](/7.18.11); the orientation mechanisms that use sensory gradients (kineses and taxes) are classified in [7.18.12](/7.18.12), and the design of chemical signals by senders in [7.18.38](/7.18.38).

## 9.1. Sensory Worlds

### 9.1.1. The Umwelt

The Baltic-German biologist Jakob von Uexküll (1934) asked his readers to imagine a soap bubble around each animal, containing only what that animal can perceive and act upon. A meadow looks different from inside the bubble of a bee, a dog or a tick: some of its colors and sounds disappear, others that we cannot sense appear, and objects are grouped by what they mean to the animal rather than by what they are.

**Definition 9.1.1 (Umwelt)** The **Umwelt** of an animal is the part of its surroundings that it can perceive through its sense organs and act upon through its effectors, organized into meaningful objects (food, mate, enemy, shelter) by its nervous system. Different species living in the same physical environment inhabit different Umwelten.

Uexküll's favorite example was the female tick. She climbs a twig and waits, often for months, for a mammal to pass. Her world consists of three cues, each of which releases one action, and each action creates the conditions for the next cue (figure below). The smell of butyric acid, which is present in the skin secretions of all mammals, makes her let go of the twig; the touch of hair makes her run about; warmth near the temperature of mammalian blood makes her bore into the skin. Shape, color, sound and size of the host play no role. The poverty of this world is not a defect: three reliable cues are enough to find a meal, and as Uexküll put it, the certainty of the tick's actions is worth more than a richer perception.

![The Umwelt of a female tick as a chain of three functional cycles (after Uexküll 1934). Each perceptual cue, detected by a particular class of receptors, releases one action: butyric acid from mammalian skin makes the tick drop from the twig, the touch of hair makes it run about, and warmth near 37 °C makes it pierce the skin and feed. The action brings the tick to the next cue (black arrows); the mammal enters the tick's world only through these three cues.](./plots/tick-umwelt.py)

The Umwelt concept anticipates two ideas that run through the rest of this course. First, perception is selective by design: sense organs and the first stages of processing pass the stimuli that matter and ignore the rest, a property that neuroethologists call stimulus filtering ([7.18.13](/7.18.13)) and that classical ethology discovered as the sign stimulus ([7.18.3](/7.18.3)). Second, we cannot assume that another animal perceives what we perceive. Humans see ultraviolet light, polarized light, magnetic fields, electric fields and ultrasound not at all, and our sense of smell is modest; many animals do not see red, cannot hear the frequencies we use in speech, or see the world in panorama. A behavioral experiment must therefore present stimuli that exist in the animal's Umwelt, and control for cues we ourselves cannot perceive.

### 9.1.2. How to Find Out What Another Animal Perceives

Since animals cannot report their sensations, their perception is inferred from their behavior or from the activity of their sense organs.

- Spontaneous responses. Many sensory abilities were discovered from natural behavior: owls striking mice in darkness ([7.18.11](/7.18.11)), moths fleeing from bats, male moths flying upwind to a caged female. The response can be used as an assay; Karl von Frisch's honey bees approaching a colored card that had been rewarded with sugar showed that bees see color.
- Trained discriminations. In the operant methods developed with pigeons, an animal is rewarded for responding to one stimulus and not to another (go/no-go) or for choosing the correct one of several (concurrent discrimination; [7.18.41](/7.18.41)). If it can learn the task, it can perceive the difference. Remy and Emmerton (1989) used a go/no-go procedure to show that pigeons see near-ultraviolet light; Palacios and Varela (1992) showed that no mixture of blue and yellow light matched green for pigeons, as it does for us, which implies more than three color channels.
- Physiology. Recordings from receptors or sensory neurons, the electroretinogram and the auditory brainstem response measure what the sense organ can transmit, which sets an upper limit on what the animal can use.

Experiments on stimuli we cannot perceive are especially prone to artifacts. Delius and colleagues (1976) trained pigeons to peck keys aligned with the plane of polarized light from above, and concluded that pigeons sense polarization; Coemans and colleagues (1990) found that pigeons could not learn a discrimination of polarized light in two months of training. Stray reflections, which depend on polarization, or sounds of the apparatus may have provided cues in the first study. A negative result is also hard to interpret, because an animal may perceive a difference but fail to associate it with the reward. The rule that follows is the same as for any behavioral experiment ([7.18.2](/7.18.2)): every alternative cue must be controlled, and abilities should be confirmed with more than one method.

### 9.1.3. Two Questions for Every Sense

Every sense system answers two questions about a stimulus: what it is (its identity, such as the kind of odor or the color of a light) and where it is (its location). Some stimuli carry both kinds of information at a distance (light, sound, odor carried by wind); others are sensed only on contact (taste, touch, temperature of a surface). The balance between the two questions differs among senses and among animals, and it determines the form of the sense organ. An eye that must locate prey needs a sharp image; an eye that must only judge the time of day does not. A nose that must find the source of an odor needs some way of sampling the odor in space or time; a tongue that judges food already in the mouth does not.

## 9.2. Principles of Transduction and Coding

### 9.2.1. Invariant Machinery, Diverse Receptors

Sense organs convert the energy of a stimulus into the electrical signals of neurons. The forms of energy differ (photons, pressure waves, deformation, molecules, heat, electric fields), but the machinery that converts them is built from a few molecular families, used again and again:

| Stimulus | Sense | Transduction | Main receptor molecules |
|---|---|---|---|
| Light | Vision, circadian photoreception | Phototransduction | Opsins (G protein–coupled) |
| Sound, vibration, touch, stretch | Hearing, touch, proprioception, lateral line | Mechanoelectrical transduction | Mechanically gated channels (TMC in hair cells, Piezo, NOMPC and other TRP channels) |
| Odorants | Olfaction | Ligand binding | Odorant receptors: G protein–coupled in vertebrates, odorant-gated channels in insects |
| Tastants | Taste | Ligand binding, ion permeation | G protein–coupled receptors (sweet, umami, bitter), ion channels (sour, salt) |
| Heat, cold | Thermoreception | Temperature-dependent gating | TRP channels |
| Electric field | Electroreception | Voltage gating | Voltage-gated $\ce{Ca^2+}$ channels |

What is conserved is the logic: a receptor protein changes conformation in response to the stimulus, and an ion channel opens or closes, either directly or through an intracellular cascade, so that the membrane potential of the receptor cell changes ([7.14.39](/7.14.39#sec-39.1)). What varies, and varies enormously among species, are four things: which receptor genes are expressed (and therefore which stimuli are detectable), the sensitivity, the range of stimuli covered (the spectrum of an eye, the frequency range of an ear), and the speed of response. These four properties are the raw material of sensory evolution. They are also what links sense organs to ecology: a fly that must track an object in flight needs photoreceptors that follow flicker above $200\,\mathrm{Hz}$ ([7.17.27](/7.17.27#sec-27.5.6)), a nocturnal mammal needs rods that respond to single photons, and a male moth needs pheromone receptors so selective that they ignore the odors of all other species.

### 9.2.2. Amplification by Receptor Cascades

The weakest stimuli an animal can detect are tiny: a single photon absorbed by a rod, a few molecules of pheromone reaching a moth's antenna. Such events change the membrane current only if their effect is amplified. In vision, in vertebrate olfaction and in the sweet, umami and bitter tastes, amplification is achieved by a **G protein–coupled receptor** cascade: one activated receptor activates many G proteins in turn, each of which activates an effector enzyme that produces or destroys many molecules of a **second messenger**, which in turn opens or closes many channels. A single rhodopsin molecule activates on the order of a hundred transducins per second, and the cascade as a whole multiplies the signal by many orders of magnitude; the gain and kinetics are analyzed in [7.16.17](/7.16.17#sec-17.2).

Amplification has costs. Every stage adds a delay and noise, and a cascade that can amplify one molecule can also be triggered by a spontaneous thermal activation of one receptor, which sets a floor on sensitivity (dark noise; [7.6.18](/7.6.18)). Cascades are therefore found where sensitivity matters more than speed. Where speed matters most, transduction is direct: the mechanically gated channels of hair cells open within microseconds, which is why hearing can resolve time differences of microseconds ([7.18.11](/7.18.11)). Insect odorant receptors also form ion channels gated by odorants ([7.14.43](/7.14.43#sec-43.5.1)), and the receptor neurons of moths can follow odor pulses several times per second, a property that the male needs when he flies through a turbulent plume (Section 9.4.4).

### 9.2.3. Two Ways of Coding Identity

How does the brain know what kind of stimulus was received? Two coding schemes are found in all senses.

- In a labeled line code, each receptor type is connected to its own central pathway, and activity in that pathway means one thing, whatever caused it: the **labeled line principle** of Müller ([7.14.39](/7.14.39#sec-39.1.2)). Pressing on the eye produces a sensation of light.
- In a combinatorial (across-fiber) code, each receptor type responds to many stimuli and each stimulus activates many receptor types; identity is read from the pattern of activity across the population (**combinatorial coding**, [7.14.43](/7.14.43#sec-43.2.2)). With $n$ receptor types, each either active or not, up to $2^n$ patterns can be distinguished, and graded responses give many more. Color vision is a combinatorial code with three channels in humans; olfaction is one with about 400 channels in humans and about 1100 in mice.

The two codes fit different behavioral tasks. A labeled line is the right design when a stimulus always calls for the same response, independent of experience: a sex pheromone that must release courtship, a bitter taste that must cause rejection, a predator odor that must cause fear. A combinatorial code is the right design when the world contains an open-ended number of stimuli whose meaning must be learned: the odors of food plants, of individual conspecifics, of places. Both designs coexist in the same animal. Male moths carry specialized pheromone receptor neurons that project to a few enlarged glomeruli (the macroglomerular complex) of the antennal lobe and from there to a region of the brain that controls upwind flight, in parallel with a combinatorial pathway for plant odors (Bradbury and Vehrencamp 2011). Mice have, besides the main olfactory system, a vomeronasal system and specialized glomeruli whose activation by a single compound triggers innate behavior (Section 9.5.2). The nematode *Caenorhabditis elegans* shows the logic of the labeled line most clearly: when the diacetyl receptor was moved from a neuron that mediates attraction to one that mediates avoidance, the worms avoided diacetyl ([7.14.43](/7.14.43#sec-43.5.1)). The meaning lies in the neuron, not in the receptor.

## 9.3. The Origins of Chemical Sensing

### 9.3.1. Bacteria: Navigating by Comparison in Time

The chemical senses are older than animals. A swimming *Escherichia coli* cell is far too small to compare concentrations at its two ends, but it can compare the concentration now with the concentration a few seconds ago. It swims in straight runs of about a second, interrupted by brief tumbles that choose a new direction at random. When the concentration of an attractant is rising, tumbles are suppressed and runs are prolonged; when it is falling, the cell tumbles at the normal rate. The result is a biased random walk that carries the population up the gradient (**chemotaxis**, [7.3.15](/7.3.15#def-15.5.2); **run-and-tumble** motility, [7.6.35](/7.6.35#sec-35.4)). The molecular network that measures the temporal change and adapts to the background concentration, so that the same relative change gives the same response over five orders of magnitude, is analyzed in [7.6.35](/7.6.35#sec-35.6).

Two features of bacterial chemotaxis recur throughout animal orientation. The cell needs no information about direction; it needs only a memory of the recent past and a rule that links the change of stimulus to the frequency of turning. This class of mechanisms, in which the turning rate or speed is changed but not the direction of turns, is a kinesis in the classification of [7.18.12](/7.18.12). And the strategy works only in a smooth, stable gradient: in a turbulent current, the concentration measured a moment ago says little about the direction of the source (Section 9.4.4).

### 9.3.2. Yeast: The First Pheromones

The budding yeast *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* has two mating types, a and α. Cells of each type secrete a peptide, a-factor or α-factor, that is detected by a G protein–coupled receptor on cells of the other type (Ste3 on α cells, Ste2 on a cells). Binding releases the $\beta\gamma$ subunits of a heterotrimeric G protein, which activate a MAP kinase cascade; the cell arrests in the G1 phase of the cell cycle, expresses mating genes and grows a projection (a "shmoo") toward the highest pheromone concentration, until it fuses with its partner. The yeast mating pathway contains the essential elements of animal chemical communication: a signal released by one individual, a receptor of the G protein–coupled family in another, a cascade that amplifies the signal, and a change of behavior (here growth) directed toward the source. It is also the model in which many components of G protein signaling were first identified genetically ([7.3.19](/7.3.19)).

## 9.4. Olfaction in Behavior

### 9.4.1. What Smell Is For

Smell detects molecules that have traveled from a distant source in air or water. It therefore works in the dark, around corners and beyond the line of sight, and it carries information that persists after the source has gone: the trail of an animal that passed an hour ago, the scent mark of a territory owner, the odor of a predator's feces. Its main functions are

- finding food and prey at a distance and tracking them by their trail;
- detecting predators: water voles avoid cages that smell of mink, although they had never met mink (Barreto and Macdonald 1999), and mice react more strongly to the feces of cats that had eaten mice than of cats fed a vegetarian diet (Berton and colleagues 1998);
- finding and evaluating mates and recognizing individuals and kin: Wilson's storm petrels recognize their mates by smell, and young storm petrels with blocked nostrils cannot find their burrows (Mínguez 1997);
- communication by pheromones (Section 9.5) and, as a side effect, eavesdropping by others: insectivorous tits find caterpillars by the pheromones of moths (Saavedra and Amo 2018);
- assigning valence: odors are strongly linked to approach and avoidance, through innate pathways for some odors and through learning for most (**odorant receptors** in [7.16.23](/7.16.23#sec-23.5)).

The compounds that serve as odors must be volatile enough to travel through air or soluble enough to travel through water; on land they are mostly small (molecular mass below about 300), lipophilic molecules. Large, nonvolatile signals such as proteins in urine are sensed only on contact, mainly by the vomeronasal organ (Section 9.5.2).

### 9.4.2. The Olfactory System in Brief

The organization of vertebrate olfaction was worked out in the 1990s (Buck and Axel 1991; Nobel Prize 2004; [7.16.23](/7.16.23#sec-23.1)). Each olfactory sensory neuron in the nasal epithelium expresses one odorant receptor gene out of a family of hundreds to about two thousand; all neurons expressing the same receptor send their axons to the same one or two glomeruli of the olfactory bulb, so that each odor is represented by a pattern of active glomeruli; mitral cells carry this pattern to the piriform cortex, where odors are learned, and to the amygdala and hypothalamus, where innate responses are organized ([7.14.43](/7.14.43#sec-43.1)). Insects arrived independently at the same architecture, with receptor neurons converging on the glomeruli of the antennal lobe and projection neurons connecting to the **mushroom body** and the lateral horn ([7.16.23](/7.16.23#sec-23.4)).

The number of functional receptor genes varies with ecology. The African elephant has about 2000, rats and mice about 1100–1200, dogs about 800 and humans about 400 (Niimura and colleagues 2014); toothed whales have lost almost all of theirs ([7.16.53](/7.16.53#sec-53.3.3)). Gene number is only one measure of olfactory ability. The olfactory epithelium of a dog covers about $150\,\mathrm{cm^2}$, that of a human about $10\,\mathrm{cm^2}$; sniffing creates a fast air current that carries odorants along the top of the nasal cavity to the sensory epithelium, separate from the respiratory airflow, and dogs can be trained to locate the live bed bugs in a hotel room with 98 % accuracy and no false alarms (Pfiester and colleagues 2008). Humans, despite their small repertoire, can follow a scent trail across a field when they crawl on all fours, and they improve with practice (Porter and colleagues 2007).

### 9.4.3. Locating a Source: Stereo Olfaction and Trails

An odor field contains no directional information other than the concentration gradient, unlike light and sound, whose direction can be measured at a single point. An animal can estimate the gradient in two ways: by comparing two sensors at the same moment (simultaneous comparison), or by comparing samples taken at different moments with one sensor moved through space (sequential comparison). The two methods correspond to the tropotaxis and klinotaxis described in [7.18.12](/7.18.12).

Simultaneous comparison requires that the two sensors be far enough apart for the concentrations to differ, and that their inputs be kept separate up to the point of comparison. Adaptations that widen the base are common. The forked tongue of snakes and monitor lizards samples two points up to twice the width of the head and delivers the samples to the left and right vomeronasal openings; a snake following the trail of prey or of a female turns toward the side that received more odor and so stays within the edges of the trail (Schwenk 1994). Moles and rats compare the inputs of their two nostrils: eastern moles find food less accurately when one nostril is blocked and err toward the open side, and when the nostrils are crossed with tubes they search in the wrong direction (Catania 2013). Sharks turn toward the side that the odor reaches first, responding to bilateral delays of a fraction of a second (Gardiner and Atema 2010); the wide head of hammerhead sharks may increase the separation of the nostrils.

Trails pose a special problem: once on a trail, an animal must determine in which direction it leads. Dogs searching for a track move fast and sniff in short bouts; once on the track they slow down, sniff at several footprints, and within a few seconds take the right direction, apparently from the small decrease of odor concentration in successively older footprints (Bradbury and Vehrencamp 2011). Ants and termites follow trails laid by nestmates with pheromones of glands in the abdomen; the trail pheromone of fire ants is so volatile that a trail fades within minutes unless it is renewed, so that the trail network tracks the current distribution of food ([7.18.36](/7.18.36)).

### 9.4.4. Plumes: Surge and Cast

In nature most odor sources are carried by currents of air or water, and the currents are turbulent. A plume downwind of a source is not a smooth cone of decreasing concentration, as averaged over long times ([7.17.27](/7.17.27#sec-27.4.4)), but a meandering train of thin filaments of high concentration separated by pockets of clean air. An animal inside a plume meets brief bursts of odor at irregular intervals, and the concentration at any moment is a poor guide to the direction of the source; a strategy based on comparing concentrations would be led astray.

Animals in plumes use two sources of information instead: the direction of the current, sensed mechanically, and the timing of odor contacts. A walking lobster or crab measures the current with mechanosensory hairs; a flying insect cannot feel the wind, because it drifts with the air, and measures it visually from the apparent motion of the ground below. Male moths searching for a calling female follow a simple rule (Kennedy 1983; Vickers and Baker 1994). On contact with a filament of pheromone they surge upwind; when the odor is lost they stop advancing and cast back and forth across the wind with sweeps of growing amplitude until they meet the plume again. The rule works because each contact resets the surge, so that a moth inside a plume makes steady upwind progress, and casting brings it back to a plume that has meandered sideways. The faster the receptor neurons follow the pulses, the more often the surge is renewed; moths flying in a homogeneous cloud of pheromone, with no pulses, fail to progress upwind and fly in place.

Robots designed to find chemical leaks or explosives use similar algorithms. Information-theoretic analyses show that when contacts are rare, the best strategy is to move so as to maximize the expected information about the location of the source rather than to climb the gradient ("infotaxis", Vergassola and colleagues 2007), and the paths of such searchers resemble the zigzags of moths.

## 9.5. Pheromones

### 9.5.1. Moth Sex Pheromones

**Pheromones** are chemical signals that act between members of the same species ([7.14.43](/7.14.43#def-43.3.1)). The first to be identified was bombykol, the sex pheromone of the female silk moth *Bombyx mori*, isolated by Adolf Butenandt in 1959 from the glands of half a million females. Males of the silk moth and of many other moths carry large plumed antennae with tens of thousands of trichoid sensilla, each containing receptor neurons tuned to a component of the pheromone; a single molecule can make a receptor neuron fire, and a few hundred molecules arriving at the antenna per second release wing fluttering and upwind search (Kaissling). The female releases the pheromone in pulses from a gland at the tip of her abdomen, often at a particular time of night, and the male's search behavior is the surge-and-cast strategy of the previous section.

Most moth pheromones are blends of two to several components, and the ratio of the components carries species identity. The European corn borer *Ostrinia nubilalis* has two races that use the same two compounds, (Z)- and (E)-11-tetradecenyl acetate, in nearly opposite ratios (about 97 : 3 and 3 : 97); males of each race fly to their own blend and seldom to the other, and the difference is controlled by a few genes in the female (the reductase that makes the pheromone) and the male (the response). A shift in ratio can thus isolate populations reproductively, which makes pheromone blends a model of speciation by signal divergence ([7.18.38](/7.18.38)). Within a species, a blend also gives graded responses: a single component may elicit upwind flight, the complete blend landing and courtship.

### 9.5.2. Mammals: The Vomeronasal Organ and Urinary Signals

Many mammals, reptiles and amphibians have a second chemosensory organ, the **vomeronasal organ** ([7.14.43](/7.14.43#sec-43.3)), specialized for large, nonvolatile molecules that are picked up by contact and pumped into the organ. Ungulates and cats show the behavior that delivers such molecules: after sniffing or licking the urine of a female, a male raises his head and curls his upper lip in the grimace called flehmen. The organ projects through the accessory olfactory bulb to the amygdala and hypothalamus, which control sexual, aggressive and parental behavior ([7.18.8](/7.18.8)).

The house mouse is the best-studied case. Male urine contains large amounts of major urinary proteins (MUPs), lipocalins that bind volatile compounds and release them slowly from scent marks and also act as signals themselves. One of them, darcin, attracts females, and a female that has contacted it learns the airborne odor of that male and prefers it afterwards (Roberts and colleagues 2010); MUPs also release aggression between males. Male tears contain the peptide ESP1, which increases the receptivity of females to mounting. Mice lacking the channel TRPC2, which is required for transduction in vomeronasal neurons, show striking deficits: knockout males do not attack intruding males and court males and females alike (Stowers and colleagues 2002), and knockout females mount other mice and emit the courtship vocalizations of males (Kimchi and colleagues 2007). The vomeronasal system thus does not create sex-typical behavior; it seems to select, by its input, which of two circuits present in both sexes is expressed.

Vomeronasal signals also have physiological effects (primer pheromones). Exposure of a recently mated female mouse to the urine of an unfamiliar male can block implantation of the embryos, and she returns to estrus (the Bruce effect, Bruce 1959); the familiar odor of her mate, which she learned at mating, does not. Female bank voles in this vulnerable early phase of pregnancy prefer the odor of their mate to that of another male (Kruczek 1998). Odors also carry individual identity: mice can distinguish individuals that differ only in genes of the **major histocompatibility complex** ([7.15.13](/7.15.13)), and many mammals recognize individuals, kin and social status by smell ([7.18.33](/7.18.33)).

### 9.5.3. Odor Imprinting in Salmon

Pacific salmon hatch in streams, migrate to the ocean, and after two to five years return to spawn in the stream where they were born. Arthur Hasler and Warren Wisby (1951) proposed that young salmon learn the odor of their home stream and later follow it upstream. The decisive test came from Lake Michigan, where coho salmon (*Oncorhynchus kisutch*) had been introduced. Scholz and colleagues (1976) exposed one group of hatchery smolts to the synthetic chemical morpholine and another to phenethyl alcohol during the period when the young fish transform for life at sea, released them in the lake, and during the spawning run eighteen months later dripped morpholine into one stream and phenethyl alcohol into another. Fish exposed to morpholine returned predominantly to the morpholine stream and those exposed to phenethyl alcohol to the other stream; unexposed controls were distributed among streams. The learning is restricted to a sensitive period around the transformation, and the odor memory is used only at the end of migration, when the fish are already near the coast; how they find the coast from the open ocean is a question of navigation ([7.18.21](/7.18.21)).

## 9.6. Taste

### 9.6.1. Five Qualities and Labeled Cells

Taste is a contact sense that judges food in the mouth. Its few qualities correspond to a few decisions: sweet signals energy (sugars), umami signals protein (L-amino acids and nucleotides), salty signals sodium, bitter signals possible toxins, and sour signals acids and spoilage. In mammals the receptors are a heterodimer of T1R2 and T1R3 for sweet, T1R1 and T1R3 for umami, a family of T2R receptors for bitter (about 25 in humans), the proton channel OTOP1 for sour, and the epithelial sodium channel ENaC for the attraction to low salt ([7.14.43](/7.14.43#sec-43.4.2)). Each quality is detected by its own class of taste cells.

Taste is the clearest case of labeled lines in mammals. Charles Zuker, Nicholas Ryba and colleagues expressed in the sweet cells of mice a receptor for a synthetic drug, which mice do not normally taste; the mice were attracted to the drug. When the same receptor was expressed in bitter cells, the mice avoided the drug (Zhao and colleagues 2003; Mueller and colleagues 2005). Whether a taste is liked or avoided depends on the cell that is activated, not on the receptor or the molecule, and the response is innate: newborn mammals accept sweet and reject bitter before any experience. Learning modifies it later, most strongly in conditioned taste aversion ([7.18.41](/7.18.41)).

### 9.6.2. Receptor Genes Follow the Diet

Because each quality depends on a few genes, the taste repertoire of a species can be read from its genome, and it changes quickly with diet. When a quality loses its use, the selection that maintains its receptor genes relaxes, and inactivating mutations accumulate until the genes become **pseudogenes**. The pattern is consistent across mammals and birds (figure below):

- All cats, from the domestic cat to the tiger, have a pseudogene of *Tas1r2* and cannot taste sugar; in behavioral tests they are indifferent to sweet solutions (Li and colleagues 2005). Several other carnivores that eat only animal food have lost *Tas1r2* independently (Jiang and colleagues 2012).
- The giant panda, a carnivoran that switched to bamboo, has a pseudogene of *Tas1r1*, the umami receptor; the inactivation dates to roughly the time of its dietary switch (Li and colleagues 2010; Zhao and colleagues 2010).
- Sea lions and the bottlenose dolphin, which swallow fish whole, have lost both sweet and umami receptors, and dolphins most bitter receptors as well (Jiang and colleagues 2012).
- Birds lack *Tas1r2* altogether. Hummingbirds, which feed on nectar, have regained the ability to taste sugar by modifying the umami receptor T1R1–T1R3 so that it responds to sugars (Baldwin and colleagues 2014). The same molecular path was taken independently by nectar-feeding songbirds.
- The number of bitter receptor genes correlates with the share of plants in the diet: herbivores and omnivores tend to have more than carnivores, which meet fewer plant toxins.

The direction of cause and effect deserves care. In the panda, the dietary change probably came first and made the umami receptor dispensable; in cats, it is debated whether the loss of sweet taste followed strict carnivory or helped to fix it. In either case, the receptor repertoire is a record of a lineage's sensory ecology, and it can be read even in species whose behavior has not been studied. The same reasoning applies to the olfactory and visual repertoires ([7.16.53](/7.16.53#sec-53.3.3), [7.18.10](/7.18.10)).

## Summary

- An animal's Umwelt (Definition 9.1.1) is the part of the world it perceives and acts upon. The tick's Umwelt consists of three cues, each releasing one action. Behavioral experiments must use stimuli inside the animal's Umwelt and control cues we cannot perceive; perception is inferred from spontaneous responses, trained discriminations and physiology.
- Every sense answers what a stimulus is and where it is. Transduction uses a few conserved molecular families (opsins and other **G protein–coupled receptors**, mechanically gated channels, TRP and voltage-gated channels); species differ in receptor genes, sensitivity, range and speed.
- Cascades amplify weak stimuli at the cost of delay and noise; direct gating is fast. Labeled lines suit stimuli with fixed meaning (pheromones, bitter), **combinatorial coding** suits open-ended, learned stimuli (food odors).
- Bacteria climb gradients by comparing concentrations in time (**run-and-tumble**); yeast mating uses a pheromone, a G protein–coupled receptor and directed growth.
- Smell finds food, mates and predators at a distance and assigns valence. Stereo olfaction (forked tongues, nostrils) and sequential sampling estimate gradients; in turbulent plumes, male moths surge upwind on each odor contact and cast crosswind when they lose it.
- Moth pheromones are blends whose ratios carry species identity; mouse vomeronasal signals (MUPs, ESP1) release sex-specific behavior and the Bruce effect; salmon imprint on the odor of their home stream during a sensitive period (Scholz and colleagues 1976).
- Taste has five labeled qualities; the preference is set by the cell, not the receptor. Taste receptor genes are lost when a diet makes them useless (cats, panda, sea lions, dolphins) and can be regained by retuning (hummingbirds).

## Questions

1. Uexküll's tick responds to butyric acid, hair and warmth. Design an experiment to show that the tick's dropping response is released by butyric acid and not by the warmth or the shadow of an approaching mammal.

   **Answer** Present the three candidate cues separately and in combination below ticks waiting on twigs, with all other cues held constant: a glass plate or a membrane at room temperature smeared with butyric acid (or with a solvent control), a warmed plate without odor, and a moving dark screen that casts a shadow without odor or heat. Randomize the order, use each tick once (or allow long recovery) to avoid habituation, and score drop rates blind to treatment. If butyric acid alone releases dropping at a rate similar to a real host and the other cues alone do not, the hypothesis is supported. Dose–response tests with different concentrations show the threshold, and tests with other fatty acids show how specific the cue is. Modern work shows that ticks also respond to carbon dioxide and other host odors, so the result may be less simple than Uexküll's account.

2. Why should a sex pheromone be detected by a labeled line, whereas the odors of food plants are coded combinatorially? Use the moth as an example.

   **Answer** A pheromone has one fixed meaning (a receptive female of the male's own species) and must release the same behavior (upwind flight, courtship) in every male, including inexperienced ones, without delay. Dedicated, narrowly tuned receptor neurons that converge on a few large glomeruli (the macroglomerular complex) and project to the premotor centers for upwind flight give maximal sensitivity, specificity for the right blend and a fast, innate response. The odors of food plants are numerous, variable and of different value in different places and seasons; a combinatorial code with broadly tuned receptors can represent many odors, including new ones, and their meaning can be learned in the mushroom bodies. The moth carries both systems in parallel.

3. A bacterium climbs a smooth gradient by comparing concentrations over time. Why does the same strategy fail for a moth in a turbulent plume, and what replaces it?

   **Answer** In a turbulent plume the odor arrives as intermittent filaments; the concentration at a given point fluctuates by orders of magnitude within fractions of a second, and in most moments it is zero. The difference between the concentration now and a second ago is dominated by these fluctuations, not by the mean gradient, so a comparison in time gives no reliable direction. The moth uses the odor only as a binary signal of being inside the plume and takes direction from the wind, measured visually by the optomotor response to ground motion: upwind surges after each contact and crosswind casting after loss. The direction information thus comes from the current, the odor only says whether the source lies upwind.

4. In the experiment of Scholz and colleagues (1976), why was it important to use synthetic chemicals that do not occur in Lake Michigan streams, and to expose control fish to neither chemical?

   **Answer** Using chemicals absent from natural streams ensured that the fish could not have learned them from any natural water and that the experimenters controlled which odor each fish learned and which stream carried it; the streams could be assigned at will, so any return to a scented stream must be due to the artificial odor. The unexposed controls showed what fraction of fish would enter each stream without imprinting (for example because of their release site or stream size). Only the difference between exposed and control fish, and the crossover pattern of the two exposed groups (morpholine fish to the morpholine stream and phenethyl alcohol fish to the other), demonstrates odor imprinting.

5. (Open question) Domestic cats are indifferent to sugar. Explain how the genome of a newly sequenced carnivore could be used to predict whether it tastes sweetness, and how the prediction should be tested.

   **Answer** Find the *Tas1r2* gene by similarity to known sequences and look for inactivating mutations: premature stop codons, frameshifts, missing exons or loss of the gene, and for elevated rates of nonsynonymous substitution that indicate relaxed selection. An intact open reading frame with conserved sites predicts a functional receptor, a pseudogene predicts no sweet taste. The prediction should be tested at two levels: functional expression of the receptor (with T1R3) in cultured cells to measure responses to sugars and sweeteners, and behavioral two-bottle preference tests (sugar solution versus water) in the animals themselves. Discrepancies can occur, for instance if another receptor has taken over the function, as in hummingbirds.
