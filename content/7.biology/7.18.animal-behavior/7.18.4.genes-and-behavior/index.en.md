Can a gene make an animal behave in a particular way? The question has been asked since Darwin argued that instincts are inherited, and it has been answered badly in both directions: by those who believed that behaviors are "genetically determined" and could not be changed, and by those who believed that behavior, especially learned behavior, has nothing to do with genes. Neither view survives scrutiny. Every behavior depends on a nervous system built and maintained by the products of thousands of genes, whose expression is itself regulated by the environment; and many differences in behavior between individuals, populations and species are caused, at least in part, by differences in their genes. Natural selection can change behavior only to the extent that such heritable differences exist.

This chapter explains how the genetic basis of behavior is studied and what has been learned. We begin with the interaction between genes and environment, illustrated by gene expression in the brains of honey bees. We then describe the methods of behavioral genetics, from crosses and artificial selection to mutant screens, mapping and gene editing, and the most instructive genes of large effect. The chapter ends with supergenes that control complex behavioral polymorphisms, with epigenetic effects of early experience, and with a critique of the idea of "a gene for a behavior". It builds on [7.18.1](/7.18.1) and [7.18.3](/7.18.3) and on the genetics course: quantitative genetics and **heritability** in [7.4.24](/7.4.24), mapping in [7.4.25](/7.4.25), inversions in [7.4.15](/7.4.15) and **epigenetics** in [7.2.15](/7.2.15). The neural circuits controlled by some of the genes discussed here, such as *fruitless* and the vasopressin receptor, are described in [7.16.36](/7.16.36). The development of behavior is the subject of the next chapter, [7.18.5](/7.18.5).

## 4.1. Behavior Requires Both Genes and Environment

### 4.1.1. The Interactive Theory of Development

The development of any trait requires both genetic information and environmental inputs. A behavior that would develop in the absence of any environment is as unthinkable as one that would develop in the absence of any genes: genes do not specify behavior directly, but encode proteins that, in the cellular environment provided by the egg, the body and the outside world, build and operate a nervous system. Some genes are switched on or off by signals from outside the organism, such as day length, food or social interactions, so that environmental differences change gene expression and, through it, the structure and function of neural circuits. This view, which we have called the interactionist view in [7.18.3](/7.18.3), is the interactive theory of development: phenotypes, behavioral and other, arise from the interaction of genotype and environment throughout life (**genotype–environment interaction**, [7.4.3](/7.4.3)).

It follows that the question "is this behavior genetic or environmental?" has no answer for a behavior as a whole. A meaningful question is about differences: does a particular difference between individuals arise from a difference in their genes, a difference in their environments, or both? Both kinds of difference are common, and the same behavior can differ between individuals for genetic reasons in one population and for environmental reasons in another.

### 4.1.2. Gene Expression in Honey Bee Workers

Worker honey bees (*Apis mellifera*) change jobs as they age. In the first days after emerging as adults they clean cells; then they become nurses that feed larvae; then they process food and build comb; and at about three weeks of age they begin to forage outside the hive for nectar and pollen, which they do until they die a few weeks later. This sequence is called age polyethism; how it develops and how it is regulated by hormones and social interactions is described in [7.18.5](/7.18.5).

Charles Whitfield, Gene Robinson and colleagues (2003) compared gene expression in the brains of nurses and foragers using microarrays representing about 5500 genes, about 40% of the bee's genes. About 40% of the genes surveyed differed in expression between the two groups, and the pattern of expression predicted with high accuracy whether an individual bee was a nurse or a forager. Because foragers are older than nurses in a normal colony, the differences might simply reflect age. To separate age from behavior, the researchers used single-cohort colonies, made up entirely of bees of the same age: in such colonies some young bees begin foraging precociously, and some old bees remain nurses. Brain expression profiles followed behavior, not age: young foragers resembled old foragers, and old nurses resembled young nurses.

This experiment illustrates several general points. Behavioral states are accompanied by large-scale changes in gene expression in the brain; these changes are caused partly by the social environment, which in single-cohort colonies induces some bees to start foraging early; and the same genome can produce very different behavioral phenotypes. Later studies of bees collected while performing different tasks found that the differentially expressed genes are organized into networks controlled by a few transcription factors, so that a behavioral state corresponds to a particular state of a gene regulatory network (**gene regulatory network**, [7.9.11](/7.9.11)). Gene Robinson summarized this view: "DNA is both inherited and environmentally responsive."

## 4.2. Methods of Behavioral Genetics

**Definition 4.2.1 (Behavioral Genetics)** **Behavioral genetics** is the study of the genetic basis of behavior: of how variation among individuals in their genes contributes to variation in their behavior, and of how particular genes and their products influence the development and operation of the mechanisms of behavior.

Behavioral geneticists use two complementary strategies. Starting from natural variation in behavior, they ask how much of it is heritable and which genetic differences cause it (forward genetics from the phenotype to the genes). Starting from genes, they ask what happens to behavior when a gene is changed (**reverse genetics**, [7.4.1](/7.4.1)). The two meet when a gene identified by one method is confirmed by the other.

### 4.2.1. Crosses, Artificial Selection and Heritability

The oldest method is to compare the behavior of different strains, populations or species when they are reared in the same environment, and to cross them. If the differences persist under common rearing, they are likely to be genetic; the behavior of hybrids and backcrosses shows how the differences are inherited.

A classic example concerns two species of African lovebirds that carry nest material differently (Dilger, 1962). Fischer's lovebird (*Agapornis fischeri*) cuts long strips of bark or leaves and carries them in its bill; the peach-faced lovebird (*A. roseicollis*) cuts strips and tucks them among the feathers of its lower back. Hybrid females tried to tuck strips into their feathers but did not let go of them or tucked them incompletely, so that the strips fell out; only after much practice, over two to three years, did they learn to carry the strips in the bill, though they still made brief tucking movements first. The hybrids' behavior shows both a genetic contribution to the difference between the species and the modification of an inherited tendency by learning.

Artificial selection tests directly whether variation in a behavior is heritable. Robert Tryon (1940) selected rats for the number of errors they made in a maze, breeding "maze-bright" rats from the best performers and "maze-dull" rats from the worst; after a few generations the two lines hardly overlapped. Later work showed that the lines did not differ in general learning ability but in specific traits, such as their reactions to the mechanical noise of the maze apparatus and their use of spatial cues; selection had acted on whatever heritable variation influenced the score. Many behaviors respond to selection in the laboratory, including geotaxis and phototaxis, aggression, activity and mating speed in *Drosophila*, nest building in mice, and the timing and intensity of migratory restlessness in birds: Peter Berthold and colleagues showed that selection in a partially migratory population of blackcaps (*Sylvia atricapilla*) changed the proportions of migrants and residents within a few generations.

The rate of response to selection depends on the heritability of the trait. For a quantitative trait, the response $R$ to one generation of selection is given by the **breeder's equation** ([7.4.24](/7.4.24)),

$$
R = h^2 S,
$$
where $S$ is the **selection differential**, the difference between the mean of the selected parents and the mean of the population, and $h^2$ is the narrow-sense heritability, the proportion of the phenotypic variance due to additive genetic variance. Estimates of heritability come from the resemblance between relatives (parents and offspring, siblings, twins) or from the realized response to selection. Behavioral traits typically have moderate heritabilities, with most estimates between about 0.1 and 0.4: lower than morphological traits, higher than life-history traits closely related to fitness.

![Artificial selection on a behavioral trait, simulated with an additive polygenic model (100 loci, population of 200, truncation selection of the top or bottom 20% each generation; the environment contributes the remaining phenotypic variance). A: mean phenotype in lines selected upward, downward and at random (control), for heritability 0.4; the lines diverge, with fluctuations due to drift, and the response slows as genetic variance is used up. B: the same with heritability 0.1; the response is about four times slower, as predicted by the breeder's equation $R = h^2 S$ (dashed lines)](./plots/selection-response.py)

A heritability is a property of a particular population in a particular environment. It says nothing about whether a trait can be changed by the environment (the heritability of human height is high, yet average height has increased greatly over the last century with better nutrition), and a high heritability within each of two populations does not imply that the difference between them is genetic ([7.4.24](/7.4.24#sec-24.8)).

### 4.2.2. Mutant Screens: Benzer and the Founding of Neurogenetics

Seymour Benzer (1921–2007), a physicist who had become a leading molecular geneticist through his fine-structure mapping of the *rII* locus of bacteriophage T4, turned in the mid-1960s to a new question: could single genes be found that affect behavior? He chose *Drosophila melanogaster*, induced mutations in males with chemical mutagens, and screened their descendants for abnormal behavior (**forward genetic screen**, [7.4.19](/7.4.19)).

His first screen (Benzer 1967) used a simple apparatus to sort flies by their phototaxis, their tendency to move toward light. The apparatus consists of two rows of tubes that can be slid relative to each other. Flies are tapped to the bottom of the first tube, and the tube is laid horizontally facing a light; flies that walk toward the light within a set time pass into the opposite tube of the second row. The rows are shifted, and both the flies that moved and those that stayed are given a new trial. After $n$ trials, the flies are distributed among $n + 1$ tubes according to the number of times they moved toward the light. If each fly moves toward the light with probability $p$ in each trial, independently, the number of moves follows a **binomial distribution**,

$$
\mathbb{P}(K = k) = \binom{n}{k} p^k (1 - p)^{n - k}, \qquad k = 0, 1, \dots, n,
$$
so a population of normal flies with high $p$ accumulates at one end of the apparatus and non-phototactic mutants at the other. Because the apparatus processes large numbers of flies at once, the method is an efficient screen as well as a quantitative measure of behavior, analogous to the countercurrent distribution used by chemists to separate molecules by their partition coefficients.

![Benzer's countercurrent apparatus for sorting flies by phototaxis. A: schematic of the procedure; in each trial, flies that walk toward the light (to the right) pass into the next tube of the opposite row. B: expected distribution of flies over the tubes after 10 trials for three values of the probability $p$ of moving toward the light in each trial (binomial distribution): a wild-type population with strong positive phototaxis ($p = 0.85$), an intermediate population and a mutant population with little phototaxis ($p = 0.15$)](./plots/countercurrent.py)

The screens found many mutants: some blind, with defects in phototransduction (such as *norpA*, which lacks a phospholipase C required for the light response); some unable to walk normally; others with abnormalities that were not sensory or motor at all. In the following decade, Benzer and his students used similar screens to isolate mutants affecting circadian rhythms (*period*, Ronald Konopka and Benzer, 1971; [7.16.35](/7.16.35)), learning (*dunce*, Yadin Dudai and colleagues, 1976; [7.16.38](/7.16.38)) and courtship. The *period* mutants were the most striking: single-gene mutations could lengthen the circadian rhythm of eclosion and activity to about 28 hours, shorten it to about 19 hours, or abolish it, and the cloning of *period* in 1984 opened the molecular analysis of the circadian clock (Nobel Prize to Jeffrey Hall, Michael Rosbash and Michael Young, 2017). Benzer's work founded neurogenetics: it showed that single genes can have specific effects on behavior, and that genetic dissection, finding mutants and then identifying the gene and the cells in which it acts, can reveal the mechanisms of behavior. Mutations in *fruitless*, which alter male courtship, are described in [7.16.36](/7.16.36#sec-36.1).

### 4.2.3. Mapping Natural Variation

Mutant screens find genes that are necessary for a behavior, but not necessarily the genes that cause natural differences between individuals or species. These are found by mapping.

In a cross between two strains or species that differ in a behavior, **quantitative trait locus** (QTL) mapping ([7.4.25](/7.4.25)) identifies regions of the genome in which the genotype of hybrid or backcross individuals is associated with their behavior. A clear example is burrowing in deer mice (Weber, Peterson and Hoekstra, 2013). The oldfield mouse (*Peromyscus polionotus*) digs long burrows with an entrance tunnel, a nest chamber and an escape tunnel that runs back toward the surface; its close relative the deer mouse (*P. maniculatus*) digs short, simple burrows. Mice of both species reared in the laboratory without ever having seen a burrow dig the burrows of their species when given soil, so the difference is largely genetic. In backcrosses, the length of the entrance tunnel was associated with three genomic regions, each adding a few centimeters, and the presence of the escape tunnel with a different region: the complex behavior had been assembled during evolution from separable genetic modules. In the same species, Andres Bendesky and colleagues (2017) mapped differences in parental care, which is shown by both parents in the monogamous oldfield mouse but only by mothers in the promiscuous deer mouse, and found that variation in nest building by parents was associated with the expression of the vasopressin gene in the hypothalamus ([§4.3.3](#sec-4.3.3)).

Within a single outbred population, the **genome-wide association study** (GWAS) tests hundreds of thousands of genetic markers across the genome for association with a trait. In humans, GWAS of behavioral traits such as educational attainment, risk taking and chronotype (morning or evening preference) have found thousands of associated loci, each with a tiny effect; together they explain only part of the heritability estimated from relatives. Behavior, like most complex traits, is typically polygenic.

### 4.2.4. Candidate Genes and Gene Editing

The candidate-gene approach starts from a gene suspected, on the basis of its known function in other species, to influence a behavior, and tests whether variation in that gene is associated with variation in behavior, or whether manipulating it changes behavior. Knockout mice have been used for decades to test the roles of genes in behavior (**knockout mouse**, [7.3.3](/7.3.3)), but CRISPR–Cas9 gene editing has now made such tests possible in non-model species. For example, mutations in the olfactory co-receptor gene *orco* of ants, which is required for the function of all odorant receptors, made clonal raider ants (*Ooceraea biroi*) and jumping ants (*Harpegnathos saltator*) unable to follow pheromone trails or to stay with their nestmates, and caused defects in the development of the antennal lobes (Trible et al. 2017; Yan et al. 2017), demonstrating the central role of olfaction in ant social life.

## 4.3. Genes of Large Effect

Most behavioral variation is polygenic, but a few genes with large effects on natural behavioral variation have been studied in depth. They are valuable precisely because they connect a genetic difference, a molecular mechanism and an ecological consequence.

### 4.3.1. *foraging* in Flies and Bees

Larvae of *Drosophila melanogaster* feeding on a yeast paste differ in how far they move while feeding. Marla Sokolowski found that "rover" larvae travel long paths through the food, and "sitter" larvae shorter ones; adults show a corresponding difference after feeding. The difference maps to a single gene, *foraging* (*for*), with two naturally occurring classes of alleles, $for^{\mathrm{R}}$ (rover, dominant) and $for^{\mathrm{s}}$ (sitter). The gene encodes a cGMP-dependent protein kinase (PKG); rovers have higher PKG activity in the head than sitters, and transgenic expression of extra *for* in sitters makes them rover-like (Osborne et al. 1997). In natural populations, about 70% of larvae are rovers and 30% sitters.

Why are both alleles maintained? Rovers do better at high larval density, when food patches are depleted quickly and moving on pays, and sitters at low density: in laboratory populations selected at high or low density, the frequency of the rover allele rose or fell accordingly (Sokolowski et al. 1997). In addition, the fitness of each morph is higher when it is rare (Fitzpatrick et al. 2007), a form of **negative frequency-dependent selection** ([7.10.5](/7.10.5)) that maintains a stable polymorphism.

A: relative fitness of the two morphs as a function of the rover frequency $p$: rovers do better when rare. B: trajectories of $p$ from different starting frequencies converge to the stable equilibrium $\hat p$. The parameter values are illustrative](./plots/rover-sitter.py)

The *foraging* gene also has a role in honey bees. In the brain of worker bees, expression of the homologous gene *Amfor* is higher in foragers than in nurses, and treatment of young bees with a cGMP analog, which activates PKG, causes them to start foraging early (Ben-Shahar et al. 2002). The same gene, inherited from the common ancestor of flies and bees, has been recruited in different ways: in flies it underlies a stable difference between individuals, in bees a change in behavior during the life of an individual. PKG genes influence foraging-related behavior in other animals too, including roaming and dwelling in the nematode *Caenorhabditis elegans*, an example of the conserved genetic "toolkit" for behavior that evolutionary developmental biology has found for body plans (**Hox gene**, [7.5.15](/7.5.15)).

### 4.3.2. *npr-1* and Social Feeding in Nematodes

Wild strains of *C. elegans* differ in their feeding behavior: some feed solitarily, dispersing over a lawn of bacteria, while others aggregate in clumps at the border of the lawn, where oxygen concentration is lower. Mario de Bono and Cornelia Bargmann (1998) showed that the difference is caused by a single amino acid in a neuropeptide receptor, NPR-1, related to the neuropeptide Y receptors of mammals: the allele with valine at position 215 (215V) produces solitary feeding, the allele with phenylalanine (215F) aggregation. The 215V allele of the standard laboratory strain N2 arose in the laboratory and spread by selection under laboratory culture conditions; wild strains carry the 215F allele, which produces lower receptor activity. Later work showed that the receptor acts in a small set of sensory neurons that respond to oxygen and other aversive stimuli, so that aggregation is an indirect consequence of avoiding high oxygen and of attraction to other worms. The example shows how a single amino acid change can switch a social behavior, by acting not on a "social" circuit but on sensory responses to the environment.

### 4.3.3. Vasopressin Receptors and Pair Bonds in Voles

Prairie voles (*Microtus ochrogaster*) form long-term pair bonds, and both parents care for the young; the closely related montane vole (*M. montanus*) and meadow vole (*M. pennsylvanicus*) are promiscuous, and males provide no care. Larry Young, Thomas Insel and colleagues found that the species do not differ in the neuropeptide **vasopressin** itself, but in the distribution of its V1a receptor in the brain: prairie vole males have dense V1a receptors in the ventral pallidum, a region of the brain's reward system, which montane and meadow voles lack. Blocking V1a receptors in the ventral pallidum of male prairie voles prevents the formation of a partner preference after mating. Conversely, using a viral vector to increase V1a receptor expression in the ventral pallidum of male meadow voles caused them to form partner preferences (Lim et al. 2004), and increasing it in prairie voles strengthened their preference for a familiar female. The gene for the receptor, *avpr1a*, has a microsatellite (**microsatellite**, [7.2.2](/7.2.2)) in its upstream regulatory region whose length varies within and between species; in laboratory prairie voles, males bred for long microsatellite alleles had higher receptor expression in some brain regions and stronger partner preferences (Hammock and Young 2005). The circuits involved, and the related role of **oxytocin** in females, are described in [7.16.36](/7.16.36#sec-36.5).

The vole story is often told as a gene for monogamy, but its details argue for caution. In wild prairie voles and in large outdoor enclosures, microsatellite length did not predict whether males formed pair bonds, although it was related to how males used space and how many offspring they sired (Ophir et al. 2008; Solomon et al. 2009); and "social monogamy" in prairie voles does not mean sexual fidelity, since extra-pair matings are common. The receptor gene contributes to differences in social behavior through its effects on a reward circuit, among many other genes and experiences.

### 4.3.4. Migratory Direction in Blackcaps

Blackcaps that breed in central Europe used to migrate southwest to Spain and North Africa for the winter, and those breeding in eastern Europe migrate southeast. Since the 1960s an increasing number of blackcaps have spent the winter in Britain and Ireland, where they were formerly rare. Peter Berthold and colleagues (1992) caught blackcaps wintering in Britain, kept them in captivity in Germany, and bred them. In autumn, the young birds, which had never migrated, and their parents were tested in orientation cages (Emlen funnels, [7.18.21](/7.18.21)), which record the direction in which a restless bird tries to take off. Both oriented west-northwest, the direction from Germany to Britain, whereas the offspring of blackcaps from the same German breeding area that winter in Iberia oriented southwest. Ring recoveries confirmed that the British wintering birds breed in central Europe. The new migration route had evolved within a few decades, presumably favored by milder winters and food provided at garden bird feeders, and by the advantage of a shorter journey back to the breeding grounds, where British-wintering birds arrive earlier. Crosses between southwest-migrating and southeast-migrating parents produced offspring that oriented roughly south, intermediate between the parental directions (Helbig 1991): migratory direction is inherited as a polygenic, additive trait.

The genes involved are not yet fully known. Among candidate genes, length polymorphism of a microsatellite in *adcyap1*, which encodes a neuropeptide involved in many physiological functions, was correlated with the intensity of migratory restlessness among individuals and with migratory behavior across populations (Mueller et al. 2011), though such associations are small and have not been replicated in all species. The duration of migratory restlessness also differs between species: in a study of short-distance migrating black redstarts (*Phoenicurus ochruros*) and long-distance migrating common redstarts (*P. phoenicurus*) raised under identical conditions, hybrids showed restlessness for an intermediate period (Berthold and Querner 1995).

### 4.3.5. Prey Preference in Garter Snakes

The western terrestrial garter snake (*Thamnophis elegans*) lives both in the dry interior of western North America, where it eats fish and frogs, and in the humid coastal forests of California, where it also eats banana slugs (*Ariolimax*), slimy molluscs avoided by most predators. Stevan Arnold (1981) brought pregnant females from both areas into the laboratory and offered their newborn young, each raised alone and never fed, a small piece of slug. Most coastal newborns ate it; most inland newborns refused. The difference also appeared in tongue-flicking responses to cotton swabs soaked in slug extract, a measure of chemosensory interest, and hybrids between the populations showed intermediate and highly variable responses. The difference in preference therefore has a genetic basis that develops without experience of slugs, and it appears to reflect differences in the chemosensory recognition of prey. In some populations, garter snakes of a related species, the common garter snake (*T. sirtalis*), eat newts of the genus *Taricha*, which contain tetrodotoxin; their resistance to the toxin is due to amino acid substitutions in the skeletal muscle sodium channel $\mathrm{Na_V}1.4$ that reduce its binding of the toxin, an arms race described in [7.18.24](/7.18.24) ([7.10.17](/7.10.17)).

## 4.4. Supergenes and Behavioral Polymorphisms

Some species contain two or more discrete, genetically determined types of individuals that differ in a whole suite of behavioral, morphological and physiological traits. How can many traits be inherited together? Often the answer is a **supergene** ([7.4.15](/7.4.15)): a cluster of linked genes that are inherited as a unit because recombination between them is suppressed, usually by a chromosomal **inversion** ([7.4.15](/7.4.15#sec-15.4)).

In the red imported fire ant (*Solenopsis invicta*), colonies are of two social forms. Monogyne colonies have a single queen; polygyne colonies have several to hundreds. The social form is controlled by a "social chromosome" carrying a supergene of several hundred genes in a region of suppressed recombination, with two variants, $SB$ and $Sb$ (Wang et al. 2013). The gene *Gp-9*, which encodes an odorant-binding protein and was the first marker of the polymorphism, lies within it. In monogyne colonies all queens and workers are $SB/SB$. In polygyne colonies, the queens are $SB/Sb$, $Sb/Sb$ individuals die young, and the workers, if they carry at least one $Sb$, kill any $SB/SB$ queens that try to join the colony (Keller and Ross 1998). The $Sb$ variant thus causes its carriers to recognize and kill individuals lacking it, a form of the green-beard effect ([7.18.33](/7.18.33)); the two forms also differ in queen size, fecundity and dispersal. A remarkably similar social supergene has evolved independently in the alpine silver ant (*Formica selysi*).

The ruff (*Calidris pugnax*), a sandpiper, has three kinds of males. "Independents", about 84% of males, have dark ornamental ruffs and head tufts and defend small courts on a lek ([7.18.30](/7.18.30)). "Satellites", about 15%, have white ruffs, do not defend courts, and join independents on their courts, where they are tolerated because their presence attracts females, and mate opportunistically. "Faeders", fewer than 1%, are female mimics with no ornamental plumage, slightly larger than females, which sneak copulations; they have unusually large testes. The three types are determined by an autosomal supergene, a 4.5-megabase inversion on chromosome 11 that arose about 3.8 million years ago (Küpper et al. 2016; Lamichhaney et al. 2016). The inverted haplotype is lethal when homozygous, because one breakpoint disrupts the essential gene *CENPN*, so faeders and satellites are heterozygous for the inversion. The faeder haplotype is the original inversion; the satellite haplotype arose later by rare recombination between the inverted and the ancestral haplotypes. Genes within the inversion include ones involved in steroid hormone metabolism, consistent with differences in testosterone levels among the male types.

In the white-throated sparrow (*Zonotrichia albicollis*), both sexes come in a white-striped and a tan-striped morph. White-striped birds are heterozygous for a large inversion on chromosome 2; they sing more, are more aggressive and less parental, and tan birds provide more parental care. Almost all breeding pairs consist of one white and one tan bird (disassortative mating), which keeps both forms in the population. The expression of the estrogen receptor $\alpha$ gene *ESR1*, located in the inversion, differs between the morphs in brain regions that control territorial aggression, and experimentally reducing its expression lowers aggression (Merritt et al. 2020).

Independents are homozygous for the ancestral arrangement; satellites and faeders are heterozygous; homozygotes for the inversion die because a breakpoint disrupts the essential gene CENPN. Supergenes allow many traits that must work together, such as the plumage, behavior and testis size of a sneaker male, to be inherited as a package without being broken up by recombination. Their discovery showed that discrete alternative strategies can be genetic, although many other alternative strategies are conditional, determined by the environment and the individual's condition ([7.18.5](/7.18.5), [7.18.28](/7.18.28)).

## 4.5. Epigenetics, Early Experience and Behavior

### 4.5.1. Maternal Care and Stress Responses in Rats

Mother rats differ in how much they lick and groom their pups and in how often they nurse in an arched-back posture. Michael Meaney and colleagues found that the adult offspring of high-licking mothers are less fearful and show smaller stress responses (lower release of corticosterone, a **glucocorticoid**, after a stressor) than the offspring of low-licking mothers. Cross-fostering pups at birth showed that the difference is caused by the care the pups receive, not by the genes inherited from their biological mothers (Francis et al. 1999). Female offspring raised by high-licking mothers in turn became high-licking mothers, so maternal behavior was transmitted across generations without any genetic difference.

The mechanism involves DNA methylation (**DNA methylation**, [7.1.11](/7.1.11)). Licking and grooming stimulates serotonin signaling in the hippocampus of the pup, which increases the expression of the transcription factor NGFI-A; NGFI-A binds to the promoter of the glucocorticoid receptor gene (*Nr3c1*) in the hippocampus, and in pups of high-licking mothers this promoter becomes less methylated during the first week of life (Weaver et al. 2004). The adult offspring therefore express more glucocorticoid receptors in the hippocampus, which strengthens the negative feedback of glucocorticoids on the **hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis** ([7.14.35](/7.14.35)), so that stress responses are terminated more quickly. Infusing a histone deacetylase inhibitor into the brain of adult offspring of low-licking mothers reduced methylation of the promoter and reversed the behavioral and hormonal differences, showing that the epigenetic mark is not fixed.

More hippocampal GR strengthens glucocorticoid negative feedback on the HPA axis (CRH, ACTH, corticosterone), so the adult is less reactive to stress; adult daughters reared by high-licking mothers become high-licking mothers themselves, closing the loop across generations. These results are important because they show a pathway by which early experience produces long-lasting changes in behavior through gene regulation, and by which a behavioral trait can be inherited nongenetically. From an evolutionary point of view, the mother's licking may serve as a signal of the environment the offspring are likely to face: in a harsh, dangerous environment, mothers lick less, and their offspring develop a more reactive stress system that may suit such conditions ([7.18.5](/7.18.5)). Many details remain debated, including how specific and how stable the methylation changes are, and how far the results generalize to humans.

### 4.5.2. Diet, Methylation and Caste in Honey Bees

In honey bees, whether a female larva develops into a queen or a worker depends on her diet: larvae destined to be queens are fed royal jelly, a secretion of the workers' head glands, throughout development, whereas worker larvae receive it only for the first days. Ryszard Kucharski and colleagues (2008) used RNA interference to reduce the expression of the DNA methyltransferase *Dnmt3* in newly hatched larvae (**RNA interference**, [7.2.18](/7.2.18)): about 72% of the treated larvae developed into queens or queen-like adults with developed ovaries, compared with fewer than a quarter of controls. DNA methylation thus contributes to the switch between the two developmental pathways, with royal jelly perhaps acting partly by reducing methylation. Methylation also changes in the brain when workers change from nursing to foraging, and reverting foragers to nursing restores the nurse pattern at many sites (Herb et al. 2012). The role of methylation in insects is still debated, however: insect genomes are much less methylated than those of mammals, and some later studies found few consistent differences between castes. Caste determination and age polyethism as developmental switches are treated in [7.18.5](/7.18.5).

## 4.6. One Gene, One Behavior?

Popular accounts often speak of a "gene for" monogamy, aggression or migration. The examples of this chapter show what such phrases can and cannot mean.

1. *Genes cause differences, not behaviors.* A "gene for" a behavior is an allele whose substitution for another allele changes the behavior, in a given genetic background and environment. The $for^{\mathrm{R}}$ allele makes larvae rove more than $for^{\mathrm{s}}$; it does not by itself produce foraging, which requires thousands of genes building a nervous system and a body.
2. *Single genes of large effect are the exception.* Most behavioral variation is polygenic, with many loci of small effect, as GWAS and QTL studies show. Genes of large effect such as *foraging*, *npr-1*, the ruff inversion and the fire ant supergene are instructive because they are rare.
3. *Effects depend on genetic background and environment.* The effect of *avpr1a* microsatellite length on partner preference is clear in laboratory prairie voles but not in the field; mutant phenotypes often differ between strains; and the effect of a gene can change with rearing conditions (**genotype–environment interaction**).
4. *Genes act through many levels.* *npr-1* changes social feeding by changing oxygen sensing; *foraging* encodes a kinase that acts in many neurons and also affects metabolism. Most genes affect several traits (**pleiotropy**, [7.4.3](/7.4.3)), and the effects of one gene depend on others (**epistasis**, [7.4.4](/7.4.4)).
5. *Environmental effects work through genes too.* Licking by a rat mother and royal jelly fed to a bee larva change behavior by changing gene regulation. The dichotomy between genetic and environmental causes is false at the level of mechanism.

Benzer's question, whether single genes could be found that affect behavior, was answered yes; but the answer did not imply that behavior is programmed gene by gene. For the evolution of behavior, what matters is that heritable variation exists, so that selection can act, and that the genetic architecture (few genes or many, large effects or small, linked in supergenes or free to recombine) shapes how fast and in what ways behavior can evolve.

## Summary

- Behavior develops through the interaction of genes and environment; only differences in behavior can be attributed to genetic or environmental differences. Brain gene expression in honey bees follows behavioral role (nurse versus forager) rather than age, and is influenced by the social environment.
- Methods of behavioral genetics (Definition 4.2.1):

  | Method | What it shows | Example |
  |---|---|---|
  | Common rearing and crosses | Genetic basis and mode of inheritance of a difference | Lovebird hybrids; blackcap crosses; garter snake hybrids |
  | Artificial selection | Heritability, $R = h^2 S$ | Tryon's maze-bright and maze-dull rats; blackcap migration |
  | Mutant screens | Genes necessary for a behavior | Benzer's phototaxis screen; *period*, *dunce* |
  | QTL mapping, GWAS | Loci causing natural variation | Burrow architecture in *Peromyscus* |
  | Candidate genes, gene editing | Causal tests of particular genes | *orco* knockout in ants; V1a receptor overexpression in voles |

- Genes of large effect: *foraging* (PKG; rover and sitter flies, maintained by density- and frequency-dependent selection; foraging onset in bees); *npr-1* (social feeding in *C. elegans*); *avpr1a* (V1a receptors in the ventral pallidum and partner preference in voles); migratory direction in blackcaps (additive inheritance, rapid evolution of a new route to Britain); slug preference in garter snakes.
- **Supergenes** maintained by inversions control discrete behavioral morphs: fire ant social form ($SB$/$Sb$), the three male types of ruffs, the white and tan morphs of white-throated sparrows.
- Early experience acts through epigenetic mechanisms: maternal licking in rats demethylates the glucocorticoid receptor promoter and reduces stress reactivity, transmitting maternal style across generations nongenetically; reducing *Dnmt3* in honey bee larvae mimics the effect of royal jelly.
- A "gene for a behavior" means an allele that changes behavior relative to another allele in a given background and environment; most behavior is polygenic, and gene effects are pleiotropic, epistatic and environment-dependent.

## Questions

1. In Benzer's apparatus, a population of flies is tested with $n = 10$ trials. Wild-type flies move toward the light with probability $p = 0.85$ in each trial. What fraction of wild-type flies is expected in the last tube ($k = 10$) and in the tubes $k \le 5$? A mutant has $p = 0.15$; in which tube is it most likely to be found?

   **Answer** $\mathbb{P}(K = 10) = 0.85^{10} \approx 0.197$. $\mathbb{P}(K \le 5) = \sum_{k=0}^{5}\binom{10}{k}0.85^k 0.15^{10-k} \approx 0.0099$, about 1%. For the mutant, the most probable number of moves is the mode of $\mathrm{Bin}(10, 0.15)$, which is $k = 1$ ($\mathbb{P} \approx 0.347$, slightly more than $\mathbb{P}(K = 2) \approx 0.276$ and $\mathbb{P}(K = 0) \approx 0.197$). The two distributions barely overlap, so a single run separates most mutants from wild-type flies.
2. A population of mice has a mean nest weight of 20 g. Breeders select as parents the mice that build the heaviest nests, with a mean of 30 g. The offspring generation has a mean nest weight of 22.5 g. Estimate the heritability of nest building. What would you predict after a second generation of selection with the same selection differential?

   **Answer** $S = 30 - 20 = 10$ g, $R = 22.5 - 20 = 2.5$ g, so the realized heritability is $h^2 = R/S = 0.25$. With the same selection differential of 10 g, a second generation should increase the mean by another $0.25 \times 10 = 2.5$ g, to about 25 g, assuming the heritability remains constant (in reality it may decline slowly as genetic variance is used up, and drift adds random variation).
3. Single-cohort honey bee colonies were essential to interpret the gene-expression differences between nurses and foragers. Why? Design a further experiment to test whether *Amfor* expression causes the onset of foraging rather than being a consequence of it.

   **Answer** In normal colonies, foragers are older than nurses, so differences in gene expression could be caused by age rather than behavior. In single-cohort colonies, bees of the same age differentiate into nurses and foragers, so age and behavior are uncoupled; expression followed behavior. To test causation, one could increase PKG activity (with a cGMP analog or by overexpressing *Amfor* through RNA or viral methods) in young bees in single-cohort colonies and test whether they forage earlier, and conversely reduce *Amfor* expression with RNA interference and test whether foraging is delayed; measuring expression in bees just before their first foraging flight, compared with same-age bees that have not yet foraged, would also show whether the change precedes the behavior.
4. In the rover–sitter model of the figure in §4.3.1, show that $\hat p$ is a stable equilibrium. What would happen if the fitness of rovers increased with their own frequency?

   **Answer** In a haploid model with relative fitnesses $w_{\mathrm{R}} = 1 + s(\hat p - p)$ and $w_{\mathrm{S}} = 1$, the change in $p$ per generation is $\Delta p = p(1-p)(w_{\mathrm{R}} - w_{\mathrm{S}})/\bar w = p(1-p)s(\hat p - p)/\bar w$. For $0 < p < \hat p$, $\Delta p > 0$; for $\hat p < p < 1$, $\Delta p < 0$; so $p$ moves toward $\hat p$ from either side, and $\hat p$ is stable. If instead $w_{\mathrm{R}} - w_{\mathrm{S}}$ increased with $p$ (positive frequency dependence), the interior equilibrium would be unstable and the population would go to fixation of whichever morph was initially more common than the threshold, so the polymorphism would be lost.
5. Why are the three male types of the ruff inherited as discrete types rather than as a continuum, and why are there no males homozygous for the inversion? What prevents the inversion from going to fixation?

   **Answer** The inversion suppresses recombination between the many genes that affect plumage, behavior, body size and hormone metabolism, so the combinations of alleles that make an independent, a satellite or a faeder are inherited as units, producing discrete types. Homozygotes for the inversion die because one breakpoint disrupts the essential gene *CENPN*; the inversion is therefore a recessive lethal and cannot go to fixation. It is maintained because heterozygous carriers (satellites and faeders) gain matings through their alternative tactics, and the success of these tactics is probably frequency-dependent: satellites and faeders do well only when independents are common.
6. A newspaper article claims that "scientists have found the monogamy gene" after the vole studies. Write a short, accurate correction.

   **Answer** The studies found that differences between vole species in where the vasopressin V1a receptor is expressed in the brain, especially in the reward-related ventral pallidum, contribute to differences in whether males form a preference for a mating partner, and that increasing receptor expression there can induce such preferences in a promiscuous species. The gene for the receptor exists in all voles; what differs is its regulation, partly influenced by a variable stretch of DNA near the gene. In wild voles, variation in that stretch does not reliably predict pair bonding, many other genes and experiences matter, and "monogamous" prairie voles often mate with other partners. The receptor gene is one contributor to social bonding, not a switch for monogamy.
7. The adult offspring of high-licking rat mothers have lower stress responses. Is this an adaptation, a constraint, or neither? Propose a hypothesis about the adaptive value of the mechanism and a way to test it.

   **Answer** The mechanism could be an adaptive form of plasticity in which maternal care acts as a forecast of the environment: mothers under stress, in dangerous or poor environments, lick less, and their offspring develop a more reactive stress system and greater vigilance, which may increase survival in such environments, whereas offspring of relaxed mothers in safe environments invest more in growth and reproduction. Alternatively, low licking might simply impair development (a constraint). A test would rear offspring of high- and low-licking mothers (or cross-fostered pups) in environments differing in predation risk or unpredictability, and measure survival and reproduction; the adaptive hypothesis predicts a crossover interaction, each phenotype doing better in the matching environment, whereas the constraint hypothesis predicts that offspring of low-licking mothers do worse everywhere ([7.18.5](/7.18.5)).
