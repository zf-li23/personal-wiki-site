Males and females of most animals behave very differently when it comes to reproduction. A male satin bowerbird spends weeks building and decorating a bower and courts every female that visits it; the female inspects many bowers, mates once with the male she prefers and then incubates and feeds the young alone. Male elephant seals fight for harems, male sage grouse display on leks, male damselflies scrub the sperm of their rivals out of the female before inseminating her. This chapter and the next explain these differences with Darwin's theory of **sexual selection**, introduced in [7.10.13](/7.10.13#def-13.2.1). There the emphasis was on the population genetics of sexually selected traits and preferences; here it is on behavior, on the experiments and field studies that show how sexual selection works, and on the decision rules of individual animals.

We start with the question why it is usually males that compete and females that choose, and with the exceptions that confirm the explanation. We then treat competition among males before mating: fighting, weapons and dominance, and the alternative tactics by which males that cannot win fights still obtain matings, which brings us back to the game theory of [7.18.25](/7.18.25). Finally we follow competition into the female's reproductive tract, where the ejaculates of different males compete and males guard their mates to keep rivals away. Mate choice and the conflicts between the sexes are the subject of [7.18.29](/7.18.29); the mating systems that result, from monogamy to leks, of [7.18.30](/7.18.30); and parental care, which is closely bound up with the sex differences treated here, of [7.18.31](/7.18.31).

## 28.1. Why the Sexes Differ

### 28.1.1. Two Kinds of Sexual Selection

Darwin (1871) wrote that "the sexual struggle is of two kinds": in one, individuals of one sex, usually males, drive away or kill their rivals; in the other, they compete to "excite or charm" members of the other sex, which choose among them. The first explains weapons and large size, the second ornaments and displays.

**Definition 28.1.1 (Intrasexual and Intersexual Selection)** **Intrasexual selection** is sexual selection that results from competition among members of one sex for access to mates, by fighting, scrambling, endurance or the competition of their gametes. **Intersexual selection** is sexual selection that results from the choice of mates by members of the other sex, which favors the traits that make individuals more likely to be chosen.

The two terms are standard but slightly misleading, as Davies, Krebs and West point out: both processes involve competition within one sex, in the first case to win mates by force and in the second by charm. They differ in who decides the outcome, the competitors or the chooser. In practice the two often act together. Red deer stags roar and fight, and hinds prefer stags that roar more; the antlers of a stag are weapons, but they may also be assessed by females. Sexual selection also continues after mating, through sperm competition and cryptic female choice (Section 28.4), so that the full picture has four parts: competition before and after mating, and choice before and after mating.

### 28.1.2. Gametes, Investment and Reproductive Rates

The fundamental difference between the sexes is the size of their gametes. Females make few, large, food-rich eggs; males make many tiny sperm. The evolution of this dimorphism, **anisogamy**, by disruptive selection on gamete size was explained in [7.10.13](/7.10.13#def-13.1.1). Once it exists, a sperm is in effect a parasite on the provisioning of an egg, and the two sexes are set on different courses.

Robert Trivers (1972) linked the difference in investment to the direction of competition: "where one sex invests considerably more than the other, members of the latter will compete among themselves to mate with members of the former". The investment includes not only the gametes but all the care that a parent gives an offspring at the expense of other offspring, *parental investment* in Trivers's sense, analyzed in [7.18.31](/7.18.31). In mammals females carry the young and suckle them; in most birds both parents feed the young, but females usually invest more; in fishes the pattern is often reversed. The key consequence, emphasized by Clutton-Brock and Parker (1992), is that the sex with the lower investment has the higher *potential reproductive rate*, the number of offspring an individual could produce per unit time if mates were unlimited. Records of maximum lifetime output show the asymmetry: in the northern elephant seal a male can father about 100 offspring and a female about 8; in the red deer, 24 against 14; in the monogamous kittiwake, where both sexes invest similarly, 26 against 28 (Clutton-Brock 1983).

The sex with the higher potential reproductive rate increases its success by obtaining more mates; the other sex increases its success by converting resources into eggs and young, and by choosing good partners. This is **Bateman's principle** ([7.10.13](/7.10.13#def-13.1.2)), named after the experiments of Angus Bateman (1948) with *Drosophila melanogaster*: males that mated with more females had more offspring, while females gained little from more than one mating. Bateman's own data have been criticized. When Patricia Gowaty and colleagues (2012) repeated his methods exactly, they found that the visible mutant markers he used to assign parentage were themselves lethal in some combinations, so that his method overestimated the number of individuals without mates and biased the estimates of offspring numbers. The principle has nevertheless been confirmed many times with molecular parentage: a meta-analysis of studies across the animal kingdom found that the regression of reproductive success on mating success, the **Bateman gradient**, is on average steeper in males than in females (Janicke and colleagues 2016), with the exceptions concentrated in species with sex-role reversal.

### 28.1.3. Measuring the Strength of Sexual Selection

How strong sexual selection is in a population can be measured in several ways, none of them perfect.

**Formula 28.1.1 (Opportunity for Sexual Selection)** If $m_i$ is the number of mates of individual $i$ of one sex, the *opportunity for sexual selection* in that sex is the squared coefficient of variation of mating success,

$$
I_s = \frac{\operatorname{Var}(m)}{\bar m^2}.
$$
It is the analog, for mating success, of the opportunity for selection $I = \operatorname{Var}(w)/\bar w^2$ of [7.10.13](/7.10.13#sec-13.1.2), and it sets an upper limit to the standardized selection differential on any trait through mating success.

In elephant seals studied by Le Boeuf and Reiter (1988) in California, as few as 5 of 180 males obtained 48–92 percent of the matings with 470 females in a year, so $I_s$ is very large among males and close to zero among females. Variance in mating success is only the opportunity for selection, however, not selection itself: mating success varies even when mating is random, and a high $I_s$ is evidence of sexual selection only if the variance exceeds that expected by chance and is associated with heritable traits (Sutherland 1985; Klug and colleagues 2010).

Variance in mating success arises because the number of individuals ready to mate is unequal in the two sexes.

**Formula 28.1.2 (Operational Sex Ratio from Time Out)** Let $N_{\mathrm{m}}$ and $N_{\mathrm{f}}$ be the numbers of adult males and females, and let $t_{\mathrm{m}}$ and $t_{\mathrm{f}}$ be the fractions of a breeding cycle that an individual of each sex spends in "time out", unavailable for mating because it is producing gametes, gestating or caring for young. The **operational sex ratio** ([7.10.13](/7.10.13#def-13.1.3)) is then

$$
\mathrm{OSR} = \frac{N_{\mathrm{m}}(1 - t_{\mathrm{m}})}{N_{\mathrm{f}}(1 - t_{\mathrm{f}})}.
$$
With an even adult sex ratio, males are in excess and compete for females whenever $t_{\mathrm{m}} < t_{\mathrm{f}}$, that is, whenever males return to the mating pool faster than females.

The formula, a simplified version of the time-in/time-out model of Clutton-Brock and Parker (1992), makes the Trivers argument quantitative: it is not investment as such but its effect on the time spent out of the mating pool that determines which sex competes. It also shows that the OSR depends on mortality (through $N_{\mathrm{m}}/N_{\mathrm{f}}$) and can change during a season. The OSR is not the whole story: the strength of sexual selection depends also on how easily the competitors can monopolize mates, which depends on the spatial and temporal distribution of the other sex ([7.18.30](/7.18.30)). Adding a male to the mating pool may increase variance in male success if the best male can take extra females, or decrease it if monopolization becomes harder (Klug and colleagues 2010).

### 28.1.4. Is Sperm Cheap?

The argument above assumes that sperm are cheap. A single sperm is, but males do not produce single sperm. A male splendid fairy-wren (*Malurus splendens*), weighing less than 10 g, holds several billion sperm in its testes, and a male coho salmon releases about $10^{11}$ sperm over a batch of 3500 eggs. Spermatogenesis and ejaculation are costly. Males of many species need time to recover after mating, and the number of sperm per ejaculate falls with mating frequency: in Soay sheep (*Ovis aries*) during the rut, large rams that mated most often transferred the fewest sperm per ejaculate (Preston and colleagues 2001). In the nematode *Caenorhabditis elegans*, males that mate live shorter lives, and the cost lies in spermatogenesis rather than mating itself. Males also age: in captive houbara bustards (*Chlamydotis undulata*), the hatching success of eggs declined with the age of the father about as steeply as with the age of the mother (Preston and colleagues 2015).

These facts do not overturn the theory. What matters is the cost of a *mating* relative to the cost of the eggs it fertilizes, and this cost is usually much smaller for males. They do explain why males of many species are choosy about which females they mate with (prudent sperm allocation) and why males facing many competitors invest more in each ejaculate (Section 28.4.3).

### 28.1.5. Reversed Sex Roles

A strong test of the theory is provided by species in which males invest more in the offspring than females. The theory predicts that in these species females should be in excess in the mating pool, compete for males, and be the larger or more ornamented sex, while males should choose. Such cases of **sex-role reversal** ([7.10.13](/7.10.13#sec-13.1.2)) exist, and they show that the decisive variable is the relative investment, not sex itself.

- *Pipefish and seahorses.* In the broad-nosed pipefish *Syngnathus typhle*, "pregnant" males carry fertilized eggs in a brood pouch for several weeks and supply them with oxygen and nutrients. In the time a male needs to brood one clutch, a female can produce enough eggs to fill the pouches of two males, so females are in excess. Females compete for males and display ornaments; large males with free pouch space choose large females, whose clutches are larger (Berglund and colleagues 1986). In the Gulf pipefish (*Syngnathus scovelli*), the temporary stripes that females display in contests with other females are under strong sexual selection, as shown by genetic measures of mating success (Flanagan and colleagues 2014). In seahorses the male carries the young in a closed pouch, but males are often monogamous and roles less reversed, a reminder that investment determines the OSR only together with ecology.
- *Katydids.* Males of many katydids transfer a large, protein-rich spermatophore that the female eats. In the Mormon cricket (*Anabrus simplex*) the spermatophore amounts to about 25 percent of the male's body mass, and a male can produce few of them. At high population density, when food is scarce and males can make spermatophores only slowly, females fight for access to singing males, and males reject light females: a male that rejects a female of 3.2 g in favor of one of 3.5 g fertilizes about 50 percent more eggs (Gwynne 1981, 1984). In the Australian katydid *Kawanaphila nartee*, sex roles change with the food supply: when pollen is scarce early in the season, females compete for males and their spermatophores, and when pollen becomes abundant and males can produce spermatophores faster, males compete for females (Gwynne and Simmons 1990).
- *Gobies.* In the two-spotted goby (*Gobiusculus flavescens*) of European coasts, males guard nests in seaweed and empty mussel shells and care for eggs. Early in the season males compete for females, but because the costs of care and competition kill many males, the sex ratio becomes female-biased during the summer, and females then compete for males and court them (Forsgren and colleagues 2004).
- *Phalaropes and jacanas.* In the three species of phalaropes, females are larger and brighter than males, court them and leave them to incubate and raise the chicks; a female may then lay a second clutch for another male. Polyandrous shorebirds and jacanas are treated in [7.18.30](/7.18.30).
- *Dance flies.* In some empidid flies, males hunt for insect prey and offer it to a female as a nuptial gift ([7.18.29](/7.18.29)). Because most males are away hunting, swarms consist mainly of females waiting for gift-bearing males. In the long-tailed dance fly (*Rhamphomyia longicauda*), females gulp air to inflate their abdomens and display fringed legs that make them look larger, apparently exploiting male preference for large, fecund females (Funk and Tallamy 2000).

The theory also predicts mutual ornamentation and mutual choice when both sexes compete. In black swans (*Cygnus atratus*), both sexes have curled wing feathers used in social displays, and sexual selection on both sexes is common in cooperatively breeding species, in which reproduction is skewed in both sexes ([7.18.35](/7.18.35)).

Why, after mating, do females still care more? David Queller (1997) showed that two common features of mating systems make care less profitable for males. First, if females mate with several males, a male's paternity of the brood he would care for is uncertain, which reduces the value of care to him. Second, when sexual selection makes mating success skewed among males, the males that have mated are a selected subset with better than average prospects of mating again: in a lek of 20 males where one male mates with all 20 females, his expected success is 20 times that of a female, and care would cost him more future success than it costs her. The feedback is positive: the more females care, the stronger sexual selection on males, and the less males should care (Kokko and Jennions 2008). The evolution of parental roles is taken up in [7.18.31](/7.18.31).

## 28.2. Competition Among Males

### 28.2.1. Forms of Competition

Males compete for mates in several ways, each favoring different traits (Andersson 1994):

| Form | How males win | Traits favored | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Contest | Fighting or threat; monopolizing females or resources | Large size, strength, weapons, fighting skill, threat displays | Elephant seals, red deer, stag beetles |
| Scramble | Finding receptive females first | Early emergence, mobility, keen senses | Male moths tracking pheromone plumes, wood frogs |
| Endurance rivalry | Remaining active in the mating arena longest | Energy reserves, survival | Red deer stags that lose condition in the rut, lekking males |
| Sperm competition | Fertilizing the eggs of a female that mates with several males | Ejaculate size and quality, sperm removal, mate guarding (Section 28.4) | Dung flies, damselflies |
| Coercion | Forcing copulation or punishing refusal | Grasping structures, harassment | Water striders, ducks ([7.18.29](/7.18.29)) |

Contests are the most conspicuous form and are treated first. Their dynamics, including the assessment of rivals and the role of ownership, were analyzed in [7.18.25](/7.18.25) and [7.18.26](/7.18.26).

### 28.2.2. Weapons and Body Size

Where females, or the resources they need, are clumped in space, males can defend them, and the largest and strongest males win. Female northern elephant seals (*Mirounga angustirostris*) haul out on a few beaches to give birth and mate again shortly afterward. The males fight for these aggregations, and the outcome determines who mates: in the study by Le Boeuf and Reiter, a few males obtained most of the matings each year. Adult males weigh three to seven and a half times as much as females, and males delay breeding attempts until they are 6–9 years old, while most females first breed at 4. A female's success depends on her ability to nurture a pup; a male's depends on winning fights.

Sexual selection for fighting ability therefore produces sexual size dimorphism, with males larger than females in species with intense male–male competition and females larger in many role-reversed species (Blanckenhorn 2005). It also produces weapons. Horns, antlers, tusks, enlarged mandibles and spiny legs used in fights with rivals have evolved many times, often in strikingly similar forms: rhinoceros-like horns are found in dung beetles, chameleons, ceratopsian dinosaurs, horned rodents and extinct ungulates (Emlen 2008). Weapons are expensive. Horn growth in dung beetles trades off against the growth of eyes, wings or genitalia, and against testis size: in *Onthophagus nigriventris*, males experimentally prevented from growing horns developed relatively larger testes (Simmons and Emlen 2006). The developmental switch that produces horned and hornless males was described in [7.18.5](/7.18.5#sec-5.6.3); the behavior of the two morphs is described below.

Not all competitive traits are used in fighting. *Badges of status*, such as the black bib of the house sparrow, signal dominance and settle disputes without fighting; their reliability is discussed with signals of aggression in [7.18.40](/7.18.40).

### 28.2.3. Dominance and Paternity

In groups whose members meet repeatedly, males compete for a position in a dominance hierarchy that later gives priority of access to females. Male yellow baboons (*Papio cynocephalus*) fight for rank and are bitten about once every six weeks, an injury rate nearly four times that of females (Drews 1996). Does it pay? In the first test, Glen Hausfater (1975) found that low-ranking males copulated as often as high-ranking ones, which seemed to show that it did not. He then realized that copulation leads to conception only when the female is ovulating, and that dominant males monopolized females during the few days of the cycle when they were fertile. Long-term studies in Amboseli, Kenya, have since confirmed with molecular paternity analysis that male rank predicts both mating success with fertile females and the number of offspring sired (Alberts and colleagues 2006).

The advantage of rank is, however, smaller than expected if the top male always had priority (Alberts and colleagues 2003). Lower-ranking males compensate in two ways. They form friendships with particular females, protecting them and their infants, and these females often prefer them as partners when they next come into estrus. They also form coalitions: in one troop of eight adult males, males ranked fifth to seventh regularly joined forces against a single higher-ranking male consorting with a fertile female, and in 18 of 28 such confrontations they forced him to give her up (Noë and Sluijter 1990). Such alternative routes to mating are the subject of the next section.

## 28.3. Alternative Reproductive Tactics

### 28.3.1. Strategies and Tactics

In many species some males do not fight or display at all but obtain matings in other ways: by sneaking, by mimicking females, by waiting beside a calling male. Such **alternative mating strategies** ([7.10.13](/7.10.13#sec-13.2.2)) raise two questions: why do they persist, and are the males that use them genetically different? The distinction drawn in [7.18.25](/7.18.25#sec-25.4.3) between strategies and tactics is essential here. A *strategy* is a genetically based decision rule; a *tactic* is a behavior pattern played as part of a strategy. Alternative phenotypes can be:

1. tactics of a single **conditional strategy** ([7.18.5](/7.18.5#def-5.6.1)), with all individuals following the same rule ("fight if large, sneak if small") but differing in condition. The tactics need not have equal success: the losers of the competition "make the best of a bad job";
2. alternative genetic strategies, a genetic polymorphism. Then the strategies can coexist only if their average fitnesses are equal at a stable frequency, maintained by **negative frequency-dependent selection**, or if their frequencies cycle.

**Definition 28.3.1 (Sneaking)** **Sneaking** is a reproductive tactic in which a male obtains fertilizations by stealth, without defending a female or a resource and without courting, by approaching a mating pair or a guarded female unnoticed, often while mimicking a female or another inconspicuous form; males using it are called *sneakers* (or *cuckolders*, *parasitic males*).

Sneaking is one of the commonest alternative tactics, particularly in fishes with external fertilization, where a sneaker need only release sperm close to the eggs at the right moment. It often goes together with large testes, because a sneaker usually spawns in competition with the territorial male: sneaking is a tactic of sperm competition as much as of mate acquisition.

### 28.3.2. Making the Best of a Bad Job

Most alternative tactics studied so far are conditional. Several examples show the characteristic pattern: the tactic depends on size, age or social status, and males switch when their circumstances change.

- *Scorpionflies.* Males of *Panorpa* scorpionflies obtain matings in three ways: by defending a dead insect, which attracts females that feed on it while they copulate; by secreting a mound of saliva on a leaf and waiting for a female to feed on it; or by grabbing a passing female and forcing her to copulate. In cages with ten males and ten females and two dead crickets, Randy Thornhill (1981) found that the largest males took the crickets and averaged about six copulations per trial, medium-sized males produced salivary gifts and averaged about two, and small males, which could neither hold crickets nor make enough saliva, attempted forced copulations and averaged about one. When Thornhill removed the cricket owners, males that had been producing saliva promptly took over the crickets, and males that had been attempting forced copulations moved to the abandoned salivary mounds. The three tactics are thus the output of one conditional strategy, and the tactic with the highest payoff is used whenever a male can get it.
- *Marine iguanas.* In dense colonies of the Galápagos marine iguana (*Amblyrhynchus cristatus*), large males defend small mating territories and frequently dislodge smaller males mounting females. Copulation takes about three minutes to ejaculation, and small males are usually pushed off sooner. Small males ejaculate in advance, using a passing female as the cue, and store the sperm in pouches; when they do manage to mount a female, the stored sperm flow down the penile groove at once (Wikelski and Bäurle 1996). They still father far fewer offspring than the few large males that monopolize most of the matings.
- *Toads, seals and horseshoe crabs.* Small male natterjack toads sit silently beside large callers and intercept females attracted by the calls; small male elephant seals sneak into harems while the harem master is fighting; male horseshoe crabs (*Limulus polyphemus*) in poor condition cluster around a spawning pair and release sperm as "satellites", while males in good condition clasp a female.
- *Dung beetles.* In *Onthophagus*, the horned major males guard the tunnel in which a female provisions dung balls for her larvae, fighting rivals at the entrance. Hornless minor males dig side tunnels that intersect the guarded one and mate with the female while the guard is at the entrance; they transfer large ejaculates, consistent with their relatively larger testes (Hunt and Simmons 2002).

### 28.3.3. Where Should the Switch Be?

A conditional strategy is a rule with a threshold, and the threshold itself is under selection. Mart Gross (1996) proposed a simple model, which he called *status-dependent selection*. Let the status $s$ of a male be his competitive ability, determined by size or condition, and let $W_{\mathrm{A}}(s)$ and $W_{\mathrm{B}}(s)$ be the expected success of tactics A (fighting) and B (sneaking) for a male of status $s$, with $W_{\mathrm{A}}$ increasing more steeply than $W_{\mathrm{B}}$.

**Proposition 28.3.1 (Switch Point of a Conditional Strategy)** Suppose that $W_{\mathrm{A}}(s) - W_{\mathrm{B}}(s)$ is increasing in $s$ and changes sign at a single status $s_0$. If the payoffs do not depend on the frequencies of the tactics, the rule that maximizes the fitness of every individual is "play A if $s > s_0$, otherwise B". If the payoffs depend on the proportion $x$ of the population playing B, written $W_{\mathrm{B}}(s; x)$ and $W_{\mathrm{A}}(s; x)$, and the status distribution has distribution function $F$, an evolutionarily stable switch point $s^*$ satisfies

$$
W_{\mathrm{A}}(s^*; F(s^*)) = W_{\mathrm{B}}(s^*; F(s^*)).
$$

**Proof** With fixed payoffs, each male maximizes his own fitness by choosing the tactic with the larger payoff for his status, which is A exactly when $s > s_0$. With frequency dependence, the population following the rule with switch point $s^*$ contains a proportion $x = F(s^*)$ of B players. A mutant with a slightly different switch point $s'$ is a rare individual whose own choice does not change $x$. It differs from the residents only for statuses between $s^*$ and $s'$, where it plays the other tactic. If $W_{\mathrm{A}} - W_{\mathrm{B}}$ is increasing in $s$ and zero at $s^*$, then for $s' > s^*$ the mutant plays B where A pays more, and for $s' < s^*$ it plays A where B pays more; in both cases it does worse than the residents. Hence $s^*$ is an ESS. $\square$

The model makes the point of Section 28.3.1 precise. At the switch point the *marginal* male does equally well with both tactics, but the *average* payoffs of the two tactics can differ greatly, because the fighters are the males of high status (panel A of the figure below). Equal average payoffs, the test of [Theorem 25.1.2](/7.18.25#thm-25.1.2), are therefore not expected for conditional tactics. Frequency dependence moves the switch point: if sneaking pays less when sneakers are common, the switch point lies where sneaking, at its stable frequency, pays the same as fighting for the marginal male.

The model has been tested in the dung beetle *Onthophagus taurus*. Hunt and Simmons (2001) measured the fertilization success of horned and hornless males of different sizes in competition and estimated the body size at which the fitness functions of the two tactics crossed; it lay close to the body size at which horn development switches on in that population. Populations of the same species introduced to different continents have diverged in their switch points within about 40 years, in parallel with differences in the intensity of competition for dung ([7.18.5](/7.18.5#sec-5.6.4)), which shows that the threshold evolves.

### 28.3.4. Bluegill Sunfish

The bluegill sunfish (*Lepomis macrochirus*) of North American lakes shows how complex a conditional strategy can be. Studied for decades by Mart Gross and later by Bryan Neff in Lake Opinicon, Ontario, it has two male life histories.

- *Parental* males delay maturity until about seven years of age, then build nests in colonies on the lake bottom, court females, spawn with them and guard and fan the eggs and fry for about ten days.
- *Cuckolder* males mature at about two years of age, at a small size, and never become parentals. While small they are sneakers that dart into a nest during spawning and release sperm. When they grow larger, at about four years, they develop the coloration of females and become *satellites*, hovering above a nest and slipping between the parental male and the female when she spawns, as if they were a second female.

The two life histories are different allocations of a male's life: parentals invest in growth and later in care, cuckolders in early reproduction and large testes relative to their body size. A cuckolder spawning in competition with a parental male fertilizes a disproportionately high share of eggs per spawning, partly because he releases sperm closer to the eggs (Fu and colleagues 2001), and paternity analyses show that cuckolders fertilize a substantial fraction of the eggs in a colony, varying between years. Gross argued that the cuckolder and parental life histories are maintained by negative frequency-dependent selection, with cuckolders doing well when they are rare and parentals abundant, and estimated that their lifetime fitnesses were similar at the observed frequencies. Whether the choice of life history is genetic or conditional is still debated: in bluegills the decision is made early in life, and both genetic differences and early growth conditions appear to play a role. The parental males also respond to the risk of being cuckolded by adjusting their care, a topic taken up in [7.18.31](/7.18.31).

### 28.3.5. Genetic Polymorphisms

In some species the alternative male types are genetically determined and fixed for life.

- *Ruffs.* In the ruff (*Calidris pugnax*), a lekking sandpiper, "independent" males with dark ruffs defend small courts on the lek, "satellites" with white ruffs join independents on their courts without defending them, and rare "faeders" resemble females and steal copulations. The three types are inherited through a **supergene**, an inversion on chromosome 11 and a recombinant derived from it ([7.18.4](/7.18.4#sec-4.4)). Independents and satellites benefit from each other: females prefer courts with satellites, while satellites obtain some of the matings on those courts (Widemo 1998), so the relation is partly mutualistic and partly competitive.
- *Marine isopods.* In *Paracerceis sculpta*, which breeds in sponges in the intertidal zone of the Gulf of California, large alpha males defend the cavities of sponges where females live, beta males of female size mimic females and are courted by alphas, and tiny gamma males sneak in. The three morphs are determined by three alleles at one locus. Shuster and Wade (1991) measured the reproductive success of each morph in artificial sponges containing different combinations of males and females, and combined these measurements with the frequencies of the combinations in a large field sample. The estimated average numbers of mates were 1.51 for alpha, 1.35 for beta and 1.37 for gamma males, not significantly different, as the hypothesis of genetic strategies at a frequency-dependent equilibrium predicts.
- *Side-blotched lizards.* Males of *Uta stansburiana* in the Californian Coast Range have orange, blue or yellow throats, inherited largely through one locus (Sinervo and Lively 1996). Orange males have high testosterone levels and defend large territories containing several females; blue males defend smaller territories and guard their mates closely; yellow males resemble females, hold no territories and sneak copulations. Each morph beats one other and loses to the third. Orange males take over the territories of blue males; yellow sneakers succeed with the many females scattered over the large territories of orange males; and blue males, guarding their few females closely, defeat yellow sneakers. In the study population the morph frequencies cycled between 1990 and 1999, with the commonest morph changing every few years, as a rock–paper–scissors game predicts (Sinervo and Lively 1996; Alonzo and Sinervo 2001).

The rock–paper–scissors game is the simplest game in which no strategy is stable, and its dynamics show why.

**Theorem 28.3.1 (Rock–Paper–Scissors Dynamics)** Let three strategies O, B, Y be played in random pairs, with O beating B, B beating Y and Y beating O; the winner gains $a > 0$, the loser loses $b > 0$, and a pairing of like strategies gives zero. With frequencies $\boldsymbol{x} = (x_{\mathrm{O}}, x_{\mathrm{B}}, x_{\mathrm{Y}})$, the **replicator dynamics** ([Definition 25.1.2](/7.18.25#def-25.1.2)) has the unique interior rest point $(\tfrac13, \tfrac13, \tfrac13)$, and the function $V = x_{\mathrm{O}}x_{\mathrm{B}}x_{\mathrm{Y}}$ satisfies

$$
\dot V = (a - b)\,V\left[1 - 3(x_{\mathrm{O}}x_{\mathrm{B}} + x_{\mathrm{B}}x_{\mathrm{Y}} + x_{\mathrm{Y}}x_{\mathrm{O}})\right].
$$
Hence if $a = b$ every interior trajectory is a closed orbit around the rest point; if $a > b$ every interior trajectory converges to it; and if $a < b$ every interior trajectory other than the rest point spirals out toward the boundary, where each strategy in turn is nearly fixed.

**Proof** Order the strategies O, B, Y. The payoff matrix is
$$
A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & a & -b \\ -b & 0 & a \\ a & -b & 0 \end{bmatrix}.
$$
Along solutions, $\dot V/V = \sum_i \dot x_i/x_i = \sum_i (A\boldsymbol{x})_i - 3\,\boldsymbol{x}^{\mathsf T}A\boldsymbol{x}$. Each column of $A$ contains one $a$ and one $-b$, so $\sum_i (A\boldsymbol{x})_i = (a - b)\sum_j x_j = a - b$. The quadratic form is $\boldsymbol{x}^{\mathsf T}A\boldsymbol{x} = (a - b)(x_{\mathrm{O}}x_{\mathrm{B}} + x_{\mathrm{B}}x_{\mathrm{Y}} + x_{\mathrm{Y}}x_{\mathrm{O}})$, which gives the formula. On the simplex, $x_{\mathrm{O}}x_{\mathrm{B}} + x_{\mathrm{B}}x_{\mathrm{Y}} + x_{\mathrm{Y}}x_{\mathrm{O}} = \tfrac12[1 - (x_{\mathrm{O}}^2 + x_{\mathrm{B}}^2 + x_{\mathrm{Y}}^2)] \leq \tfrac13$, with equality only at the center, so the bracket is positive away from the center. If $a = b$, $V$ is constant and its level sets are closed curves around the center. If $a > b$, $V$ increases strictly along every non-stationary interior trajectory, and since $V$ is maximal at the center, the trajectory converges to it ($V$ is a Lyapunov function, [2.4.11](/2.4.11)). If $a < b$, $V$ decreases, and trajectories approach the boundary. The rest point is interior because, at $(\tfrac13, \tfrac13, \tfrac13)$, all strategies earn $(a - b)/3$; it is unique because $A\boldsymbol{x}$ has equal entries only there. $\square$

The theorem shows that cycles are expected whenever each morph is vulnerable to another, and that whether they damp out or grow depends on whether the gain from winning exceeds the loss from losing. In real populations, with finite numbers, overlapping generations and density effects, the cycles neither persist exactly nor grow without limit, but the order of succession (orange, then yellow, then blue, then orange) is a robust prediction that matched the lizard data. Lizard morphs with similar color polymorphisms in other populations and species have been reported to lose one morph or to settle at stable frequencies, as expected if the payoffs differ between environments.

## 28.4. Sperm Competition

### 28.4.1. Competition After Mating

Darwin's theory concerned competition for mates. A century later, Geoff Parker (1970), watching male yellow dung flies (*Scathophaga stercoraria*) fighting over females at cowpats, realized that sexual selection must continue after mating. A female often mated with several males and stored their sperm in her spermathecae, so the ejaculates of the different males competed to fertilize her eggs, a process he named **sperm competition** ([7.10.13](/7.10.13#def-13.2.2)). As with competition before mating, there are two processes after mating: competition between the sperm of rival males, analogous to male–male combat, and **cryptic female choice** ([7.10.13](/7.10.13#rem-13.2.1)), analogous to mate choice.

The outcome of sperm competition is measured by the proportion of offspring fathered by the last of two males to mate with a female, $P_2$. Parker estimated it with the sterile-male technique: one of the two males is irradiated, so that eggs he fertilizes do not hatch. In the dung fly $P_2$ is about 0.8, and it varies with the duration of copulation, which gave Parker the data for an optimality model of how long a male should copulate ([7.18.22](/7.18.22)). In many insects $P_2$ is high (last-male precedence); in birds it is also usually above one-half, because the sperm of the last mating before an egg is fertilized are most numerous in the female's storage tubules; in some mammals the first male has the advantage. Molecular parentage has since shown how common multiple paternity is: in socially monogamous songbirds, extra-pair offspring have been found in nearly 90 percent of the species studied ([7.18.30](/7.18.30)). Why females mate with several males, when one mating often provides enough sperm, is considered in [7.18.29](/7.18.29).

### 28.4.2. Adaptations of Males

Sperm competition has favored a remarkable range of male adaptations, which fall into two groups: those that reduce the chance that the female mates again, and those that improve a male's success when she does.

- *Sperm removal.* In the black-winged damselfly *Calopteryx maculata*, males defend territories with floating vegetation where females lay eggs, and a female often mates with several territory owners. Copulating damselflies hold the "wheel" position, with the female's genital opening placed against the male's secondary genitalia at the base of his abdomen. The male first pumps his abdomen rhythmically while the backward-pointing spines and lateral horns on his penis scrub out the contents of the female's sperm storage organs, and only then releases his own sperm, which he had transferred earlier from the testes at the tip of his abdomen. Jon Waage (1979) found that males removed 90–100 percent of rival sperm. Other odonates displace rather than remove rival sperm, pushing it to the back of the storage organs.
- *Copulatory plugs.* Males of many insects, spiders, snakes, rodents and primates seal the female's genital opening with a secretion after mating. In some spiders the male breaks off a part of his copulatory organ in the female's genital opening. In the extreme case of the orb-weaving spider *Argiope aurantia*, the male dies during his second insertion and his body remains attached to the female, blocking access for other males (Foellmer and Fairbairn 2003). Such extreme commitment is expected where the chance of finding another female is very low.
- *Ejaculate size and testis size.* A male that cannot prevent competition can outnumber his rivals' sperm. Across primates, species in which females mate with several males in each estrus (chimpanzees, macaques) have much larger testes for their body size than monogamous species or species with single-male harems (gorillas) (Harcourt and colleagues 1981). Similar relationships between the risk of sperm competition and testis size have been found in birds, bats, butterflies and fishes; in a comparison of 89 species of fishes, testis size increased with the estimated intensity of sperm competition (Stockley and colleagues 1997).
- *Sperm quality and cooperation.* In primates and rodents, species with promiscuous females have longer, faster sperm. In the wood mouse (*Apodemus sylvaticus*), sperm attach to each other in "trains" of hundreds of cells that swim faster than single sperm (Moore and colleagues 2002). In *Peromyscus* mice, sperm of the promiscuous deer mouse (*P. maniculatus*) aggregate more often, and preferentially with sperm of the same male, than sperm of the monogamous oldfield mouse (*P. polionotus*) (Fisher and Hoekstra 2010).
- *Chemical manipulation.* The seminal fluid of male *Drosophila* contains accessory gland proteins that reduce the female's receptivity to further matings and incapacitate rival sperm; their costs to females are discussed with sexual conflict in [7.18.29](/7.18.29).

### 28.4.3. How Much to Invest in an Ejaculate

If a male's reproductive budget is limited, every unit spent on sperm is a unit not spent on finding further females. Parker (1990) analyzed the trade-off with "raffle" models, in which the eggs of a female are shared among the males that mated with her in proportion to the numbers of sperm they transferred, like tickets in a raffle. A simple version shows the main result.

**Proposition 28.4.1 (ESS Ejaculate Expenditure)** A male with a fixed reproductive budget pays $c$ to obtain each mating (searching, courting) and $s$ for the ejaculate, so that he obtains a number of matings proportional to $1/(s + c)$. With probability $q$ (the *risk* of sperm competition) the female also mates with one rival, who spends $s^*$, and the eggs are shared in proportion $s/(s + s^*)$; otherwise the male fertilizes all her eggs. The evolutionarily stable expenditure is

$$
s^* = \frac{qc}{4 - 3q},
$$
and the fraction of the reproductive budget spent on ejaculates is $s^*/(s^* + c) = q/(4 - 2q)$.

**Proof** The fitness of a mutant spending $s$ in a population spending $s^*$ is proportional to $W(s) = [(1 - q) + qs/(s + s^*)]/(s + c)$. At the ESS, $s^*$ must be the best reply to itself, so $\mathrm{d}\ln W/\mathrm{d}s = 0$ at $s = s^*$. Differentiating,

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}\ln W}{\mathrm{d}s} = \frac{qs^*/(s + s^*)^2}{(1 - q) + qs/(s + s^*)} - \frac{1}{s + c},
$$
and at $s = s^*$ the first term is $\dfrac{q/(4s^*)}{1 - q/2}$. Setting it equal to $1/(s^* + c)$ gives $q(s^* + c) = s^*(4 - 2q)$, that is $s^* = qc/(4 - 3q)$. The second derivative is negative there, so this is a maximum. Finally $s^*/(s^* + c) = q/(q + 4 - 3q) = q/(4 - 2q)$. $\square$

The investment rises steeply with the risk of sperm competition, as the comparative data on testis size suggest, and when every mating is contested ($q = 1$) a male should spend as much on sperm as on obtaining the mating. Within species, males also adjust ejaculates to the perceived risk: male field crickets and many other insects transfer more sperm when a rival is present during mating, and male fowl transfer more sperm to attractive, high-quality females. More detailed models distinguish the *risk* of competition (the probability that a female mates with more than one male) from its *intensity* (the number of competing ejaculates), and predict that ejaculate size should increase with risk but decrease again when the number of competitors becomes large, since the return from each extra sperm then falls.

### 28.4.4. Mate Guarding

The other way to win sperm competition is to prevent it.

**Definition 28.4.1 (Mate Guarding)** **Mate guarding** is behavior by which a male stays close to a female, before or after copulation, and prevents other males from mating with her during the period in which she can be fertilized. *Precopulatory* guarding secures a female before she becomes receptive (common in crustaceans, in which females can mate only just after molting); *postcopulatory* guarding protects the male's sperm from being displaced or diluted.

Guarding is costly: a guarding male is not looking for other females, feeds less and fights more. Males of the western Mexican whiptail lizard (*Aspidoscelis costata*) that guard mates forage less (Ancona and colleagues 2010), and male New Zealand stitchbirds (*Notiomystis cincta*) lose about 4 percent of their body mass while chasing rivals from a fertile partner (Low 2006). Guarding should therefore evolve only when its benefits exceed these costs.

**Example 28.4.1 (Guarding in the Blue Milkweed Beetle)** Males of the blue milkweed beetle (*Chrysochus cobaltinus*) remain mounted on a female's back for some time after copulation. Janis Dickinson (1995) separated pairs after mating. Within 30 minutes about 25 percent of the separated males had found a new mate, the cost of guarding; and about 50 percent of the separated females had mated with another male, which shows what guarding prevents. Suppose a male that leaves finds a new female with probability $m$ during the time he would otherwise guard, an unguarded female remates with probability $r$, and the second male then fertilizes a fraction $P_2$ of her eggs. Measured in units of one female's eggs, a guarding male obtains 1. A male that leaves keeps $1 - rP_2$ of the eggs of his first female and obtains, with probability $m$, a second female worth $1 - rP_2$ to him, since she too may remate. Guarding pays if

$$
1 > (1 - rP_2)(1 + m), \qquad\text{that is}\qquad P_2 > \frac{m}{r(1 + m)}.
$$
With $m = 0.25$ and $r = 0.5$, the threshold is $P_2 = 0.25/0.625 = 0.4$: guarding is favored if the second male fertilizes more than 40 percent of the eggs. This is the value Dickinson obtained, and it is lower than $P_2$ in most insects that have been measured.

The benefit of guarding was demonstrated experimentally in the Seychelles warbler (*Acrocephalus sechellensis*), in which a male normally follows his mate closely during her fertile period before she lays her single egg. Jan Komdeur and colleagues (1999) placed a model egg in nests a few days before the female was due to lay, and the males, taking it as a cue that the fertile period was over, stopped guarding early. Intrusions by neighboring males and extra-pair copulations increased at once, and the probability that a nestling was sired by another male increased with the number of days the female had been left unguarded (Komdeur and colleagues 2007). Guarding is also flexible: males guard more intensely when rivals are numerous and when the female is most fertile.

Birds that cannot guard continuously, because both partners must forage or because the male must defend the nest, often use another form of paternity assurance: frequent copulation. In some raptors, in which the male hunts away from the nest while the female stays at it, pairs copulate hundreds of times per clutch, far more than needed for fertilization, which is thought to swamp the sperm of any extra-pair partner. Males also respond to the risk of losing paternity by reducing their care, which links sperm competition to the evolution of paternal care ([7.18.31](/7.18.31)).

The most extreme form of male–male competition over reproduction is infanticide. Male Hanuman langurs and lions that take over a group often kill the unweaned young sired by their predecessors, which removes the offspring of rivals and brings the mothers back into breeding condition sooner; the evidence that this behavior is sexually selected rather than pathological was examined in [7.18.1](/7.18.1#sec-1.4.3). Females in turn have evolved counterstrategies, such as mating with several males to confuse paternity, which are part of the conflict between the sexes treated in the next chapter.

## Summary

- Sexual selection (Definition 28.1.1) has an intrasexual component (competition) and an intersexual component (choice), both before and after mating:

  | | Competition | Choice |
  |---|---|---|
  | Before mating | Fighting, scrambles, endurance, coercion (Section 28.2) | Mate choice ([7.18.29](/7.18.29)) |
  | After mating | Sperm competition (Section 28.4) | Cryptic female choice ([7.18.29](/7.18.29)) |

- Anisogamy and unequal parental investment give the less investing sex a higher potential reproductive rate. Bateman gradients are steeper in males in most species despite flaws in Bateman's original data. The opportunity for sexual selection $I_s$ (Formula 28.1.1) bounds the strength of selection on mating success; the operational sex ratio (Formula 28.1.2) depends on the time each sex spends out of the mating pool.
- Sperm are cheap individually but not in quantity; males allocate ejaculates prudently. Sex-role reversal occurs where males invest more (pipefish, katydids when food is scarce, gobies late in the season, phalaropes, dance flies), as the theory predicts. Paternity uncertainty and skewed mating success make care less profitable for males (Queller).
- Contests favor large male size and weapons (elephant seals, dung beetles); dominance brings paternity in baboons, but subordinates compensate by friendships and coalitions.
- Alternative tactics may be tactics of a conditional strategy (scorpionflies, marine iguanas, dung beetles), with an ESS switch point where the marginal male does equally well with both (Proposition 28.3.1) and unequal average payoffs; or genetic strategies with equal payoffs (isopods, ruffs) or cycling frequencies (side-blotched lizards, Theorem 28.3.1). Bluegill cuckolders and parentals are alternative life histories maintained by frequency dependence.
- Sperm competition (last-male precedence, $P_2$) has favored sperm removal (damselflies), plugs, large testes where females are promiscuous, and ejaculate investment rising with risk (Proposition 28.4.1). Mate guarding (Definition 28.4.1) pays when last males fertilize enough eggs (Example 28.4.1: $P_2 > 0.4$ in milkweed beetles) and protects paternity in Seychelles warblers.

## Questions

1. In the Mormon cricket, sex roles reverse at high population density. Using Formula 28.1.2, explain why, and predict how the operational sex ratio and the direction of mate choice should change if the crickets are given abundant protein-rich food.

   **Answer** At high density food is scarce, males need a long time to produce the large spermatophore (high male time out $t_{\mathrm{m}}$), while females can produce eggs and are ready to remate; when $t_{\mathrm{m}} > t_{\mathrm{f}}$ the OSR falls below 1, females are in excess, compete for males, and males choose heavy, fecund females. With abundant food, males make spermatophores faster, $t_{\mathrm{m}}$ falls, the OSR becomes male-biased again, and the normal roles should return: males compete (calling, approaching females) and females choose. This is what was found in *Kawanaphila* katydids when pollen became abundant, and in experiments with Mormon crickets given extra food.
2. A biologist finds that small male frogs that act as satellites beside large callers have lower average mating success than callers. Does this show that satellite behavior is maladaptive? What observations or experiments would distinguish a conditional strategy from a genetic polymorphism?

   **Answer** No. If calling and satelliting are tactics of a conditional strategy, satellites are the males for which calling would pay even less ("best of a bad job"), and the average payoffs need not be equal (Proposition 28.3.1). Tests: (1) remove callers and see whether satellites start calling (as in Thornhill's removal experiment with scorpionflies); (2) manipulate the condition (for example, feed some males) and see whether the tactic changes; (3) check whether individuals switch tactics over a season or a lifetime; (4) estimate heritability of the tactic in breeding experiments. A genetic polymorphism would show fixed tactics, heritability, and equal average fitness at the stable frequencies.
3. Using Theorem 28.3.1, explain why the frequencies of the three morphs of the side-blotched lizard are expected to cycle, and in which order a population dominated by orange males should change.

   **Answer** No morph is an ESS: whichever morph is common is beaten by another morph that can invade it, so the frequencies keep changing. When orange is common, yellow sneakers do well (they steal matings on the large orange territories), so yellow increases next; when yellow is common, blue mate-guarders do well, so blue increases; and when blue is common, orange takes over blue territories. The order is therefore orange → yellow → blue → orange. Whether the cycles damp out, persist or grow depends on whether the gain of winning exceeds the loss of losing ($a > b$, $a = b$ or $a < b$).
4. Derive the guarding threshold of Example 28.4.1 and use it to predict how guarding should change (a) with an increasing density of receptive females; (b) in species with first-male sperm precedence; (c) when females become more likely to remate.

   **Answer** Guarding gives the male all of one female's eggs; leaving gives $(1 - rP_2)$ of her eggs plus, with probability $m$, another female worth $(1 - rP_2)$, so guarding pays when $1 > (1 - rP_2)(1 + m)$, that is $P_2 > m/[r(1 + m)]$. (a) More receptive females raise $m$ and the threshold, so males should guard less and search more. (b) With first-male precedence $P_2$ is small, so postcopulatory guarding is rarely worthwhile (precopulatory guarding of virgin females would be favored instead). (c) A higher $r$ lowers the threshold, so guarding should increase.
5. Across primate species, relative testis size is larger in species with multimale groups than in monogamous species. Explain this with Proposition 28.4.1, and describe two problems in interpreting such a comparison and how they can be addressed.

   **Answer** In multimale groups, females in estrus usually mate with several males, so the risk $q$ of sperm competition is high, and the ESS investment in sperm increases steeply with $q$; larger testes produce more sperm. Problems: (1) species are not independent data points, because related species share traits by common descent; this is addressed by phylogenetic comparative methods such as independent contrasts (7.10.26). (2) Testis size scales with body size, so the comparison must use testis size relative to body size (residuals from an allometric regression); other confounding variables, such as the number of matings per female or breeding seasonality, must be measured or controlled.
6. (Open question) In bluegill sunfish, design a study to test whether cuckolders and parental males have equal lifetime reproductive success, and explain what result would support a conditional strategy and what result a genetic polymorphism.

   **Answer** Mark large numbers of juvenile males and follow them through life (or reconstruct life histories from age structure), recording survival to each age and, for each reproductive bout, paternity of eggs in nests by genotyping fry. Combine survival and fertilization success into lifetime reproductive success for each life history, and repeat over years with different frequencies of cuckolders to test whether the success of cuckolders declines when they are common (frequency dependence). In parallel, rear offspring of known parents under common conditions to test whether the life history is heritable, and manipulate early growth (food) to test whether it is condition-dependent. Equal lifetime success with heritable life histories and negative frequency dependence would support a genetic polymorphism; unequal success with life history set by early growth and no heritability would support a conditional strategy; intermediate results (heritable thresholds that respond to condition) would indicate a conditional strategy whose switch point varies genetically.
