Every year, billions of animals travel between places that are good at different times. More than a million wildebeest follow the rains around the Serengeti, Arctic terns fly from the Arctic to the Antarctic and back, eels cross the Atlantic to spawn in the Sargasso Sea, monarch butterflies fly from Canada to the mountains of central Mexico, and zooplankton in every ocean rise hundreds of meters to the surface each night and sink again at dawn. Migration is one of the great phenomena of the living world, and it raises questions at every level of explanation: what triggers it, how animals fuel journeys that in some cases last days without food or rest, how they find their way, how the behavior is inherited, how it evolved, and how it can be protected when the places that migrants depend on are thousands of kilometers apart.

This chapter treats migration as a behavior and as part of a life history. Section 20.1 defines migration and its types; 20.2 surveys migrants across the animal kingdom; 20.3 describes how migrations are tracked; 20.4 analyzes costs and benefits, including fuel and the decisions of stopover; 20.5 the variation and genetics of migratory behavior; 20.6 its evolution; and 20.7 the threats to migrants and their conservation. The orientation and navigation mechanisms that guide migrants follow in [7.18.21](/7.18.21). The patterns of bird migration, the power curve of flight and the flyways of China are described in [7.17.53](/7.17.53#sec-53.10), insect migration in [7.17.29](/7.17.29#sec-29.8), and the annual timing of migration by photoperiod and circannual clocks in [7.18.18](/7.18.18#sec-18.5). In population genetics, "migration" means gene flow between populations ([7.10](/7.10)); here it means the movement of animals.

## 20.1. What Is Migration?

### 20.1.1. Migration, Dispersal and Dormancy

Animals that live in a seasonal environment have two ways to survive the bad season: to wait in place in a state of reduced activity, or to move to a better place. The first includes **diapause** in insects ([7.17.29](/7.17.29#sec-29.7)), **torpor** and **hibernation** in mammals and birds, and aestivation in the dry season; the second includes migration and dispersal.

**Definition 20.1.1 (Migration)** **Migration** is persistent, directed movement of an animal, made by its own locomotion or by active use of currents of air or water, that carries it between regions used at different times, typically in a regular, often seasonal, round trip between breeding and nonbreeding areas. During migration the animal is in a distinct behavioral and physiological state: it is not distracted by resources and stimuli that would stop or redirect routine movements, and the movement is preceded and followed by specific changes in behavior and physiology.

The definition combines the behavioral view of John Kennedy and Hugh Dingle, which emphasizes the state of the migrant (straightened-out, undistracted movement, as in the definition of **insect migration** in [7.17.29](/7.17.29#def-29.8.1)), with the ecological view of a return movement between areas. **Dispersal** ([7.10.10](/7.10.10)) differs from migration in direction and in consequence: it is movement away from the birthplace or a previous breeding site to the place where the animal will breed, usually one way and not in a common direction for the whole population ([7.18.26](/7.18.26)). Migration is directional and, for most migrants, repeated. Routine movements within a home range, such as foraging trips, are neither.

The round trip may span one individual's lifetime or several generations:

- *Repeated round trips*: most migratory birds, bats, whales, sea turtles and ungulates make the journey every year, and adults can return to the same breeding and wintering sites year after year.
- *Once-in-a-lifetime round trips*: Pacific salmon (*Oncorhynchus*) migrate to the sea as juveniles and return once, years later, to spawn and die in their natal stream; European eels (*Anguilla anguilla*) grow for years in fresh water and make one journey to the Sargasso Sea to spawn.
- *Multigenerational cycles*: the eastern population of the monarch butterfly (*Danaus plexippus*) migrates south in autumn as a single generation in reproductive diapause, but the return northward in spring and summer takes three or four generations, so that no individual completes the cycle; the painted lady (*Vanessa cardui*) migrates between Africa and Europe in six or more generations.

### 20.1.2. Types of Migration

Migrations are classified by their geometry:

| Type | Movement | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Latitudinal | Between high-latitude breeding areas and low-latitude nonbreeding areas | Most migratory birds; blue whales; monarchs |
| Longitudinal | Mainly east–west, often between continental interiors and coasts or across oceans | Green turtles between Brazil and Ascension Island; many Asian waterfowl; some Eurasian passerines |
| Altitudinal | Between high and low elevations of mountains | Many tropical birds (three-wattled bellbird); Dall's and thinhorn sheep, elk; Himalayan pheasants |
| Diel vertical | Daily, between deep water by day and surface water by night | Copepods, krill, lanternfishes: the largest daily movement of biomass on Earth |
| Breeding (reproductive) | Between feeding habitats and breeding sites | Toads and newts to ponds; salmon and eels; Christmas Island red crabs to the sea |

Other distinctions are made by the proportion of the population that moves. In *partial migration*, some individuals of a population migrate and others stay (common blackbirds, many fishes and ungulates); in *differential migration*, the sexes or age classes migrate different distances (in many birds, females and juveniles winter farther south than adult males); *irruptions* (crossbills, waxwings, nutcrackers) and *nomadism* (zebra finches, many desert birds) are irregular movements in response to unpredictable food.

Diel vertical migration is an antipredator behavior: phytoplankton and the small animals that feed on it are concentrated near the surface, where light allows visual predators (fishes) to hunt, so zooplankton feed at night and hide in the dark at depth by day. The cost is the energy of swimming and the lower temperature and food at depth. In lakes, *Daphnia* migrate vertically only when fish are present, and chemical cues from fish (kairomones) induce the behavior (Dodson 1988); in the Southern Ocean, Antarctic krill (*Euphausia superba*) migrate regularly in spring and autumn, when days and nights alternate, but less in the near-continuous daylight of midsummer (Bahlburg and colleagues 2023).

## 20.2. Migrants Across the Animal Kingdom

### 20.2.1. Mammals

- *Wildebeest.* About 1.3 million wildebeest (*Connochaetes taurinus*), with several hundred thousand plains zebras and Thomson's gazelles, move each year between the short-grass plains of the southern Serengeti, where they calve in the wet season, and the woodlands of the north and the Maasai Mara in Kenya in the dry season. Their movements track the rains and grass growth; the timing of the departure north is predicted by the rise in salinity of the remaining water holes (Wolanski and colleagues 1999). GPS-collared animals showed that wildebeest move in response to the quality of forage and largely ignore predators, whereas zebras respond to both forage and predation risk (Hopcraft and colleagues 2014).
- *Blue whales.* Blue whales (*Balaenoptera musculus*) and other baleen whales feed in summer on krill in productive cold waters at high latitudes and migrate thousands of kilometers to low latitudes, where they give birth and where the young grow without the cost of thermoregulation in cold water, and with fewer killer whales.
- *Bats.* Mexican free-tailed bats (*Tadarida brasiliensis*) migrate from wintering areas in Mexico to maternity caves in Texas and the southwestern United States, where colonies of millions form in summer (Bracken Cave holds perhaps 15–20 million bats); nightly, they consume large quantities of agricultural pest moths. Several European bats, such as Nathusius's pipistrelle, migrate more than 1 000 km between summer and winter roosts.

### 20.2.2. Birds

About 40 % of the world's roughly 11 000 bird species are regular migrants (7.17.53). Some of the most studied cases from East Asia and elsewhere:

- *Barn swallow.* The barn swallow (*Hirundo rustica*) breeds across the Northern Hemisphere in six or more subspecies. In Gansu province of China, the western subspecies *H. r. rustica* and the eastern subspecies *H. r. gutturalis* meet in a hybrid zone. Tracking with light-level geolocators showed that birds from the western, *rustica*-like populations near Jiuquan and Zhangye and birds from the eastern, *gutturalis*-like population near Lanzhou take different routes around the Tibetan Plateau to different wintering areas: the hybrid zone coincides with a *migratory divide*, a boundary between populations with different migratory directions (Turbek and colleagues 2022). Hybrids at such divides may inherit intermediate directions that lead them over inhospitable terrain, a form of selection against hybrids that can maintain the divergence between subspecies. In the Southern Hemisphere, barn swallows from North America began to breed in Argentina only in the 1980s, and this new population now migrates within South America (Winkler and colleagues 2017).
- *Beijing swift.* The Beijing population of the common swift (*Apus apus pekinensis*), which nests in the gate towers and roofs of the old buildings of Beijing, was tracked with geolocators from 2014. The birds winter in southern Africa, travelling through Central Asia and the Arabian Peninsula, a round trip of about 30 000 km; like other common swifts, they stay airborne for most of the nonbreeding season.
- *Bar-headed goose.* Bar-headed geese (*Anser indicus*) breed on the high lakes of Central Asia and the Tibetan Plateau and winter in South Asia; they cross the Himalaya in a single day, climbing from near sea level to over 5 000 m, and occasionally above 7 000 m (Hawkes and colleagues 2011; Bishop and colleagues 2015). They fly mostly at night and in the morning, when the air is cool and dense, following the terrain rather than staying at constant high altitude. Their physiology is adapted to hypoxia: hemoglobin with a higher oxygen affinity, owing to an amino acid substitution in the α chain, larger lungs and more effective ventilation, and denser capillaries and more mitochondria in the flight muscles (Scott and colleagues 2015).
- *Arctic tern.* Arctic terns (*Sterna paradisaea*) breeding in Greenland and Iceland flew an average of about 70 000 km a year, from the Arctic to the Weddell Sea and back, following an S-shaped route over the Atlantic that uses prevailing winds (Egevang and colleagues 2010); birds from the Netherlands travelled about 90 000 km (Fijn and colleagues 2013).
- *Bar-tailed godwit.* Bar-tailed godwits of the subspecies *Limosa lapponica baueri* fly in autumn nonstop from western Alaska across the Pacific Ocean to New Zealand and eastern Australia, without feeding, drinking or (as far as is known) sleeping. In 2021 an adult male tagged 4BBRW flew about 13 000 km in 239 hours; in 2022 a juvenile flew 13 560 km in 11 days from Alaska to Tasmania, the longest nonstop flight recorded for any bird. In spring the same birds return in two steps, staging for five to six weeks in the tidal flats of the Yellow Sea.

### 20.2.3. Reptiles, Amphibians and Fishes

- *Sea turtles.* Leatherback turtles (*Dermochelys coriacea*) are the most wide-ranging reptiles: a female tracked from her nesting beach in Papua, Indonesia, swam about 20 500 km across the Pacific to feeding grounds off Oregon over 647 days (Benson and colleagues 2007). Green turtles (*Chelonia mydas*) that feed on sea-grass pastures off the coast of Brazil nest on Ascension Island, a speck of land 2 200 km away in the middle of the Atlantic, and find it again after years at sea ([7.18.21](/7.18.21)).
- *Common toad.* In early spring, common toads (*Bufo bufo*) migrate from their wintering sites in woodland to the ponds where they breed, often the ponds where they were born, and back to the woods after spawning. Such breeding migrations bring thousands of toads across roads in a few nights, and tunnels under roads and temporary fences are used to protect them.
- *Blue shark.* Blue sharks (*Prionace glauca*) make some of the longest migrations of any fish, around the North Atlantic following the Gulf Stream; males and females segregate, and females move between mating areas and pupping areas on different sides of the ocean basin.
- *Sockeye salmon.* Sockeye salmon (*Oncorhynchus nerka*) of the Fraser River in British Columbia spend two to three years at sea, then return to their natal streams, swimming up to 1 000 km or more upriver without feeding; they spawn and die (**semelparity**). Smolts migrating to the sea suffer heavy predation: in one population 32 % died along a 14-km stretch of river, and smolts there migrate at night and in large groups that swamp predators (Furey and colleagues 2016).

### 20.2.4. Invertebrates

- *Monarch butterfly.* In autumn, monarchs of eastern North America fly up to about 4 000 km to a few overwintering sites in oyamel fir forests at 3 000 m altitude in central Mexico, where tens of millions cluster on trees in reproductive diapause. The microclimate under the dense canopy keeps them cool, so they conserve their fat reserves, and protects them from freezing; thinning the forest exposes them to wetting and freezing, and storms have killed millions in a night (Anderson and Brower 1996). Monarchs migrate south using a time-compensated sun compass ([7.18.18](/7.18.18#sec-18.6.2)) and a magnetic compass.
- *Christmas Island red crab.* At the start of the wet season, tens of millions of red crabs (*Gecarcoidea natalis*) of Christmas Island in the Indian Ocean migrate from the forest to the coast, mate, and the females release their larvae into the sea at the turn of the high tide before dawn during the last quarter of the moon, a timing that combines the seasonal rains, the lunar cycle and the tides ([7.18.18](/7.18.18#sec-18.4.3)).
- *Antarctic krill* and other zooplankton migrate vertically every day (20.1.2).
- *Spiny lobsters.* In autumn, after the first strong storm, Caribbean spiny lobsters (*Panulirus argus*) of the Bahamas walk in single-file queues of dozens of animals, each touching the one in front, from shallow to deeper water; the queue reduces drag and predation (Herrnkind 1969).
- *Insects.* Migration by insects is the largest movement of terrestrial animals: about 3.5 trillion insects migrate each year at high altitude over southern Britain alone (Hu and colleagues 2016). Its physiology and the migrant pests of East Asia are described in [7.17.29](/7.17.29#sec-29.8).

## 20.3. Tracking Migrants

How do we know where migrants go? Until recently, the routes of most migrants were inferred from sightings and from recoveries of marked animals. Each method answers different questions:

| Method | Principle | Suited to | Limitations |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ringing (banding) | Numbered rings (since Mortensen, Denmark, 1899) recovered or resighted | Large samples, survival estimates | Recovery rates often below 1 %; only start and end points |
| Light-level geolocators | Day length gives latitude, time of noon gives longitude; logged and retrieved | Small birds (tags below 1 g) | Accuracy about 150–200 km; useless near the equinoxes for latitude; tag must be recovered |
| GPS loggers and satellite tags | Positions from GPS satellites, transmitted via Argos or mobile networks | Large animals: storks, raptors, geese, turtles, whales | Mass of tag (rule of thumb: below 3–5 % of body mass) |
| Automated radio telemetry | Networks of receivers detect coded radio tags (the Motus network) | Small birds, bats, insects at stopovers | Only near receivers |
| Radar | Weather and dedicated radars detect flocks and individual large insects | Numbers, altitude, timing, speed of nocturnal migration | Species identity unknown |
| Stable isotopes | Ratios of $^2\mathrm{H}$ / $^1\mathrm{H}$ in feathers reflect precipitation of the region where they grew | Linking breeding and wintering populations | Coarse resolution |
| Genetic markers | Assignment of individuals to breeding populations | Migratory connectivity | Requires differentiated populations |

The term *migratory connectivity* describes how strongly the individuals of particular breeding populations are linked to particular nonbreeding areas. Stable hydrogen isotopes in the feathers of black-throated blue warblers (*Setophaga caerulescens*), which are grown in North America after breeding, showed that birds wintering in the western Caribbean come from the north of the breeding range and those wintering in the eastern Caribbean from the southern Appalachians (Rubenstein and colleagues 2002). Strong connectivity has consequences for conservation: geolocators showed that golden-winged warblers (*Vermivora chrysoptera*) of the declining Appalachian population winter in northern South America, where forest loss has been severe, whereas populations wintering in Central America are stable (Kramer and colleagues 2018). The threat to a breeding population can lie on another continent.

## 20.4. Costs and Benefits

### 20.4.1. Benefits

Migration pays when it gives access to resources that are abundant in different places at different times. Insectivorous birds breeding in the north of the temperate zone exploit a summer flush of insects and long days for foraging, and leave before the insects disappear. Wildebeest follow the rains; whales exploit the summer blooms of krill; salmon grow at sea and spawn in streams that are safe for eggs. Migration can also reduce predation and parasitism, for example by moving away from areas where parasites accumulate ("migratory escape") and by culling infected individuals that cannot complete the journey ("migratory culling"; Altizer, Bartel and Han 2011): monarchs infected with the protozoan parasite *Ophryocystis elektroscirrha* fly less well, and migratory populations have lower infection rates than resident ones. And it can reduce competition: in partially migratory common blackbirds, migrants had a 16 % higher probability of surviving the winter than residents in one study (Zúñiga and colleagues 2017).

### 20.4.2. Costs and Fuel

The costs are energetic, temporal and in risk of death. Raptors tracked by satellite died at six times the rate during migration as during stationary periods, and more than half of their annual mortality occurred on migration (Klaassen and colleagues 2014).

Most birds fuel migration with fat, which yields about $39\,\mathrm{kJ\,g^{-1}}$, eight to ten times more than glycogen or protein stored with water. Before departure, migrants become hyperphagic and deposit fat at rates of a few percent of lean mass per day; small songbirds that cross the Sahara or the Gulf of Mexico roughly double their mass, and bar-tailed godwits before the Pacific crossing carry fat amounting to about 55 % of their body mass. The body is remodeled: before their nonstop flight, godwits shrink their gizzard, intestine, liver and kidneys, organs useless in flight, and enlarge their flight muscles and heart (Piersma and Gill 1998); after arrival the digestive organs are rebuilt. Red knots staging in Delaware Bay on their way from southern South America to the Arctic gain more than 70 g on top of a lean mass of about 110 g in two weeks by eating the eggs of horseshoe crabs; when overharvesting reduced the horseshoe crabs by 90 %, the red knots using the bay declined by about 75 %, and knots that left lighter were less likely to return (Baker and colleagues 2004; Niles and colleagues 2009).

Migrants reduce the costs of flight by behavior. Large birds fly in V-formation: great white pelicans flying behind others in a V had heart rates 11–14 % lower than when flying alone (Weimerskirch and colleagues 2001), and northern bald ibises in a V position themselves and phase their wingbeats to exploit the upwash of the bird ahead (Portugal and colleagues 2014). Storks, raptors and cranes soar in thermals and glide between them, and therefore avoid crossing seas, concentrating at narrow crossings such as Gibraltar and the Bosporus. Many songbirds migrate at night, when the air is cooler and calmer and predators fewer, and feed by day. Migrants select altitudes and nights with favorable winds; flying at the maximum-range speed of the power curve ([7.17.53](/7.17.53#sec-53.5.2)) minimizes energy per unit distance.

### 20.4.3. Stopovers

**Definition 20.4.1 (Stopover Site)** A **stopover site** is a place where migrants interrupt migration to rest and to replenish their fuel before continuing. Sites that are used by large numbers of migrants for long refueling periods, often at the edge of an ecological barrier, are called *staging sites*.

A migrant spends far more time at stopovers than in flight: a songbird that migrates 4 000 km may fly for 100–150 hours but take 40–60 days, most of them spent refueling. How much fuel to take on, and when to leave, are therefore central decisions, and they can be analyzed with optimality models ([7.18.2](/7.18.2#def-2.4.1)). If the currency is the overall speed of migration (time minimization), the problem is formally the same as the **marginal value theorem** for a forager in a patch ([7.18.22](/7.18.22)).

**Example 20.4.1 (Optimal Departure Fuel Load)** Let the flight range gained from a fuel load $f$ (as a fraction of lean mass) be $r(f)$, increasing with diminishing returns because extra fuel makes the bird heavier and flight more expensive (a simple form is $r(f) = R\,(1 - 1/\sqrt{1 + f})$). At a stopover the bird spends a search and settling time $t_0$ before it starts to gain mass, then deposits fuel at a rate $k$ per day, so that after a total time $t_0 + f/k$ it has load $f$. The overall speed of migration is

$$
v(f) = \frac{r(f)}{t_0 + f/k},
\tag{20.1}
$$
and it is maximized where $\mathrm{d}v/\mathrm{d}f = 0$, that is, where $r'(f)\,(t_0 + f/k) = r(f)/k$: graphically, where a line from the point $(-t_0, 0)$ is tangent to the curve of range against stopover time. The model predicts that a bird should leave with more fuel the higher its fuel deposition rate $k$, and that a longer settling time $t_0$ also increases the optimal load (Alerstam and Lindström 1990). Birds that find a good site should stay longer and become fatter; birds at poor sites should leave early with small loads and try their luck at the next site.

B: the optimal fuel load at departure increases with the deposition rate, and so does the resulting speed of migration.](./plots/stopover-model.py)

Tests support the qualitative predictions: in several species, migrants given extra food at a stopover leave later with larger fuel loads, and migrants' departure decisions depend on fuel, wind and the barrier ahead. Red-eyed vireos (*Vireo olivaceus*) captured in autumn on the coast of Alabama were tested in orientation cages: lean birds, with less than about 5 g of fat, oriented west-northwest at dusk, along the coast, whereas fat birds oriented south, across the Gulf of Mexico (Sandberg and Moore 1996). Blackpoll warblers (*Setophaga striata*), weighing about 12 g, fatten in autumn in New England and the Canadian Maritimes and fly nonstop over the western Atlantic to the Caribbean and South America, 2 270–2 770 km in two to three days (DeLuca and colleagues 2015), departing with cold fronts that give them tailwinds. Migrants also trade time against energy and safety: many species avoid long sea crossings, and the songbirds of Europe funnel to the narrow Strait of Gibraltar rather than crossing the Mediterranean at its widest.

## 20.5. Variation and Genetics of Migratory Behavior

### 20.5.1. Migratory Restlessness

Caged migratory birds reveal their migratory program.

**Definition 20.5.1 (Migratory Restlessness)** **Migratory restlessness** (*Zugunruhe*) is the increased locomotor activity, typically nocturnal hopping and wing-whirring in nocturnal migrants, shown by caged migratory birds during the seasons of migration. Its timing, amount and direction correspond to the timing, distance and direction of migration in the wild population.

Eberhard Gwinner and Peter Berthold used migratory restlessness to analyze the program of warblers of the genus *Sylvia*. Hand-raised birds that had never migrated showed restlessness in autumn for a number of weeks that, across species and populations, was proportional to the length of their population's migration: long-distance migrants such as garden warblers were restless for longer than short-distance migrants, and the resident blackcaps of the Canary Islands showed little restlessness. Since a bird flies at a roughly constant speed, a program of "fly for $n$ weeks in direction $\theta$", the *vector navigation* or *clock-and-compass* program, would bring a naive bird to the wintering area of its population. The direction is measured in orientation cages ([7.18.21](/7.18.21)): garden warblers kept in cages in Germany changed the direction of their restlessness in autumn from southwest to south-southeast, as birds of their population do in the wild when they pass the Iberian Peninsula (Gwinner and Wiltschko 1978).

### 20.5.2. Inheritance of Direction and Distance

The program is largely inherited. Berthold and Querner (1981) crossed blackcaps (*Sylvia atricapilla*) from southern Germany, which migrate to the Mediterranean, with blackcaps from the Canary Islands, which are largely resident: the hybrids showed amounts of restlessness intermediate between their parents. Blackcaps from western Europe migrate southwest and those from eastern Austria southeast, around the Alps and the Mediterranean; hybrids of the two oriented about south (Helbig 1991). Partial migration is also heritable: selective breeding from a partially migratory population in southern France produced an almost completely migratory line within three generations and a nearly resident line within six (Berthold and colleagues 1990). Quantitative genetic analyses give heritabilities of migratory activity and its timing of about 0.4–0.6; Pulido and colleagues (1996) modelled migratoriness as a threshold trait, in which an underlying continuous liability, with a threshold, decides whether an individual migrates or stays.

The heritability of the program makes rapid evolution possible, and the blackcap provides the best example. Since the 1960s, increasing numbers of blackcaps from central Europe winter in Britain and Ireland, northwest of their breeding grounds, instead of in Iberia. Berthold and colleagues (1992) caught blackcaps wintering in Britain, bred them in Germany and tested their hand-raised offspring in orientation cages: the young oriented west-northwest, like their parents, whereas young of birds from the same breeding area wintering in Spain oriented southwest. The new direction was heritable and had spread in about 30 years. British winters had become milder, garden bird feeders supply food, and the shorter route allows birds wintering in Britain to return earlier to the breeding grounds and occupy the best territories; the two groups mate assortatively because of their different arrival times, and they have begun to diverge genetically and in morphology (rounder wings, narrower beaks in the northwestern wintering birds; Rolshausen and colleagues 2009).

Hybrids at migratory divides can show what happens when two programs are combined. Swainson's thrushes (*Catharus ustulatus*) of the Pacific coast migrate along the coast to Central America, whereas birds of the inland subspecies fly east across North America and then south to South America, a route that retraces the postglacial expansion of the species (Ruegg and Smith 2002). Hybrids from the contact zone in British Columbia, tracked with geolocators, took intermediate routes over the arid interior or more variable routes (Delmore and Irwin 2014), a disadvantage that may maintain the boundary between the subspecies.

### 20.5.3. Conditional and Flexible Migration

Not all variation is genetic. In partially migratory common blackbirds (*Turdus merula*), individuals can be migrants in one year and residents in another, and migratory and resident birds of the same population differ in the expression of only a handful of genes (Franchini and colleagues 2017). Migration in these populations behaves as a **conditional strategy** ([7.18.5](/7.18.5#def-5.6.1)): young and subordinate birds tend to migrate, and individuals switch to residence as they become older and dominant (Lundberg 1988), presumably because residents gain priority for territories in spring but only dominants can secure the limited winter resources near the breeding grounds. In many species of birds and fishes the decision depends on body condition, and in ungulates it depends on the density of the population and on learned routes: bighorn sheep and moose translocated to new areas need decades to learn to "surf the green wave" of spring vegetation up the mountains, so that migratory knowledge is in part culturally transmitted (Jesmer and colleagues 2018; [7.18.46](/7.18.46)).

## 20.6. The Evolution of Migration

How could a behavior as elaborate as long-distance migration evolve? Migration is extremely labile in evolution: it has been gained and lost many times within families and even within species (the barn swallows of Argentina, the British-wintering blackcaps), and resident and migratory populations of the same species often differ only quantitatively in their program. Migration therefore does not require many novel traits; it is built from capacities that most animals possess, such as seasonal fattening, a compass and a clock, and is turned on or off by quantitative changes.

The geographical question is where migratory lineages came from. Two hypotheses have been debated for more than a century:

- *Southern (tropical) origin.* Migration evolved in tropical lineages whose populations moved toward temperate regions to breed, exploiting the summer flush of food and escaping competition, while returning to their ancestral tropical range in winter. Short-distance and long-distance migrants occur together in nine families of songbirds of tropical origin (Levey and Stiles 1992), and in the thrush genus *Catharus*, in which the resident species live in Mexico and Central America, migration appears to have evolved three times from tropical residents (Outlaw and colleagues 2003; Winker and Pruett 2006).
- *Northern (temperate) origin.* Migration evolved in lineages that bred in the temperate zone, as some individuals moved south for the winter to escape cold and food shortage, while keeping their breeding range. In the New World warblers (Parulidae), sedentary species appear to have evolved from migratory ones repeatedly (Winger and colleagues 2011), and in an analysis of more than 800 species of New World emberizoid passerines, long-distance migration evolved mainly by shifts of the winter range southward out of North America, not by shifts of the breeding range northward out of the tropics (Winger and colleagues 2014).

The two hypotheses are not mutually exclusive, and the current evidence for New World passerines favors the second, together with the general view that the ancestral range of a migrant lineage tells us where breeding took place, not where migration began. A third, older idea, that the advance of glaciers in the ice ages drove birds south from their northern homes and the retreat let them return, contains a truth: the glacial cycles of the last 2.6 million years repeatedly shifted breeding ranges and routes, and many present routes, such as the eastern route of Swainson's thrushes or the routes of northern wheatears that breed in Alaska and still winter in Africa, retrace postglacial range expansions. But migration is older than the Pleistocene glaciations and occurs in the tropics and the Southern Hemisphere, where there were no ice sheets.

A further hypothesis, that continental drift carried birds northward and that their attempts to return to their southern homeland became migration, is not tenable. The continents have been close to their present positions for tens of millions of years, and they move a few centimeters per year, a few hundred kilometers in ten million years. Migratory routes, by contrast, are labile on time scales of decades to thousands of years, as the blackcaps and swallows show, and the same species includes resident and migratory populations. Continental drift explains some distributions of lineages over geological time ([7.17.69](/7.17.69)), but not the behavior of their members.

## 20.7. Migrants in a Changing World

### 20.7.1. Climate Change and Phenological Mismatch

**Definition 20.7.1 (Phenological Mismatch)** A **phenological mismatch** is a loss of temporal overlap between the seasonal activities of interacting species, for example between the period when a bird's nestlings need food and the peak of their insect food, that arises when the species respond differently to environmental change.

Long-distance migrants are especially vulnerable to mismatch, because they cannot sense the conditions on their breeding grounds from their wintering grounds thousands of kilometers away. Their departure is timed by photoperiod and circannual rhythms ([7.18.18](/7.18.18)), which are not affected by warming. In the Netherlands, the caterpillar peak in oak forests advanced by about two weeks over two decades of warming. Resident great tits advanced their laying, though not fully; pied flycatchers (*Ficedula hypoleuca*), which winter in West Africa, advanced their laying date by shortening the interval between arrival and laying, but their arrival date was nearly unchanged, so they could not catch up with the food peak (Both and Visser 2001). Between 1987 and 2003, flycatcher populations in areas where the food peak was earliest declined by about 90 %, whereas those in areas with a late food peak declined little (Both and colleagues 2006). Selection for earlier arrival and laying is now acting on the flycatchers, and some populations have advanced their arrival.

### 20.7.2. Stopover Habitats and Flyways

**Definition 20.7.2 (Flyway)** A **flyway** is the geographic region in which a group of populations of migratory birds completes its annual cycle, including breeding areas, stopover sites and nonbreeding areas, and the routes connecting them. Flyways are used as units for international conservation.

Nine major flyways are recognized for waterbirds. The East Asian–Australasian Flyway, which connects breeding grounds from Siberia and Alaska to nonbreeding grounds in Southeast Asia, Australia and New Zealand, carries about 50 million waterbirds of more than 200 species ([7.17.53](/7.17.53#sec-53.10.3)). Most of its shorebirds depend on the intertidal flats of the Yellow Sea and Bohai Gulf for refueling, particularly in spring. Between the 1950s and the 2010s, land reclamation for agriculture, aquaculture, ports and industry destroyed a large fraction of these tidal flats, more than a quarter since the 1980s alone (Murray and colleagues 2014). The shorebird species that depend most on the Yellow Sea declined fastest, by up to 8 % per year (Studds and colleagues 2017): great knots, curlew sandpipers, far eastern curlews and bar-tailed godwits. The spoon-billed sandpiper, which breeds only in Chukotka, has declined to a few hundred breeding pairs. A survey of 14 major stopover sites in the Yellow Sea and Bohai Gulf found that the numbers of shorebirds at a site were related to the area of remaining tidal flat (Wang and colleagues 2022). Since 2018 China has stopped approving new reclamation of coastal wetlands for commercial purposes, and the migratory bird sanctuaries along the coast of the Yellow Sea–Bohai Gulf were inscribed as a World Heritage Site in 2019 and extended in 2024.

The weakest link of a migratory system limits it: a population can decline because of losses at a stopover that individuals use for a few weeks a year. Other threats are hunting (the yellow-breasted bunting, *Emberiza aureola*, once abundant across northern Eurasia, declined by about 90 % from 1980 to 2013, largely through illegal trapping on migration and in winter in China; Kamp and colleagues 2015), collisions with buildings, power lines and wind turbines, artificial light at night, which attracts and disorients nocturnal migrants, and barriers on rivers, which block the migrations of salmon, sturgeons and eels. Protecting migrants requires international cooperation along the whole route, from the conservation of staging sites to reducing light pollution in cities during migration nights.

## Summary

- Migration (Definition 20.1.1) is persistent, directed movement between regions used at different times, in a distinct physiological and behavioral state; it differs from **dispersal** and from routine movements, and is one of two responses to seasonality, the other being dormancy (**diapause**, **torpor**, **hibernation**). Round trips can be repeated annually, made once in a life (salmon, eels) or completed over several generations (monarchs).
- Types: latitudinal, longitudinal, altitudinal, diel vertical (zooplankton follow an isolume to avoid visual predators) and breeding migrations; partial, differential, irruptive and nomadic movements.
- Migrants include wildebeest, whales and bats; barn swallows (a migratory divide in the Gansu hybrid zone), Beijing swifts (to southern Africa), bar-headed geese (over the Himalaya), Arctic terns (70 000–90 000 km a year) and bar-tailed godwits (13 560 km nonstop); leatherback and green turtles, toads, blue sharks and salmon; monarchs, red crabs, krill, spiny lobsters and insects.
- Migrations are tracked by rings, geolocators, GPS and satellite tags, radio networks, radar, stable isotopes and genetic markers; migratory connectivity links breeding populations to their nonbreeding areas and matters for conservation.
- Benefits: seasonal resources, escape from parasites and competition, higher survival; costs: energy, time and mortality. Fat fuels flight; migrants remodel their organs; V-formation, soaring, nocturnal flight and wind selection reduce costs. At stopover sites (Definition 20.4.1) the optimal departure fuel load of a time-minimizer increases with the fuel deposition rate (Example 20.4.1, a marginal-value problem); departure decisions depend on fuel and barriers (red-eyed vireos).
- Migratory restlessness (Definition 20.5.1) reveals an inherited clock-and-compass program of timing, distance and direction; crosses (blackcaps), selection experiments and the rapid evolution of a new northwestern route to Britain show its heritability; hybrids at migratory divides take intermediate routes. Partial migration can be a conditional strategy (blackbirds) or a heritable threshold trait; ungulates learn routes culturally.
- Migration is evolutionarily labile; in New World passerines it evolved mainly by southward shifts of winter ranges of temperate lineages. Glacial cycles reshaped routes; continental drift is far too slow to explain migration.
- Threats: phenological mismatch (Definition 20.7.1; pied flycatchers declined 90 % where food peaks advanced most), loss of stopover habitat on flyways (Definition 20.7.2; the Yellow Sea tidal flats), hunting, collisions, light and dams. Conservation must protect whole flyways.

## Questions

1. Why is the distance travelled a poor criterion for distinguishing migration from other movements? Give an example of a short migration and of a long movement that is not migration.

   **Answer** Migration is defined by the state and pattern of the movement, not by its length: it is persistent, directed, undistracted by resources and associated with specific physiological preparation and a regular return. Diel vertical migration of zooplankton covers only a few hundred meters, and the altitudinal migration of a tropical bird a few tens of kilometers, yet both are directed, regular movements between habitats used at different times. Conversely, a wolf dispersing from its natal pack may travel more than 1 000 km to find a territory, but the movement is one way and not part of a population-wide seasonal cycle, so it is dispersal; and a foraging albatross may fly thousands of kilometers in a loop within its foraging range during the breeding season, which is a foraging trip.

2. (Calculation) In Example 20.4.1, show that for a linear range function $r(f) = af$ the time-minimizing bird would never leave, and explain why diminishing returns are essential to the model.

   **Answer** With $r(f) = af$, the speed is $v(f) = af/(t_0 + f/k) = a k f/(k t_0 + f)$, which increases monotonically with $f$ toward the limit $ak$ as $f \to \infty$: there is no interior maximum, so the bird should keep fattening indefinitely. The model has an optimum only because $r$ is concave: each additional unit of fuel adds less range, since the bird must carry its own fuel, and the cost of transport rises with mass. At the optimum, the marginal gain in range from staying a little longer, $r'(f)\,k$ per day, equals the average speed of migration so far, as in the marginal value theorem. In reality there is also an upper limit on fuel load set by flight performance and predation risk (heavy birds take off more slowly), which caps the optimum.

3. The question of how a naive migrant finds its wintering area arises for every young bird that migrates alone at night. What evidence from blackcaps shows that the direction is inherited, and how could the inherited program lead a bird astray when the environment changes?

   **Answer** Hand-raised blackcaps that had never migrated or seen their parents migrate oriented in orientation cages in the direction typical of their population (southwest for western European, southeast for eastern Austrian birds); hybrids between the two oriented intermediately (south); and the offspring of blackcaps wintering in Britain, raised in Germany, oriented west-northwest like their parents. These results show that the direction is genetically encoded and inherited additively. A fixed program can mislead when the environment changes faster than the program evolves: hybrids at migratory divides may be sent over barriers; migrants whose departure date is fixed by photoperiod arrive too late after warming advances the food peak; and birds displaced by storms (vagrants) continue to follow their program from the wrong starting point, ending far from the normal wintering area. The British-wintering blackcaps show that, where the new environment rewards the deviant direction, the program can also change quickly by selection.

4. A shorebird population declines by 6 % per year. Its breeding success, measured on the Arctic breeding grounds, has not changed, and adult survival during the nonbreeding season in Australia is high. Where would you look for the cause, and how would you test it?

   **Answer** The remaining components are survival during migration and at stopovers, and the carry-over of poor condition from stopovers to breeding (which could reduce the number of birds that breed rather than their success when breeding). I would look at the staging sites, especially the Yellow Sea for East Asian–Australasian Flyway shorebirds. Tests: estimate seasonal survival from resightings of individually marked birds (multistate capture–recapture models separating the northward migration from other periods); compare survival between years and with the area of tidal flat remaining at stopovers; measure fuel deposition rates and departure masses at stopover sites over years; and track birds with geolocators or GPS to see where they die or stay longer. If survival during northward migration has declined and correlates with the loss of stopover habitat or with lower fuel deposition, the stopover is the limiting link, as Studds and colleagues found for the species most dependent on the Yellow Sea.

5. (Open question) What can individuals and cities do to reduce the mortality of migratory birds? Propose measures and how their effect could be measured.

   **Answer** Collisions with glass and light pollution are major causes of death for nocturnal migrants in cities. Measures include turning off or dimming unnecessary lighting in tall buildings and landmark lights during peak migration nights, forecast by weather radar (as in "lights out" programs); treating windows with visible patterns (dots or lines spaced at most 5–10 cm apart) or ultraviolet-reflecting films; keeping cats indoors; planting native vegetation in parks and green roofs that offer food and shelter for stopovers; and supporting the protection of wetlands and tidal flats, reporting illegal trapping and supporting monitoring by citizen science (bird counts, ringing). Effects can be measured by standardized carcass searches around buildings before and after treatment, compared with control buildings (a before–after–control–impact design), by radar measures of the numbers of birds attracted to lit areas, and by counts of migrants using restored urban habitats.
