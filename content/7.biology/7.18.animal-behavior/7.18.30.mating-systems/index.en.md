The two previous chapters dealt with the competition and choice that determine which individuals mate ([7.18.28](/7.18.28), [7.18.29](/7.18.29)). The outcome, summed over a population, is its mating system: how many partners each male and each female has, how the partners are acquired, and how long they stay together. The variety is bewildering. A male elephant seal may mate with dozens of females in a season and never see his pups; a pair of oystercatchers may stay together for twenty years; a female spotted sandpiper lays clutches for several males in succession and leaves each to incubate; dunnocks in one English garden may be monogamous, polygynous, polyandrous or both at once.

Since an influential paper by Stephen Emlen and Lewis Oring (1977), behavioral ecologists have explained this diversity as the outcome of individuals of each sex trying to maximize their own reproductive success under ecological constraints. Two factors are decisive: how the members of each sex are distributed in space and time, which determines how easily one sex can monopolize the other, and the pattern of parental care, which determines what each sex loses by sharing or deserting a mate. We first classify mating systems and set out the ecological framework, then examine polygyny, leks, monogamy and polyandry in turn. The evolution of parental care, which this chapter takes as given, is the subject of [7.18.31](/7.18.31), and the cooperative breeding systems in which helpers raise others' young are treated in [7.18.35](/7.18.35). The genetic consequences of mating systems in plants, selfing and outcrossing, were discussed in [7.10.14](/7.10.14#sec-14.4).

## 30.1. Classifying Mating Systems

**Definition 30.1.1 (Mating System)** The **mating system** of a population is the pattern of mating among its members: the number of mates each individual of each sex acquires in a breeding season, the way the mates are acquired, the duration and exclusivity of the bonds between them, and the associated pattern of parental care.

**Definition 30.1.2 (Types of Mating System)** In **monogamy**, a male and a female mate only with each other, for one breeding season or longer. In **polygyny**, one male mates with several females in a breeding season while each female mates with one male. In **polyandry**, one female mates with several males in a breeding season while each male mates with one female. In **polygynandry**, two or more males form a stable association with two or more females and each mates with several of the other sex. In **promiscuity**, both males and females mate with several partners without forming lasting bonds. *Polygamy* is a general term for any system in which individuals of either sex have more than one mate.

Polygyny and polyandry can be *simultaneous*, with several partners at the same time, or *sequential*, with one partner after another in the same season. The terms describe what individuals do, not a fixed property of a species: within a population of dunnocks or red-winged blackbirds, different individuals live in different mating systems, and a species' mating system is the distribution of these.

A further distinction became essential with molecular parentage analysis. The *social mating system* describes who associates with whom, who shares a nest or territory and cares for the young; the *genetic mating system* describes who actually fathers and mothers the offspring. Since the 1980s, DNA fingerprinting and later microsatellite genotyping, which compare the alleles of offspring with those of putative parents at many highly polymorphic loci, have shown that the two often differ. Most songbirds are socially monogamous but genetically polygynandrous, because extra-pair copulations are common (Section 30.4.3).

## 30.2. The Ecological Framework

### 30.2.1. Females Follow Resources, Males Follow Females

When males give no parental care, the reproductive success of females is limited mainly by resources (food, breeding sites), and that of males mainly by access to females ([7.18.28](/7.18.28#sec-28.1.2)). Emlen and Oring proposed that the mating system is then the outcome of a two-step process. Females distribute themselves in relation to resources, modified by the costs and benefits of living in groups (predation, competition; [7.18.27](/7.18.27)). Males then distribute themselves in relation to females, competing for them either directly, by defending the females themselves, or indirectly, by defending the resources that females need. Whether a male can monopolize several females depends on their distribution in space, which sets the *environmental potential for polygyny*, and in time. If females are clumped, a male that defends a clump gains many mates while others gain none; if they are dispersed, each male can defend at most one or two. If all females become receptive at the same moment, a male has time to mate with only one or two before the season ends, however they are distributed.

Timing matters as much as space. Common toads (*Bufo bufo*) are *explosive breeders*: all females in a pond spawn within a few days, the operational sex ratio is strongly male-biased for a short time, and a male has time to clasp one female, or at most two, before the season ends. North American bullfrogs (*Lithobates catesbeianus*) are *prolonged breeders*: females arrive over several weeks, and males that defend the best spawning sites can mate with up to six females in a season (Wells 1977).

The causal sequence has been tested experimentally.

- *Gray-sided voles.* Rolf Anker Ims (1987) provided food at particular sites and found that the ranges of female gray-sided voles (*Myodes rufocanus*) became smaller and overlapped in the rich areas, and that males moved there too. To find out whether males were following the females or the food, Ims (1988) kept females in small cages on a wooded island in Norway, moving the cages each day to simulate their movements. When the caged females were spaced out, the radio-tracked free-ranging males spread out over the female ranges; when the cages were clumped, the males aggregated on the clumps. When males were caged instead, the dispersion of free-ranging females did not respond to changes in the dispersion of males. The causal links run from resources to females and from females to males.
- *Bluehead wrasse.* Females of the Caribbean bluehead wrasse (*Thalassoma bifasciatum*) spawn at particular sites on the downcurrent edges of reefs, where their pelagic eggs are swept quickly away from egg predators, and large males defend territories at these sites. Robert Warner (1987, 1990) replaced either all the males or all the females of isolated local populations with fish from elsewhere. When the males were replaced, the same spawning sites continued to be used; when the females were replaced, the sites changed, even though some males initially went on defending the old ones. Females choose the sites, and males compete for the sites that females prefer.

### 30.2.2. A Comparative Survey of Mammals

Mammals illustrate how the distribution of females shapes the mating system, because male mammals cannot lactate and rarely care for the young, so the simple two-step scheme applies well. In a survey of mammalian mating systems, Tim Clutton-Brock (1989) distinguished cases by the size of female ranges, the size of female groups and the seasonality of breeding:

| Females | Male tactic | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Solitary, ranges small enough to defend | Territory overlapping one or several female ranges: polygyny, or monogamy if a male can defend only one female | Most rodents, nocturnal prosimians; monogamous dik-diks |
| Solitary, ranges too large to defend | Roving between females, consorting with each while she is in estrus | Moose, orangutans |
| Small groups, defensible range | Permanent harem in a territory, sometimes defended by a coalition | Black-and-white colobus, Hanuman langurs; lions (coalitions) |
| Groups with predictable movements | Small territories on routes or at water holes | Topi, Grevy's zebra |
| Groups with unpredictable movements | Following females; seasonal or permanent harems; multimale groups | Red deer, elephant seals (seasonal); hamadryas and gelada baboons, plains zebra (permanent); buffalo, olive baboons (multimale) |
| Large ranges, males cannot defend females or resources | Leks | Uganda kob, topi and fallow deer at high density, hammer-headed bat |

In more than 60 percent of mammal species females are solitary, and males give some care, such as defending, carrying or feeding the young, in only a few percent of species, concentrated among primates, carnivores and rodents.

## 30.3. Polygyny

### 30.3.1. Female Defense Polygyny

**Definition 30.3.1 (Female Defense Polygyny)** In **female defense polygyny** (harem polygyny), males compete directly for females and monopolize several of them by guarding them, which is possible when females live in groups for reasons other than mating, such as predation or the localized distribution of breeding sites.

The harems of elephant seals are the classic case ([7.18.28](/7.18.28#sec-28.2.2)). Females haul out on a few traditional beaches to give birth and mate again soon afterward, so they are clumped by the scarcity of suitable beaches. Being a harem master is so exhausting that few males hold a harem for more than a year or two. Harems may be seasonal or permanent. Red deer hinds all come into estrus within about a month in autumn, and stags build up reserves during the summer and then defend harems during the rut, barely feeding; by the end they are "rutted out", in very poor condition. Where females come into estrus at different times of the year, males may defend harems for years, as in hamadryas and gelada baboons and plains zebras, whose single-male units often travel together in larger bands. When female groups are too large for one male to control, several males associate with them and compete for each estrous female, forming multimale groups (African buffalo, olive baboons), in which dominance rank determines mating success ([7.18.28](/7.18.28#sec-28.2.3)).

Males taking over a harem often kill the infants sired by their predecessors, which brings the mothers back into estrus sooner ([7.18.1](/7.18.1#sec-1.4.3)). A harem master may also trample pups when he rushes at rival males, a cost to females that is no cost to him, since the pups were probably sired by the previous year's master.

### 30.3.2. Resource Defense Polygyny

**Definition 30.3.2 (Resource Defense Polygyny)** In **resource defense polygyny**, males compete indirectly for females by defending resources that females need for breeding, such as food, nest sites or spawning sites; males holding the best resources attract the most females.

Bullfrog males defend territories in ponds with warm water and few predatory leeches, where eggs survive best, and the best territories attract the most females (Howard 1978). Most regular polygyny in birds is of this kind, because birds have little scope for herding females. Whether a female suffers by sharing a male with others depends on what the male provides. In yellow-headed blackbirds (*Xanthocephalus xanthocephalus*), whose females nest in marshes but feed in fields elsewhere, and in yellow-rumped caciques (*Cacicus cela*), which nest close together in safe colonies, females appear to suffer no cost from sharing a male and settle almost at random with respect to his mating status. In many species, however, females share the male's help or the resources of his territory. Why should a female then choose an already mated male?

### 30.3.3. The Polygyny Threshold Model

Jared Verner and Mary Willson (1966) suggested that a female may choose polygyny if the benefits of better resources outweigh the costs of sharing, and Gordon Orians (1969) put the idea into a graphical model.

**Definition 30.3.3 (Polygyny Threshold Model)** The **polygyny threshold model** assumes that the reproductive success of a female increases with the quality of the breeding situation (territory) she settles in, and that settling as a secondary female with an already mated male costs her an amount $C$ of success. Females settle where their expected success is highest. The *polygyny threshold* is the difference in territory quality between a mated male and an unmated male at which a female does equally well with either.

**Proposition 30.3.1 (The Threshold)** Let $W(q)$ be the success of a monogamous female on a territory of quality $q$, an increasing function, and $W(q) - C$ that of a secondary female. A female choosing between an unmated male on a territory of quality $q_{\mathrm{B}}$ and a mated male on a territory of quality $q_{\mathrm{A}}$ should choose polygyny if and only if $W(q_{\mathrm{A}}) - W(q_{\mathrm{B}}) > C$. If $W$ is linear with slope $a$, this is

$$
q_{\mathrm{A}} - q_{\mathrm{B}} > \mathrm{PT} = \frac{C}{a}.
$$

**Proof** The female compares $W(q_{\mathrm{A}}) - C$ with $W(q_{\mathrm{B}})$, which gives the first condition; for $W(q) = W_0 + aq$ the difference is $a(q_{\mathrm{A}} - q_{\mathrm{B}})$. $\square$

The model is an ideal free distribution ([7.18.25](/7.18.25#def-25.3.1)) in which the "habitats" are male territories and the decline in payoff with the number of settlers is the cost of sharing. Like the ideal free distribution, it predicts that, if females are free to choose and settle one after another, the expected success of the females that settle late should be about equal whatever their choice: a secondary female on a good territory should do about as well as a monogamous female on a poorer one. This prediction depends on the distribution of territory quality, however, and need not hold for every female (Altmann and colleagues 1977). In the settlement sequence of the figure below, each newcomer takes the best option available to her, the best males become polygynous and the worst remain unmated, and the secondary females do as well as or better than the monogamous females that settled after them.

A female choosing between an unmated male on a poor territory and a mated male on a good territory should choose the mated male if the difference in quality exceeds the polygyny threshold PT. B: sequential settlement of six females on six territories of decreasing quality (A–F), each choosing the option with the highest expected success, assuming the first female on a territory does not suffer from a second; numbers give the order of settlement. Two species have provided the best tests.

- *Great reed warblers* (*Acrocephalus arundinaceus*). Bensch and Hasselquist (1992) radio-tracked females newly arrived in a Swedish reed bed. Most paired within 24 hours, after visiting the territories of three to eleven males, sometimes returning to a male visited earlier, and some chose already mated males although they had visited unmated ones. Females sample territories as the model assumes. At Lake Biwa in Japan, 30–80 percent of males were polygynous each year, with up to four females; they held territories with dense reeds, where nest predation was lowest, and secondary females did at least as well as monogamous females nesting at the same time on poor territories (Ezaki 1990).
- *Red-winged blackbirds* (*Agelaius phoeniceus*). In an Ontario marsh, Stanislav Pribil, Jaroslav Picman and William Searcy tested the assumptions with a series of experiments on pairs of adjacent territories matched for quality and number of settled females. By removing females they showed that polygyny was costly: secondary females suffered more nest predation and received less male help (Pribil 2000). When the next arriving female could choose between a territory with one female and an equivalent territory with none, all 16 females chose the unmated male (Pribil and Picman 1996), so male mating status matters. Then they improved one territory of each pair by adding nesting platforms of cattails over water, where nests are safer from raccoons and weasels, and left it with one female, while the other territory had its females removed and its nesting cover over water trimmed. In 12 of 14 pairs, the next female chose the mated male on the improved territory (Pribil and Searcy 2001). Nesting over water raised a female's success by 1.02 young, while polygyny cost her 0.62 young, so choosing polygyny on the good territory was adaptive. In a Pennsylvania population, by contrast, females suffered no cost from polygyny (Searcy 1988): the costs vary with local predation and food.

### 30.3.4. Conflict and Deception

The polygyny threshold model assumes that females are free to choose and that nobody interferes. Often both assumptions fail. Primary females may lose from the arrival of a second female and try to prevent it; males may try to attract more females than is good for the females.

The pied flycatcher (*Ficedula hypoleuca*), studied by Rauno Alatalo, Arne Lundberg and colleagues near Uppsala, is the best-documented case. A male defends a nest hole and sings to attract a female; once she has laid, he moves to another hole, on average about 200 m away and sometimes several kilometers, and tries to attract a second female. About 10–15 percent of males succeed; they then return to help the first female, and the second female, raising her brood mostly alone, fledges on average only about 60 percent of the young a monogamous female would. Why do secondary females accept this? Three hypotheses were tested.

1. *Sexy sons.* The secondary female might be compensated by sons that inherit their father's ability to attract several mates (Weatherhead and Robertson 1979). For the pied flycatcher, the heritability of male mating status would have to be about 0.85 to make up for the loss; since the probability that a male is polygynous in successive years is only 0.29, an upper limit to the heritability, the hypothesis can be rejected.
2. *Deception.* Males may hold widely separated territories (*polyterritoriality*) so that females cannot tell mated from unmated males (Alatalo and colleagues 1981).
3. *Unmated males are hard to find.* Alternatively, secondary females may choose polygyny because searching longer costs more, and males hold distant territories to avoid aggression from their first female.

Alatalo and colleagues (1990) arranged, by putting up nest boxes in sequence, for neighboring boxes less than 100 m apart to be held by an unmated male and by a mated male whose first female was nesting 100–300 m away, at random sites. Females could sample both males easily. In 20 such choices, 9 females settled with the unmated male and 11 with the mated male, no preference at all, and those that chose the mated male raised fewer young. The females could not distinguish mated from unmated males although it would have paid them to, which supports the deception hypothesis.

Conflict between females is clearest in the burying beetle *Nicrophorus*, in which both parents feed the larvae on a buried carcass ([7.18.31](/7.18.31#sec-31.3.3)). On a large carcass, a male adopts a "headstand" posture and releases a pheromone to attract a second female; she increases his success but reduces that of the first female, who receives less help (Eggert and Müller 1992). The first female interferes: when the male calls, she mounts him, pushes him over or bites his abdomen (Eggert and Sakaluk 1995).

### 30.3.5. Scramble Competition Polygyny

**Definition 30.3.4 (Scramble Competition Polygyny)** In **scramble competition polygyny**, males can defend neither females nor resources because females are widely dispersed and receptive for a short time, and males compete by searching for receptive females as quickly and widely as possible; mating success depends on mobility and searching ability rather than on fighting ability.

Male thirteen-lined ground squirrels (*Ictidomys tridecemlineatus*) search over large areas during the brief breeding season, in which each female is receptive for only a few hours on a single day. The males that traveled farthest and found the most females had the highest mating success; body size, which would matter in fights, did not predict success (Schwagmeyer 1988). In explosively breeding frogs and toads, males scramble to clasp females as they arrive at the pond, and in many insects, such as moths tracking pheromone plumes ([7.18.9](/7.18.9)), males race each other to newly emerged females; sensory and locomotor traits are then the targets of sexual selection.

## 30.4. Leks

### 30.4.1. Lek Mating Systems

In some species males aggregate and each defends a tiny mating territory that contains no resources at all, often a bare patch of ground a few meters across, where they display to visiting females. This is a **lek** ([Definition 13.2.4](/7.10.13#def-13.2.4)). Four criteria are generally used to recognize lekking (Bradbury 1981): males display in aggregations; they contribute nothing to their mates or offspring beyond sperm; the choosing sex visits the arena only to mate; and it is free to choose among the displaying individuals. Where males display out of sight of each other but within hearing, as in some bowerbirds and hummingbirds, the aggregation is called an *exploded lek*.

Leks are rare. They have been described in a handful of mammals (the walrus, the hammer-headed bat and a few ungulates) and in a few dozen bird species, including grouse, some shorebirds, hummingbirds, cotingas, manakins, birds of paradise, the kakapo and the great bustard, and in some frogs, fishes and insects. Mating success on leks is extremely skewed: in sage grouse, Uganda kob and white-bearded manakins, one or a few males obtain most of the copulations. Leks are thought to arise when males cannot economically defend either the females themselves or the resources they need, because females range widely over dispersed food or because high densities make defense too costly. In both antelopes and grouse, the lekking species have the largest female home ranges (Bradbury and colleagues 1986), and Uganda kob, topi and fallow deer lek at high density but defend resource territories or harems at low density.

### 30.4.2. Why Do Males Aggregate?

A lek poses two questions: why males cluster, and why females mate with so few of them. Several hypotheses have been proposed for the first (Bradbury and Gibson 1983); they are not mutually exclusive.

- *Hotspots.* Males settle where female encounter rates are highest, for instance where the routes of females between their foraging and nesting areas converge (Bradbury and colleagues 1986). Sage grouse leks lie on the routes that females follow between their wintering and nesting ranges, and the number of males on a lek is related to the number of females nesting within 2 km (Gibson 1996). In the lekking sandfly *Lutzomyia longipalpis*, hundreds of males gather on the vertebrate hosts that females visit for blood meals, each defending a territory a few centimeters across; when the number and position of caged chicken hosts were changed, males quickly aggregated on the new hotspots, with larger leks on more hosts (Jones and Quinnell 2002). But in many species females visit the lek only to mate, and males are packed more closely than the female distribution alone would predict.
- *Predation.* Calling males of the túngara frog (*Physalaemus pustulosus*) are safer from frog-eating bats in larger choruses, through the **dilution effect** ([7.18.27](/7.18.27#def-27.2.1)); in most bird leks, however, predation seems to be low.
- *Increased attraction.* Males displaying together may attract females from farther away. In experimental leks of caged tephritid fruit flies, larger leks attracted more females per male in one species but not in another (Shelly 2001).
- *Hotshots.* Less attractive males may cluster around very attractive males to intercept females drawn to them (Beehler and Foster 1988). Removal experiments argue against this as a general explanation. When the most successful males are removed from leks of sharp-tailed grouse or white-bearded manakins, other males quickly take over their territories and their success, which suggests that something about the site matters. In fallow deer (*Dama dama*), Clutton-Brock and colleagues (1989) covered the territories of the most successful males with black polythene, forcing them to move several hundred meters; females followed the males, but the other males did not, as the hotshot model would predict.
- *Female preference.* Females may prefer aggregations because they make comparison easier and mating safer. Jiguet and Bretagnolle (2006) set up artificial leks of decoy males of the little bustard (*Tetrax tetrax*) and found that females visited groups of four decoys more than smaller (or larger) groups.

Why females are so choosy on leks, when they gain nothing but sperm, is the problem of indirect benefits discussed in [7.18.29](/7.18.29#sec-29.3). Female black grouse prefer the males with the most vigorous displays, and these males survive better (Alatalo and colleagues 1991), consistent with leks being arenas in which males reveal their condition. Cooperation can occur even on leks. Pairs of male long-tailed manakins (*Chiroxiphia linearis*) perform a coordinated dual display; only the alpha male mates, but the beta male inherits the display site and its female visitors when the alpha dies (McDonald and Potts 1994). Leks also occur in sex-role-reversed species: in the worm pipefish *Nerophis lumbriciformis*, females display on patches of the sea floor, the most ornamented females occupy the center of the aggregation, and males that mate with them receive larger broods (Monteiro and colleagues 2017).

## 30.5. Monogamy

### 30.5.1. Monogamy in Birds

David Lack (1968) found that about 90 percent of bird species are socially monogamous and proposed that "each male and each female will, on average, leave most descendants if they share in raising a brood". This explains *obligate monogamy*, found in many seabirds and raptors, in which incubation is shared or the male feeds the incubating female, and in which the loss of either partner causes breeding failure. In such species pairs often stay together for many years, and their success may improve with the duration of the pair bond itself. In oystercatchers (*Haematopus ostralegus*) on a Dutch island, reproductive success increased with pair-bond duration for five to seven years, independently of the age of the partners and the quality of the territory; when a partner was experimentally removed, success with the new partner was lower and then increased over the following years (van de Pol and colleagues 2006). Nevertheless, about 8 percent of pairs divorced each year. Birds that deserted their mates usually moved to better territories and gained, whereas birds forced out by competitors lost (Heg and colleagues 2003): divorce has initiators and victims.

Lack's hypothesis does not explain monogamy in many songbirds, in which removal experiments show that females can raise young without help: in song sparrows, broods raised by females alone fledged 51 percent as many young as broods fed by pairs, in seaside sparrows 66 percent and in dark-eyed juncos 38 percent. If desertion reduces a brood to a fraction $1/x$ of a pair-fed brood, a male gains by deserting if he can obtain more than $x$ further females, and he gains from polygyny even with two females if he helps at least one of them. Males of songbirds do readily become polygynous when given the chance, for example when a neighbor is removed, and occasional polygyny has been recorded in 39 percent of 122 well-studied European songbird species. Monogamy in such birds is better explained by limited opportunities for polygyny, because of competition among males for females and aggression between females, than by an advantage of monogamy to males.

### 30.5.2. Monogamy in Mammals

Social monogamy is rare in mammals, found in less than 10 percent of species, and its origin has been debated. Three hypotheses have been proposed.

- *Mate guarding.* A male may stay with a single female because females are so widely dispersed that he cannot defend more than one, and a male that guards one female ensures his paternity. A comparative analysis of more than 2500 mammal species concluded that social monogamy evolved from ancestors in which females were solitary and ranged widely at low density, and that paternal care evolved after monogamy rather than causing it (Lukas and Clutton-Brock 2013).
- *Paternal care.* Where a male's help substantially increases the survival of the young, monogamy may be favored. In the California mouse (*Peromyscus californicus*), males huddle with and groom the pups; pairs reared litters of four in the laboratory much better than single females, and in the field the number of young reared fell when the father was removed, mainly because pups were kept less warm (Gubernick and Teferi 2000). The proximate mechanisms of pair bonding and paternal behavior, studied especially in prairie voles (*Microtus ochrogaster*), involve oxytocin and vasopressin ([7.18.19](/7.18.19)).
- *Infanticide.* A male may stay with a female to defend her young against infanticidal males. In white-handed gibbons, infants disappeared less often from monogamous groups than from groups with several adult males (Borries and colleagues 2011), and a phylogenetic analysis of 230 primate species suggested that male infanticide preceded the transitions from polygyny to monogamy (Opie and colleagues 2013), although this conclusion was disputed by the authors of the mammal-wide analysis.

The most extreme form of monogamy is found in some deep-sea anglerfishes (Ceratioidei). In these species the males are dwarfs, in some only 6–7 mm long, with large nostrils and eyes for finding a female in the dark; when a male finds one, he bites into her skin and fuses with her, their circulatory systems join, and he becomes a permanent sperm-producing appendage nourished by her blood. In the vast, sparsely populated deep sea, the chance of meeting a partner twice is tiny, and a male that attaches himself to the first female he finds secures his only chance to reproduce. Females of some species carry several attached males. The fusion of genetically different individuals is possible because these species have lost key components of the adaptive immune system, a finding from genome sequencing that explains how they avoid rejecting the grafted male (Swann and colleagues 2020).

### 30.5.3. Extra-Pair Paternity

Social monogamy rarely means genetic monogamy.

**Definition 30.5.1 (Extra-Pair Paternity)** **Extra-pair paternity** is the fathering of offspring by a male other than the social partner of their mother, the result of *extra-pair copulations*; offspring so fathered are *extra-pair young*.

Molecular parentage analyses have found extra-pair young in about 90 percent of the bird species examined (Griffith and colleagues 2002), typically 10–40 percent of the young in socially monogamous songbirds, but with wide variation, from essentially none in some seabirds to more than half in the superb fairy-wren (*Malurus cyaneus*), whose females make predawn forays into the territories of males with the earliest molt into breeding plumage. In a marsh in Ontario, extra-pair fertilizations accounted for an average of 21 percent of the reproductive success of male red-winged blackbirds, and most cuckolders were near neighbors (Gibbs and colleagues 1990). A male's apparent success, the number of young in nests on his territory, was thus a poor estimate of his genetic success.

The behavioral consequences have been discussed in the previous chapters: males guard their mates during the fertile period and copulate frequently when guarding is impossible ([7.18.28](/7.18.28#sec-28.4.4)), females seek extra-pair partners with more elaborate ornaments ([7.18.29](/7.18.29#sec-29.3.3)), and males that lose paternity may reduce their care ([7.18.31](/7.18.31)). Microsatellite analyses of mammals tell a similar story. In banded mongooses (*Mungos mungo*), which live in mixed-sex groups, the three oldest males of each group sired on average 85 percent of the pups, by concentrating their mate guarding on the oldest and most fecund females (Nichols and colleagues 2010).

## 30.6. Polyandry

### 30.6.1. Why Should a Female Mate with Several Males?

Females may gain direct and indirect benefits from polyandry, from extra sperm and nuptial gifts to genetic benefits for their offspring ([7.18.29](/7.18.29#sec-29.3.3)). In species with paternal care, the most important direct benefit is often additional care: a male that has mated with a female, and so may be the father of her young, may help to raise them.

**Example 30.6.1 (The Dunnock)** The dunnock (*Prunella modularis*), a small European songbird studied by Nick Davies in the Cambridge Botanic Garden, has an exceptionally variable mating system: in the same population there are monogamous pairs, polygynous trios (one male, two females), polyandrous trios (two unrelated males sharing one female) and polygynandrous groups (two males sharing two females). Females defend exclusive territories; males defend territories that may encompass one or two female territories, either alone or together with a second, subordinate (beta) male.

Measurements of reproductive success, with maternity and paternity assigned by DNA fingerprinting, show why the system is variable. A female raises the most young in polyandry, where both males help to feed her brood, and the fewest in polygyny, where she shares one male's help with another female. A male does best in polygyny, where two females with part-time help together raise more young than one female with full-time help, and worst as an alpha or beta male in polyandry, where the extra young produced by a third feeder do not compensate for shared paternity. Male help depends on mating: a male feeds a brood only if he has copulated with the female during her fertile period, and the amount he feeds increases with his share of the matings, which predicts his share of paternity. A female maximizes the total help she receives by sharing her matings equally between the two males (Davies and colleagues 1992).

The behavior of each bird matches its interests. In polygyny, the dominant female tries to drive the other female away, while the male tries to keep both. In polyandry, the female solicits copulations from the beta male, often out of sight of the alpha, while the alpha male guards her closely and chases the beta away. Polygynandry is a stalemate, in which the alpha male cannot evict the beta male and neither female can evict the other. Which outcome arises depends on the competitive abilities of the individuals (young males are more often beta males), on the adult sex ratio (after hard winters, which kill more females, the population is male-biased and polyandry more common) and on habitat (in dense vegetation a female can more easily escape the alpha male's guarding).

### 30.6.2. Cooperative Polyandry

In dunnocks polyandry benefits the female at the expense of the alpha male, who tries to prevent it. Under some conditions, however, the extra success of a team of males may outweigh the cost of sharing paternity, and males should agree to share a female, a *polyandry threshold* analogous to the polygyny threshold (Gowaty 1981). This is expected when food is scarce, so that more than two adults are needed to raise the young, or when competition for territories or females is intense, so that a team holds them more effectively. Male lions in larger coalitions take over prides more often and hold them longer, and their lifetime success increases with coalition size; coalitions often consist of brothers, so **kin selection** ([7.10.16](/7.10.16#def-16.3.1)) contributes, but even unrelated males do better in pairs than alone (Packer and colleagues 1991). In some populations of the Galápagos hawk (*Buteo galapagoensis*), groups of two to eight unrelated males defend a territory with a single female, share matings without squabbling and share paternity roughly equally (Faaborg and colleagues 1995; DeLay and colleagues 1996). The large size of the female, which may let her control matings, and the long tenure of territories defended by large teams, which compensates each male for sharing in any one year, may explain the difference from dunnocks.

### 30.6.3. Sex-Role-Reversed Polyandry

In most birds, if one parent deserts it is the male, because he has the opportunity to desert first and more to gain from additional mates. Some birds show that the roles can be reversed. In the snail kite (*Rostrhamus sociabilis*) of Florida, either parent may desert, and which one does depends on the operational sex ratio and thus on which sex has the better chance of finding a new mate; desertion is more frequent when food is abundant, so that the remaining parent can raise the young alone (Beissinger and Snyder 1987).

Shorebirds (Charadriiformes) include most of the known cases of polyandry. In phalaropes a female defends one male, lays a clutch for him to incubate and then seeks another (sequential polyandry). In spotted sandpipers (*Actitis macularius*) and jacanas, a female defends a large territory in which several males incubate her clutches or care for her chicks at the same time (resource defense polyandry). Shorebirds lay a fixed clutch of at most four large eggs that fit snugly together; experiments in which a fifth egg was added reduced hatching success. If the clutch cannot be enlarged, the only way a female can increase her output when conditions are good is to lay more clutches, which requires more males. In spotted sandpipers, productive breeding grounds allow a female to lay up to five clutches of four eggs in 40 days, a mass of eggs about four times her own body mass; her success is limited by the number of males she can find to incubate them. Females are about 25 percent larger than males and compete for them (Lank and colleagues 1985), a case of **sex-role reversal** ([7.18.28](/7.18.28#sec-28.1.5)).

In jacanas, the female's territory may overlap the smaller territories of up to four males. Females compete to take over neighboring territories and sometimes kill the chicks of other females to make their males available for new clutches (Emlen and colleagues 1989), the sex-reversed counterpart of male infanticide in lions. Polyandry has a cost for each male, because his co-mates may fertilize the eggs he incubates: in the wattled jacana (*Jacana jacana*) in Panama, males with a female of their own fathered all the chicks they raised, whereas in polyandry 41 percent of broods contained chicks sired by another male of the harem, 17 percent of all chicks (Emlen and colleagues 1998).

## 30.7. A Hierarchical View

Three groups of factors have appeared in this chapter: life-history constraints (whether the young can feed themselves, whether males can contribute to feeding them), ecology (the distribution of food, breeding sites and mates) and social conflicts (coercion, deception and resistance within a population). Ian Owens and Peter Bennett (1997) suggested that each is most important at a different level of comparison:

| Comparison | Main factors | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Between orders or families | Life-history constraints | Pheasants (precocial young, males can desert) versus hawks (altricial young need both parents) |
| Between species within a genus | Ecology | Seed-eating weaverbirds (rich, easily found food) are more often polygynous than insect-eating ones |
| Between individuals in a population | Social conflicts | Dunnocks, burying beetles, pied flycatchers |

The hierarchy is a useful guide to method. Differences between families are best studied by phylogenetic comparative methods; differences between related species by comparing their ecology; and differences within populations by field experiments that manipulate the options of individuals, as in the red-winged blackbird, pied flycatcher and dunnock studies.

## Summary

- A mating system (Definition 30.1.1) describes who mates with whom and how many partners each sex has: monogamy, polygyny, polyandry, polygynandry and promiscuity (Definition 30.1.2). Social and genetic mating systems often differ.
- Without male care, females distribute themselves according to resources and males according to females (Emlen and Oring); the spatial clumping and temporal synchrony of receptive females set the potential for polygyny. Experiments with voles and bluehead wrasse confirm the causal sequence; mammals illustrate the comparative pattern.
- Forms of polygyny:

  | Form | Males compete by | Examples |
  |---|---|---|
  | Female defense (Definition 30.3.1) | Guarding groups of females | Elephant seals, red deer, gelada baboons |
  | Resource defense (Definition 30.3.2) | Defending sites females need | Bullfrogs, wrasse, blackbirds, great reed warblers |
  | Scramble competition (Definition 30.3.4) | Searching | Ground squirrels, explosive breeders |
  | Lek ([Definition 13.2.4](/7.10.13#def-13.2.4)) | Displaying on resource-free arenas | Sage grouse, manakins, kob |

- The polygyny threshold model (Definition 30.3.3, Proposition 30.3.1): a female should choose a mated male if the gain in territory quality exceeds the cost of sharing; supported by experiments in red-winged blackbirds, while pied flycatcher females are deceived by polyterritorial males and burying beetle females resist polygyny.
- Males aggregate on leks because of hotspots, predation, attraction, hotshots or female preference for aggregations; the hotspot model explains clustering but not the extreme skew of mating success.
- Obligate monogamy (seabirds, oystercatchers) arises where both parents are needed; in songbirds monogamy reflects limited opportunities for polygyny; in mammals mate guarding, paternal care and infanticide have been proposed. Extra-pair paternity (Definition 30.5.1) is widespread.
- Polyandry brings extra care (dunnocks, Example 30.6.1, where the mating system reflects sexual conflict), cooperative benefits to males (lions, Galápagos hawks) or allows females with a fixed clutch to lay more clutches (spotted sandpipers, jacanas).
- Life history, ecology and social conflict explain mating systems at successively lower taxonomic levels (Owens and Bennett).

## Questions

1. Why are most mammals polygynous while most birds are socially monogamous?

   **Answer** In mammals, gestation and lactation are done by females alone, so a male can contribute little to raising the young (except by defense, carrying or provisioning in a few groups), and a male's reproductive success increases mainly with the number of females he can monopolize; females have little to lose from sharing a male. In most birds, the young are fed by the parents, both sexes can incubate and feed, and a male's help often increases the number of young raised substantially, so both sexes gain from staying together, and females lose from sharing a male's help. Moreover, many bird species are monogamous because competition among males and aggression among females limit the opportunities for polygyny, as removal experiments show, rather than because monogamy maximizes male success.
2. In the polygyny threshold model, a secondary female on a good territory and a monogamous female on a poor territory that settled at the same time should have about equal reproductive success. Explain why, and give two reasons why this prediction may fail in a field study.

   **Answer** If females are free to settle and choose the best available option, each female's choice should equalize, at the margin, the success expected from the alternatives: a female settles as secondary on a good territory only if her success there is at least that of the best unmated male's territory, and later females fill the options until they are equivalent (an ideal free distribution). The prediction fails if (1) females are not "free": primary females exclude secondary ones, or settlement is constrained; (2) females are not "ideal": they cannot assess territory quality or male mating status (pied flycatchers, deceived by polyterritoriality); (3) the distribution of territory quality is coarse, so a female may have to choose between options that differ substantially; (4) the measure of success misses components such as offspring quality or the female's own survival.
3. Use the dunnock data in Example 30.6.1 to calculate the total number of young raised per adult in each mating combination, and discuss why the "best" system for the population need not be the one that evolves.

   **Answer** Monogamy: 5.0 young per pair, 2.5 per adult. Polygyny: two females raising 3.8 each, 7.6 young for three adults, 2.53 per adult. Polyandry: 6.7 young for three adults, 2.23 per adult. The differences per adult are small, and the outcome in each group is not chosen to maximize group productivity but results from the conflicting interests of individuals: the alpha male tries to bring about polygyny, females try to bring about polyandry, and which system results depends on who wins, as with any conflict of interest. Selection acts on individual strategies, so a system that is worse for the group (here, polyandry) can be common when females win.
4. Design an experiment to distinguish between the hotspot and hotshot hypotheses for a lekking bird.

   **Answer** Remove the most successful males from the lek. The hotshot hypothesis predicts that the remaining males reorganize around the next most attractive male, and that the location of the lek may shift with him; the hotspot hypothesis predicts that other males take over the vacated central territories and their mating success, and that the lek stays in place. Alternatively, force the most successful males to move (for example by making their territories unsuitable) and see whether the other males follow them (hotshot) and whether females follow them (female choice of males) or stay at the site (hotspot). Measuring female movement routes and nesting density around leks, and manipulating them (for example by creating new foraging areas), tests the hotspot hypothesis directly.
5. In spotted sandpipers, females compete for males. Why does a fixed clutch size of four eggs favor polyandry, and what would you predict for female competition in years of poor food supply?

   **Answer** With a fixed clutch, a female cannot convert extra resources into a larger clutch; she can only increase her output by laying more clutches, each of which needs a male to incubate it. When food is abundant, a female can produce eggs faster than males can incubate them, so males become the limiting resource, the operational sex ratio becomes female-biased, and females compete for males and desert each after laying. In years of poor food, females can produce fewer clutches, so they should need fewer males, compete less, and more often remain monogamous, possibly helping with incubation; the degree of polyandry should fall.
6. (Open question) The extra-pair paternity rate varies from near zero in some seabirds to more than 50 percent in superb fairy-wrens. Propose hypotheses for this variation and describe a comparative test.

   **Answer** Hypotheses: (1) the value of male care: where male care is essential (seabirds), females risk losing it if they are caught seeking extra-pair copulations, and males retaliate by reducing care, so extra-pair mating is rare; (2) breeding density and synchrony: higher density increases encounters with potential extra-pair males, and synchrony may increase or decrease the opportunity for comparison; (3) genetic variation: where there is more genetic variance in fitness among males, females gain more by seeking good or compatible genes; (4) female control: in species where females can easily escape guarding, extra-pair mating is common; (5) life history: long-lived species with long pair bonds may gain more from fidelity. A comparative test would collect extra-pair paternity rates for many species with molecular data, together with measures of male care (removal experiments), breeding density, synchrony, genetic diversity and adult survival, and test the predicted correlations with phylogenetic comparative methods, controlling for shared ancestry.
