This chapter brings the course to two of its boundaries. The first is human language, the communication system that most sharply separates our species from others and that has repeatedly been used to test the limits of animal cognition. We review the attempts to teach language-like systems to apes, dolphins, parrots and dogs; the features that distinguish language from other communication; and the development, brain mechanisms and evolution of human speech, which parallel those of birdsong in instructive ways. Human behavior is treated only where it continues the comparative argument of earlier chapters, in speech and in nonverbal emotional expression; the evolutionary analysis of human behavior in general is in [7.10.34](/7.10.34#sec-34.7). The second boundary is practical: what behavioral biology contributes to the welfare of animals kept by people, to the conservation of wild species, to the control of pests and disease vectors, and to medicine. Communication in general is treated in [7.18.37](/7.18.37)–[7.18.40](/7.18.40), song learning in [7.18.17](/7.18.17), and the cognitive capacities that language may build on in [7.18.43](/7.18.43)–[7.18.46](/7.18.46).

## 47.1. Teaching Language to Animals

### 47.1.1. Speech and Sign

The first attempts to give an ape language raised it as a child. Winthrop and Luella Kellogg raised the young chimpanzee Gua with their son Donald in 1931–1932; after nine months Gua understood some spoken commands but produced no words. Keith and Catherine Hayes raised Viki from infancy in the late 1940s; after years of training she could produce four approximations of words ("mama", "papa", "cup" and "up") with great effort, often shaping her lips with her hand. Both families noted that the young apes did not babble or spontaneously imitate sounds, as human infants do.

The failure was long attributed to the ape vocal tract, which has a high larynx and a tongue largely confined to the mouth ([7.10.33](/7.10.33#sec-33.4)). Modeling of the vocal tract of macaques from X-ray recordings, however, showed that a primate vocal tract could produce many distinct vowels and intelligible speech if a brain capable of controlling it were present (Fitch and colleagues 2016): the main limitation is neural, the lack of voluntary control over the larynx and of vocal learning (Section 47.3.2).

Beatrice and Allen Gardner reasoned that chimpanzees, which communicate with many gestures, might learn a gestural language. From 1966 they raised Washoe, a wild-born chimpanzee about a year old, in a trailer in which her human companions communicated only in American Sign Language (ASL). Washoe rarely imitated signs spontaneously, and most were taught by *molding*, physically guiding her hands; after about four years of training she used 132 signs. The gorilla Koko, trained by Francine Patterson with ASL-based signs and spoken English, was reported to use several hundred signs, and the chimpanzee Nim Chimpsky (named after Noam Chomsky), trained by Herbert Terrace, learned about 125 signs in under four years. By comparison, a typical child of two learns about ten new words a day.

The decisive analysis was Terrace's own (Terrace and colleagues 1979). Frame-by-frame analysis of videotapes showed that most of Nim's multi-sign utterances repeated, in part, signs his teacher had just made; that he seldom initiated conversations or waited his turn; and that his utterances did not grow longer with age as children's do: their mean length stayed at about 1.1–1.6 signs, whereas the mean length of utterance of deaf and hearing children rose steeply in the third year. Nim's longest utterance, of 16 signs, was "Give orange me give eat orange me eat orange give me eat orange give me you": clear in meaning, but without grammatical structure. Similar analyses of films of Washoe suggested the same pattern of prompting and imitation.

### 47.1.2. Symbols: Lana, Sarah and Kanzi

Other projects used visual symbols. Duane Rumbaugh trained the chimpanzee Lana to press keys on a computer keyboard embossed with *lexigrams*, arbitrary geometric symbols, in "Yerkish", a simple artificial grammar; she learned sequences such as "Please machine give M&M period". David Premack taught the chimpanzee Sarah to arrange colored plastic tokens on a magnetic board ([7.18.44](/7.18.44#sec-44.4.1)). Lana's sequences, critics noted, were trained chains of key presses in which the "please" and "machine" keys had no reason to mean anything to her; pigeons can learn to peck keys in a fixed order too ([7.18.44](/7.18.44#sec-44.4.3)). Sarah's performance included learning a new word from a description: told "brown color-of chocolate" with no chocolate present, she later picked the brown disk when asked for "brown", an instance of reference to an absent object.

The most impressive case was Kanzi, a bonobo (*Pan paniscus*) born in 1980. While his adoptive mother Matata was being trained, unsuccessfully, to use lexigrams, the infant Kanzi was present but not trained; when Matata was removed, he turned out to use the lexigrams spontaneously to request food and places, and to understand spoken English words. Sue Savage-Rumbaugh and colleagues (1993) tested Kanzi at eight years and a two-year-old girl, Alia, with several hundred novel spoken requests ("Put the pine needles in your ball", "Go get the carrot that's in the microwave"), with the speaker out of sight to avoid Clever Hans cues; both responded correctly to about 70 % of them. Kanzi used lexigrams to name places he wanted to go to, a form of displaced reference. Skeptics noted that most of the requests could be carried out correctly by understanding the nouns and the context alone ("carry the straw" has only one sensible interpretation), that in the few pairs of reversible sentences ("make the doggie bite the snake" versus "make the snake bite the doggie") his performance was weak on strict scoring, and that about 94 % of his own productions were single lexigrams. Kanzi comprehended far more than he produced, and he acquired his symbols by observation, as children do, which suggests that early exposure matters; but his productions never showed the grammatical expansion of a three-year-old child.

### 47.1.3. Dolphins, Parrots and Dogs

Louis Herman and colleagues (1984) studied comprehension rather than production. The bottlenose dolphin Akeakamai learned a gestural language of about 50 signs with a strict word order, in which "pipe hoop fetch" meant "take the hoop to the pipe" and "hoop pipe fetch" meant "take the pipe to the hoop". She carried out 85 % of 193 novel sentences of up to five signs correctly, distinguished sentences that differed only in word order, searched for objects named but out of view, and ignored semantically anomalous commands. Her performance shows the comprehension of a simple syntax, in which the order of signs determines the roles of objects, by an animal trained only on comprehension. A California sea lion, Rocky, showed similar abilities with a different gestural system.

Irene Pepperberg trained the African gray parrot Alex (1976–2007) with the model–rival method ([7.18.46](/7.18.46)) to use English vocal labels for objects, colors, shapes, materials and numbers, and to answer questions such as "What color is the four-corner wood?" with labels he produced himself; his abilities with concepts and number are in [7.18.43](/7.18.43). The very wide range of possible answers made Clever Hans effects unlikely, but Pepperberg did not test Alex's grammar.

Dogs have added a new dimension. Rico, a border collie, knew the names of about 200 objects and could fetch them on command; when asked for an object with a name he had never heard, from a set containing one unfamiliar object, he chose the new object and retained the association weeks later, a form of the *fast mapping* by which children learn words (Kaminski, Call and Fischer 2004). Chaser, a border collie trained by John Pilley over three years, learned the names of more than 1000 objects, with no sign that her vocabulary was saturating, understood three verbs ("take", "paw", "nose") combined with any object name, and learned common nouns for classes of objects ("ball", "Frisbee", "toy") (Pilley and Reid 2011). Such dogs are exceptional: most dogs, even after months of training, fail to learn more than a couple of object names, and the rare "gifted word learners" are mostly border collies living with very engaged owners (Fugazza and colleagues 2021).

### 47.1.4. Evaluation

The animal language projects show that great apes, dolphins, sea lions, parrots and some dogs can learn arbitrary symbols for objects, actions and properties, can use some of them for absent things, and can comprehend simple combinations whose meaning depends on order. They have not shown the spontaneous, productive combination of symbols into sentences with hierarchical structure, or the use of symbols to comment on the world rather than to request things, that characterize the language of a three-year-old child. Much early enthusiasm came from labeling the animals' signs with English words and interpreting their combinations generously; anecdotes such as Washoe's "water bird" for a swan cannot be distinguished from naming two things in view. The projects remain informative for the questions they raised: which components of language does each species have, and how much depends on early immersion in a language-using community?

## 47.2. What Is Special about Language?

### 47.2.1. Design Features

Rather than asking whether an animal "has language", the linguist Charles Hockett asked which properties of human language are present in other communication systems.

**Definition 47.2.1 (Design Features of Language)** The **design features of language** are the properties by which Charles Hockett (1960) characterized human language and compared it with animal communication. They include the vocal-auditory channel; interchangeability (any speaker can also be a listener); semanticity (signals have specific meanings); arbitrariness (the form of a signal does not resemble its meaning); discreteness (signals are built from distinct units); displacement (reference to things remote in time or space); productivity (new messages can be produced and understood); cultural transmission (the conventions are learned); duality of patterning (meaningless units, phonemes, combine into meaningful units, words, which combine into sentences); and prevarication (the ability to lie or speak of the hypothetical).

![Some of Hockett's design features in five communication systems. Filled: present; half-filled: limited or debated; empty: absent. The honey bee dance refers to food that is not present (displacement), but it is graded and partly iconic; vervet monkey alarm calls are arbitrary and functionally referential ([7.18.40](/7.18.40)), but their form is innate and they are not combined; songbird song is learned and recombines syllables, but changing the order does not change a meaning; language-trained apes use arbitrary, discrete symbols, sometimes for absent things, with little productivity. Only human language has all the features, and duality of patterning is found in no other system.](./plots/hockett.py)

Each feature, taken alone, occurs somewhere in animal communication. What is unique is their combination, and above all productivity built on duality of patterning: a few dozen phonemes combine into tens of thousands of words, and words into an unlimited number of sentences, whose meanings are composed from the meanings of their parts and their arrangement.

### 47.2.2. Combinatorial Signals in Animals

Many animals combine signal units. Birdsong has *phonological syntax*: syllables are arranged in species-typical orders, and songs with abnormal order are less effective, but the combinations do not carry different meanings. Some animals do combine meaningful units. Male Campbell's monkeys (*Cercopithecus campbelli*) add an acoustic suffix "-oo" to their alarm calls, which changes a specific predator alarm into a more general alert (Ouattara, Lemasson and Zuberbühler 2009). Japanese tits (*Parus minor*) give an "ABC" call that elicits vigilance and a "D" call that elicits approach, and combine them in the order ABC–D when mobbing a predator. Playbacks of ABC–D elicited both vigilance and approach, whereas the reversed order D–ABC, which tits do not produce, elicited a weaker response; tits also responded to ABC followed by the recruitment call of willow tits, a combination never heard in nature, suggesting that they compose the meanings of the parts (Suzuki, Wheatcroft and Griesser 2016). Recent work has reported combinatorial structure in the vocalizations of bonobos and chimpanzees as well. These systems show that combining meaningful units is not uniquely human, but they involve two-unit combinations of a small set of calls, not open-ended hierarchical structure.

### 47.2.3. Recursion and the Faculty of Language

Marc Hauser, Noam Chomsky and Tecumseh Fitch (2002) distinguished the *faculty of language in the broad sense*, which includes the sensory-motor and conceptual systems used in language (perception of speech sounds, vocal imitation, concepts, memory, theory of mind), most of which are shared with other animals, from the *faculty of language in the narrow sense*, which they hypothesized consisted only of *recursion*, the ability to embed structures within structures of the same kind ("the cat [that the dog [that the man owned] chased] ran away").

The hypothesis stimulated experiments on artificial grammars. Cotton-top tamarins learned to discriminate sequences of the form (AB)ⁿ, a finite-state pattern, but not AⁿBⁿ, which requires counting or nesting, whereas human participants learned both (Fitch and Hauser 2004). European starlings, after tens of thousands of training trials, discriminated AⁿBⁿ sequences of starling song elements from other patterns (Gentner and colleagues 2006), and Bengalese finches responded more to song sequences that violated a center-embedded structure than to novel sequences that respected it (Abe and Watanabe 2011). In both cases critics showed that the birds could have used simpler acoustic or positional cues: in the finch study, grammatical test songs were acoustically more similar to the training songs than ungrammatical ones (Beckers and colleagues 2012). No experiment has yet shown that animals represent recursive structure, and Fitch, Hauser and Chomsky (2005) later emphasized that the uniqueness of human language may lie less in any single ability than in the way several abilities are combined in the service of communication.

## 47.3. Human Speech

### 47.3.1. Development

Children acquire their native language without explicit instruction in grammar; parents correct the truth of what children say much more than its grammar. The development of speech has a sensitive phase. Newborns discriminate the speech sounds of all languages, but during the second half of the first year their discrimination narrows to the contrasts of the language they hear: Japanese infants lose the ability to discriminate English /r/ and /l/, and English infants lose non-native contrasts of Hindi, by 10 to 12 months (Werker and Tees 1984). Infants babble from about six months, producing repetitive syllables whose sounds gradually come to resemble the surrounding language, and at about the same age they shift their attention from the eyes to the mouth of a speaking adult, returning to the eyes once they begin to speak (Lewkowicz and Hansen-Tift 2012). The ability to acquire native-like pronunciation and grammar declines with the age of first exposure: grammar can be learned to a native level if learning begins before about ten years of age, and the ability declines gradually afterward. Language acquisition thus has a **critical period** in the developmental sense ([7.16.49](/7.16.49#sec-49.4.2)), although a gradual rather than a sharp one.

Social interaction gates speech learning. Nine-month-old American infants exposed to Mandarin in twelve play sessions with a live speaker retained the ability to discriminate a Mandarin consonant contrast, but infants exposed to the same speech from video or audio recordings did not (Kuhl, Tsao and Liu 2003). The parallels with song learning in birds are striking ([7.18.17](/7.18.17)): a sensory phase in which a template of the species' or population's sounds is acquired, a sensorimotor phase of practice (babbling, subsong) refined by auditory feedback, a decline of plasticity with age, and the importance of live social tutors. Both systems produce **dialects** that differ among localities and can mark group membership.

### 47.3.2. The Neural Basis of Speech

Since the nineteenth century, damage to *Broca's area* in the left inferior frontal cortex has been associated with difficulties in producing fluent, grammatical speech, and damage to *Wernicke's area* in the left posterior temporal cortex with difficulties in understanding. These regions are parts of a larger, left-lateralized network of frontal and temporal areas and their connections, which is activated by language whether spoken, written or signed ([7.16.49](/7.16.49#sec-49.4.1)). Speech perception is multimodal: when a video of a face saying "ga" is dubbed with the sound "ba", most listeners hear "da" (the *McGurk effect*, McGurk and MacDonald 1976), and the superior temporal sulcus, which responds to the movements of the mouth, eyes and hands of others, integrates seeing and hearing speech.

Speech is a form of **vocal learning**, which is rare among animals: it has evolved independently in humans, cetaceans, pinnipeds, elephants, bats, and three orders of birds (songbirds, parrots and hummingbirds). Erich Jarvis and colleagues argued that vocal learners share a specific circuit (figure below). In humans, the laryngeal motor cortex projects directly to the motor neurons of the larynx in the nucleus ambiguus of the brainstem; in monkeys, which do not learn their calls, this direct projection is absent or very sparse, and calls are controlled through the midbrain periaqueductal gray. The songbird analogue is the projection from the pallial nucleus RA to the tracheosyringeal motor neurons. Both groups also have a forebrain loop through the **basal ganglia** that is necessary for learning. Comparison of gene expression across the brains of songbirds, parrots, hummingbirds, humans and non-learning species found that the brain regions of vocal learning circuits share specializations of gene expression that are absent in non-learners, convergent at the molecular level (Pfenning and colleagues 2014).

The transcription factor *FOXP2* connects the two systems. Mutations in one copy of the gene cause a disorder of the coordination of mouth and face movements in speech, with effects on grammar, in the members of a British family; in zebra finches, *FoxP2* is expressed in Area X, and reducing its expression there impairs song imitation ([7.18.17](/7.18.17)). The gene is highly conserved and not a "language gene"; the history of its supposed recent selection in humans is discussed in [7.10.33](/7.10.33#sec-33.4).

### 47.3.3. The Evolution and Adaptive Value of Language

When language evolved is uncertain; estimates range from about two million to fifty thousand years ago, depending on whether one asks about speech anatomy, about the cognitive capacities used in language, or about fully modern syntax. Neanderthals shared the modern human form of *FOXP2* and had a hyoid bone of modern form, which suggests that some capacity for speech is at least several hundred thousand years old, but neither fossils nor genes reveal grammar.

Several hypotheses have been proposed for the selective advantages that favored language:

- *Gestural origins.* Great apes produce several dozen types of intentional gestures, directed at particular recipients, adjusted to whether the recipient is looking, and repeated or elaborated when unsuccessful (Hobaiter and Byrne 2011); their vocalizations, in contrast, are largely involuntary. Language may have begun in gesture and only later moved into the vocal channel.
- *Teaching technology.* In experiments with modern humans learning to make stone flakes, teaching with gestures and especially with speech transmitted the skill much better than imitation or reverse engineering (Morgan and colleagues 2015), consistent with a coevolution of tool making, teaching and language over the last two million years ([7.18.46](/7.18.46)).
- *Social bonding.* Robin Dunbar proposed that language replaced grooming as a means of maintaining relationships when human groups grew too large to groom everyone, and that much of conversation is about other people (gossip); dialects allow speakers to identify members of their own group, an *affiliation* function.
- *Sexual selection.* Geoffrey Miller argued that verbal display is a courtship signal, which would explain the redundancy of human vocabularies. The evidence is weak: there are no sex differences in vocabulary size, and if verbal skill is used in mate choice by both sexes, selection would be mutual.

These hypotheses are not mutually exclusive, and they are hard to test because language evolved once, in one lineage. What is clear is that language is extremely costly in brain tissue and extended development, and that it multiplies the value of social learning: it allows causal explanations, instructions about absent things and the transmission of knowledge across generations with high fidelity, the conditions for cumulative culture ([7.18.46](/7.18.46)).

### 47.3.4. Emotional Expression and Nonverbal Communication

Speech is embedded in an older system of nonverbal signals that humans share with other primates. Darwin's *The Expression of the Emotions in Man and Animals* (1872) argued that many expressions are inherited and comparable across species. Some human expressions have clear homologues in apes. Laughter derives from the open-mouth "play face" and panting vocalization of apes at play: acoustic analysis of tickling-induced vocalizations of human infants and the great apes produced a tree that matched the phylogeny of the species (Davila Ross, Owren and Zimmermann 2009). The human smile shares features with the silent bared-teeth display, which in many monkeys signals submission and in chimpanzees also affiliation (van Hooff 1972).

Paul Ekman proposed that a small set of basic emotions (happiness, sadness, fear, anger, surprise, disgust and, by some accounts, contempt) have universal facial expressions, recognized across cultures and produced even by people blind from birth. The claim of universality is now debated: people in small-scale societies with little contact with Western media agree less with Western observers on the meaning of posed expressions, and facial movements depend on context and culture more than the basic emotion model allows (Gendron and colleagues 2014; Barrett and colleagues 2019). As in other animals, the honesty of human expressive signals depends on how hard they are to fake: genuine smiles involve the muscle around the eye (orbicularis oculi) that few people can contract voluntarily; the pitch and formant frequencies of the voice are partly reliable cues of body size and strength, **index signals** in the sense of [7.18.39](/7.18.39); and people detect lies only slightly better than chance, at about 54 % accuracy on average (Bond and DePaulo 2006).

## 47.4. Applying Behavioral Biology

### 47.4.1. Animal Welfare

People keep billions of animals on farms, in laboratories, in zoos and as companions, and the question of how these animals fare is partly a question for behavioral biology.

**Definition 47.4.1 (Animal Welfare)** **Animal welfare** is the state of an individual animal as regards its attempts to cope with its environment (Broom 1986), including its physical health and its affective experiences; it varies on a scale from very poor to very good and can be assessed scientifically, whereas judgments about how animals ought to be treated belong to ethics.

Three conceptions of welfare have shaped its study (Fraser and colleagues 1997): *biological functioning* (health, growth, reproduction), *affective states* (the absence of suffering and the presence of positive experiences) and *natural living* (the ability to perform behaviors important to the species). They often agree but sometimes conflict: a hen in a cage may be healthy but unable to dust-bathe or nest. Two frameworks are widely used:

- *The Five Freedoms*, formulated after the British Brambell report of 1965 and codified by the Farm Animal Welfare Council (1979): freedom from hunger and thirst; from discomfort; from pain, injury and disease; to express normal behavior; and from fear and distress.
- *The Five Domains* (Mellor and Reid 1994; Mellor and colleagues 2020), in which four physical or functional domains, nutrition, physical environment, health and behavioral interactions, give rise to experiences in the fifth domain, the mental state, which is the animal's welfare. The model emphasizes that good welfare requires positive experiences, such as play, exploration and comfort, and not only the absence of negative ones.

Because animals cannot report their feelings, welfare is assessed through physiological, behavioral and cognitive indicators:

- *Stereotypies*, repetitive, invariant behaviors without apparent function (pacing, bar biting, tongue rolling, feather pecking), develop in barren or restrictive environments and are reduced by enrichment. Among captive carnivores, stereotypic pacing and infant mortality are highest in species that in the wild have large home ranges and travel long distances daily, such as polar bears and lions, which suggests that these species are poorly suited to confinement (Clubb and Mason 2003).
- *Preference and motivation tests* ask animals what they want and how much they will work for it. Marian Dawkins proposed borrowing *demand* analysis from economics: if an animal continues to pay for a resource as its price rises (inelastic demand), the resource is important. American mink in farm housing worked as hard for access to swimming water as for food, and their urinary cortisol rose as much when access to water was blocked as when food was withheld (Mason, Cooper and Clarebrough 2001).
- *Cognitive bias tests* measure the interpretation of ambiguous stimuli. Rats trained that one tone predicts food and another an unpleasant noise responded to intermediate tones as if expecting the unpleasant outcome after being housed in unpredictable, poor conditions, a "pessimistic" bias analogous to that of anxious or depressed people (Harding, Paul and Mendl 2004). Similar tests have been used in many species, including honeybees.
- *Qualitative behavior assessment* asks trained observers to describe the demeanor of animals ("relaxed", "fearful", "playful"), and welfare protocols for farms combine such measures with health and behavioral records.

*Environmental enrichment*, the addition of structures, objects, social companions and foraging opportunities to captive environments, is the main practical response. In research, the principles of the "3Rs" (replacement, reduction and refinement; Russell and Burch 1959) govern animal use, and the ethics committees that review studies of vertebrates apply welfare science to experimental design.

### 47.4.2. Conservation Behavior

Behavior affects the success of conservation in many ways: animals choose habitats, migrate, avoid threats and reproduce by behavioral rules that evolved in environments different from those humans are now creating.

- *Migration and stopover sites.* Migratory birds depend on chains of **stopover sites** along their **flyways** ([7.18.20](/7.18.20)). The loss of tidal flats along the Yellow Sea, the critical refueling site of the East Asian–Australasian Flyway, has been followed by rapid declines in shorebirds that depend on it, such as the great knot and the bar-tailed godwit (Studds and colleagues 2017). Climate warming creates **phenological mismatch** between the timing of migration or breeding and the timing of food peaks.
- *Noise.* Anthropogenic noise masks acoustic signals and reduces their **active space** ([7.18.38](/7.18.38)). Great tits in noisy urban areas sing with higher minimum frequencies than in quiet forests (Slabbekoorn and Peet 2003); many species sing louder (the Lombard effect), at different times of day or with altered song structure, and noise from roads and industrial sites can reduce the density and breeding success of birds and alter the composition of communities (Francis, Ortega and Cruz 2009).
- *Light.* Artificial light at night disrupts orientation and rhythms. Sea turtle hatchlings, which crawl toward the brighter horizon over the sea, crawl toward illuminated roads and buildings instead; migrating birds are attracted to and collide with lit buildings and towers; nocturnal insects are trapped by lights. Such cases are **ecological traps** ([7.18.26](/7.18.26)), in which a cue that once indicated a suitable habitat or route now leads animals astray.
- *Captive breeding and reintroduction.* Animals raised in captivity may lack behaviors needed in the wild: recognition and avoidance of predators, foraging skills, migration routes and appropriate mate preferences. Captive-bred animals can be trained to recognize predators by pairing a predator model with alarm signals or mild aversive events, an application of observational conditioning and the social learning of enemies ([7.18.46](/7.18.46)). California condor chicks were raised with hand puppets resembling adult condors so that they would not imprint on humans (**filial imprinting**, [7.18.5](/7.18.5)). Reintroduced whooping cranes were taught a migration route by following ultralight aircraft; in the reintroduced population, the accuracy of migration improved with the age of the oldest birds in the flock, showing that migratory skill is socially learned and accumulates with experience (Mueller and colleagues 2013).

### 47.4.3. Pests and Disease Vectors

Knowledge of the behavior of harmful animals allows them to be controlled with fewer broad-spectrum poisons:

- *Pheromones.* Synthetic sex **pheromones** are used to monitor insect pests in traps, to remove males by mass trapping, and to disrupt mating by saturating the air so that males cannot find females, as in the control of codling moths in orchards and the pink bollworm in cotton. Pheromones are species-specific and leave natural enemies unharmed.
- *Light and food lures.* Many night-flying insects are attracted to ultraviolet light, and light traps (black-light lamps) are used to monitor and reduce moth pests; mixtures of sugar, vinegar and alcohol attract armyworm moths.
- *Push–pull.* In East Africa, maize fields are intercropped with *Desmodium*, whose volatiles repel stemborer moths (push), and bordered with Napier grass, which attracts them to lay eggs on a plant on which their larvae survive poorly (pull); the method reduces stemborer damage and also suppresses the parasitic weed *Striga* (Khan and colleagues 2011).
- *Rodents.* Rats are neophobic and learn quickly to avoid foods that make them ill (**conditioned taste aversion**, [7.18.41](/7.18.41)), so fast-acting poisons produce bait shyness; slow-acting anticoagulants, whose effects appear days after feeding, and prebaiting with unpoisoned bait exploit these behavioral rules. Social transmission of food preferences ([7.18.46](/7.18.46)) spreads acceptance of baits that others have eaten safely.
- *Disease vectors.* Female mosquitoes find hosts by the carbon dioxide, odors and heat they emit, and many malaria vectors bite indoors late at night, which is why insecticide-treated bed nets and indoor spraying are effective. Their widespread use has selected not only for physiological resistance but for behavioral change: in some regions, vector populations now bite earlier in the evening or outdoors, escaping the nets (*behavioral resistance*). Odor-baited traps and spatial repellents target host-seeking behavior directly.
- *Sterile insect technique.* Mass-reared males sterilized by radiation are released to mate with wild females, which then lay infertile eggs; the method eradicated the New World screwworm from North America. Its success depends on the mating competitiveness of the released males, a behavioral property that mass rearing can degrade.

These methods are components of **integrated pest management**, which combines biological, cultural, behavioral and chemical control to keep pests below damaging levels.

### 47.4.4. Evolutionary Medicine and the Study of Human Behavior

**Evolutionary medicine** ([7.10.34](/7.10.34#sec-34.6)) applies evolutionary reasoning to health and disease, and behavior is part of it. Many symptoms of infection, including fever, lethargy, loss of appetite and social withdrawal, are not failures of the body but an organized *sickness behavior*, produced by the action of cytokines on the brain, which conserves energy and supports immune defense (Hart 1988); suppressing them may not always help. The rise of obesity is an **evolutionary mismatch** ([7.10.33](/7.10.33#sec-33.6.3)) between appetites shaped by scarce and fluctuating food and the modern abundance of energy-dense food. The "thrifty gene" hypothesis, that alleles favoring fat storage were selected during famines, has received little support from genetic data; the alternative "drifty gene" hypothesis proposes that once the risk of predation, which had favored lean bodies, was relaxed, the upper limit of body fat drifted without strong selection (Speakman 2008). Comparative studies of social behavior inform research on autism: genes associated with social unresponsiveness in honeybees overlap significantly with genes implicated in autism in humans (Shpigler and colleagues 2017), and the role of **oxytocin** in the social bonds of prairie voles ([7.16.36](/7.16.36)) has motivated clinical trials of oxytocin for social difficulties, with mixed results.

The application of behavioral biology to humans also carries lessons from history. John Watson's conditioning of fear in the infant "Little Albert" (1920) would not be permitted today and was never properly replicated; it is remembered as much for its ethics as for its results. Egas Moniz received the Nobel Prize in 1949 for prefrontal leucotomy, the surgical severing of frontal lobe connections in psychiatric patients, which was performed on tens of thousands of people before its devastating effects on personality were widely acknowledged; the procedure rested on observations of calmer behavior in operated chimpanzees and on very thin clinical evidence. Konrad Lorenz, who later shared the Nobel Prize for founding ethology ([7.18.1](/7.18.1)), joined the National Socialist party and in 1940 wrote that domestication caused genetic degeneration in humans as in domestic animals and should be counteracted by eugenic measures, writings he later regretted. These episodes show how easily biological ideas about behavior can be used to justify policies, and why the fact that a behavior is natural, evolved or adaptive implies nothing about whether it is desirable or right (the naturalistic fallacy, [7.10.34](/7.10.34#sec-34.7.1)). The study of human behavior from an evolutionary perspective, discussed in [7.10.34](/7.10.34#sec-34.7.3), is most useful when it generates testable hypotheses and least useful when it is used to excuse what people do.

## Summary

- Chimpanzees raised as children (Gua, Viki) did not learn to speak, mainly for lack of vocal control rather than anatomy. Signing apes (Washoe, Koko, Nim) learned 100–250 signs, but their combinations were short, prompted and without grammar; symbol-using apes (Lana, Sarah) learned trained sequences; Kanzi acquired lexigrams by observation and understood many spoken requests, but produced mostly single symbols. Akeakamai understood word order in a gestural language, Alex used vocal labels, and the dogs Rico and Chaser learned hundreds to over 1000 object names by fast mapping.
- The design features of language (Definition 47.2.1) are individually present in animal systems, but their combination, especially duality of patterning and productivity, is unique to humans. Japanese tits and Campbell's monkeys combine meaningful calls; no animal has been shown to represent recursive structure.
- Speech develops with a sensitive phase of perceptual narrowing and babbling, gated by social interaction, paralleling song learning. It depends on a left-lateralized network, multimodal perception, and a direct cortical projection to the laryngeal motor neurons and a basal ganglia loop, convergent with the song system of birds; *FOXP2* affects both.
- Hypotheses for the evolution of language include gestural origins, teaching technology, social bonding and sexual selection; language makes cumulative culture possible. Human emotional expressions have primate homologues (laughter, smile), but the universality of basic emotions is debated.
- Animal welfare (Definition 47.4.1) is assessed by stereotypies, preference and demand tests, cognitive bias and qualitative assessment within the Five Freedoms and Five Domains frameworks. Behavioral biology informs conservation (stopover sites, noise, light, reintroduction), pest and vector control (pheromones, push–pull, bait shyness, behavioral resistance, sterile males) and evolutionary medicine; its history warns against the naturalistic fallacy.

## Questions

1. Terrace concluded that Nim did not learn language. What features of Nim's signing supported this conclusion, and why did they require videotape analysis to detect?

   **Answer** Most of Nim's multi-sign utterances repeated, partly or wholly, signs that his teacher had just produced; he rarely initiated signing or took turns; his mean length of utterance did not increase with age, unlike children's; and his long utterances were repetitive strings without grammatical structure. These features are invisible in summary records that list the signs used; only a frame-by-frame analysis of the interaction shows what the teacher signed just before each utterance and the timing of turns.
2. Using Hockett's design features, explain why vervet alarm calls are considered more language-like than the honey bee dance in some respects and less in others.

   **Answer** Vervet calls are vocal, arbitrary (the leopard call does not resemble a leopard), discrete and functionally referential, and their use (though not their form) is learned and refined by young monkeys. The honey bee dance is not vocal, is graded rather than discrete, and is partly iconic (the angle of the waggle run corresponds to the direction of food relative to the sun); its form is innate. But the dance has displacement, referring to food that may be kilometers away, and a limited productivity, since it can convey any combination of distance and direction, whereas vervet calls refer only to immediately present predators and cannot be combined to form new messages.
3. Why is the comparison of the Japanese tit calls ABC–D and D–ABC informative, and what further experiment strengthens the conclusion that the tits combine meanings?

   **Answer** The two sequences contain the same units, so a difference in response shows that the order matters, a property of combinatorial structure rather than of the calls themselves. The tits responded strongly only to the natural order ABC–D. The stronger evidence is the playback of ABC followed by the willow tit's recruitment call, a combination tits never hear: they responded with both vigilance and approach, as if they composed the meanings of the parts rather than learning the combination as a single signal.
4. Compare the development of song in zebra finches with the development of speech in human infants. Identify three parallels and one difference.

   **Answer** Parallels: (1) a sensitive period in which auditory experience of adult sounds shapes a template, followed by a decline of plasticity with age; (2) a phase of practice (subsong, babbling) in which vocal output is refined by auditory feedback, and deafening or lack of feedback disrupts learning; (3) social tutors are more effective than recordings (live Mandarin speakers versus video for infants; live tutors for many songbirds). A difference: human speech is used to convey an open-ended set of meanings through grammar, whereas song conveys species, individual and motivational information but not compositional meaning; also, in many songbirds only males learn to sing.
5. A zoo records that its polar bear paces for several hours a day. Using welfare science, explain how you would assess whether the bear's welfare is poor and what changes you would test.

   **Answer** Pacing is a stereotypy, which in carnivores with large ranges is associated with restricted environments. One would quantify the time spent pacing and when it occurs (for example, before feeding, suggesting frustrated foraging motivation), measure physiological indicators (fecal glucocorticoid metabolites), examine health and possibly cognitive bias, and test the bear's preferences and willingness to work for resources (larger or more complex areas, pools, foraging puzzles, variable feeding times). Enrichment that lets the bear search for and process food, and more space and complexity, should reduce pacing if it reflects frustrated motivation; a reduction in pacing together with improvements in other indicators would show that welfare improved. Because stereotypies can persist as habits after conditions improve, pacing alone is not a sufficient measure.
6. Explain why fast-acting poisons are less effective against rats than slow-acting anticoagulants, and why prebaiting helps.

   **Answer** Rats are neophobic and sample new foods in small amounts; if a food makes them ill soon afterward, they form a strong conditioned taste aversion and avoid it (bait shyness), and survivors may even influence others. Slow-acting anticoagulants cause symptoms only days after feeding, too late to be associated with the bait, so rats continue to eat it. Prebaiting with unpoisoned bait first overcomes neophobia, so that the rats eat a lethal amount when the poison is introduced; social transmission of food preference from rats that have eaten the safe bait further increases acceptance.
7. (Open question) Mosquito populations exposed to insecticide-treated bed nets have shifted toward outdoor and early-evening biting in some regions. Discuss how this could be distinguished from an ecological change in the mosquito community, and what it implies for vector control.

   **Answer** A shift could result from selection for heritable changes in biting time and location within a species (behavioral resistance), from phenotypic plasticity (mosquitoes deterred by nets feed elsewhere), or from a change in species composition, if outdoor-biting species become relatively more abundant when indoor biters are suppressed. To distinguish them, one would identify species by molecular methods, compare biting times before and after net distribution within each species, rear mosquitoes from different populations in common conditions to test for heritable differences, and look for genetic associations with biting behavior. The implications are that control must target behaviors that remain, using outdoor and odor-baited traps, spatial repellents, larval control, house improvement and, possibly, gene drives, and that monitoring of vector behavior must accompany monitoring of insecticide resistance.
