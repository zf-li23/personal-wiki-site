The previous four chapters treated the pumping and rhythm of the heart ([7.14.12](/7.14.12), [7.14.13](/7.14.13)), pressure and flow in the vessels ([7.14.14](/7.14.14)), and exchange and local regulation in the microcirculation ([7.14.15](/7.14.15)). This chapter assembles them into a controlled system: what quantity the body regulates, with which sensors, where the signals are integrated, through which effectors, and which mechanisms are responsible on the three time scales of seconds, minutes and days. The basis of neural control (**reflex arc**, **autonomic nervous system**, nucleus of the solitary tract) is in Chapters 5–7 ([7.14.7](/7.14.7)), the concepts of feedback and gain are in Chapter 1 ([7.14.1](/7.14.1)), and the renal mechanisms of long-term regulation are developed in Chapter 24 ([7.14.24](/7.14.24)). Because this is the last chapter on the circulatory system, its final section gives a comparative summary of the circulatory systems of the various animal groups.

## 16.1. Controlling Pressure or Controlling Flow

### 16.1.1. The Reasoning from Parallel Resistances

The blood flow each organ receives is determined by the arterial pressure and the resistance of that organ, $F_i = P/R_i$; all organs are in parallel, so the total flow is $F = P/R_{\text{total}}$ with $1/R_{\text{total}} = \sum_i 1/R_i$ ([7.14.14](/7.14.14#sec-14.3.6)). The body can choose to stabilize one of two quantities: cardiac output $F$ or arterial pressure $P$. Suppose skeletal muscle needs more blood flow during exercise, and compare the two designs.

If cardiac output is controlled ($F$ fixed): the resistance of skeletal muscle $R_{\text{muscle}}$ falls, the total parallel resistance falls with it, and $P = F R_{\text{total}}$ falls. The muscle blood flow $P/R_{\text{muscle}}$ may increase, but the increase is limited by the fall in $P$; the $R_i$ of the other organs are unchanged, and their blood flow $P/R_i$ decreases passively. If they resist by also lowering their resistance, $P$ falls further and the perfusion of all organs is compromised. This is a "zero-sum game": whatever one organ gains, the others must lose.

If arterial pressure is controlled ($P$ under feedback): the resistance of skeletal muscle falls and the total resistance falls, but $P$ is kept constant, so the cardiac output $P/R_{\text{total}}$ increases and the increment in muscle blood flow is substantial. Among the other organs, the viscera are influenced by the autonomic nerves and their resistance rises moderately, but because $P$ is unchanged their blood flow is reduced only slightly; the critical organs (brain and heart) have strong **autoregulation**, their resistance is nearly unchanged or even lowered, and their blood flow is assured.

**Example 16.1.1 (Quantitative comparison of the two designs)** At rest, let the arterial pressure be $100\,\mathrm{mmHg}$ and the resistances of skeletal muscle, viscera and brain be $4$, $2$ and $4\,\mathrm{PRU}$; the total resistance is $1/(0.25 + 0.5 + 0.25) = 1\,\mathrm{PRU}$, the cardiac output is $100\,\mathrm{mL\,s^{-1}}$, and the three receive $25$, $50$ and $25\,\mathrm{mL\,s^{-1}}$. During exercise the resistance of skeletal muscle falls to $0.5\,\mathrm{PRU}$.

- Cardiac output fixed: total resistance $= 1/(2 + 0.5 + 0.25) = 0.364\,\mathrm{PRU}$, $P = 36\,\mathrm{mmHg}$; muscle $73$, viscera $18$, brain $9\,\mathrm{mL\,s^{-1}}$: the brain receives only 36% of its former flow, and the person would lose consciousness immediately.
- Arterial pressure fixed (with visceral resistance rising to $4\,\mathrm{PRU}$ through sympathetic action): muscle $200$, viscera $25$, brain $25\,\mathrm{mL\,s^{-1}}$, and cardiac output rises to $250\,\mathrm{mL\,s^{-1}}$, 2.5 times the resting value.

:::

In sum, in a system that controls pressure, blood pressure remains stable when an organ "demands more blood": the increase in total output is the main source of supply (increment first), the moderate release of flow by non-critical organs is a secondary supplement (stock adjustment), and the critical organs are given priority protection (critical protection). The heart is therefore designed as a pressure generator: its output is determined by venous return and neural regulation so as to meet the total demand of the organs, while blood pressure is strictly stabilized as the common "water supply pressure" of the whole body ([7.14.12](/7.14.12#sec-12.4.3)). Global control of pressure with local control of flow is the starting point for understanding all the mechanisms of this chapter.

### 16.1.2. Components of the Regulatory System

A regulatory system needs four parts: a sensor that monitors the controlled quantity, an integrating center that compares it with the set point, an effector that makes the change, and the afferent and efferent pathways connecting the three. For arterial blood pressure:

| Part | Implementation in the circulation |
|---|---|
| Controlled quantity | Arterial blood pressure (short term); extracellular fluid volume and arterial blood pressure (long term) |
| Sensors | Arterial baroreceptors, low-pressure receptors of the heart and lungs, chemoreceptors; the afferent arteriole and macula densa of the kidney |
| Integrating center | Medullary cardiovascular center, modulated by the hypothalamus and cortex |
| Effectors | Heart (rate, contractility), arterioles (resistance), veins (capacity), adrenal medulla, kidney (excretion of salt and water) |

The basic elements of bulk flow (pressure difference, one-way valves, conduits, medium; [7.14.12](/7.14.12#sec-12.1.2)) make up the controlled "apparatus"; the regulatory system is the fifth element it lacks. This regulation is mainly **negative feedback**, with **feedforward control** in a few cases (the central command at the onset of exercise, Section 16.7.1) and dangerous **positive feedback** (decompensated shock, Section 16.7.2).

## 16.2. Regulation of Cardiac Output

### 16.2.1. Intrinsic Determinants of Stroke Volume

**Cardiac output** is $\mathrm{CO} = \mathrm{HR}\times\mathrm{SV}$. Heart rate is determined by the **sinoatrial node** and regulated by the autonomic nerves and by factors such as extracellular $\ce{K+}$ and $\ce{Ca^2+}$ that affect the time parameters of the sinoatrial action potential ([7.14.13](/7.14.13#sec-13.5)). Stroke volume is $\mathrm{SV} = \mathrm{EDV} - \mathrm{ESV}$ (Definition 12.5.1), and each of the two terms has its own determinants.

End-diastolic volume (EDV) is determined by three intrinsic factors:

1. Ventricular filling pressure: the higher the atrial pressure (the more venous return), the larger the EDV.
2. Filling time: the longer the filling time, the larger the EDV; the faster the heart rate, the shorter the diastole and the smaller the EDV. At resting heart rates filling occurs mainly in early diastole, so heart rate has little effect below about $150\,\mathrm{min^{-1}}$.
3. Ventricular **compliance**: when compliance increases, EDV is larger at the same filling pressure; myocardial hypertrophy, fibrosis and ischemia reduce compliance and EDV (diastolic heart failure).

End-systolic volume (ESV) is determined by three factors:

1. **Preload** (EDV itself): increased EDV makes myocardial contraction stronger (the **Frank–Starling mechanism**) and ejects more, but the absolute value of ESV may also increase.
2. **Afterload** (arterial pressure): when afterload increases, ESV increases.
3. Contractility: when myocardial contractility is enhanced (sympathetic nerves, catecholamines, inotropic drugs), ESV falls. An increased heart rate is itself accompanied by increased contractility (the force–frequency relationship), which lowers ESV.

The change in SV is the net result of changes in EDV and ESV; the expression of these factors on the **pressure–volume loop** is described in [7.14.12](/7.14.12#sec-12.5.2). Extrinsic regulation acts on them through three routes: the high-pressure arterial baroreceptors, the chemoreceptors and the low-pressure receptors of the heart and lungs (Section 16.3).

### 16.2.2. Venous Return and Mean Circulatory Filling Pressure

The heart can pump out only what flows back to it. In the steady state cardiac output must equal venous return, so the question becomes: what determines venous return?

**Definition 16.2.1 (Mean Circulatory Filling Pressure and Venous Return)** The pressure in the circulatory system after the heart is stopped and blood has redistributed until the pressure is equal everywhere is called the **mean circulatory filling pressure**, about $7\,\mathrm{mmHg}$; when only the systemic circulation is considered it is called the mean systemic filling pressure $P_{\mathrm{msf}}$, with a similar value. The volume of blood flowing from the peripheral veins back to the right atrium per unit time is called **venous return**.

The mean filling pressure expresses how full the vascular system is: the more blood relative to vascular capacity, and the higher the tension of the vessels (especially the veins), the higher it is. Infusion of blood equal to 20% of blood volume raises it to about $14\,\mathrm{mmHg}$, maximal sympathetic stimulation raises it to about $17\,\mathrm{mmHg}$, and hemorrhage or complete sympathetic inhibition lowers it. Guyton regarded it as the "upstream pressure" that drives venous return:

$$
\mathrm{VR} = \frac{P_{\mathrm{msf}} - P_{\mathrm{ra}}}{R_{\mathrm{VR}}}, \tag{16.1}
$$

where $P_{\mathrm{ra}}$ is the right atrial pressure (**central venous pressure**, [7.14.14](/7.14.14#def-14.6.1)) and $R_{\mathrm{VR}}$ is the resistance to venous return, normally about $1.4\,\mathrm{mmHg\,min\,L^{-1}}$ and located mostly in the veins. Normally $(7 - 0)/1.4 = 5\,\mathrm{L\,min^{-1}}$.

**Definition 16.2.2 (Venous Return Curve and Cardiac Function Curve)** The curve of venous return as a function of right atrial pressure (Eq. (16.1)) is called the **venous return curve**; the curve of cardiac output as a function of right atrial pressure is called the **cardiac function curve**.

The venous return curve is a straight line of slope $-1/R_{\mathrm{VR}}$: venous return is zero when right atrial pressure rises to $P_{\mathrm{msf}}$; when right atrial pressure falls below about $-2$ to $-4\,\mathrm{mmHg}$, the large extrathoracic veins collapse where they enter the thorax ([7.14.14](/7.14.14#sec-14.6.1)), return no longer increases, and a plateau appears. The cardiac function curve is another way of drawing the **Frank–Starling mechanism**: the higher the right atrial pressure (filling pressure), the greater the cardiac output, up to a plateau.

### 16.2.3. The Intersection of the Two Curves

The heart and the vascular system each give a relation between cardiac output and right atrial pressure, and both must be satisfied simultaneously, so the operating point is the intersection of the two curves (Guyton 1955).

B: the intersection of the renal pressure natriuresis curve (schematic) with salt and water intake determines long-term arterial pressure: doubling intake raises blood pressure only from 100 to about 112 mmHg; shifting the pressure natriuresis curve right by 30 mmHg (impaired renal sodium excretion) raises blood pressure to 130 mmHg](./plots/guyton-curves.py)

The graphical analysis illustrates several important facts:

1. Merely strengthening cardiac contraction (shifting the cardiac function curve upward) with the venous return curve unchanged increases cardiac output only a little: the heart pumps the right atrial pressure low and soon meets the plateau of venous return. For cardiac output to increase 4–5-fold in exercise, $P_{\mathrm{msf}}$ must be raised at the same time (venoconstriction, **muscle pump**) and $R_{\mathrm{VR}}$ lowered (dilation of skeletal muscle and visceral vessels lowers the resistance to return), moving the venous return curve up and making it steeper.
2. Transfusion or infusion raises $P_{\mathrm{msf}}$, the venous return curve shifts in parallel to the right, and cardiac output increases. Cardiac output is proportional to circulating blood volume: this is the result of the cooperation of the baroreceptor reflex, the Bainbridge reflex and the Frank–Starling mechanism.
3. In heart failure the cardiac function curve shifts downward, and at the operating point right atrial pressure is raised and cardiac output lowered. Subsequent renal retention of salt and water and sympathetic excitation raise $P_{\mathrm{msf}}$, the venous return curve shifts to the right, and cardiac output is partly restored, at the price of raised venous and capillary pressure (**edema**, [7.14.15](/7.14.15#sec-15.3.5)).

## 16.3. Neural Regulation of Cardiovascular Activity

### 16.3.1. Innervation of the Heart and Vessels

The heart receives dual innervation from the **sympathetic nervous system** and the **parasympathetic nervous system** ([7.14.7](/7.14.7), [7.14.13](/7.14.13)): the preganglionic neurons of the cardiac sympathetic nerves lie in the intermediolateral column of the thoracic spinal cord, and the postganglionic fibers release norepinephrine, which acts on $\beta_1$ receptors to speed the heart rate, accelerate atrioventricular conduction and strengthen contractility (positive chronotropic, dromotropic and inotropic effects); the preganglionic neurons of the cardiac vagus lie in the nucleus ambiguus and the dorsal motor nucleus of the vagus in the medulla, and the postganglionic fibers release acetylcholine, which acts on M₂ receptors to slow the heart rate, slow atrioventricular conduction and weaken atrial contractility. At rest vagal **tone** predominates.

Most blood vessels are innervated only by sympathetic vasoconstrictor fibers: postganglionic fibers release norepinephrine, which binds $\alpha_1$ receptors on the smooth muscle and causes contraction. Sympathetic innervation is densest in skin vessels, followed by skeletal muscle and viscera, and sparsest in coronary and cerebral vessels. At rest the sympathetic vasoconstrictor fibers fire continuously at 1–3 impulses per second, keeping the vessels in a certain degree of constriction (vascular tone), so that vessels can be constricted or dilated simply by raising or lowering this **tone**. Constriction of arterioles increases resistance, and constriction of veins reduces capacity and raises the mean filling pressure. There are also a few vasodilator fibers: in some nonprimate animals (cat, dog) skeletal muscle has sympathetic cholinergic vasodilator fibers that dilate the muscle vessels at the onset of the emergency reaction and take no part in the reflex regulation of blood pressure; a few organs (salivary glands, gastrointestinal glands, external genitalia) have parasympathetic vasodilator fibers that regulate only local blood flow; and there are peptidergic and nitrergic fibers releasing VIP, NO and others.

### 16.3.2. The Medullary Cardiovascular Center

**Definition 16.3.1 (Cardiovascular Center)** The groups of neurons in the central nervous system concerned with regulating cardiovascular activity are called cardiovascular centers; the part located in the medulla that maintains basal tonic cardiovascular activity and carries out the basic cardiovascular reflexes is called the **medullary cardiovascular center**.

If the brainstem is cut above the medulla, the animal's blood pressure is essentially unchanged; if it is cut between the medulla and the spinal cord, blood pressure falls immediately to about $40\,\mathrm{mmHg}$: tonic cardiovascular activity therefore originates in the medulla. Its main components are:

- The nucleus of the solitary tract (NTS): located dorsally in the medulla, it receives almost all afferents from baroreceptors, chemoreceptors and cardiopulmonary receptors and is the first integrating station.
- The rostral ventrolateral medulla (RVLM, containing the adrenergic neurons of the C1 area): the "generator" of sympathetic vasoconstrictor and cardiac sympathetic activity; its neurons discharge continuously and, through the sympathetic preganglionic neurons of the spinal cord, maintain vascular tone and cardiac sympathetic tone; if it is destroyed, blood pressure falls to the spinal level.
- The caudal ventrolateral medulla (CVLM, containing the A1 area): inhibitory interneurons that convert the excitation from the NTS into inhibition of the RVLM.
- Cardiac vagal neurons: the nucleus ambiguus and the dorsal motor nucleus of the vagus, the "cardioinhibitory area".

Interneurons of the NTS thus have two kinds of projection, excitatory and inhibitory: the excitatory one to the cardioinhibitory area, the inhibitory one (through the CVLM) to the vasoconstrictor area. Above the medulla, the hypothalamus (defense reaction, distribution of blood flow in thermoregulation), the limbic system and the cortex (emotion, anticipation of exercise) can all modulate these centers and change cardiovascular activity from the top down; the sympathetic preganglionic neurons of the spinal cord can also gradually recover some tone after transection, but cannot carry out reflex regulation.

### 16.3.3. The Baroreceptor Reflex

**Definition 16.3.2 (Baroreceptor Reflex)** When arterial blood pressure rises, the afferent discharge of the baroreceptors in the carotid sinus and aortic arch increases; through the medullary cardiovascular center this strengthens cardiac vagal tone and weakens cardiac sympathetic and sympathetic vasoconstrictor tone, so that heart rate slows, cardiac output falls, peripheral resistance decreases and blood pressure returns downward; when blood pressure falls the opposite occurs. This **negative feedback** reflex is called the **baroreceptor reflex** (also the depressor reflex).

The discovery of the baroreceptors involved an ingenious experiment. In the early twentieth century it was known that stimulating the carotid sinus nerve could lower blood pressure, but it was unclear whether the central nervous system sensed blood pressure directly. In the 1920s and 1930s Heymans performed cross-perfusion experiments in dogs: the head of the recipient animal was connected to the body only by nerves and was perfused by a donor dog, so that the perfusion pressure of the recipient's carotid sinus and aortic arch could be changed independently while the blood environment of its central nervous system remained unchanged. He found that even with unchanged blood pressure in the brain, changing the perfusion pressure of the carotid sinus alone caused reflex changes in the recipient's heart rate and respiration. This proved that the regulation of blood pressure depends on baroreceptors in the carotid sinus and aortic arch acting on the central nervous system through neural reflexes, and not on direct sensing of blood pressure by the center. For this work (together with his work on chemoreceptors) Heymans received the 1938 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine.

Baroreceptors are the endings of sensory neurons embedded in the elastic tissue of the vessel wall; what they sense is not pressure itself but the stretch of the wall. They are concentrated in the carotid sinus (the dilation at the origin of the internal carotid artery) and the aortic arch: in these two places the wall is compliant, the elastic fibers and muscle layer are thin, and the curvature is large, so that the same pressure causes a larger deformation. The afferent fibers of the carotid sinus join the glossopharyngeal nerve (cranial nerve IX) through the sinus nerve (Hering's nerve), with cell bodies in the petrosal ganglion; the afferents of the aortic arch travel in the vagus nerve (cranial nerve X), with cell bodies in the nodose ganglion. Both terminate in the NTS, with glutamate as transmitter acting on AMPA receptors containing the GluR2 subunit. The molecules of mechanotransduction remained unknown for a long time, until Zeng and colleagues (2018) showed that knocking out both **mechanosensitive channels** Piezo1 and Piezo2 in the nodose and petrosal ganglia of mice abolishes the baroreceptor reflex and markedly increases fluctuations of blood pressure, whereas the reflex is essentially normal when only one of them is knocked out (the structure and gating of Piezo are in [7.6.25](/7.6.25)).

Baroreceptors respond both to static pressure and to the rate of pressure change: carotid sinus baroreceptors hardly fire below about $50$–$60\,\mathrm{mmHg}$, after which firing increases with pressure and reaches a maximum at about $170$–$180\,\mathrm{mmHg}$. The gain of the reflex (the response per unit pressure change) is greatest near the normal mean arterial pressure of about $100\,\mathrm{mmHg}$, so the reflex is most sensitive to small deviations at the normal operating point; at a given mean pressure, the faster the pressure rises and the larger the pulsation, the more they fire. The receptors at the two sites differ somewhat in their properties. Compared with the carotid sinus, aortic arch baroreceptors have a higher threshold for static and dynamic pressure changes (by about $30\,\mathrm{mmHg}$), a wider dynamic range, are less sensitive to the rate of pressure change, and are more sensitive to pressure rises than to falls; carotid sinus receptors are especially important at low pressures (such as the fall of head pressure on standing), and the two together control systemic arterial pressure.

The effectors of the reflex are the heart (rate and contractility), arterioles (resistance), veins (capacity) and the adrenal medulla (release of epinephrine, which strengthens cardiac output and constricts vessels, the **sympathoadrenal system**). When blood pressure rises, the NTS is excited and, through the CVLM, inhibits the RVLM, so sympathetic activity falls: heart rate decreases, cardiac output falls and vessels dilate; at the same time the NTS excites the cardioinhibitory area, parasympathetic activity increases, and heart rate falls further. The two pathways differ in speed: the vagal effect on heart rate appears within one or two cardiac cycles, whereas the sympathetic vascular effect takes several seconds to more than ten seconds.

The baroreceptor reflex is a rapid buffering system for blood pressure. In dogs with both sinus nerves and aortic nerves cut (denervation), mean arterial pressure over a long time is similar to that of normal dogs, but the fluctuation of blood pressure during the day is much greater: in normal dogs mean arterial pressure mostly stays between $85$ and $115\,\mathrm{mmHg}$ over 24 hours, whereas after denervation it can vary widely between $50$ and $160\,\mathrm{mmHg}$. The model of panel C reproduces this: the role of the reflex is to reduce fluctuations, not to determine the long-term mean. From the definition of **feedback gain** ([7.14.1](/7.14.1)), the disturbance in panel B causes a fall of about $33\,\mathrm{mmHg}$ in open loop (denervated) and $10\,\mathrm{mmHg}$ in closed loop, so the gain is about $33/10 - 1 \approx 2.3$, comparable to the baroreceptor reflex gain measured in humans and animals (about 2, far below the "infinite" gain of the renal-body fluid mechanism, Section 16.5).

Baroreceptors reset (adapt) within a day or two to sustained high or low pressure, which is why they were long thought not to take part in long-term blood pressure regulation. But recent studies show that chronic electrical stimulation of the carotid sinus afferents (baroreflex activation therapy) lowers blood pressure persistently in patients with resistant hypertension, mainly by reducing renal sympathetic nerve activity, so the baroreceptor reflex does contribute to long-term blood pressure control as well.

The most common challenge to the baroreceptor reflex is a change of posture: on suddenly standing from the supine position, about $500$–$700\,\mathrm{mL}$ of blood pools in the veins of the lower limbs within seconds and arterial pressure at the head falls; without the reflex, mean arterial pressure would fall by about $40\,\mathrm{mmHg}$; the decreased carotid sinus firing speeds the heart and constricts vessels within seconds, and arterial pressure remains nearly unchanged (Section 16.7.3).

### 16.3.4. The Chemoreceptor Reflex

**Definition 16.3.3 (Chemoreceptor Reflex)** The peripheral chemoreceptors in the carotid bodies and aortic bodies sense a decrease in arterial $P_{\ce{O2}}$, an increase in $P_{\ce{CO2}}$ and an increase in $\ce{H+}$ concentration; afferent impulses travel through the sinus nerve and vagus nerve to the NTS and mainly deepen and quicken breathing and excite the vasoconstrictor center. This reflex is called the **chemoreceptor reflex**.

The carotid bodies and aortic bodies are small structures near the carotid bifurcation and the aortic arch with an extremely high blood flow (about $2\,\mathrm{L\,min^{-1}}$ per 100 g, so the arteriovenous oxygen difference is very small); they can sense the composition of arterial blood rather than that of tissue; the mechanism of their sensing cells (glomus cells) is in [7.14.20](/7.14.20). The chemoreceptor reflex is mainly a respiratory reflex; its direct effect on the circulation is to strengthen sympathetic vasoconstrictor tone and raise peripheral resistance, and its direct effect on heart rate is slowing, but because of the deepened breathing (afferents from pulmonary stretch receptors inhibit the vagus) and the release of catecholamines, in the intact animal it usually appears as an increased heart rate and cardiac output, with increased blood flow to the brain and heart and decreased flow to other organs. Normally it contributes little to blood pressure regulation and acts appreciably only when arterial pressure falls below about $80\,\mathrm{mmHg}$ (inadequate blood flow to the chemoreceptors, local hypoxia) and in hypoxia, asphyxia and acidosis. During apnea, the chemoreceptor excitation is not accompanied by changes in breathing, and the direct effect on the heart (bradycardia) and on peripheral vessels (constriction) is revealed; this is precisely the pattern of the diving reflex (Section 16.8.5).

### 16.3.5. Low-Pressure Receptors and the Bainbridge Reflex

Stretch receptors are also present in the walls of the atria, the ventricles and the large vessels of the pulmonary circulation, called cardiopulmonary receptors; because the pressure at their location is low, they are also called low-pressure receptors. What they sense is blood volume (degree of filling), not arterial pressure. Atrial receptors are of two types:

- Type A: they fire during atrial contraction, in step with it, and reflect heart rate.
- Type B: they fire in the late phase of atrial filling (during ventricular systole), and the fuller the atrium, the higher the firing rate, so they measure venous return and are related to both circulating blood volume and central venous pressure.

Afferent fibers travel in the vagus to the NTS and other medullary nuclei and ascend to the paraventricular nucleus of the hypothalamus; the efferent output acts through the autonomic nerves on the heart, vessels and kidney, and through the hypothalamus affects hormone secretion from the pituitary. The effects of low-pressure receptor activation differ from those of the high-pressure receptors:

| | High-pressure receptors (arterial) | Low-pressure receptors (atrial) |
|---|---|---|
| Stimulus | Raised arterial pressure | Increased blood volume, atrial stretch |
| Heart rate | Decreases | Increases (Bainbridge reflex) |
| Vessels | Generalized dilation | Mainly renal vasodilation (reduced renal sympathetic activity) |
| Hormones | | Reduced **antidiuretic hormone** secretion; atrial myocytes, when stretched, secrete **atrial natriuretic peptide** directly |
| Overall effect | Lowers blood pressure | Increases excretion of salt and water and reduces total body fluid |

**Definition 16.3.4 (Bainbridge Reflex)** When pressure in the atria (especially the right atrium) rises and they are stretched, a reflex through vagal afferents increases the heart rate; this is called the **Bainbridge reflex**.

Stretching of the right atrium also directly stretches the sinoatrial node and increases its automaticity by 10%–15%; the reflex itself can increase heart rate by a further 40%–60%. Its significance is to prevent blood from pooling in the veins, atria and pulmonary circulation: when venous return increases the heart rate speeds up, matching cardiac output to venous return. When a large volume of fluid is infused, the Bainbridge reflex speeds the heart whereas the baroreceptor reflex (arterial pressure raised) slows it; the net effect depends on the initial heart rate, the former predominating at a low rate and the latter at a high rate.

At the same time, atrial stretch reduces the secretion of antidiuretic hormone (vasopressin), dilates the afferent arterioles of the kidney, lowers renal sympathetic activity, and atrial natriuretic peptide secreted by the atria makes the kidney excrete more sodium and water ([7.14.24](/7.14.24)). This group of responses originating in the atria and regulating blood volume is often called the volume reflex: when blood volume rises markedly (as in rapid infusion), urine output and sodium excretion can increase several-fold. The volume reflex links the short-term mechanism of blood pressure regulation (neural) with the long-term mechanism (renal).

### 16.3.6. Other Cardiovascular Reflexes

**Definition 16.3.5 (CNS Ischemic Response)** When arterial pressure falls below about $60\,\mathrm{mmHg}$ and blood flow to the medulla is inadequate, $\ce{CO2}$ and acidic metabolites accumulate locally and directly and strongly excite the vasoconstrictor center, so that sympathetic vasoconstrictor tone is extremely strengthened and arterial pressure rises; this is called the **CNS ischemic response**.

It is strongest at an arterial pressure of about $15$–$20\,\mathrm{mmHg}$ and can raise mean arterial pressure to $250\,\mathrm{mmHg}$ within a few minutes, one of the most powerful forms of activation of the sympathetic vasoconstrictor system. But it is not a mechanism of normal blood pressure regulation; it is the last line of defense against a further fall in arterial pressure in emergencies such as severe hemorrhage. A special case is the Cushing response: when intracranial pressure rises to near arterial pressure, the cerebral arteries are compressed and the brain becomes ischemic, causing arterial pressure to rise so that blood can flow into the brain again, while the baroreceptor reflex slows the heart.

There are other reflexes as well: somatic pain usually raises blood pressure (severe pain or strong visceral stimulation can also lower blood pressure and even cause fainting); the exercise pressor reflex caused by muscle mechanoreceptors and metaboreceptors during exercise (group III and IV afferent fibers); pulmonary stretch receptors speed the heart (one source of respiratory sinus arrhythmia); and strong emotion causes, through the hypothalamus and limbic system, a defense reaction (faster heart rate, dilation of skeletal muscle vessels, constriction of visceral and skin vessels) or, conversely, vasovagal syncope (vagal excitation and sympathetic inhibition cause a sudden fall in heart rate and blood pressure).

## 16.4. Humoral Regulation

Hormones and paracrine factors in the blood regulate the cardiovascular system from the other side. Local factors were discussed in [7.14.15](/7.14.15#sec-15.4.5); the main systemic hormones are listed here:

| Hormone | Source and stimulus | Actions |
|---|---|---|
| **Epinephrine** | Adrenal medulla, on sympathetic excitation (emergency, exercise, hypoglycemia) | Strong action on $\beta_1$ receptors: speeds heart rate, strengthens contractility and increases cardiac output; acts on both $\alpha$ and $\beta_2$ receptors: constricts vessels of skin, kidney and gastrointestinal tract and dilates vessels of skeletal muscle, liver and (at low concentration) coronary arteries, with little change in total peripheral resistance; used clinically as a cardiac stimulant |
| Norepinephrine | Mainly from sympathetic postganglionic fibers, a little from the adrenal medulla | Acts mainly on $\alpha_1$ receptors, causing widespread vasoconstriction, increased peripheral resistance and a marked rise in blood pressure; its direct positive chronotropic effect on $\beta_1$ receptors is masked by the heart rate slowing of the baroreceptor reflex; used clinically as a pressor |
| Angiotensin II | Juxtaglomerular cells of the kidney release renin when renal perfusion pressure falls, renal sympathetic activity rises or the $\ce{NaCl}$ reaching the **macula densa** decreases; renin converts angiotensinogen to angiotensin I, which angiotensin-converting enzyme (mainly in lung endothelium) converts to angiotensin II | Strongly constricts arterioles (and also veins), increasing peripheral resistance; acts on the central nervous system to strengthen sympathetic vasoconstrictor tone, cause thirst and promote release of antidiuretic hormone; promotes norepinephrine release from sympathetic nerve endings; stimulates secretion of **aldosterone** from the zona glomerulosa of the adrenal cortex and acts directly on renal tubules to increase reabsorption of $\ce{Na+}$ and water ([7.14.24](/7.14.24)) |
| Antidiuretic hormone (vasopressin) | Synthesized in the supraoptic and paraventricular nuclei of the hypothalamus and released by the neurohypophysis; when plasma osmolality rises or blood volume falls | At physiological concentrations acts mainly on V₂ receptors of the renal collecting duct to increase water reabsorption; in severe hemorrhage its concentration rises and it constricts vessels strongly through V₁ receptors, one of the most powerful vasoconstrictors |
| Atrial natriuretic peptide | When atrial myocytes are stretched | Dilates vessels, causes natriuresis and diuresis, inhibits secretion of renin, aldosterone and antidiuretic hormone, and reduces blood volume ([7.14.24](/7.14.24)) |
| Kinins, histamine, prostaglandins | Produced locally | Dilate vessels and increase permeability ([7.14.15](/7.14.15#sec-15.4.5)) |

The division of labor between humoral and neural regulation lies mainly in time scale: neural regulation acts within seconds, epinephrine and norepinephrine act within ten-odd seconds to minutes, angiotensin II reaches full effect in about 20 minutes, and the renal actions of antidiuretic hormone and aldosterone act within hours to days.

## 16.5. Long-Term Blood Pressure Regulation: The Renal-Body Fluid Mechanism

The baroreceptor reflex resets within days, and the vasoconstrictor action of humoral regulation also weakens through receptor downregulation. What determines long-term arterial pressure is another mechanism: the kidney regulates **extracellular fluid** volume by changing the excretion of salt and water, and thereby regulates blood volume and mean filling pressure. Its core is **pressure natriuresis** (pressure diuresis): when arterial pressure rises, even without changes in nerves or hormones, the sodium and water excreted by the kidney increase sharply (mechanism in [7.14.24](/7.14.24)).

Guyton's argument is as follows (panel B):

1. In the long-term steady state, excretion of salt and water must equal intake, otherwise extracellular fluid volume would increase or decrease without limit.
2. Renal excretion is a steep function of arterial pressure (the renal function curve): when arterial pressure rises from 100 to $150\,\mathrm{mmHg}$, excretion increases several-fold; at about $50\,\mathrm{mmHg}$ there is almost no urine.
3. Long-term arterial pressure can therefore lie only at the intersection of the renal function curve and the intake line. If arterial pressure is above this point, excretion exceeds intake, extracellular fluid volume and blood volume decrease, mean filling pressure falls, and cardiac output and arterial pressure fall until the intersection is regained; below this point the opposite occurs.

The feature of this feedback is that, as long as the renal function curve and intake are unchanged, it can always return blood pressure completely to the former equilibrium point with no residual error, equivalent to an infinite **feedback gain** ([7.14.1](/7.14.1)); but it is very slow, taking hours to days. Two determinants of long-term blood pressure follow: the position of the renal function curve and the intake. Normally the renal function curve is very steep: on the schematic curve of panel B, doubling intake raises blood pressure by only about $12\,\mathrm{mmHg}$; angiotensin II and aldosterone make the curve even steeper (they are suppressed when intake increases, so that more sodium is excreted), and in the intact body a several-fold increase in salt intake raises blood pressure by only a few mmHg, so the blood pressure of normal people is rather insensitive to salt intake. If the renal capacity to excrete sodium is impaired (renal disease, angiotensin II or aldosterone that cannot be suppressed normally), the renal function curve shifts to the right and long-term blood pressure must rise to the new intersection, which is the mechanism of many forms of hypertension; increasing peripheral resistance without changing the renal function curve cannot sustain a rise in blood pressure over the long term, because the increased pressure causes pressure diuresis and reduces blood volume until the pressure returns to its former value (unless the increase in peripheral resistance also occurs in the renal vessels). This argument has been very influential in the study of hypertension, and also controversial: the effects of mechanisms other than the sympathetic nerves and the kidney on the renal function curve itself are still under study.

The mechanisms regulating arterial pressure can be arranged by time of onset: within seconds, the baroreceptor reflex, the chemoreceptor reflex and the CNS ischemic response; minutes to hours, the vasoconstrictor action of the renin–angiotensin system, stress relaxation of vessels ([7.14.14](/7.14.14#sec-14.4.4)), and fluid shifts between capillaries and interstitial fluid ([7.14.15](/7.14.15#sec-15.2.4)); days, the renal-body fluid mechanism and the action of aldosterone, whose gain on long time scales far exceeds that of the other mechanisms.

## 16.6. Circulation of Special Organs

### 16.6.1. Coronary Circulation

The coronary circulation supplies the myocardium: two coronary arteries arise from the root of the aorta, with a resting flow of about $200\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}}$ (table in [7.14.15](/7.14.15#sec-15.4.1); about $70$–$80\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}}$ per 100 g of myocardium), 4%–5% of cardiac output, which can increase 3–4-fold in strenuous exercise. It has three features:

1. High extraction: at rest the myocardium extracts 70%–80% of the $\ce{O2}$ from coronary blood ([7.14.15](/7.14.15#sec-15.2.3)), so an increase in oxygen consumption can be met almost only by increased flow. Coronary blood flow is therefore regulated mainly by metabolism, with adenosine one of the main mediators and NO, $\ce{K+}$, $\ce{CO2}$ and $\ce{H+}$ also taking part; the direct neural action (sympathetic constriction, vagal dilation of coronary arteries) is weak and is masked by the metabolic vasodilation that follows the sympathetic increase in myocardial metabolism.
2. Flow varies with the cardiac cycle: when the ventricle contracts, the myocardium squeezes the vessels within it, and flow within the left ventricular myocardium (especially the subendocardium) is greatly reduced or even reversed during systole; about 70%–80% of left coronary flow occurs during diastole. Diastolic pressure and the length of diastole are therefore the main determinants of coronary perfusion: coronary supply is inadequate when the heart rate is very fast (diastole shortened) or diastolic pressure is very low (as in aortic regurgitation). The pressure in the right ventricle is low, and its flow is reduced very little in systole.
3. The subendocardium is most vulnerable to ischemia: the squeezing in systole is strongest in the subendocardium, and the vasodilator reserve of subendocardial vessels is already partly used at rest, so myocardial ischemia and infarction often begin in the subendocardium. Coronary collaterals enlarge gradually in slowly progressing stenosis and can partly compensate; in acute occlusion there is no time for them to form.

### 16.6.2. Cerebral Circulation

The brain makes up about 2% of body weight, with a resting blood flow of about $750\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}}$ (about $50$–$55\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}}$ per 100 g), about 15% of cardiac output, and oxygen consumption of about 20% of the whole body. The brain has almost no energy reserve: consciousness is lost within 5–10 seconds of cessation of flow, and irreversible damage occurs within a few minutes. Features of the cerebral circulation are:

- Very strong autoregulation: cerebral blood flow is essentially unchanged as mean arterial pressure varies between about $60$ and $150\,\mathrm{mmHg}$ (this range shifts to the right in patients with chronic hypertension).
- Extreme sensitivity to $\ce{CO2}$: when arterial $P_{\ce{CO2}}$ rises from 40 to $70\,\mathrm{mmHg}$, cerebral blood flow roughly doubles. $\ce{CO2}$ crosses the **blood–brain barrier** freely and generates $\ce{H+}$ in brain tissue, dilating arterioles; hypocapnia caused by hyperventilation constricts cerebral vessels and causes dizziness. This mechanism keeps the pH of brain tissue stable and interacts with central chemoreception of respiration ([7.14.20](/7.14.20)).
- Coupling of local neural activity to blood flow (neurovascular coupling): in an active brain region blood flow increases within a second or two, mediated by $\ce{K+}$, NO, prostaglandins and adenosine released by neurons and astrocytes; the BOLD signal of functional magnetic resonance imaging is based on the local change in oxyhemoglobin caused by this coupling.
- The cranial cavity is a rigid closed space: the total volume of brain tissue, blood and cerebrospinal fluid is essentially constant (the Monro–Kellie doctrine), and an increase in any one must be compensated by a decrease in the others; raised intracranial pressure reduces cerebral perfusion pressure (mean arterial pressure minus intracranial pressure). The effect of sympathetic nerves is small, but when arterial pressure rises abruptly they constrict the large cerebral arteries and protect the small vessels from excessive distension.
- Blood–brain barrier: the tight junctions of capillary endothelium let most water-soluble substances pass only through specific transporters ([7.14.7](/7.14.7#def-7.2.1)).

### 16.6.3. Skeletal Muscle Circulation

Skeletal muscle makes up about 40% of body weight; its resting blood flow is only about $3$–$4\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}}$ per 100 g, about $750\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}}$ in total; in strenuous exercise it can increase to $50$–$80\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}}$ per 100 g, about 20-fold, and in well-trained athletes can account for more than 80% of cardiac output. The increased flow comes from metabolic vasodilation (local $\ce{K+}$, adenosine, $\ce{H+}$, $\ce{CO2}$, low $P_{\ce{O2}}$, raised temperature; [7.14.15](/7.14.15#sec-15.4.2)) and capillary recruitment (by the classical estimate only about 20%–25% carry flow at rest, almost all open in exercise); the upstream large vessels follow through flow-mediated NO dilation. Rhythmic contraction makes flow intermittent: in strong contraction intramuscular pressure exceeds arterial pressure and flow stops, resuming on relaxation; at the same time the **muscle pump** increases venous return ([7.14.14](/7.14.14#sec-14.6.3)). During exercise sympathetic vasoconstrictor activity increases and acts on non-exercising tissue, whereas in exercising muscle it is counteracted by local metabolites (functional sympatholysis), so that flow is concentrated on the exercising muscle while arterial pressure is maintained.

### 16.6.4. Skin Circulation

Skin blood flow far exceeds its metabolic needs: it serves mainly thermoregulation, and can be as low as $20$–$50\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}}$ in the cold and as high as $7$–$8\,\mathrm{L\,min^{-1}}$ for the whole skin in a hot environment, nearly 1.5 times resting cardiac output. The skin of the hands, feet, ears, nose and lips has many arteriovenous anastomoses and is controlled almost only by sympathetic vasoconstrictor fibers: in the cold, sympathetic excitation closes the anastomoses and blood returns through the deep veins, reducing heat loss; when warm, sympathetic inhibition opens the anastomoses and a large volume of warm blood flows through the subcutaneous venous plexus to lose heat. The skin of the trunk also has an active vasodilator mechanism accompanying sweating (possibly mediated by transmitters released from sympathetic cholinergic fibers and by bradykinin and NO produced by the sweat glands). Skin vessels also show local responses: prolonged cold causes skin vessels to dilate periodically after constricting (cold-induced vasodilation, the "hunting reaction"), protecting the fingers from frostbite. The integrated mechanisms of thermoregulation are in [7.14.31](/7.14.31).

## 16.7. Integrated Responses

### 16.7.1. Exercise

In strenuous exercise cardiac output can increase from about $5$ to $20$–$25\,\mathrm{L\,min^{-1}}$ (up to $35\,\mathrm{L\,min^{-1}}$ in endurance athletes), mean arterial pressure rises by only 20%–40%, and total peripheral resistance falls to about one third of its former value. Three classes of mechanism act together:

1. Central command: as the motor cortex issues the movement command, it lowers vagal tone and raises sympathetic tone through the cardiovascular centers of the hypothalamus and brainstem, and heart rate rises within a second or two of the onset of exercise, even beginning before exercise (anticipation). This is **feedforward control** in cardiovascular regulation: adjustments are made before blood pressure and metabolism have changed.
2. Reflexes from exercising muscle: mechanoreceptors and metaboreceptors in muscle (sensing $\ce{K+}$, $\ce{H+}$, lactate and so on) cause the exercise pressor reflex through group III and IV afferent fibers, raising sympathetic activity and arterial pressure further; the set point of the baroreceptor reflex is reset to a higher level, so that blood pressure rises in exercise without being opposed by the reflex.
3. Local regulation: metabolic vasodilation in exercising muscle greatly lowers its resistance (a parallel branch, Section 16.1), while the sympathetic nerves constrict viscera, kidney and skin (early in exercise) vessels and the veins; the muscle pump and the respiratory pump increase venous return. As body temperature rises, skin vessels dilate again to lose heat and in a hot environment compete with muscle for blood flow.

|---|---|---|
| Skeletal muscle | about 1 | about 20 |
| Heart (coronary) | 0.25 | about 1 |
| Skin | 0.4 | about 0.6–2 |
| Brain | 0.75 | about 0.75–0.9 |
| Viscera and kidney | about 2.5 | about 1 |
| Total (cardiac output) | about 5 | about 23–25 |

On the Guyton diagram the increase in cardiac output appears as a simultaneous movement of both curves: sympathetic excitation shifts the cardiac function curve up, venoconstriction and the muscle pump raise the mean filling pressure, and dilation of muscle vessels lowers the resistance to venous return (Section 16.2.3).

### 16.7.2. Hemorrhage

In a healthy adult, loss of 10% of blood volume (about $500\,\mathrm{mL}$, the amount given in a blood donation) leaves arterial pressure and cardiac output almost unchanged; with 20%–25% loss cardiac output falls and arterial pressure begins to fall; with 30%–40% loss severe shock appears. Compensation proceeds in steps:

1. Seconds: the baroreceptor reflex (arterial pressure falling, pulsation weakening) and the unloading of the cardiopulmonary receptors speed the heart, strengthen contractility and constrict arterioles and veins; the chemoreceptor reflex joins when arterial pressure is below about $80\,\mathrm{mmHg}$, and the CNS ischemic response when below about $60\,\mathrm{mmHg}$. The skin is pale, cold and clammy (sympathetic excitation of the sweat glands), and the pulse is fast and weak. Through these reflexes arterial pressure can still be maintained at about half of normal after a loss of 30%–40%, whereas without the reflexes a loss of 15%–20% is fatal.
2. Minutes to hours: reverse stress relaxation of the vessels adapts vascular capacity to the reduced blood volume ([7.14.14](/7.14.14#sec-14.4.4)); the fall in capillary pressure draws interstitial fluid into the vessels (transcapillary refill, which can exceed $0.5\,\mathrm{L}$ per hour but dilutes plasma protein and erythrocytes); the renin–angiotensin system and antidiuretic hormone increase vasoconstriction and reduce urine output; thirst.
3. Days to weeks: aldosterone and antidiuretic hormone make the kidney retain water and salt and restore plasma volume; the liver synthesizes plasma proteins; **erythropoietin** stimulates the bone marrow to produce erythrocytes, and the **hematocrit** recovers within 4–8 weeks ([7.14.11](/7.14.11)).

If hemorrhage is excessive, the reduction of coronary flow weakens myocardial contraction, cardiac output falls further, and arterial pressure and coronary flow fall further: this is **positive feedback**, progressive (decompensated) shock. Failure of the vasomotor center from ischemia, increased capillary permeability from tissue hypoxia, and acidosis and the release of toxins accelerate this vicious cycle, which finally enters the irreversible stage. The earlier the transfusion or infusion, the more likely it is to interrupt the cycle.

### 16.7.3. Standing

On standing from the supine position, about $500$–$700\,\mathrm{mL}$ of blood pools within seconds in the veins of the lower limbs and abdomen; **central venous pressure**, **stroke volume** and cardiac output fall (stroke volume by about 30%–40%), and arterial pressure at the head falls. Within seconds to ten-odd seconds the baroreceptor reflex and the cardiopulmonary receptor reflex raise the heart rate by 10–20 beats/min and increase peripheral resistance, so that arterial pressure is restored or even slightly raised; the muscle pump reduces venous pooling during walking. When the reflexes are deficient (autonomic neuropathy, prolonged bed rest, reduced blood volume in astronauts after returning to Earth), standing causes orthostatic hypotension and, in severe cases, fainting; after fainting, lying flat quickly restores cerebral perfusion, so fainting itself is a protective outcome.

## 16.8. Evolution and Comparison: A Comparative Summary of Circulatory Systems

The degree of separation of the heart and the pulmonary circulation in vertebrates is set out in [7.14.12](/7.14.12#sec-12.8.2), and the diversity of open and closed circulations in [7.14.12](/7.14.12#sec-12.8.1). This section compares functionally how the various groups solve several basic problems of the circulation: how one pump serves both gas exchange and supply to the whole body, how the systemic and pulmonary circulations obtain different pressures, and how the circulation redistributes itself during diving and at altitude.

### 16.8.1. Fishes: The Single Circulation

The fish heart consists of the sinus venosus, atrium, ventricle and conus arteriosus (cartilaginous fishes) or bulbus arteriosus (bony fishes) in series, and there is only one pump: heart → gills → body → heart. The gill capillaries are in series with the systemic circulation, and blood pressure falls markedly after the gills: in rainbow trout the mean pressure in the ventral aorta (pre-branchial) is about $30$–$40\,\mathrm{mmHg}$ and in the dorsal aorta (post-branchial) about $20$–$30\,\mathrm{mmHg}$, so the systemic pressure of fishes is low, which limits the speed and distribution of blood flow. By **Laplace's law** and the **Windkessel model**, the bulbus arteriosus of bony fishes acts as an elastic reservoir: it is made of elastin-rich smooth muscle rather than cardiac muscle, has a very high compliance, and buffers the intermittent ejection of the ventricle into a continuous flow through the gills, protecting the delicate gill lamellar capillaries ([7.14.14](/7.14.14#sec-14.5.2)).

The single circulation works in water because the exchange area of the gills is large, gravity in water is offset by buoyancy ([7.14.14](/7.14.14#sec-14.7.2)), and ectothermic fishes have a low metabolic rate. High-metabolism fishes such as tuna approach the supply capacity of **endotherms** with a higher heart rate and blood pressure (the ventral aorta can exceed $90\,\mathrm{mmHg}$), a larger heart and gill area, and **countercurrent heat exchange** in the muscles. The myocardium of fishes is itself supplied with oxygen by the deoxygenated venous blood flowing through the heart (spongy myocardium), and in active fishes there is in addition a coronary supply from post-branchial vessels that serves the compact outer layer of the myocardium.

### 16.8.2. Lungfishes and Amphibians: Partial Separation

Breathing with lungs raises a new problem: if oxygenated blood returning from the lung enters the same heart and mixes completely with systemic venous blood, the value of the lung is greatly reduced. In lungfishes the atrium has an incomplete septum and the ventricle has a partial septum and a spiral valve, so that pulmonary venous blood and systemic venous blood are roughly separated in the heart: more oxygenated blood enters the first few branchial arch arteries leading to the head, and more deoxygenated blood enters the posterior arches leading to the lung. Amphibians have two atria and one ventricle, and the spiral valve in the conus arteriosus and the spongy trabeculae of the ventricular wall reduce mixing to some degree; their skin is another important gas exchange surface, and the pulmocutaneous artery supplies both lung and skin, so even if the blood received by the systemic circulation is mixed, its oxygen content is not low. The systemic and pulmonary pressures of amphibians are similar (about $25$–$35\,\mathrm{mmHg}$), generated by the same ventricle.

### 16.8.3. Reptiles: Intracardiac Shunts

In non-crocodilian reptiles (turtles, lizards, snakes) the ventricle is divided by incomplete muscular ridges into several communicating chambers (cavum venosum, cavum arteriosum and cavum pulmonale), and the left and right systemic aortic arches and the pulmonary artery all arise from the ventricle. This heart can direct blood to different outlets within the ventricle, producing two kinds of shunt: left-to-right (oxygenated pulmonary venous blood re-enters the pulmonary circulation) and right-to-left (deoxygenated systemic venous blood bypasses the lung and enters the systemic circulation).

**Example 16.8.1 (Fluid mechanics of shunting)** If one ventricle ejects into the systemic and pulmonary circulations at the same pressure $P$, the two are in parallel and flow is inversely proportional to resistance: $Q_{\mathrm{p}}/Q_{\mathrm{s}} = R_{\mathrm{s}}/R_{\mathrm{p}}$ ([7.14.14](/7.14.14#sec-14.3.6)). In a turtle during breathing, pulmonary vascular resistance is low, $R_{\mathrm{p}}/R_{\mathrm{s}} \approx 0.5$, and pulmonary blood flow is about twice systemic flow (net left-to-right shunt); during a breath-hold dive, the pulmonary arterioles constrict under vagal (cholinergic) action, $R_{\mathrm{p}}$ rises to several times $R_{\mathrm{s}}$, and pulmonary blood flow falls to a fraction of systemic flow (net right-to-left shunt). The size and direction of the shunt can therefore be regulated by pulmonary vascular resistance alone, without opening and closing of valves; at the same time, the systemic and pulmonary pressures are necessarily the same. In a completely divided heart the two ventricles are in series, $Q_{\mathrm{p}} = Q_{\mathrm{s}}$ regardless of resistance, and the pressures on the two sides can be entirely different.

Although the ventricles of pythons and monitor lizards communicate, the muscular ridge presses against the ventricular wall in systole to form a functional separation, and the systolic systemic pressure can reach 3–5 times the pulmonary pressure: an anatomically incompletely divided heart can also generate two pressures functionally. This occurs in the highly active monitor lizards and in pythons, which need high pressure to support digestion (postprandial metabolic rate can rise more than tenfold), indicating that pressure separation is a recurrent answer to metabolic demand.

The crocodilian ventricle is completely divided, but crocodilians retain two systemic aortic arches: the right aortic arch arises from the left ventricle and the left aortic arch from the right ventricle; at the base outside the heart the two arches communicate through a small opening (the foramen of Panizza), and in the abdominal cavity they are connected by an anastomosis. During normal breathing the left ventricular pressure is high, the left aortic arch is filled during most of systole by oxygenated blood from the right aortic arch, and the right ventricle ejects only into the pulmonary artery, as in mammals. During diving, the connective-tissue "toothed valves" in the pulmonary outflow tract contract actively under adrenergic and cholinergic control, the resistance of the pulmonary outflow rises, right ventricular pressure rises above the pressure in the left aortic arch, and blood from the right ventricle enters the left aortic arch: crocodilians thus achieve a controllable right-to-left shunt in a completely divided heart.

What is the use of shunting? Proposed explanations include: reducing blood flow to the unventilated lung during breath-holding, saving cardiac work; letting the systemic blood use up the $\ce{O2}$ in the lung faster during diving; sending $\ce{CO2}$-rich venous blood to the stomach after a meal to help secrete gastric acid (the left aortic arch of crocodilians supplies the stomach); and regulating the rate of heating and cooling in thermoregulation. Crocodilians and turtles in which the shunt was closed surgically showed little change in growth and diving ability in experiments, so the adaptive significance of shunting is still debated; one possible answer is that in **ectotherms** that breathe intermittently the metabolic rate is low and the cost of a shunt is small, so it need not have a large benefit and may partly be a developmental by-product of cardiac structure.

### 16.8.4. Birds and Mammals: Two Independent Complete Separations

Birds and mammals both have a completely divided four-chambered heart and a complete double circulation, but they evolved independently from different reptilian ancestors: birds, like crocodilians, are archosaurs and retain the right aortic arch; mammals arose from synapsids and retain the left aortic arch. Both independent evolutions were accompanied by the appearance of **endothermy**: the resting metabolic rate of an endotherm is about 5–10 times that of an ectotherm of the same size, requiring high cardiac output and systemic pressure; and the high-pressure systemic circulation and the low-pressure pulmonary circulation must be separated, otherwise the pulmonary capillaries would be burst by the high pressure and **pulmonary edema** would result ([7.14.15](/7.14.15#sec-15.2.5), [7.14.18](/7.14.18)). The heart of a bird is about 50% larger than that of a mammal of the same body weight, with a larger stroke volume and a slightly lower heart rate, and both cardiac output and blood pressure are higher (the mean arterial pressure of the chicken is about $130$–$150\,\mathrm{mmHg}$, higher in turkeys and some small birds), which fits the high metabolic rate of flight; rupture of arteries (aortic aneurysm) is also more common in high-pressure breeds of birds.

### 16.8.5. The Diving Reflex

**Definition 16.8.1 (Diving Reflex)** When breathing stops and the face is immersed in cold water, bradycardia, strong peripheral vasoconstriction and reduced cardiac output appear, while blood flow to the brain and heart is maintained; this reflex is called the **diving reflex** (also the diving response).

The diving reflex is widespread in air-breathing diving vertebrates, such as seals, whales, sea otters, penguins and diving ducks, and also in humans (immersion of the face in cold water slows the heart rate by 10%–25%). In the classic experiments of Scholander and Irving in the 1930s and 1940s, seals were forced to dive: heart rate fell from about $100\,\mathrm{min^{-1}}$ to about $10\,\mathrm{min^{-1}}$, the arteries to muscle, viscera and skin were almost completely closed, and arterial pressure was essentially unchanged. By the reasoning of Example 16.1.1, this is an extreme of a system that controls pressure: total peripheral resistance rises sharply as most of the parallel branches are closed, cardiac output falls in proportion, arterial pressure remains unchanged, and only the brain and heart (and oxygen-sensitive organs) continue to receive flow.

The mechanism of the diving reflex is a combination of the reflexes of this chapter: the trigeminal nerve senses the cold water on the face, the afferent input from pulmonary stretch receptors is lost during apnea, and the lowered arterial $P_{\ce{O2}}$ and raised $P_{\ce{CO2}}$ excite the chemoreceptors; without a change in breathing, the chemoreceptor reflex appears directly as vagal bradycardia and sympathetic peripheral vasoconstriction (Section 16.3.4). The brain and coronary vessels are regulated mainly by metabolism and do not obey the sympathetic constriction. Tissues whose blood flow is shut off switch to anaerobic metabolism, and lactate accumulates in the muscle and enters the blood only after surfacing and restoration of flow.

The responses of free-diving animals in the wild are more graded. Weddell seals in routine dives of up to 20 minutes lower heart rate to about $20$–$40\,\mathrm{min^{-1}}$, constrict the peripheral vessels only partly, rely almost entirely on aerobic metabolism, and show no rise in lactate; extreme bradycardia and lactate accumulation appear only in long dives beyond their "aerobic dive limit" (about 20 minutes). Diving mammals also have a set of adaptations in oxygen stores: a large blood volume (about 15% of body weight in the Weddell seal, about 7% in humans), a high **hematocrit** (about 60%) and a myoglobin concentration more than 10 times that of humans; the spleen is a huge reservoir of erythrocytes, and during a dive it contracts and releases a large number of concentrated erythrocytes into the circulation, raising the hematocrit ([7.14.14](/7.14.14#sec-14.6.4)). These stores give the seal a total oxygen store (per body weight) about 4–5 times that of humans, most of it held in blood and muscle rather than in the lungs (they dive after exhaling, to avoid decompression sickness and the uptake of nitrogen at depth).

### 16.8.6. High-Altitude Adaptation

At altitude the $P_{\ce{O2}}$ of inspired air is lowered, and the response of the circulation varies with time scale and population. In acute hypoxia, the chemoreceptor reflex increases heart rate and cardiac output; the pulmonary arterioles constrict (**hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction**), raising pulmonary arterial pressure ([7.14.18](/7.14.18)), which in some people causes high-altitude pulmonary edema. After days to weeks, **erythropoietin** raises the hematocrit, plasma volume decreases, and cardiac output returns close to the sea-level value. Long-resident highland populations have taken different evolutionary paths: Andeans rely mainly on a higher hemoglobin concentration; Tibetans have a hemoglobin concentration only slightly higher than lowlanders, carry adaptive alleles of genes such as EPAS1 (encoding HIF-2α) that suppress excessive erythrocytosis, and have higher exhaled NO and forearm blood flow and weaker hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction, ensuring tissue oxygen supply by increasing flow rather than oxygen content ([7.14.11](/7.14.11#sec-11.2.5)). High-altitude animals have similar strategies: yaks and pikas have very weak hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction and no pulmonary hypertension, whereas lowland cattle moved to altitude often develop "brisket disease" (edema of the brisket) from pulmonary hypertension and right heart failure.

Several main lines in the evolution of circulatory systems can be read from this table. First, the type of circulation is determined mainly by body size, metabolic rate and mode of oxygen supply: where diffusion suffices there is no need for a circulation, where tracheae supply oxygen directly there is no need for the circulation to carry oxygen, and a high metabolic rate requires a high-pressure closed circulation ([7.14.12](/7.14.12#sec-12.8.1)). Second, the separation of high and low pressure is a recurrent problem: cephalopods boost the pressure before the gills with branchial hearts and after them with the systemic heart, fishes accept the low post-branchial pressure, reptiles allocate pressure by resistance or muscular ridges in incompletely divided hearts, and birds and mammals each independently divided the two ventricles completely. Third, incomplete division is not primitive: intermittently breathing ectotherms trade a regulable shunt for flexibility. Fourth, the principle of regulation is the same in all groups: global control of pressure and local control of flow, with sensors at key positions (the aortic arch and carotid sinus; fishes also have baroreceptors in the branchial arteries), and diving and altitude merely push the same set of reflexes to the extreme.

## Summary

- Global control of pressure, local control of flow (Section 16.1): organs are in parallel, $F_i = P/R_i$; stabilizing arterial pressure means that the demand of an organ is met mainly by an increase in cardiac output (increment first), non-critical organs give up some flow (stock adjustment), and brain and heart are protected (critical protection).
- Cardiac output: $\mathrm{SV} = \mathrm{EDV} - \mathrm{ESV}$; EDV depends on filling pressure, filling time and ventricular compliance, and ESV on preload, afterload and contractility. Venous return is $\mathrm{VR} = (P_{\mathrm{msf}} - P_{\mathrm{ra}})/R_{\mathrm{VR}}$ (Definition 16.2.1, $P_{\mathrm{msf}} \approx 7\,\mathrm{mmHg}$); the operating point is the intersection of the cardiac function curve and the venous return curve (Definition 16.2.2): increasing cardiac output requires raising both the pumping capacity of the heart and venous return.
- Neural regulation: the heart has dual sympathetic ($\beta_1$) and vagal (M₂) innervation, and vessels are innervated mainly by tonic sympathetic vasoconstrictor fibers ($\alpha_1$); the medullary cardiovascular center (Definition 16.3.1) consists of the NTS, RVLM, CVLM and cardiac vagal neurons.
- Baroreceptor reflex (Definition 16.3.2): stretch receptors of the carotid sinus (IX) and aortic arch (X), transduced by Piezo1/2 and integrated in the NTS; gain is greatest near $100\,\mathrm{mmHg}$ and the reflex is a rapid buffering system (gain about 2) that, after denervation, leaves the mean pressure unchanged but increases fluctuation, and it resets within days. The chemoreceptor reflex (Definition 16.3.3) acts below about $80\,\mathrm{mmHg}$, in hypoxia and in acidosis; atrial type A and type B receptors measure heart rate and filling and cause the Bainbridge reflex (Definition 16.3.4), reduced antidiuretic hormone and secretion of atrial natriuretic peptide; the CNS ischemic response (Definition 16.3.5) is the last line of defense below $60\,\mathrm{mmHg}$.
- Humoral regulation: epinephrine (cardiac stimulation), norepinephrine (vasoconstriction), angiotensin II, antidiuretic hormone, atrial natriuretic peptide. Long-term blood pressure is determined by the intersection of the renal pressure natriuresis curve with salt and water intake, with infinite gain but slow speed.
- Special organs: coronary (extraction 70%–80%, left coronary flow mainly in diastole, adenosine); brain (15% of cardiac output, autoregulation over $60$–$150\,\mathrm{mmHg}$, sensitivity to $\ce{CO2}$, neurovascular coupling); skeletal muscle (about 20-fold increase in exercise); skin (thermoregulation, arteriovenous anastomoses).
- Integrated responses: exercise (feedforward from central command, exercise pressor reflex, redistribution by local dilation and sympathetic constriction); hemorrhage (reflexes within seconds, transcapillary refill and hormones within minutes to hours, kidney and hematopoiesis within days to weeks; positive feedback in decompensation); standing (pooling of $500$–$700\,\mathrm{mL}$ in the lower limbs compensated by reflexes).
- Evolution: single circulation in fishes, low post-branchial pressure, bulbus arteriosus as elastic reservoir; partial separation in lungfishes and amphibians; reptiles regulate intracardiac shunts by $Q_{\mathrm{p}}/Q_{\mathrm{s}} = R_{\mathrm{s}}/R_{\mathrm{p}}$, pythons and monitor lizards separate functionally, and crocodilians achieve controllable shunting with the foramen of Panizza and toothed valves; birds and mammals separate completely and independently; the diving reflex (Definition 16.8.1) is the extreme of a pressure-controlling system; different adaptive strategies of highland populations.

## Questions

1. Using the model of Example 16.1.1 (arterial pressure $100\,\mathrm{mmHg}$; resistances of skeletal muscle, viscera and brain $4$, $2$ and $4\,\mathrm{PRU}$), calculate what happens if the body keeps arterial pressure unchanged but the maximal output of the heart is only $150\,\mathrm{mL\,s^{-1}}$ (as in heart failure) and the resistance of skeletal muscle falls to $0.5\,\mathrm{PRU}$ in exercise. How should the body allocate flow?

   **Answer** If arterial pressure remained $100\,\mathrm{mmHg}$ with muscle resistance at $0.5$, the required cardiac output would be $100\times(2 + 0.5 + 0.25) = 275\,\mathrm{mL\,s^{-1}}$, exceeding the maximal output, so arterial pressure must fall: with a cardiac output of $150$ the total resistance is $0.364\,\mathrm{PRU}$, $P = 55\,\mathrm{mmHg}$, and brain flow falls to $14\,\mathrm{mL\,s^{-1}}$, insufficient to maintain consciousness. The body's solution is to limit the dilation of skeletal muscle (sympathetic vasoconstrictor tone partly offsets the local dilation) and to constrict the viscera strongly: for example, with visceral resistance raised to $8\,\mathrm{PRU}$ and muscle resistance kept at $1\,\mathrm{PRU}$, the total resistance is $1/(1 + 0.125 + 0.25) = 0.73\,\mathrm{PRU}$, a cardiac output of $137\,\mathrm{mL\,s^{-1}}$ is needed to maintain $100\,\mathrm{mmHg}$, which can be achieved; muscle receives only $100\,\mathrm{mL\,s^{-1}}$ and exercise capacity is limited. This is the reason for the reduced exercise tolerance of patients with heart failure: the pressure-controlling system protects blood pressure and the brain at the expense of exercising muscle.

2. Using Guyton's graphical analysis, explain: (a) why does an injection of norepinephrine change cardiac output little but raise arterial pressure markedly? (b) why does a severe arteriovenous fistula (a large shunt between artery and vein) greatly increase cardiac output?

   **Answer** (a) Norepinephrine constricts mainly the arterioles, increasing total peripheral resistance, and also constricts veins (raising $P_{\mathrm{msf}}$), but arteriolar constriction also increases the resistance to venous return, flattening the venous return curve; at the same time the rise in arterial pressure triggers the baroreceptor reflex, and vagal slowing of the heart offsets its $\beta_1$ action, so the cardiac function curve changes little, cardiac output at the intersection is essentially unchanged, and $P = \mathrm{CO}\times R$ rises because $R$ rises. (b) The fistula lets a large volume of blood return directly to the veins without passing the high-resistance arterioles, the resistance to venous return falls greatly, the venous return curve becomes steeper ($P_{\mathrm{msf}}$ unchanged, slope increased), the intersection with the cardiac function curve moves up, and cardiac output increases; long-standing fistulae cause cardiac enlargement (high-output heart failure).

3. In animals deprived of baroreceptor afferents (sinus nerves and aortic nerves), long-term mean arterial pressure is similar to that of normal animals but fluctuates much more. What does this say about the role of the baroreceptor reflex in blood pressure regulation? Why can it not determine the long-term level of blood pressure?

   **Answer** The baroreceptor reflex is a rapid buffering system: on a time scale of seconds it opposes disturbances of blood pressure (posture, emotion, exercise, eating) and keeps blood pressure near the set point, so fluctuation increases after denervation; its gain is limited (about 2), and baroreceptors reset to a sustained pressure change within a day or two, so it cannot sustain correction of blood pressure changes lasting several days. The long-term level of blood pressure is determined by the renal-body fluid mechanism: extracellular fluid volume is stable only when excretion of salt and water equals intake, and excretion is a steep function of arterial pressure, so long-term blood pressure is fixed at the intersection of the renal function curve and the intake line; the gain of this feedback is infinite on long time scales.

4. After rapid intravenous infusion of $1\,\mathrm{L}$ of saline, the heart rate may increase or decrease. Why? Write out the reflexes involved and their directions.

   **Answer** Infusion raises $P_{\mathrm{msf}}$ and venous return and stretches the atria: the Bainbridge reflex (atrial type B receptors with vagal afferents) and direct stretch of the sinoatrial node speed the heart; at the same time the increased cardiac output raises arterial pressure, and the baroreceptor reflex slows the heart. The net effect depends on the initial state: when the initial heart rate is slow (high vagal tone) the Bainbridge reflex predominates and heart rate increases; when the initial rate is fast the baroreceptor reflex predominates and heart rate decreases. In addition, atrial stretch reduces antidiuretic hormone secretion, increases atrial natriuretic peptide secretion and lowers renal sympathetic activity, and the excess fluid is excreted within hours (the volume reflex).

5. Why does coronary flow occur mainly in diastole, and why is tachycardia especially dangerous for patients with myocardial ischemia?

   **Answer** When the ventricle contracts, the pressure within the myocardium approaches or even exceeds the coronary pressure and squeezes the vessels inside the myocardium; flow in the left ventricular subendocardium almost stops in systole, and 70%–80% of left coronary flow occurs in diastole. Tachycardia mainly shortens diastole, reducing the time for coronary perfusion; at the same time the faster rate increases myocardial oxygen consumption (both the number of contractions per minute and their force increase). The extraction ratio of the myocardium is already 70%–80%, so an increase in oxygen consumption can be met only by increased flow, and in patients with coronary stenosis the diastolic reserve is already used up; the imbalance of supply and demand makes the subendocardium ischemic first, causing angina.

6. How will the hematocrit of a person who has lost 1 L of blood change over the next hour? Why?

   **Answer** In hemorrhage plasma and erythrocytes are lost in their original proportion, so immediately after the loss the hematocrit is unchanged. Capillary pressure then falls (arteriolar constriction and fall in venous pressure), Starling forces draw interstitial fluid into the vessels (transcapillary refill), plasma volume is partly restored while erythrocytes are not, and the hematocrit gradually decreases over an hour; later, renal retention of water and sodium also restores plasma first. Erythrocytes recover only after several weeks, when erythropoietin has stimulated the bone marrow. The fall in hematocrit several hours after hemorrhage therefore reflects the degree of transcapillary refill and is one basis for estimating the amount of blood lost; contraction of the spleen releases concentrated erythrocytes and can partly offset this fall (especially evident in dogs and diving mammals).

7. In a turtle during diving, pulmonary vascular resistance rises to 4 times the systemic resistance. Using the model of Example 16.8.1, find the ratio of pulmonary to systemic blood flow, and the change in pulmonary blood flow compared with breathing (pulmonary vascular resistance half the systemic). Could this regulation still be achieved if the turtle's heart were completely divided?

   **Answer** $Q_{\mathrm{p}}/Q_{\mathrm{s}} = R_{\mathrm{s}}/R_{\mathrm{p}} = 1/4$; during breathing it is 2. If cardiac output (total flow) is unchanged, pulmonary flow is $2/3$ of the total during breathing and $1/5$ during diving, so pulmonary blood flow falls to $0.2/0.67 \approx 30\%$ of its former value and the rest of the blood bypasses the lung into the systemic circulation (right-to-left shunt). In a completely divided heart the two ventricles are in series, $Q_{\mathrm{p}} = Q_{\mathrm{s}}$, and a rise in pulmonary vascular resistance would only raise right ventricular pressure without reducing pulmonary flow, so this regulation could not be achieved; crocodilians therefore retain the left aortic arch arising from the right ventricle and the foramen of Panizza, giving right ventricular blood another outlet when the resistance of the pulmonary outflow rises.

8. (Open question) Which reflexes of this chapter share mechanisms with the diving reflex? Why is it called "the extreme of a pressure-controlling system"? What responses occur, and what are the risks, when a person holds the breath while diving or in cold water?

   **Answer** The diving reflex contains the direct effects of the chemoreceptor reflex during apnea (vagal bradycardia, sympathetic peripheral vasoconstriction), the vagal reflex caused by trigeminal sensing of cold water, and the baroreceptor reflex: the sharp rise in peripheral resistance tends to raise arterial pressure, the baroreceptor reflex reinforces the bradycardia, and cardiac output falls in proportion, so arterial pressure remains unchanged. It is "the extreme of a pressure-controlling system" because most parallel branches are closed, total peripheral resistance and cardiac output change together while arterial pressure is constant, and the limited flow is reserved entirely for brain and heart, which is exactly the extreme form of the "critical protection" of Section 16.1. When a person's face is immersed in cold water the heart rate slows by 10%–25% and peripheral vessels constrict, which can prolong breath-holding; in well-trained free divers the heart rate can fall below $30\,\mathrm{min^{-1}}$, and contraction of the spleen briefly raises the hematocrit. The risks include: simultaneous strong vagal and sympathetic excitation ("autonomic conflict") can cause arrhythmias; hyperventilation during cold shock lowers $P_{\ce{CO2}}$ and lengthens breath-holding beyond the safe range, so that consciousness is lost from hypoxia in the last stage of ascent (shallow-water blackout).
