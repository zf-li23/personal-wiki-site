The previous chapter discussed the lower levels of motor control: spinal reflexes and rhythm-generating circuits, and the reticular and vestibular nuclei of the brainstem that maintain posture ([7.14.44](/7.14.44)). This chapter discusses the upper levels: how the motor areas of the cerebral cortex formulate and issue commands for voluntary movement, and how the cerebellum and basal ganglia, two "side loops," correct and select these commands. Neither the cerebellum nor the basal ganglia projects directly to the spinal cord, and damage to them does not cause paralysis, but movement becomes inaccurate (cerebellum) or difficult to initiate and to inhibit (basal ganglia). The circuits of both return to the cortex through the thalamus and constitute another dimension of the motor system beside the hierarchy: feedback correction and action selection. The general organization of cortex and thalamus is in the next chapter ([7.14.46](/7.14.46)), and somatosensory cortex and proprioception are in [7.14.39](/7.14.39). The models in this chapter are phenomenological and serve to illustrate physiological mechanisms; computational theories of the cerebellum and basal ganglia (internal models, reinforcement learning) belong to computational neuroscience ([7.16](/7.16)).

## 45.1. Motor Cortex

### 45.1.1. Primary Motor Cortex

Primary motor cortex (M1, Brodmann area 4) lies in the precentral gyrus in front of the central sulcus, immediately anterior to the primary somatosensory cortex behind the sulcus. There are many interconnections between somatosensory and motor cortex, and sensory information continually enters motor cortex and is used to adjust ongoing movement.

Motor cortex also has six layers ([7.14.46](/7.14.46)), but unlike sensory cortex its layer 4 (the internal granular layer, which in sensory cortex receives the specific thalamic input) is very thin and almost invisible, so it is called agranular cortex; input from the ventrolateral thalamic nucleus (which relays the outputs of the cerebellum and basal ganglia) terminates mainly in layers 3 and 5, and the output comes from the large pyramidal cells of layer 5. The neurons in the cortex arranged perpendicular to the surface form columns, and each column is a relatively independent integrative unit that combines information from several sources and issues one motor signal; a column usually controls the contraction of one muscle or of a group of synergists.

M1 has a somatotopic arrangement: from the lateral fissure upward come the face and mouth, hand and fingers, upper limb, and trunk, with the lower limb on the medial side in the longitudinal fissure. The area occupied by each part is proportional to the fineness of movement of that part, not to the size of the muscles: the regions that control the fingers and speech (mouth, tongue, larynx) occupy more than half of M1. Stimulating a site in M1 with a microelectrode almost always causes coordinated contraction of a group of muscles (such as flexion of the fingers) and only rarely of a single muscle; conversely, the same muscle has several scattered representations in M1. This shows that M1 encodes movements, not just a list of single muscles.

### 45.1.2. Premotor Area and Supplementary Motor Area

In front of M1 is area 6, which has two parts:

- Premotor area (PMA): the lateral part in front of M1, extending at its lower end to the lateral fissure and meeting the supplementary motor area at its upper end. Its somatotopy is roughly the same as that of M1. The PMA produces movement patterns more complex than those of M1, such as the synergy in which the shoulder and upper arm place the hand in a suitable position to hold something; part of its output goes directly to M1 and part goes through the basal ganglia and thalamus back to M1. Neurons in the PMA begin to fire in the preparatory period before the movement is executed and are concerned with planning; the mirror neurons among them fire both when the animal performs an action itself and when it sees another individual perform the same action (Rizzolatti et al., 1996).
- Supplementary motor area (SMA): above the PMA, mainly on the medial surface of the hemisphere within the longitudinal fissure. Stimulating the SMA often causes bilateral simultaneous movements, such as grasping with both hands; together with the PMA it coordinates movements of the body as a whole, provides the postural background (fixing some parts of the body so that another part can move), and takes part in action sequences initiated by an internal plan (rather than external cues). About $1\,\mathrm{s}$ before a person makes a voluntary movement, a slowly rising negative potential is recorded on the scalp, called the readiness potential (Bereitschaftspotential; Kornhuber and Deecke, 1965), whose early component comes mainly from the SMA.

There are several other specialized motor areas in front of and above the PMA:

- Broca's area (the motor speech area, areas 44 and 45): in front of the PMA above the lateral fissure, controlling the coordinated articulation of the larynx, mouth, and tongue; damage to it leaves a person knowing what to say but unable to say it ([7.14.46](/7.14.46)). Nearby there are also regions that control breathing, so that vocalization is coordinated with respiration.
- Frontal eye field: above Broca's area, controlling voluntary **saccades**. After damage, the eyes cannot leave an object being fixated at will and are "locked" onto it; fixation of the eyes during gaze is controlled mainly by the visual cortex of the occipital lobe and by the superior colliculus.
- Head rotation area: immediately next to the frontal eye field, coordinating the rotation of the head with eye movements through the motor nuclei of the brainstem.
- Area for hand skills: in the PMA in front of the hand area of M1; after damage hand movements become uncoordinated and lose their purpose, called motor apraxia.

### 45.1.3. Population Vector Coding

How do individual neurons in M1 encode the direction of movement? Georgopoulos et al. (1982) trained monkeys to push a handle from the center toward targets in 8 directions around it and recorded the firing of M1 neurons. Each neuron fires most in one direction (the preferred direction $\theta_i$) and less and less to either side, which can be fitted well by a cosine function:

$$
r_i(\theta) = b_i + k_i \cos(\theta - \theta_i). \tag{45.1}
$$

Here $b_i$ is the baseline firing rate and $k_i$ is the depth of tuning. The tuning of a single neuron is very broad (there is still baseline-level firing $90^\circ$ from the preferred direction), so it cannot indicate direction precisely; but the preferred directions of the neurons in the population are distributed over all directions. Treat each neuron as a unit vector $\boldsymbol{u}_i$ along its preferred direction and sum them weighted by the change of firing relative to baseline to obtain the *population vector*:

$$
\boldsymbol{P} = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \frac{r_i - b_i}{k_i}\,\boldsymbol{u}_i. \tag{45.2}
$$

If the preferred directions are uniformly distributed, then $\sum_i \cos(\theta - \theta_i)\,\boldsymbol{u}_i \approx \frac{N}{2}(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$, and $\boldsymbol{P}$ points in the direction of the actual movement. The population vector calculated by Georgopoulos et al. (1986) from several hundred neurons was very close to the actual direction of movement and already pointed in the direction of the coming movement about $100$–$150\,\mathrm{ms}$ before the movement began. Because the firing of each neuron fluctuates randomly, the directional error of the population vector decreases roughly as $N^{-1/2}$ with the number of neurons $N$ (figure): a population of broadly tuned, noisy neurons gives an accurate direction by "voting."

![Cosine-tuned motor cortex neurons and the population vector (simulation of Eqs. (45.1) and (45.2), $b = 25$, $k = 20$ per s, spike counts in a $0.4\,\mathrm{s}$ counting window drawn from a Poisson distribution). A: tuning curves (solid lines) of four neurons with different preferred directions, and firing rates counted in 8 directions (dots). B: preferred directions of 60 neurons weighted by their firing change (thin lines) and the resulting population vector (thick arrow, cross marks the target direction) for movements to 8 targets in a center-out task. C: mean directional error of the population vector as a function of the number of neurons $N$, decreasing approximately as $N^{-1/2}$.](./plots/population-vector.py)

The population vector is a phenomenological readout method; it shows that information about movement direction is distributed over many neurons and later became one of the earliest algorithms by which brain–machine interfaces use cortical signals to control a robotic arm. But the firing of M1 neurons is also related to force, joint torque, and muscle activity, and the population activity of motor cortex is more like a dynamical system that generates motor commands than a mere code for direction; these questions are discussed in [7.16](/7.16).

## 45.2. Descending Motor Pathways

### 45.2.1. The Corticospinal Tract

**Definition 45.2.1 (Corticospinal Tract)** The bundle of nerve fibers that arises from the layer 5 pyramidal cells of the cerebral cortex (mainly the motor and somatosensory areas), descends through the internal capsule and brainstem, and terminates directly in the spinal cord is called the **corticospinal tract**; because it passes through the pyramids of the medulla, it is also called the pyramidal tract.

About 30% of the fibers of the corticospinal tract come from M1, 30% from the PMA and SMA, and 40% from the somatosensory areas behind the central sulcus (mainly regulating the entry of sensory signals in the dorsal horn of the spinal cord). After leaving the cortex the fibers descend through the posterior limb of the internal capsule, pass through the cerebral peduncles of the midbrain and the pons, and gather in the ventral medulla to form the pyramids (about $10^6$ axons on each side). Most of the fibers (about $75$–$90\,\%$) cross to the opposite side in the lower medulla and descend in the lateral funiculus of the spinal cord, forming the lateral corticospinal tract; the minority of uncrossed fibers descend ipsilaterally in the anterior funiculus, forming the anterior corticospinal tract, most of which also finally cross in the cervical and thoracic segments and mainly control trunk and proximal muscles. Because of the crossing, one cortex controls the movement of the opposite side of the body, and hemorrhage in the internal capsule on one side (the commonest site of stroke) causes contralateral hemiplegia.

Most corticospinal fibers end on interneurons in the middle of the spinal gray matter, which coordinate several muscles ([7.14.44](/7.14.44#sec-44.1.2)); a minority synapse directly on the motor neurons of the ventral horn, and this direct cortico-motoneuronal connection is found mainly on the motor neurons that control the fingers in primates (Section 45.6). M1 contains a particularly large type of pyramidal cell called the Betz cell (soma up to $100\,\mu\mathrm{m}$), about $3\times10^4$ on each side, making up only about 3% of the corticospinal fibers; their axons are about $16\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ in diameter with a conduction velocity of about $70\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, the fastest pathway from brain to spinal cord. Most of the remaining corticospinal fibers are less than $4\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ in diameter.

**Example 45.2.1** In a person the distance from cortex to lumbar spinal cord is about $1\,\mathrm{m}$. Compare the time needed to reach the lumbar cord for an axon of a Betz cell ($70\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$) and for a fine fiber of diameter $2\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ (the conduction velocity of a myelinated fiber is about $6\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ per micrometer of diameter, that is, about $12\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, [7.14.4](/7.14.4)).

The fast fiber takes about $1/70\,\mathrm{s} \approx 14\,\mathrm{ms}$ and the fine fiber about $1/12\,\mathrm{s} \approx 80\,\mathrm{ms}$. On transcranial magnetic stimulation of M1, the latency of the motor evoked potential recorded on a calf muscle is about $30\,\mathrm{ms}$, which includes processing within the cortex, about $14\,\mathrm{ms}$ of corticospinal conduction, and peripheral conduction of about $1\,\mathrm{m}$ from spinal cord to muscle ($\alpha$ fibers about $60\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, about $17\,\mathrm{ms}$), consistent with the estimate for the fast fibers; the fine fibers form a slower component.

:::

Motor cortex also sends many fibers to the basal ganglia, thalamus, red nucleus, brainstem reticular formation, pontine nuclei (and through them to the cerebellum), and inferior olivary nucleus, so that these structures "know" what commands the cortex has issued.

### 45.2.2. The Corticorubrospinal Tract and the Two Motor Systems

Another, indirect pathway from the cortex passes through the red nucleus of the midbrain: fibers from M1 reach the magnocellular part of the red nucleus, and the rubrospinal tract from the red nucleus crosses at once in the midbrain (ventral tegmental decussation), descends along a route parallel to the lateral corticospinal tract, and terminates on interneurons and anterior horn motor neurons, with a few fibers to the cerebellum. The magnocellular part of the red nucleus also has a somatotopic arrangement and, like the corticospinal tract, mainly controls the muscles of the distal limbs. When the corticospinal fibers are destroyed while the corticorubrospinal pathway is intact, discrete limb movements can still occur but fine movements of the fingers and hand are markedly impaired.

**Definition 45.2.2 (Lateral and Medial Motor Systems)** The lateral corticospinal tract and rubrospinal tract, which descend in the lateral funiculus of the spinal cord and mainly control fine movements of the distal limb muscles, are together called the **lateral motor system**; the reticulospinal, vestibulospinal, and tectospinal tracts, which descend in the ventromedial spinal cord and mainly control trunk and proximal muscles to maintain posture and balance, are together called the **medial motor system** ([7.14.44](/7.14.44#sec-44.5.1)).

In primates, the magnocellular red nucleus and the rubrospinal tract are far less developed than in cats and rodents, and the corticospinal tract takes over most of the distal control. Chinese textbooks call the descending pathways other than the pyramidal tract the extrapyramidal system, a historical name based on anatomical division: the outputs of the basal ganglia and cerebellum act mainly through the thalamus on the cortex and then descend through the pyramidal tract, so functionally they cannot be sharply separated from the pyramidal system.

Damage to motor cortex and the corticospinal tract ("upper motor neuron" damage) causes impairment of voluntary movement of the contralateral limbs, especially fine finger movements; muscle tone may be reduced early on, and later increased muscle tone (spasticity), hyperactive tendon reflexes, and the Babinski sign (dorsiflexion of the great toe with fanning of the other toes on stroking the lateral sole, whereas normal adults show plantar flexion) are common. Animals with lesions of the pyramids alone mainly show loss of distal fine movements and reduced muscle tone; spasticity comes mainly from the simultaneously damaged cortical projections that descend through the brainstem (which unbalance the brainstem facilitatory system, a mechanism similar to that of **decerebrate rigidity**, [7.14.44](/7.14.44#sec-44.5.2)). In contrast, damage to the motor neurons themselves or to their axons ("lower motor neuron" damage) causes flaccid paralysis, loss of reflexes, and muscle atrophy.

## 45.3. The Cerebellum

### 45.3.1. Function and Lesions of the Cerebellum

The cerebellum regulates the timing of muscle activity so that one movement passes quickly and smoothly into the next, adjusts the strength of contraction when the muscle load changes, and controls the instantaneous interaction between agonist and antagonist muscles. Its core features are:

| Feature | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Does not produce movement directly | does not issue the final motor command but regulates other motor systems |
| Damage does not cause paralysis | muscle strength is largely preserved |
| Damage causes incoordination | ataxia, intention tremor, dysmetria, and so on |
| Center of motor coordination and motor learning | continually compares the "planned movement" with the "actual movement," corrects errors, and improves with repetition |

Symptoms of cerebellar damage:

- Ataxia: the direction, force, and speed of movement are uncoordinated, and the gait is wide-based and staggering, like that of a drunk person.
- Dysmetria: on reaching for an object, the hand falls short of or overshoots the target, because the cerebellum cannot issue at the proper time the signal that decelerates the movement.
- Intention tremor: as a voluntary movement approaches the target, increasingly obvious back-and-forth oscillation appears (overshoot and repeated correction during movement) and disappears at rest; this is the opposite of the resting tremor of Parkinson disease (Section 45.4.5).
- Dysdiadochokinesia: inability to alternate opposite movements rapidly (such as rapid pronation and supination of the hand), because the previous movement cannot be predicted and terminated on time.
- Dysarthria and nystagmus: the syllables of speech are discontinuous and uneven in loudness; when gazing at an object to one side, the eyes oscillate back and forth (damage to the flocculonodular lobe).
- Reduced muscle tone: the tonic facilitation of spinal and brainstem motor nuclei by the deep nuclei is weakened (different from the inhibition of extensors by the anterior lobe of the cerebellum in animals, [7.14.44](/7.14.44#sec-44.5.2)).

### 45.3.2. Anatomical Divisions

Two transverse fissures divide the cerebellum into three lobes: the anterior lobe, the posterior lobe, and the flocculonodular lobe below. Longitudinally, on both sides of the narrow vermis at the midline lies the intermediate zone, and farther out the large lateral zone (the main part of the cerebellar hemisphere). All output of the cerebellar cortex passes through the deep nuclei embedded in the white matter (the exceptions are the Purkinje cells of the flocculonodular lobe and of part of the vermis, which project directly to the vestibular nuclei): from medial to lateral, these are the fastigial nucleus, the interposed nuclei (globose and emboliform nuclei), and the dentate nucleus, which receive the projections of the vermis, intermediate zone, and lateral zone, respectively.

The vermis and intermediate zone have a somatotopic arrangement: the trunk and proximal parts are in the vermis, and the distal limbs and face in the intermediate zone. They receive signals from the corresponding parts of the body, motor cortex, and brainstem, and send motor signals back to the corresponding regions of the cortex, red nucleus, and brainstem. The lateral zone has no somatotopy; it receives input from motor cortex and **association cortex** (through the pontine nuclei) and plays a role in coordinating the timing and planning of sequential movements.

### 45.3.3. Input and Output

The cerebellum has about 40 times as many input fibers as output fibers, which fall into two classes:

**Definition 45.3.1 (Mossy Fibers and Climbing Fibers)** The cerebellar afferent fibers from the pontine nuclei, spinal cord (dorsal and ventral spinocerebellar tracts), vestibular nuclei, and reticular formation, which synapse with granule cells in the granular layer and act indirectly on large numbers of Purkinje cells through the parallel fibers of the granule cells, are called **mossy fibers**; the afferent fibers from the inferior olivary nucleus of the medulla, each of which winds around the dendrites of one Purkinje cell and forms hundreds of excitatory synapses, are called **climbing fibers**.

The structures of the two inputs are almost opposite:

| | Mossy fibers | Climbing fibers |
|---|---|---|
| Origin | pontine nuclei (cortical information), spinal cord, vestibular nuclei, reticular formation | inferior olivary nucleus |
| Relay | granule cells (about $7\times10^{10}$ in humans, the majority of neurons of the whole brain, [7.14.7](/7.14.7#sec-7.2.6)) → parallel fibers | none, directly to Purkinje cells |
| Divergence and convergence | one mossy fiber to hundreds of granule cells; one Purkinje cell receives about $10^5$–$2\times10^5$ parallel fibers | one Purkinje cell has only one climbing fiber; one climbing fiber innervates about 10 Purkinje cells |
| Response of the Purkinje cell | simple spikes, $50$–$100$ per s, continuously modulated with movement | complex spikes (a large Ca²⁺-dependent depolarization with several small spikes superimposed), about 1 per s |

Both kinds of fibers give off collaterals before entering the cerebellar cortex that directly excite neurons of the deep nuclei.

The output of the cerebellum goes through three main pathways: the vermis through the fastigial nucleus to the vestibular nuclei and reticular formation of the medulla and pons (working with the medial motor system to control posture and balance); the intermediate zone through the interposed nuclei to the ventrolateral and ventral anterior thalamic nuclei and then to the cortex, and to the magnocellular red nucleus of the midbrain (working with the lateral motor system); and the lateral zone through the dentate nucleus to the ventrolateral and ventral anterior thalamic nuclei, and then to motor cortex, premotor cortex, and prefrontal cortex.

### 45.3.4. The Functional Unit

The cerebellar cortex consists of almost identical units repeated about $3\times10^7$ times, and the core of each unit is a Purkinje cell and the deep nuclear cell that corresponds to it. The Purkinje cell is the only output neuron of the cerebellar cortex; it is GABAergic and inhibits the deep nuclear cell, which in turn receives excitation from both mossy fiber and climbing fiber collaterals. The cerebellar cortex also contains three kinds of inhibitory interneurons: basket cells and stellate cells are excited by parallel fibers and inhibit neighboring Purkinje cells laterally (**lateral inhibition**, which sharpens the signal); Golgi cells are excited by parallel fibers and mossy fibers and inhibit granule cells by feedback.

On and off signals. When a movement begins, the signals of the same set of mossy fibers first excite the deep nuclear cells directly through collaterals and issue a strong "on" signal (for example, exciting the agonist); the same signals reach the Purkinje cells through granule cells and parallel fibers, which involves several more synapses and is therefore a little later, and the Purkinje cells then inhibit the deep nuclear cells and issue the "off" signal. This "excitation first, inhibition later" time course (the same principle as **feedforward inhibition**, [7.14.6](/7.14.6#sec-6.3.1)) makes the start of each movement quick and its termination timely, and the alternation of agonist and antagonist unfolds with this timing. The level of activity of the Purkinje cells determines the degree and timing of muscle on and off.

The cerebellum and the closed loop of movement. In a quick reach, the command issued by motor cortex tells the cerebellum through the pontine nuclei "what is intended," and the spinocerebellar tracts and vestibular afferents tell the cerebellum "what is actually being done." The cerebellum compares the two and sends out correction signals through the thalamus and red nucleus; at the same time, before the movement is about to reach the target, it sends out in advance a signal that makes the antagonist contract and decelerates the limb. Quick movements are completed within tens of milliseconds, shorter than the time needed for sensory feedback to traverse a complete loop, so this action of the cerebellum must be predictive: it predicts the result of the movement from past experience and does not simply wait for errors to appear before correcting them. This is also the answer to the problem raised in [7.14.44](/7.14.44#sec-44.3.2), that delayed feedback is insufficient to stabilize quick movements.

### 45.3.5. The Three Functional Zones

The cerebellum regulates movement at three levels, corresponding to three functional zones:

1. Vestibulocerebellum (flocculonodular lobe and adjacent vermis): connected to the vestibular nuclei of the brainstem, it maintains balance and posture and controls eye movements. It takes part in the gain adjustment of the **vestibulo-ocular reflex** (when the head turns, the eyes rotate in the opposite direction at the same speed so that the image on the retina remains stable, [7.14.42](/7.14.42)); damage causes disturbance of balance and nystagmus, while limb movements are unaffected.
2. Spinocerebellum (vermis and intermediate zone, most of the anterior and posterior lobes): receives **proprioception** from the spinal cord and motor commands from the cortex, compares the two, coordinates movements of the distal limbs (hands, feet), and provides damping so that movements do not overshoot.
3. Cerebrocerebellum (lateral zone): connected almost only to the cerebral cortex (input through the pontine nuclei, output through the dentate nucleus and thalamus), it takes part in movement planning and in the timing of sequential movements: while the current movement is in progress it already prepares for the next one. It also takes part in judging time intervals and in cognitive processes such as language and working memory; patients with lesions of the lateral zone often have deficits of executive function and verbal fluency in addition to movement disorders (cerebellar cognitive affective syndrome).

### 45.3.6. Motor Learning

The circuit structure of the cerebellum suggests a learning mechanism. Marr (1969) and Albus (1971) each proposed that the many parallel fibers expand the context of a movement (a combination of sensory signals and commands) into a high-dimensional representation in granule cells, and each climbing fiber reports an "error" or "teaching" signal; when the activity of a parallel fiber and the signal of the climbing fiber arrive together, the efficacy of that parallel fiber–Purkinje cell synapse changes, so that the next time in the same context the output of the Purkinje cell is corrected. Albus predicted that this change is a weakening.

Physiological evidence:

- Ito and Kano (1982) found in the rabbit cerebellum that paired stimulation of parallel fibers and climbing fibers causes a lasting weakening of transmission at the parallel fiber–Purkinje cell synapse, that is, cerebellar **long-term depression** ([7.14.5](/7.14.5#sec-5.5.2)); the mechanism involves $\ce{Ca^2+}$ entry caused by the climbing fiber, metabotropic glutamate receptors, and protein kinase C, and finally endocytosis of postsynaptic AMPA receptors.
- Adaptation of the vestibulo-ocular reflex: when a person or an animal wears prisms that reverse or magnify the visual field left–right, the image on the retina is no longer stable when the head turns, and climbing fibers report this "retinal slip" error; within days the gain of the vestibulo-ocular reflex gradually changes until the image is again stable. If the flocculus is destroyed or LTD in it is blocked, this adaptation no longer occurs (Ito, 1984).
- Eyeblink conditioning: when a tone (conditioned stimulus, through mossy fibers) is repeatedly paired with a puff of air to the cornea that arrives shortly afterward (unconditioned stimulus, through climbing fibers), the animal learns to blink in time before the puff; this learned response depends on the cerebellar interposed nucleus and the corresponding cerebellar cortex and disappears after destruction of the interposed nucleus (Thompson et al.).

Later studies showed that parallel fiber LTD is not the only site of plasticity: transgenic mice lacking LTD can still accomplish some motor learning (Schonewille et al., 2011), and LTP at parallel fiber synapses, synapses of interneurons, and plasticity in the deep nuclei all take part; the memory that is learned may form first in the cerebellar cortex and then gradually transfer to the deep nuclei. Computational theories of the cerebellum as an "error corrector" and "internal model" are in [7.16](/7.16).

## 45.4. The Basal Ganglia

### 45.4.1. Components

**Definition 45.4.1 (Basal Ganglia)** The group of nuclei lying deep in the cerebral hemispheres, lateral to the thalamus, that form loops with the cerebral cortex and thalamus is called the **basal ganglia**; they comprise five parts: the caudate nucleus, putamen, globus pallidus (external segment GPe and internal segment GPi), substantia nigra (pars compacta SNc and pars reticulata SNr), and subthalamic nucleus (STN).

The caudate nucleus and putamen are one unit in development and function, together called the striatum (the ventral striatum includes the nucleus accumbens); the putamen and globus pallidus are together called the lentiform nucleus. The substantia nigra is in the midbrain and the subthalamic nucleus in the diencephalon, and they are assigned to the basal ganglia by function.

The relation of the basal ganglia to the internal capsule needs to be kept clear: the internal capsule is a layer of white matter, the place through which almost all the fibers running between the cerebral cortex and the brainstem and spinal cord (including the corticospinal tract and thalamocortical fibers) pass together, and it runs between the caudate nucleus and thalamus (medially) and the lentiform nucleus (laterally). It is therefore wrong to say that "almost all motor and sensory fibers pass through the basal ganglia": these fibers pass through the internal capsule between the basal ganglia, and the internal capsule itself does not belong to the basal ganglia; the two must not be equated. The clinical significance of this anatomical relation is that hemorrhage near the internal capsule often damages both the internal capsule (causing contralateral hemiplegia and hemisensory loss) and the neighboring basal ganglia.

About 95% of the neurons of the striatum are GABAergic medium spiny neurons (MSNs), which receive glutamatergic input from almost the entire cerebral cortex and dopaminergic input from the substantia nigra pars compacta; the internal segment of the globus pallidus and the substantia nigra pars reticulata are the output nuclei of the basal ganglia, are also GABAergic, fire continuously at a high frequency of $60$–$80$ per s, and tonically inhibit the ventral anterior and ventrolateral nuclei of the thalamus (and the superior colliculus of the midbrain, which controls saccades).

### 45.4.2. Direct and Indirect Pathways

The MSNs of the striatum fall into two groups that give rise to two pathways to the output nuclei:

**Definition 45.4.2 (Direct and Indirect Pathways)** The pathway in which MSNs of the striatum that express D1-type dopamine receptors directly inhibit the internal globus pallidus and the substantia nigra pars reticulata, thereby releasing the thalamus from inhibition by the output nuclei and facilitating movement, is called the **direct pathway**; the pathway in which MSNs of the striatum that express D2-type dopamine receptors inhibit the external globus pallidus, thereby increasing the activity of the subthalamic nucleus, which in turn excites the output nuclei, strengthens inhibition of the thalamus, and suppresses movement, is called the **indirect pathway**.

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\text{direct pathway:}\ \text{cortex} \xrightarrow{+} \text{striatum (D1)} \xrightarrow{-} \text{GPi/SNr} \xrightarrow{-} \text{thalamus} \xrightarrow{+} \text{cortex},\\
&\text{indirect pathway:}\ \text{cortex} \xrightarrow{+} \text{striatum (D2)} \xrightarrow{-} \text{GPe} \xrightarrow{-} \text{STN} \xrightarrow{+} \text{GPi/SNr} \xrightarrow{-} \text{thalamus} \xrightarrow{+} \text{cortex}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Count the minus signs on each pathway: the direct pathway has two inhibitions, with the net effect of exciting thalamus and cortex; the indirect pathway has three inhibitions and one excitation, with the net effect of inhibition. Both pathways make use of **disinhibition** ([7.14.6](/7.14.6#sec-6.3.3)): the basal ganglia do not drive movement directly but act like a gate that is normally shut tight (the output nuclei continuously inhibit the thalamus), opens briefly for the selected action, and shuts even tighter on competing actions. In addition, the cortex excites the subthalamic nucleus directly, called the hyperdirect pathway, which quickly and broadly strengthens inhibition before an action begins and acts as a "pause."

Dopamine from the substantia nigra pars compacta has opposite effects on the two pathways: through D1 receptors ($\mathrm{G_s}$-coupled) it enhances the response of direct-pathway MSNs to cortical input, and through D2 receptors ($\mathrm{G_i}$-coupled) it weakens the response of indirect-pathway MSNs. The two actions of dopamine are therefore in the same direction and both make movement easier to occur.

### 45.4.3. A Rate Model

Writing these connections as equations in a **rate model** (Definition 6.4.1) shows the action of dopamine intuitively. Let the firing rate $x$ of each population satisfy $\tau\,\mathrm{d}x/\mathrm{d}t = -x + F(u)$ with $F(u) = \max(0, u)$, let the cortical input be $c(t)$ and the dopamine level be $d$ (0 in the normal state):

$$
\begin{aligned}
u_{\mathrm{D1}} &= g(1 + d)\,c - \theta, & u_{\mathrm{D2}} &= h\,g(1 - d)\,c - \theta,\\
u_{\mathrm{GPe}} &= I_{\mathrm{GPe}} - w_1 x_{\mathrm{D2}} + w_2 x_{\mathrm{STN}}, & u_{\mathrm{STN}} &= I_{\mathrm{STN}} - w_3 x_{\mathrm{GPe}} + w_4 c,\\
u_{\mathrm{GPi}} &= I_{\mathrm{GPi}} - w_5 x_{\mathrm{D1}} + w_6 x_{\mathrm{STN}}, & u_{\mathrm{Th}} &= I_{\mathrm{Th}} - w_7 x_{\mathrm{GPi}} + w_8 c.
\end{aligned} \tag{45.3}
$$

$g$ is the gain of the MSNs, $\theta$ is the threshold of the MSNs (MSNs have a very negative resting membrane potential and hardly fire, firing only under strong cortical input), $I$ is the tonic drive of each nucleus, $w$ are connection strengths, and $h$ is the fraction of indirect-pathway MSNs preserved (1 in the normal state). The simulation (figure) shows:

- In the normal state, the output nuclei fire tonically at about $70$ per s and the thalamus is almost silent; when a cortical command arrives, the direct pathway makes the firing of the output nuclei fall and the thalamus is released (the indirect pathway at the same time partly cancels this action, so that the gate opens only to a certain degree).
- Reduced dopamine ($d < 0$, similar to Parkinson disease): the direct pathway is weakened and the indirect pathway is strengthened, and the cortical command instead raises the firing of the output nuclei, so the thalamus cannot be released and movement is difficult to initiate.
- Loss of indirect-pathway MSNs ($h$ reduced, similar to early Huntington disease): the gate opens too wide, the output of the thalamus is too strong, and unwanted movements are not suppressed.

This "direct–indirect pathway" model (Albin, Young, and Penney, 1989; DeLong, 1990) explains the two large classes of symptoms of basal ganglia disease: reduced movement (Parkinson disease) and excessive movement (chorea, hemiballismus). It also has shortcomings: for example, it predicts that destroying the internal globus pallidus should cause excessive movement, but in fact pallidotomy relieves Parkinson disease without causing chorea; and the MSNs of the direct and indirect pathways are activated together at the onset of movement rather than one after the other. It is now thought that the two pathways cooperate to select one action and suppress the other competing ones, and the basal ganglia also influence movement through rhythmic synchronous activity (the enhanced $13$–$30\,\mathrm{Hz}$ $\beta$ oscillations in Parkinson disease).

### 45.4.4. Putamen and Caudate Loops

The basal ganglia and cortex form several parallel, mutually segregated loops, each starting from a particular cortical area, passing through a particular part of the striatum, the output nuclei, and the thalamus, and returning to the cortical area where it began.

- The putamen loop (motor loop): input comes mainly from premotor, supplementary motor, primary motor, and somatosensory cortex, through the putamen, internal globus pallidus, and ventrolateral/ventral anterior thalamic nuclei, and back to the SMA and M1. It helps to execute learned sequential and fine movements, such as writing, using scissors, or shooting a basketball, movement patterns that can be performed unconsciously after practice. Damage to different nuclei in this loop causes different involuntary movements: damage to the globus pallidus causes athetosis, continuous slow writhing movements of the hand, arm, neck, or face; damage to the subthalamic nucleus (often a small stroke) causes sudden, large flinging movements of the entire contralateral limb, called hemiballismus; multiple small lesions in the striatum cause chorea, rapid, irregular, dance-like movements of the hand, face, and other parts; and damage to the substantia nigra causes the rigidity, akinesia, and tremor of Parkinson disease.
- The caudate loop (cognitive loop): the caudate nucleus runs from head to tail through the depths of almost all the lobes and receives much input from association cortex, the regions that integrate sensory information and stored memory; its output returns through the thalamus to prefrontal cortex, premotor area, and supplementary motor area. It takes part in cognition-based motor control: deciding "what to do and in what order" from sensory input and past experience, for example when on seeing a lion a person recognizes the danger without deliberation, turns away, and begins to run.
- There is also a limbic loop that passes through the ventral striatum (nucleus accumbens) back to the orbitofrontal cortex and anterior cingulate gyrus, concerned with motivation and reward ([7.14.46](/7.14.46)).

Dopamine neurons of the substantia nigra pars compacta and the ventral tegmental area fire briefly to an unexpected reward, do not respond to a reward that has been predicted, and decrease their firing when an expected reward does not appear (Schultz et al., 1997). This "reward prediction error" signal is thought to change the efficacy of corticostriatal synapses (dopamine-dependent plasticity), so that actions that lead to reward are more easily selected later. It forms a contrast with the climbing fiber signal of the cerebellum: the cerebellum learns from errors in movement how to do it accurately, and the basal ganglia learn from the goodness of outcomes which action to select. The learning theories of the two (**supervised learning** and **reinforcement learning**) are in [7.16](/7.16).

### 45.4.5. Parkinson Disease and Huntington Disease

*Parkinson's disease* is the second most common neurodegenerative disease and is caused mainly by the progressive loss of dopaminergic neurons of the substantia nigra pars compacta; the surviving neurons often contain Lewy bodies composed mainly of $\alpha$-synuclein. Because of compensation, by the time symptoms appear about half of the dopamine neurons have been lost and the dopamine in the striatum has decreased by about 80%. The main motor symptoms:

- Bradykinesia and akinesia: movements are difficult to initiate, slow, and reduced in amplitude; facial expression is reduced (masked face), handwriting becomes smaller and smaller, steps in walking are small and hurried (festinating gait), and turning is difficult. This is the core symptom and corresponds to the failure of the gate to open in the rate model.
- Resting tremor: at rest the fingers rub at a frequency of about $4$–$6\,\mathrm{Hz}$ ("pill-rolling"), lessening in voluntary movement and disappearing in sleep.
- Rigidity: on passive movement of a joint the resistance is uniformly increased throughout the range (lead-pipe), and cogwheel-like when tremor is superimposed; this differs from the spasticity of upper motor neuron damage (resistance increases with the speed of stretch, with a "clasp-knife" phenomenon).
- Postural instability: falls are common in late stages.

There are also many nonmotor symptoms, such as reduced sense of smell, constipation, rapid eye movement sleep behavior disorder, depression, and cognitive decline, some of which appear years before the motor symptoms, reflecting that the pathology is not confined to the substantia nigra. Treatment: L-DOPA, the precursor of dopamine, crosses the **blood–brain barrier** (dopamine itself cannot, [7.14.7](/7.14.7#sec-7.2.2)) and is converted to dopamine in the surviving dopaminergic terminals, and is often combined with a peripheral decarboxylase inhibitor; in addition there are dopamine receptor agonists, monoamine oxidase B inhibitors, and, for patients in late stages, deep brain stimulation (stimulating the subthalamic nucleus or internal globus pallidus with a current of about $130\,\mathrm{Hz}$, with an effect similar to a lesion).

*Huntington's disease* is an autosomal dominant disease caused by the expansion of a CAG trinucleotide repeat in exon 1 of the *HTT* gene (fewer than 27 repeats is normal, and 40 or more has full penetrance), and the encoded huntingtin protein carries an overlong polyglutamine tract. The more repeats, the earlier the onset, and in transmission across generations (especially through the father) the repeat tends to expand further, causing earlier onset in the offspring (**anticipation**). The pathology first causes the loss of the indirect-pathway MSNs of the striatum (containing enkephalin and expressing D2 receptors) that project to the external globus pallidus, so the early manifestation is chorea; as the disease progresses, direct-pathway MSNs and cortical neurons are also lost, and reduced movement, rigidity, dementia, and psychiatric symptoms appear, with a course of $15$–$20$ years.

## 45.5. Integration of the Motor System

Putting Chapter 44 and this chapter together, the division of labor among the parts of motor control is as follows:

| Level | Main function | Consequence of damage |
|---|---|---|
| Spinal cord | simple muscle reflexes, patterns of rhythmic movement (CPG), the **final common path** | flaccid paralysis (motor neurons); spinal shock below transection |
| Brainstem | maintains antigravity muscle tone, postural reflexes and balance, eye and head movements | decerebrate rigidity, abnormal postural reflexes |
| Motor cortex | formulates coherent movement patterns, controls their strength and timing, initiates voluntary movement (lateral system) | loss of contralateral fine movement, spasticity and Babinski sign |
| Cerebellum | works with the other motor systems, controls the accuracy and timing of movement, predicts and corrects errors, motor learning | ataxia, dysmetria, intention tremor, without paralysis |
| Basal ganglia | select and initiate actions, help the cortex perform experience-based unconscious actions and parallel or sequential action sequences | reduced movement (Parkinson disease) or involuntary movements (chorea, hemiballismus, athetosis) |

Take reaching for a glass of water as an example: prefrontal cortex and the caudate loop decide "to go and take it"; the PMA and SMA, with the participation of the putamen loop, prepare the action sequence; M1 issues the command through the corticospinal tract, and the medial system adjusts posture in advance; the cerebellum predicts the trajectory of the hand from the command and the proprioception coming from the spinal cord and decelerates in time before approaching the glass; and the spinal reflex immediately increases the force of the biceps when the glass is heavier than expected ([7.14.44](/7.14.44)). Most of these parts work at the same time, and the levels are parallel rather than commanding stage by stage.

## 45.6. Evolution and Comparative Aspects

### 45.6.1. Cerebellar Size and Mode of Locomotion

The cerebellum is found in all jawed vertebrates (lampreys have only a small cerebellum-like structure, and hagfishes have none), and its relative size varies with the complexity of locomotion and with special sensory needs, not just with body size (an overview of the groups is in [7.14.7](/7.14.7#sec-7.5)).

- Cartilaginous fishes: at the same body size, sharks that swim fast, hunt in open water, and can perform complex maneuvers (such as requiem sharks and mackerel sharks) have a relatively larger cerebellum with more folds on its surface; benthic, slow-moving species (such as some dogfish sharks and skates) have a smaller and smoother cerebellum (Yopak et al., 2010).
- Birds: the cerebellum is well developed in all and has several transverse folia; species that fly and manipulate in complex space (such as woodpeckers, and parrots that manipulate objects with the beak and feet) have more folds. The enlargement of the cerebellum in birds and mammals occurred independently and corresponds in parallel to the need for fine motor control.
- Mammals: the numbers of neurons in the cerebellum and **neocortex** increase roughly in proportion across mammals, with about 4 times as many neurons in the cerebellum as in the cortex (Herculano-Houzel, 2010), and this ratio is not changed in humans: the expansion of human cognition is accompanied by coordinated expansion of cortex and cerebellum.

In weakly electric fishes (mormyrids) the valvula of the cerebellum is so large that it covers the whole dorsal surface of the brain; they sense their surroundings with electric discharges ([7.14.43](/7.14.43)). The electrosensory lateral line lobe of electric fish is a cerebellum-like structure: it has a granule cell–parallel fiber–principal cell structure similar to that of the cerebellum, the parallel fibers convey a copy of the fish's own discharge command (corollary discharge) and information on body posture, and the parallel fiber synapses on the principal cells change according to an anti-Hebbian rule, learning within minutes to predict and cancel the sensory signal produced by the fish's own discharge, so that the faint perturbations caused by external objects can be detected (Bell, 1981). The dorsal cochlear nucleus of mammals has the same structure. This shows that a basic function of cerebellum-like circuits is to subtract the predictable part from sensation, the same principle as the predictive role of the cerebellum in movement.

### 45.6.2. Area X of Songbirds

Songbirds (oscine passerines), parrots, and hummingbirds can learn vocalizations by imitation, called vocal learning; among mammals only a few groups such as humans, cetaceans, bats, pinnipeds, and elephants have this ability. A young zebra finch first listens to the song of an adult male (the "tutor") and forms a memory, then, through weeks of practice like "babbling," gradually adjusts its own song to resemble the remembered template.

Song is controlled by two pathways: the vocal motor pathway (HVC → RA → the vocal and respiratory motor nuclei of the brainstem) produces the song that is crystallized in adulthood; the anterior forebrain pathway (HVC → Area X → the dorsolateral thalamic nucleus DLM → LMAN → RA) is a cortical–basal ganglia–thalamic loop that is indispensable for learning and almost unnecessary for the crystallized song of adult birds. Area X is a specialized nucleus in the songbird striatum that has both striatal and pallidal cell types and receives dopaminergic input from the ventral tegmental area of the midbrain. Variability in firing introduced by LMAN into RA makes each rendition of the song slightly different, which is the "exploration" of movement; when a variation makes the song closer to the template, the signal of dopamine neurons is enhanced, so that this variation is retained later (Gadagkar et al., 2016). If Area X or LMAN of a young bird is destroyed, song learning cannot be completed normally. This is the same mechanism as dopamine-guided action selection in the mammalian basal ganglia, and vocal learning in birds and humans is a case of **convergent evolution**: both depend on cortical–basal ganglia loops and on a direct projection from motor cortex (RA in birds) to the vocal motor neurons of the brainstem, whereas closely related species that cannot learn vocalizations (chickens, macaques) lack this direct projection. Mutations of the *FOXP2* gene cause disorders of language and orofacial movement in humans, and lowering its expression in Area X of zebra finches also disrupts the imitation of song (Haesler et al., 2007).

The basic structure of the basal ganglia is highly conserved in vertebrates: the lamprey already has homologs of the striatum, globus pallidus, subthalamic nucleus, and substantia nigra pars compacta, with direct and indirect pathways expressing D1 and D2 receptors (Grillner and Robertson, 2016). This shows that the scheme of "selecting among several competing actions with a disinhibitory gate" had already appeared in the vertebrate ancestor at least 500 million years ago.

### 45.6.3. Evolution of the Corticospinal Tract

The corticospinal tract is a pathway unique to mammals, and distal movements in other vertebrates are controlled mainly by brainstem pathways (the rubrospinal and reticulospinal tracts). Within mammals, the places where corticospinal fibers end and their numbers differ greatly: in marsupials and many rodents they end in the dorsal horn and intermediate zone and mainly regulate sensory input and interneurons; mainly in primates do corticospinal fibers form many synapses directly on the motor neurons of the ventral horn, especially those that control the fingers. The density of this direct cortico-motoneuronal connection increases in turn from monkeys to apes to humans and is related to the ability to move the fingers independently (Lemon, 2008); raccoons, which can manipulate skillfully with their forelimbs, also have this connection, while in rodents it exists only briefly early in development. Evidently the dexterity of the hand comes not from more muscles but from more direct control of single motor neuron pools by the cortex.

### 45.6.4. Distributed Motor Control in the Octopus

The 8 arms of the octopus have no skeleton, and each arm has almost unlimited degrees of freedom, which makes it hard to plan in terms of joint angles as in vertebrates. About two thirds of the approximately $5\times10^8$ neurons of the octopus are in the arms ([7.14.7](/7.14.7#sec-7.5)), the axial nerve cord of each arm contains many sensory and motor neurons, and each arm can produce complex movements independently: when an arm that has been separated from the brain is stimulated, it can still produce an extension movement like that of the intact animal, a bend that propagates from the base of the arm to its tip (Sumbre et al., 2001). The brain need only issue a simple command of "where to reach and when to start," and the details of the extension are accomplished by the neural circuits in the arm. When bringing food to the mouth, the octopus temporarily bends the arm into three sections, forming a "quasi-joint" arrangement like the upper arm, forearm, and hand of a human, reducing the unlimited degrees of freedom to a few (Sumbre et al., 2006). This is a solution different from that of vertebrates: control is distributed into the body, and the center is responsible only for selection and coordination, similar in idea to the decentralized coordination of walking in insects in Chapter 44 ([7.14.44](/7.14.44#sec-44.6.3)).

## Questions

1. A patient reaching for a cup on a table has a hand that shakes more and more as it nears the cup but does not shake when sitting still; another patient's fingers rub continually when sitting still, and the shaking lessens when reaching for the cup, but the patient is slow to start. Infer the site of the lesion in each and explain the difference in mechanism between the two tremors.

   **Answer** The first is intention tremor, and the lesion is in the cerebellum (the intermediate or lateral zone and its output nuclei). The cerebellum issues in advance, before the movement approaches the target, a signal that makes the antagonist contract and decelerates the limb; without this predictive control the movement can be corrected only by delayed sensory feedback, each correction overshoots, and an increasingly large back-and-forth oscillation results (the same as the oscillation of **negative feedback** with delay in [7.14.44](/7.14.44#sec-44.3.2)). At rest there is no movement to correct, so there is no shaking. The second is the resting tremor of Parkinson disease ($4$–$6\,\mathrm{Hz}$), and the lesion is in the dopamine neurons of the substantia nigra pars compacta. It is related to abnormal rhythmic synchronous activity in the basal ganglia–thalamus–cortex loop and is interrupted and lessened by voluntary movement; the slowness to start is bradykinesia, that is, the gate of the basal ganglia is hard to open.

2. Use the direct–indirect pathway model of Eq. (45.3) to explain: (a) why the actions of dopamine D1 and D2 receptors are "opposite" while the total effect of dopamine on movement is consistent; (b) why destroying or stimulating the subthalamic nucleus at high frequency can relieve the bradykinesia of Parkinson disease.

   **Answer** (a) D1 receptors excite the MSNs of the direct pathway and D2 receptors inhibit the MSNs of the indirect pathway. The net effect of the direct pathway is to facilitate movement and that of the indirect pathway is to suppress movement, so strengthening the former and weakening the latter both facilitate movement. In the model, increasing $d$ increases $x_{\mathrm{D1}}$ and decreases $x_{\mathrm{D2}}$, and both lower $x_{\mathrm{GPi}}$ and release the thalamus. (b) When dopamine is reduced the indirect pathway is too strong: GPe is inhibited, STN loses the inhibition from GPe and is overactive, and it overexcites GPi, so that inhibition of the thalamus is too strong. Destroying STN corresponds in the model to removing the $w_6 x_{\mathrm{STN}}$ term, the firing of the output nuclei falls, and the thalamus can again be disinhibited. The effect of high-frequency deep brain stimulation is similar to a lesion, possibly because it blocks abnormal rhythmic activity in STN and not merely silences the neurons.

3. The gain $G$ of the vestibulo-ocular reflex is defined as the ratio of the angular velocity of eye rotation to the angular velocity of head rotation, normally about 1. A person puts on spectacles that magnify by a factor of 2 and takes them off a few days later. What happens to the vestibulo-ocular reflex? What role does the cerebellum play?

   **Answer** With 2-fold magnifying spectacles on, head rotation makes the image in the visual field move at twice the speed, a reflex with gain 1 cannot stabilize the image, and slip appears on the retina; climbing fibers send this error signal to the Purkinje cells of the flocculus, and plastic changes (LTD and others) occur at the synapses of the coactive parallel fibers that convey vestibular and eye movement information, so that the gain of the reflex gradually increases to nearly 2 within days. Immediately after the spectacles are removed the gain is still too high, the eyes rotate too much when the head turns, the visual field slips in the opposite direction, and the person feels dizzy; after some more time the gain readapts to about 1. Animals with a destroyed flocculus cannot accomplish this adaptation, which shows that the cerebellum is the site of this motor learning.

4. Why is it wrong to say that "the basal ganglia are the internal capsule of the brain"? Point out what symptoms hemorrhage in the posterior limb of the internal capsule on one side causes, and explain why.

   **Answer** The internal capsule is a layer of white matter composed of the projection fibers running between the cerebral cortex and the brainstem and spinal cord (corticospinal tract, corticobulbar tract, thalamocortical sensory fibers, optic radiation, and so on), and it runs between the caudate nucleus, thalamus, and lentiform nucleus; the basal ganglia are a group of gray matter nuclei. The projection fibers pass through the internal capsule, not through the basal ganglia themselves. The posterior limb of the internal capsule gathers the corticospinal tract and the fibers from the thalamus to somatosensory cortex, as well as the optic and auditory radiations at its posterior part, so hemorrhage on one side causes contralateral hemiplegia (upper motor neuron damage: flaccid early, spastic later, positive Babinski sign) and contralateral hemisensory loss, and when the lesion involves the posterior part also contralateral homonymous hemianopia, the "triple hemi" syndrome.

5. (Open question) The cerebellum and the basal ganglia both act on the cortex through the thalamus and both take part in motor learning. Compare their inputs, outputs, teaching signals, and symptoms of damage, and explain what each of them "learns."

   **Answer** Key points: the input of the cerebellum is the motor command and sensory state conveyed by mossy fibers, together with the climbing fibers of the inferior olive (movement errors, such as retinal slip or unexpected touch); its output goes through the deep nuclei to the thalamus and brainstem and is an excitatory correction signal; what it learns is "how to make a movement accurate," that is, it stores the relation between command and result as a predictive mapping (supervised learning), and after damage movement can still be initiated but is inaccurate and uncoordinated and cannot adapt to new conditions. The input of the basal ganglia is the contextual information from almost the whole cortex, and the teaching signal is the reward prediction error of dopamine neurons; its output is tonic inhibition that is selectively released; what it learns is "which action to select in what context" (reinforcement learning) and turning repeatedly rewarded action sequences into habits, and after damage the execution of the action itself may be normal, but initiation is difficult (Parkinson disease) or unwanted actions cannot be suppressed (chorea). The two cooperate at the cortical level: one decides what to do and the other how to do it.
