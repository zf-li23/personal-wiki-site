Chapters 17 and 18 described how gas is delivered to the alveoli and how the alveoli and the pulmonary circulation form an exchanger. This chapter deals with the exchange itself and with the transport that follows it: how $\ce{O2}$ and $\ce{CO2}$ cross the respiratory membrane (Section 19.1), how they equilibrate with their surroundings in pulmonary and tissue capillaries (Section 19.2), and how blood carries large amounts of two gases that are both poorly soluble. Almost all the $\ce{O2}$ is bound to hemoglobin (Section 19.3), whereas $\ce{CO2}$ is carried mainly as bicarbonate (Section 19.4). The two transports reinforce each other through the Bohr and Haldane effects. Section 19.5 relates transport to tissue demand and discusses oxygen delivery and hypoxia; Section 19.6 compares the respiratory pigments of different animals and the adaptation of their $\ce{O2}$ affinity. The molecular mechanism by which hemoglobin binds $\ce{O2}$ (the T and R states, **cooperativity**, the **Hill equation**, and the structural basis of the **Bohr effect** and of **2,3-bisphosphoglycerate**) was treated in detail in [7.1.7](/7.1.7), so here we discuss only their physiological consequences. The red blood cell itself is covered in Chapter 11, and the link between $\ce{CO2}$ transport and acid–base balance in Chapter 25.

## 19.1. The Respiratory Membrane and the Pulmonary Diffusing Capacity

### 19.1.1. The Respiratory Unit and the Respiratory Membrane

The respiratory bronchiole, alveolar duct, alveolar sacs and the **alveoli** on them together form a respiratory unit (a pulmonary lobule). The dense capillary network in the alveolar wall is almost continuous, so that blood is spread like a thin sheet between the alveoli. At any instant the pulmonary capillaries contain only about $70\,\mathrm{mL}$ of blood, but it is spread over an area of about $70\,\mathrm{m^2}$, with an average thickness of only about $1\,\mathrm{\mu m}$. A pulmonary capillary is about $5\,\mathrm{\mu m}$ in diameter, narrower than a red blood cell. The cell must deform to squeeze through, its membrane lies directly against the vessel wall, and $\ce{O2}$ and $\ce{CO2}$ entering or leaving the cell no longer have to cross a layer of plasma. Gas exchange takes place in all parts of the respiratory unit, not only in the alveoli.

**Definition 19.1.1 (Respiratory Membrane)** The structures between alveolar gas and capillary blood that a gas must cross during exchange are called the **respiratory membrane**. It has six layers: the fluid film containing surfactant lining the alveolus, the alveolar epithelium, the epithelial basement membrane, the interstitial space, the capillary basement membrane (fused with the epithelial one in many places) and the capillary endothelium.

The average (harmonic mean) thickness of the respiratory membrane is about $0.6\,\mathrm{\mu m}$, and the thinnest parts are about $0.2\,\mathrm{\mu m}$ (Section 18.1.3). According to **Fick's law** (Eq. (17.2) in Chapter 17), the rate at which a gas crosses the membrane is proportional to the area, the partial-pressure difference and the Krogh diffusion constant of the gas, and inversely proportional to the thickness. Disease acts mainly through area and thickness: **pulmonary edema** and pulmonary fibrosis thicken the membrane, and emphysema and lung resection reduce its area. Once the area falls below 1/3–1/4 of normal, exchange at rest is clearly impaired; during exercise, when more exchange is needed, even a small loss of area may be insufficient.

### 19.1.2. The Pulmonary Diffusing Capacity

**Definition 19.1.2 (Pulmonary Diffusing Capacity)** The amount of a gas that diffuses across the respiratory membrane per minute per $1\,\mathrm{mmHg}$ of mean partial-pressure difference between alveolar gas and pulmonary capillary blood is called the **pulmonary diffusing capacity** $D_{\mathrm{L}}$ of that gas:

$$
D_{\mathrm{L}} = \frac{\dot V}{P_{\mathrm{A}} - \bar P_{\mathrm{c}}}.
$$

$D_{\mathrm{L}}$ combines the effects of area, thickness and diffusion constant in Eq. (17.2) into a single quantity. In a healthy young man at rest, $D_{\mathrm{L,O_2}} \approx 21\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}\,mmHg^{-1}}$: with a mean transmembrane $\ce{O2}$ pressure difference of about $11\,\mathrm{mmHg}$, about $230\,\mathrm{mL}$ diffuses per minute. The diffusion constant of $\ce{CO2}$ is about 20 times that of $\ce{O2}$, and $D_{\mathrm{L,CO_2}}$ is about $400$–$450\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}\,mmHg^{-1}}$; it is so large that the mean pressure difference cannot be measured directly.

$D_{\mathrm{L}}$ is adjustable. During exercise $D_{\mathrm{L,O_2}}$ can rise to about $65\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}\,mmHg^{-1}}$, mainly because capillaries that were closed are opened and those already open are distended (Section 18.4.2), so that both the exchange area and the capillary blood volume increase. Blood flow to the lung apex increases the most in exercise, making the **ventilation–perfusion ratio** more uniform.

In the clinic, the diffusing capacity is measured with CO. CO has a very high affinity for hemoglobin, so when a small amount is inhaled its plasma partial pressure is almost zero, the mean pressure difference equals the alveolar CO pressure, and $D_{\mathrm{L,CO}}$ can be calculated directly; multiplying by 1.23 converts it to $D_{\mathrm{L,O_2}}$. Roughton and Forster (1957) divided the diffusing capacity into two parts in series:

$$
\frac{1}{D_{\mathrm{L}}} = \frac{1}{D_{\mathrm{M}}} + \frac{1}{\theta V_{\mathrm{c}}},
\tag{19.1}
$$

where $D_{\mathrm{M}}$ is the diffusing capacity of the membrane, $\theta$ is the reaction rate of the gas with a unit volume of blood, and $V_{\mathrm{c}}$ is the pulmonary capillary blood volume. Even if the membrane were infinitely thin, the exchange rate would still be limited by the rate of the chemical reaction inside the red blood cells and by the capillary blood volume; the two resistances are roughly equal. Anemia reduces the hemoglobin in $V_{\mathrm{c}}$ and lowers $D_{\mathrm{L}}$, whereas polycythemia raises it.

## 19.2. Gas Exchange in the Lungs and Tissues

### 19.2.1. Equilibration along the Pulmonary Capillary

When mixed venous blood enters the pulmonary capillary, $P_{\ce{O2}} \approx 40\,\mathrm{mmHg}$ and $P_{\ce{CO2}} \approx 46\,\mathrm{mmHg}$, whereas alveolar gas is at about $100$–$104$ and $40\,\mathrm{mmHg}$. The initial pressure difference is about $60\,\mathrm{mmHg}$ for $\ce{O2}$ but only about $6\,\mathrm{mmHg}$ for $\ce{CO2}$. At rest a red blood cell takes about $0.75\,\mathrm{s}$ to pass through the pulmonary capillary (the capillary blood volume of $70\,\mathrm{mL}$ divided by a flow of $5.6\,\mathrm{L\,min^{-1}}$), yet $\ce{O2}$ has already equilibrated with alveolar gas within about $0.25\,\mathrm{s}$, that is, about 1/3 of the way along the capillary; $\ce{CO2}$ also equilibrates in less than half of the length.

A: normal respiratory membrane. At rest (solid line, $D_{\mathrm{L}} = 21$ mL/(min·mmHg), capillary blood volume 70 mL, transit time 0.75 s, venous blood at 40 mmHg), the alveolar value of 100 mmHg is nearly reached in about 0.3 s, and the rest of the time is reserve. In exercise (dashed line, $D_{\mathrm{L}}$ increased to 65 by recruitment, blood volume 110 mL, transit time shortened to 0.25 s, venous blood down to 25 mmHg), blood still comes close to equilibrium before leaving. The dotted line marks 0.25 s. The middle of the curve is flatter because hemoglobin binds a large amount of $\ce{O2}$ between 40 and 60 mmHg, so the same diffused amount raises the partial pressure only slowly. B: a thickened respiratory membrane ($D_{\mathrm{L}}$ reduced to 1/3, as in pulmonary fibrosis). At rest the blood is still not fully equilibrated when it leaves, and in exercise it is far from equilibrium, producing exercise-induced hypoxemia. **Definition 19.2.1 (Diffusion Limitation and Perfusion Limitation)** If the amount of a gas transferred across the lung depends mainly on pulmonary capillary blood flow (blood has equilibrated with alveolar gas before leaving the capillary), the transfer is called **perfusion limitation**; if it depends mainly on the diffusing capacity of the respiratory membrane (blood has not yet equilibrated when it leaves), it is called **diffusion limitation**.

Two extreme examples illustrate the distinction. $\ce{N2O}$ does not bind to hemoglobin; once dissolved, its plasma partial pressure rises rapidly and soon equals that in the alveolus, after which no more is transferred, and the amount transferred can be raised only by increasing blood flow. It is the classic perfusion-limited gas. CO binds tightly to hemoglobin, its plasma partial pressure stays close to zero, the pressure difference is almost constant along the whole capillary, and the amount transferred is set by the diffusing capacity of the membrane. It is the classic diffusion-limited gas. At rest in healthy subjects $\ce{O2}$ is perfusion-limited, because it equilibrates in the first third of the capillary.

$\ce{O2}$ transfer becomes diffusion-limited in the following situations: strenuous exercise (transit time shortened to 0.25 s or less; elite endurance athletes have a very large **cardiac output**, and some of them show a fall in arterial $\ce{O2}$ saturation even at sea level), thickening or loss of area of the respiratory membrane, and high altitude (alveolar $P_{\ce{O2}}$ is lower, the initial pressure difference is smaller, and equilibration is slower). This is why hypoxemia in patients with lung disease often appears first during exercise.

### 19.2.2. Arterial $P_{\ce{O2}}$

Blood leaving the pulmonary capillaries has equilibrated with alveolar gas (about $100$–$104\,\mathrm{mmHg}$), yet the arterial $P_{\ce{O2}}$ in the left atrium and aorta is only about $95\,\mathrm{mmHg}$. The reason is two kinds of "admixture": about 2% of venous blood reaches the arteries without passing through the alveolar capillaries (bronchial veins draining into the pulmonary veins and the smallest cardiac veins emptying directly into the left heart, Section 18.4.1), and the inhomogeneity of the **ventilation–perfusion ratio** within the lung (Section 18.6).

**Example 19.2.1 (How Much Does a 2% Shunt Lower the Arterial $P_{\ce{O2}}$?)** End-capillary blood has $P_{\ce{O2}} = 104\,\mathrm{mmHg}$ and an $\ce{O2}$ content of about $19.9\,\mathrm{mL}/100\,\mathrm{mL}$; venous blood has $P_{\ce{O2}} = 40\,\mathrm{mmHg}$ and a content of about $15.2\,\mathrm{mL}/100\,\mathrm{mL}$. Mixed 98∶2, the content is $0.98 \times 19.9 + 0.02 \times 15.2 \approx 19.8\,\mathrm{mL}/100\,\mathrm{mL}$, a fall of only about $0.1\,\mathrm{mL}/100\,\mathrm{mL}$. But on the flat upper part of the oxygen dissociation curve (Section 19.3.2), a change of $1\,\mathrm{mmHg}$ in partial pressure corresponds to a change of only about $0.016\,\mathrm{mL}/100\,\mathrm{mL}$ in content, so $P_{\ce{O2}}$ falls by about $0.1/0.016 \approx 6\,\mathrm{mmHg}$. What is conserved on mixing is the gas content, not the partial pressure; in a flat region of the curve, a small amount of blood of low content can lower the partial pressure appreciably.

### 19.2.3. Gas Exchange in the Tissues

When arterial blood enters a tissue capillary, $P_{\ce{O2}} \approx 95\,\mathrm{mmHg}$, whereas the $P_{\ce{O2}}$ of the interstitial fluid averages about $40\,\mathrm{mmHg}$ and that inside cells about $23\,\mathrm{mmHg}$ (varying between about $5$ and $40\,\mathrm{mmHg}$ with distance from the capillary; the **Krogh cylinder model** of Chapter 15). $\ce{O2}$ diffuses rapidly out of the capillary, and blood at the venous end equilibrates with the interstitial fluid at $P_{\ce{O2}} \approx 40\,\mathrm{mmHg}$. Cytochrome oxidase in **mitochondria** has a very high affinity for $\ce{O2}$, so as long as the $P_{\ce{O2}}$ at the mitochondria exceeds about $1\,\mathrm{mmHg}$, the rate of **oxidative phosphorylation** is essentially not limited by $\ce{O2}$; an intracellular value of 23 mmHg is a large safety margin.

The interstitial $P_{\ce{O2}}$ is set by the balance of two processes: blood flow brings $\ce{O2}$, and the tissue consumes it. When blood flow increases, the interstitial $P_{\ce{O2}}$ rises toward the arterial 95 mmHg; when consumption increases, the interstitial $P_{\ce{O2}}$ falls, and a larger flow is needed to maintain it.

$\ce{CO2}$ moves in the opposite direction: intracellular $P_{\ce{CO2}} \approx 46\,\mathrm{mmHg}$, interstitial about $45\,\mathrm{mmHg}$, arterial blood entering the capillary $40\,\mathrm{mmHg}$ and leaving it at about $45$–$46\,\mathrm{mmHg}$. $\ce{CO2}$ diffuses about 20 times faster than $\ce{O2}$, so a pressure difference of only about 1 mmHg suffices for it to leave the cells. Likewise, an increase in blood flow lowers the tissue $P_{\ce{CO2}}$, and an increase in metabolism raises it.

## 19.3. Oxygen Transport

### 19.3.1. Dissolved and Bound Oxygen

The solubility of $\ce{O2}$ in plasma is only $0.003\,\mathrm{mL}/(100\,\mathrm{mL} \cdot \mathrm{mmHg})$, so at $P_{\ce{O2}} = 100\,\mathrm{mmHg}$ only $0.3\,\mathrm{mL}/100\,\mathrm{mL}$ is dissolved. About 98.5% of the $\ce{O2}$ in blood is bound to hemoglobin, and only about 1.5% is physically dissolved. Yet dissolution is the obligatory route to binding: $\ce{O2}$ must first dissolve in plasma and enter the red blood cell before it can bind to hemoglobin, and the $P_{\ce{O2}}$ of blood is determined only by the dissolved $\ce{O2}$. Hemoglobin raises the oxygen-carrying capacity of blood about 65-fold (Example 11.2.1).

Binding of $\ce{O2}$ to hemoglobin is rapid (under 0.01 s) and reversible and needs no enzyme; after binding, the $\ce{Fe^2+}$ is still ferrous, and the process is called oxygenation rather than oxidation. A hemoglobin tetramer has four hemes and can bind at most four $\ce{O2}$.

**Definition 19.3.1 (Oxygen Capacity, Oxygen Content and Oxygen Saturation)** The maximum amount of $\ce{O2}$ that the hemoglobin in $100\,\mathrm{mL}$ of blood can bind is called the **oxygen capacity** of the hemoglobin; the amount actually bound is called the **oxygen content**; the oxygen content as a percentage of the oxygen capacity is called the **oxygen saturation** $S_{\ce{O2}}$. When the dissolved $\ce{O2}$ is small, these are also called the blood oxygen capacity, content and saturation, respectively.

Each gram of hemoglobin binds about $1.34\,\mathrm{mL}$ of $\ce{O2}$, so blood with a hemoglobin concentration of $150\,\mathrm{g\,L^{-1}}$ has an oxygen capacity of about $20\,\mathrm{mL}/100\,\mathrm{mL}$ (often written 20 vol%). Arterial blood is about 97% saturated, with an oxygen content of about $19.5\,\mathrm{mL}/100\,\mathrm{mL}$; mixed venous blood is about 75% saturated, with a content of about $15\,\mathrm{mL}/100\,\mathrm{mL}$.

$$
C_{\ce{O2}} = 1.34\,[\mathrm{Hb}]\,S_{\ce{O2}} + 0.003\,P_{\ce{O2}}\quad (\mathrm{mL}/100\,\mathrm{mL};\ [\mathrm{Hb}]\ \text{in}\ \mathrm{g}/100\,\mathrm{mL}).
\tag{19.2}
$$

Oxyhemoglobin is bright red and deoxyhemoglobin is dark purplish red. When capillary blood contains more than about $50\,\mathrm{g\,L^{-1}}$ of deoxyhemoglobin, the skin and mucous membranes look bluish, a condition called cyanosis. Cyanosis depends on the absolute amount of deoxyhemoglobin, not on the degree of hypoxia: a patient with severe anemia is hypoxic but not cyanotic (too little hemoglobin altogether), whereas a person with polycythemia at altitude may be cyanotic without being hypoxic. The pulse oximeter uses the difference in absorption of red and infrared light by the two forms of hemoglobin to measure $S_{\ce{O2}}$ noninvasively.

### 19.3.2. The Oxygen Dissociation Curve

**Definition 19.3.2 (Oxygen Dissociation Curve and P₅₀)** The curve relating the oxygen saturation of blood to its $P_{\ce{O2}}$ is called the **oxygen dissociation curve**. The $P_{\ce{O2}}$ at which saturation is 50% is called **P₅₀**; it measures the affinity of hemoglobin for $\ce{O2}$: the larger the P₅₀, the lower the affinity.

For normal adult blood (pH 7.40, $P_{\ce{CO2}}$ 40 mmHg, 37 °C), P₅₀ is about $26.8\,\mathrm{mmHg}$ ($3.6\,\mathrm{kPa}$). Because of the **cooperativity** among subunits ([7.1.7](/7.1.7)), the curve is sigmoid and can be described approximately by the **Hill equation**:

$$
S_{\ce{O2}} = \frac{P_{\ce{O2}}^{\,n}}{P_{\ce{O2}}^{\,n} + P_{50}^{\,n}},\qquad n \approx 2.7.
\tag{19.3}
$$

Here $n$ is the **Hill coefficient**. The Hill equation is not accurate at very low and very high saturations; for precise calculations an empirical formula (such as the Severinghaus equation) or the MWC model is used.

The shape of the curve has a clear physiological meaning, which is easiest to see by dividing it into three segments:

| Segment | $P_{\ce{O2}}$ / mmHg | Feature | Significance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Upper | 60–100 | Flat: saturation is still about 90% at 60 mmHg and about 97% at 100 mmHg | When alveolar $P_{\ce{O2}}$ falls over a wide range (moderate altitude, mild lung disease), the oxygen content of arterial blood changes very little; conversely, raising alveolar $P_{\ce{O2}}$ (oxygen breathing, hyperventilation) hardly increases the content |
| Middle | 40–60 | Steeper: about 75% at 40 mmHg | At rest the tissues unload $\ce{O2}$ in this segment, each $100\,\mathrm{mL}$ of blood releasing about 5 mL of $\ce{O2}$ |
| Lower | 15–40 | Steepest | When consumption increases, tissue $P_{\ce{O2}}$ needs to fall only slightly for hemoglobin to release a large amount of $\ce{O2}$; in strenuous exercise tissue $P_{\ce{O2}}$ falls to about 15 mmHg and each $100\,\mathrm{mL}$ of blood can release about 15 mL, about 75% |

The percentage of the arterial oxygen content released as blood flows through the tissues is called the oxygen utilization coefficient; it is about 25% at rest, can reach 75%–85% for the whole body in strenuous exercise, and approaches 100% in working muscle.

**Example 19.3.1 (Hemoglobin as a Buffer of Tissue $P_{\ce{O2}}$)** Use Eqs. (19.2) and (19.3) (hemoglobin 15 g/100 mL). For alveolar $P_{\ce{O2}}$ of 60, 104 and 200 mmHg, arterial saturation is about 90%, 97.5% and 99.6%, and the oxygen content about 18.2, 19.9 and 20.6 mL/100 mL (in the last, dissolved $\ce{O2}$ already accounts for 0.6). Alveolar $P_{\ce{O2}}$ changes more than threefold, yet arterial oxygen content changes by only about 13%. Each $100\,\mathrm{mL}$ of blood must give up 5 mL of $\ce{O2}$ in the tissues: blood with a content of 19.9 falls to 14.9, at which $P_{\ce{O2}}$ is about 40 mmHg, and blood with a content of 18 falls to 13, at about 35 mmHg. Tissue $P_{\ce{O2}}$ is almost unchanged. Hemoglobin thus acts as an "$\ce{O2}$ buffer", keeping tissue $P_{\ce{O2}}$ within 15–40 mmHg regardless of large changes in external $\ce{O2}$.

### 19.3.3. Factors Affecting the Oxygen Dissociation Curve

A rightward shift of the curve (larger P₅₀) means a lower affinity, so more $\ce{O2}$ is released at the same $P_{\ce{O2}}$; a leftward shift means the opposite.

| Factor | Right shift (lower affinity) | Mechanism ([7.1.7](/7.1.7)) |
|---|---|---|
| pH | Lower pH (more $\ce{H+}$) | $\ce{H+}$ binds histidine and other residues of deoxyhemoglobin and stabilizes the T state (Bohr effect) |
| $P_{\ce{CO2}}$ | Higher | Partly through $\ce{H+}$ (produced by hydration of $\ce{CO2}$); in addition, $\ce{CO2}$ forms carbamate with the N-terminal amino groups of globin, stabilizing the T state |
| Temperature | Higher | Oxygenation is exothermic, so warming shifts the equilibrium toward dissociation |
| 2,3-BPG | Increased | Binds in the central cavity between the two $\beta$ chains of the T state and stabilizes it |

The physiological significance of the **Bohr effect** is a twofold convenience: in the tissues, $\ce{CO2}$ and acidic metabolites lower the pH, shifting the curve to the right and promoting unloading of $\ce{O2}$; in the lungs, $\ce{CO2}$ is expelled, the pH rises, and the curve shifts to the left, promoting uptake of $\ce{O2}$. Working muscle has a higher temperature, a higher $P_{\ce{CO2}}$ and a lower pH at the same time, all of which shift the curve to the right, exactly matching its greater need for oxygen.

**2,3-Bisphosphoglycerate** in red blood cells is produced by a side branch of **glycolysis**, at a concentration similar to that of hemoglobin (about $5\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$). Chronic hypoxia (altitude, anemia, chronic lung disease) raises it within hours to days, shifting the curve to the right and favoring unloading of $\ce{O2}$ in the tissues; at very high altitude, where uptake of $\ce{O2}$ in the lung is itself limiting, the benefit of the right shift is cancelled and a left shift is actually more advantageous (Section 19.6.2). After 1–3 weeks of storage, banked blood has lost much of its 2,3-BPG, and the curve is shifted to the left; after transfusion, its ability to release $\ce{O2}$ is temporarily reduced: 2,3-BPG recovers about halfway in a day and essentially fully only after two to three days.

### 19.3.4. Carbon Monoxide Poisoning

CO binds to the same heme site as $\ce{O2}$, with an affinity about 240 times that of $\ce{O2}$ (the Haldane constant). Therefore a $P_{\mathrm{CO}}$ in the air of only about $0.4\,\mathrm{mmHg}$ (about 0.06%) is enough to convert half of the hemoglobin into carboxyhemoglobin (COHb). CO poisoning is harmful in two ways:

1. Hemes occupied by CO cannot transport $\ce{O2}$, so the oxygen capacity falls.
2. Hemoglobin that has bound CO is in the R state, the remaining hemes have a higher affinity for $\ce{O2}$, and the curve shifts to the left, so the remaining $\ce{O2}$ is also hard to release in the tissues.

![Oxygen and carbon dioxide dissociation curves of blood (schematic). A: shifts of the oxygen dissociation curve (Hill equation, $n = 2.7$). The black line is normal adult blood (P₅₀ 26.8 mmHg); lower pH, higher $P_{\ce{CO2}}$, higher temperature or more 2,3-BPG shift the curve to the right (red dashed line, P₅₀ about 33). Fetal blood (green, P₅₀ about 19) and llama blood (dash-dotted line, about 21, similar to human blood at pH 7.6) have higher affinity; the curve of myoglobin (P₅₀ about 2.8 mmHg) is a hyperbola and stays nearly saturated even at a very low $P_{\ce{O2}}$. B: oxygen content curves. In anemia (hemoglobin halved, blue dashed line) the oxygen capacity is halved but the shape of the curve is unchanged, and at a tissue $P_{\ce{O2}}$ of 40 mmHg some $\ce{O2}$ can still be unloaded; with 50% carboxyhemoglobin (red) the capacity is likewise halved, but the remaining curve is shifted to the left and almost no $\ce{O2}$ is released down to 40 mmHg, so the tissue must fall to a much lower $P_{\ce{O2}}$ to obtain $\ce{O2}$; this is why CO poisoning is far more dangerous than anemia of the same degree. C: $\ce{CO2}$ dissociation curves. At the same $P_{\ce{CO2}}$, deoxygenated blood (blue) carries more $\ce{CO2}$ than oxygenated blood (red) (the Haldane effect). The line joining arterial point a ($P_{\ce{CO2}}$ 40 mmHg, saturation 97.5%) and venous point v (46 mmHg, 75%) is the physiological curve in the body: venous blood carries about 4 mL/100 mL more $\ce{CO2}$ than arterial blood, and a considerable part of this comes from deoxygenation itself.](./plots/dissociation-curves.py)

In a patient with CO poisoning the arterial $P_{\ce{O2}}$ can be normal (dissolved $\ce{O2}$ is unaffected), and the chemoreceptors of the carotid body sense $P_{\ce{O2}}$ rather than oxygen content, so there is no marked increase in ventilation, and the patient is not cyanotic (carboxyhemoglobin is cherry red). An ordinary pulse oximeter cannot distinguish COHb from oxyhemoglobin and gives a falsely normal reading. CO dissociates very slowly: the half-life of COHb is about 4–5 h on room air, about 1 h on pure oxygen, and about 20 min in hyperbaric oxygen (2.5–3 atmospheres), where the high $P_{\ce{O2}}$ displaces CO according to competitive binding while the large amount of dissolved $\ce{O2}$ supplies the tissues directly.

### 19.3.5. Myoglobin and Fetal Hemoglobin

Myoglobin is a single-subunit heme protein in skeletal and cardiac muscle with a P₅₀ of about $2.8\,\mathrm{mmHg}$; its binding curve is a noncooperative hyperbola ([7.1.7](/7.1.7)). Its affinity is far higher than that of hemoglobin, so it remains largely saturated at the $P_{\ce{O2}}$ inside muscle cells (a few to a few tens of mmHg) and serves two functions. It acts as a local $\ce{O2}$ store, supplying $\ce{O2}$ when blood flow is temporarily obstructed during muscle contraction (systole in cardiac muscle, **tetanic contraction** of skeletal muscle). It also facilitates the diffusion of $\ce{O2}$ in the cytoplasm from the cell membrane to the mitochondria (myoglobin carrying $\ce{O2}$ itself diffuses, increasing the flux of $\ce{O2}$, which is facilitated diffusion). People and animals that exercise regularly for endurance or that live at altitude have a higher myoglobin concentration.

Fetal hemoglobin (HbF, $\alpha_2\gamma_2$) has a P₅₀ of about $19\,\mathrm{mmHg}$, lower than that of adult hemoglobin (HbA, $\alpha_2\beta_2$). The reason is not that the $\gamma$ chain itself binds $\ce{O2}$ more tightly but that it binds 2,3-BPG weakly: the histidine at position 143 of the $\beta$ chain that binds BPG is a serine in the $\gamma$ chain. The high affinity of fetal blood allows it to take up $\ce{O2}$ from maternal blood in the placenta: the $P_{\ce{O2}}$ of maternal blood in the placenta is only about $30$–$50\,\mathrm{mmHg}$ and that of fetal umbilical venous blood only about $30\,\mathrm{mmHg}$, but its saturation can reach about 80%. The placenta also shows a "double Bohr effect": after fetal blood gives up $\ce{CO2}$, its pH rises and its affinity increases, whereas after maternal blood takes up $\ce{CO2}$, its pH falls and its affinity decreases; both promote the transfer of $\ce{O2}$ from mother to fetus. After birth the $\gamma$ chain is gradually replaced by the $\beta$ chain (**globin switching**, [7.5.29](/7.5.29)), and by about 1 year of age HbF falls below 2% (below 1% in adults).

## 19.4. Carbon Dioxide Transport

### 19.4.1. Three Forms of Transport

$\ce{CO2}$ is carried in blood in three forms. Their proportions of the total blood $\ce{CO2}$ differ from their proportions of the arteriovenous difference (the part actually transported), so both columns are given below (approximate values):

| Form | Fraction of total arterial $\ce{CO2}$ (about 48 mL/100 mL) | Fraction of the arteriovenous difference (about 4 mL/100 mL) |
|---|---|---|
| Physically dissolved | about 5% | about 10% |
| Bicarbonate ($\ce{HCO3-}$) | about 90% | about 60%–70% |
| Carbaminohemoglobin | about 5% | about 20%–30% |

The two sets of proportions are often confused (some textbooks give 5% dissolved, 88% bicarbonate and 7% carbaminohemoglobin as fractions of the total, while others give 7%, 70% and 23% as fractions of the amount transported). Carbaminohemoglobin makes up little of the total but varies the most between artery and vein, because its formation is directly linked to the oxygenation of hemoglobin (Section 19.4.3).

Dissolved $\ce{CO2}$ is the starting point for the other two forms: $\ce{CO2}$ diffuses from the tissues into plasma, and most of it then enters the red blood cells.

**Definition 19.4.1 (Carbonic Anhydrase and the Chloride Shift)** The zinc-containing enzyme that catalyzes $\ce{CO2 + H2O <=> H2CO3}$ is called **carbonic anhydrase**. The concentration of carbonic anhydrase in red blood cells is very high, making this reaction about 5000 times faster than in plasma. The $\ce{HCO3-}$ formed is exchanged for plasma $\ce{Cl-}$ by the $\ce{Cl-}$/$\ce{HCO3-}$ exchanger in the red cell membrane (band 3 protein, AE1), $\ce{HCO3-}$ leaving and $\ce{Cl-}$ entering; this process is called the **chloride shift** (Hamburger shift).

In the tissue capillaries:

$$
\ce{CO2 + H2O <=>[\text{carbonic anhydrase}] H2CO3 <=> H+ + HCO3-}.
$$

The second step is a spontaneous, rapid dissociation. Most of the $\ce{H+}$ formed is bound by deoxyhemoglobin (deoxyhemoglobin is a better $\ce{H+}$ acceptor than oxyhemoglobin, the other face of the Bohr effect), so the reaction can proceed continuously to the right with only a small fall in pH (venous blood pH about 7.36, arterial 7.40); $\ce{HCO3-}$ enters plasma through the chloride shift and is carried to the lungs as plasma $\ce{HCO3-}$. The increase in solute inside the red cell draws water in, so red cells in venous blood are slightly larger than in arterial blood (venous **hematocrit** is about 3% higher). In the pulmonary capillaries all the reactions run in reverse: $\ce{HCO3-}$ enters the red cell and combines with $\ce{H+}$ (released when hemoglobin is oxygenated), and carbonic anhydrase dehydrates the product to $\ce{CO2}$, which diffuses into the alveoli; $\ce{Cl-}$ returns to the plasma. Inhibition of carbonic anhydrase (for example by acetazolamide) markedly raises tissue $P_{\ce{CO2}}$.

Converting $\ce{CO2}$ to $\ce{HCO3-}$ for transport ties $\ce{CO2}$ transport closely to acid–base balance: the plasma $\ce{HCO3-}$/$\ce{CO2}$ pair constitutes the **bicarbonate buffer system**, and the lungs change the blood pH by adjusting $P_{\ce{CO2}}$ (Chapter 25).

### 19.4.2. Carbaminohemoglobin

$\ce{CO2}$ can bind directly to the free amino groups of the globin chains of hemoglobin (mainly the N-terminal ones):

$$
\ce{Hb-NH2 + CO2 <=> Hb-NHCOO- + H+}.
$$

The reaction needs no enzyme and is rapid and reversible. Deoxyhemoglobin binds far more $\ce{CO2}$ than oxyhemoglobin, so in the tissues hemoglobin binds more $\ce{CO2}$ after releasing $\ce{O2}$, and in the lungs hemoglobin releases $\ce{CO2}$ after being oxygenated. Plasma proteins can also form small amounts of carbamino compounds.

### 19.4.3. The $\ce{CO2}$ Dissociation Curve and the Haldane Effect

The curve relating the $\ce{CO2}$ content of blood to $P_{\ce{CO2}}$ is called the $\ce{CO2}$ dissociation curve. Unlike the oxygen dissociation curve, it is nearly linear over the physiological range, with no saturation plateau, and its slope is about $0.4$–$0.5\,\mathrm{mL}/(100\,\mathrm{mL} \cdot \mathrm{mmHg})$, far greater than that of the upper part of the oxygen dissociation curve. This means that the capacity of blood to carry $\ce{CO2}$ is large, and that a small change in $\ce{CO2}$ content causes only a very small change in $P_{\ce{CO2}}$, which helps keep $P_{\ce{CO2}}$ (and hence pH) stable.

**Definition 19.4.2 (Haldane Effect)** The phenomenon that binding of $\ce{O2}$ to hemoglobin promotes release of $\ce{CO2}$, while deoxygenation allows the blood to bind more $\ce{CO2}$, is called the **Haldane effect** (Christiansen, Douglas and Haldane, 1914). On the $\ce{CO2}$ dissociation curve, at the same $P_{\ce{CO2}}$ the $\ce{CO2}$ content of deoxygenated blood is higher than that of oxygenated blood.

The Haldane effect has two sources, both the other face of the Bohr effect: oxyhemoglobin is the stronger acid and releases $\ce{H+}$, which combines with $\ce{HCO3-}$ to form $\ce{CO2}$; and oxyhemoglobin is also less able to form carbamate. Its contribution to $\ce{CO2}$ transport is large: between the arterial point ($P_{\ce{CO2}} = 40$, saturation 97%) and the venous point (46, 75%), the $\ce{CO2}$ content increases by about 4 mL/100 mL, of which about half comes from the rise in $P_{\ce{CO2}}$ and about half from deoxygenation itself (panel C). Without the Haldane effect, the arteriovenous difference in $P_{\ce{CO2}}$ would have to be about twice as large to transport the same amount of $\ce{CO2}$.

The Bohr effect and the Haldane effect are two statements of the same thermodynamic coupling: binding of $\ce{O2}$ and binding of $\ce{H+}$ (and $\ce{CO2}$) are antagonistic. In the tissues, the entry of $\ce{CO2}$ and $\ce{H+}$ promotes release of $\ce{O2}$ (Bohr), and the release of $\ce{O2}$ in turn promotes binding of $\ce{CO2}$ and $\ce{H+}$ (Haldane); in the lungs both proceed in reverse.

### 19.4.4. The Respiratory Exchange Ratio

At rest, each $100\,\mathrm{mL}$ of blood flowing through the tissues gives up about 5 mL of $\ce{O2}$ and carries away about 4 mL of $\ce{CO2}$, a ratio of about 0.8, which is the steady-state value of the **respiratory exchange ratio** (Definition 17.6.1). It reflects the fuel oxidized by the tissues: in complete oxidation of carbohydrate, one $\ce{CO2}$ is produced per $\ce{O2}$ consumed and $R = 1.0$; in oxidation of fat, part of the $\ce{O2}$ combines with hydrogen to form water and $R \approx 0.7$. In hyperventilation, extra $\ce{CO2}$ is expelled from body stores, and $R$ can transiently exceed 1; in strenuous exercise, buffering of lactate by bicarbonate releases $\ce{CO2}$, and $R$ also exceeds 1 (Chapter 30).

## 19.5. Oxygen Delivery and Hypoxia

The amount of $\ce{O2}$ delivered to the tissues of the whole body per minute (oxygen delivery) equals **cardiac output** times the arterial oxygen content:

$$
\dot D_{\ce{O2}} = \dot Q \times C_{\mathrm{aO_2}} \approx 5\,\mathrm{L\,min^{-1}} \times 195\,\mathrm{mL\,L^{-1}} \approx 1000\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}}.
$$

Resting oxygen consumption is about $250\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}}$, so only about 1/4 is extracted (the **Fick principle**: $\dot V_{\ce{O2}} = \dot Q\,(C_{\mathrm{aO_2}} - C_{\bar{\mathrm{v}}\mathrm{O_2}})$). Oxygen delivery has a reserve of about fourfold, and since cardiac output can increase about fivefold in exercise, maximal oxygen consumption can reach 15–20 times the resting value.

The state in which tissues do not receive enough $\ce{O2}$, or cannot use it, is called hypoxia. According to which link in the oxygen-delivery equation fails, hypoxia is divided into four types:

| Type | Cause | Arterial $P_{\ce{O2}}$ | Arterial oxygen content | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hypoxic (hypoxemic) | Arterial $P_{\ce{O2}}$ reduced | Low | Low | Altitude, hypoventilation, ventilation–perfusion mismatch, shunt, diffusion impairment |
| Anemic (hematologic) | Amount or function of hemoglobin reduced | Normal | Low | Anemia, CO poisoning, methemoglobinemia |
| Circulatory (stagnant) | Blood flow reduced | Normal | Normal | Shock, heart failure, local ischemia |
| Histotoxic (tissue) | Cells cannot use $\ce{O2}$ | Normal | Normal (venous oxygen content is actually raised) | Inhibition of cytochrome oxidase by cyanide |

Only hypoxic hypoxia effectively stimulates ventilation through the **peripheral chemoreceptors** (Chapter 20), and only in it can the hypoxemia be markedly corrected by oxygen breathing (shunt excepted; question 4 of Chapter 18); in CO poisoning oxygen acts mainly by speeding the dissociation of CO (Section 19.3.4).

## 19.6. Evolution and Comparison

### 19.6.1. Respiratory Pigments

At least three classes of respiratory pigments have arisen independently in animals: hemoglobin ($\ce{Fe}$, heme), hemocyanin ($\ce{Cu}$) and hemerythrin ($\ce{Fe}$, nonheme). Their distribution, color and location are given in Section 11.8.2. From the standpoint of transport, several points deserve comparison:

- Oxygen-carrying capacity: the oxygen capacity of vertebrate blood is about $15$–$20\,\mathrm{mL}/100\,\mathrm{mL}$; the concentration of hemocyanin dissolved in hemolymph is limited by **colloid osmotic pressure** and viscosity, and the oxygen capacity is usually only $1$–$4\,\mathrm{mL}/100\,\mathrm{mL}$ (higher in cephalopods, about $4$–$5$). The cardiac output of octopuses and squids therefore has to be very large, and a swimming squid extracts almost all the $\ce{O2}$ in its blood.
- Cooperativity and regulation: the giant extracellular hemocyanins and erythrocruorins are composed of dozens to hundreds of subunits, with Hill coefficients of 4–9 or even higher and a strong Bohr effect; the hemocyanins of crustaceans are also regulated by lactate, urate, and $\ce{Mg^2+}$ and $\ce{Ca^2+}$, acting like 2,3-BPG in vertebrates.
- Heterotropic regulators also differ among vertebrates: mammals use 2,3-BPG, birds inositol pentaphosphate (IPP), and fish ATP and GTP; the hemoglobin of crocodilians has its affinity strongly lowered by $\ce{HCO3-}$, so that during prolonged dives, as $\ce{CO2}$ accumulates, they can release almost all of the bound $\ce{O2}$ (Komiyama et al., 1995).

Antarctic icefish have no hemoglobin at all and live on dissolved $\ce{O2}$, the extreme case of "no need for a respiratory pigment" (Section 11.8.2).

### 19.6.2. Adaptation of Affinity: Altitude and Body Size

At altitude the $P_{\ce{O2}}$ of inspired air is low and uptake in the lung becomes limiting; here hemoglobin of high affinity (low P₅₀) is advantageous, because it remains nearly saturated at a low alveolar $P_{\ce{O2}}$. Many high-altitude species have independently evolved high-affinity hemoglobins:

| Species | Change in P₅₀ | Molecular mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| Bar-headed goose (flies over the Himalayas) | About 10 mmHg lower than the lowland greylag goose | Pro→Ala at position 119 of the $\alpha^{\mathrm{A}}$ chain weakens one contact at the $\alpha_1\beta_1$ interface and destabilizes the T state (inferred by Perutz and later confirmed experimentally) |
| Andean goose | Likewise raised affinity | Leu→Ser at position 55 of the $\beta$ chain, on the other side of the same interface |
| Llama, vicuña (Andes) | P₅₀ about 20 mmHg, lower than in most mammals | His→Asn at position 2 of the $\beta$ chain, weakening 2,3-BPG binding |
| Deer mouse (North American high-altitude populations) | Higher affinity in high-altitude populations | Several sites in the $\alpha$ and $\beta$ globins, with additive effects (Storz et al.) |

The two geese reached the same result with different mutations, an example of **convergent evolution** at the molecular level. Humans have adapted to altitude in different ways: in short-term acclimatization 2,3-BPG increases and the curve shifts to the right, and hemoglobin increases with the rise in **erythropoietin** (Chapter 11, Section 20.6.3); long-resident Andeans have a high hemoglobin concentration, whereas Tibetans have a hemoglobin concentration close to that of lowlanders, and their adaptation is associated with alleles of *EPAS1* (encoding HIF-2$\alpha$) in the hypoxia-inducible factor pathway, an allele that may have come from **gene flow** with **Denisovans** (Huerta-Sánchez et al., 2014).

Affinity also varies with body size: small mammals have blood with a higher P₅₀ and a stronger Bohr effect (about 40 mmHg in mouse, about 22 mmHg in elephant; Schmidt-Nielsen and Larimer, 1958). Small animals have a high oxygen consumption per unit body mass, and a lower affinity lets tissues unload $\ce{O2}$ at a higher $P_{\ce{O2}}$, maintaining a steeper diffusion gradient; this is related to their living near sea level, where uptake in the lung is not limiting. Burrowing mammals (such as moles and gophers) have blood of higher affinity, adapted to the low-$\ce{O2}$, high-$\ce{CO2}$ air of burrows.

### 19.6.3. $\ce{O2}$ Stores in Diving Mammals

Deep-diving seals and whales do not carry much $\ce{O2}$ in the lungs (they let the alveoli collapse during a dive, Section 18.7.5), but store it in the blood and muscle:

| Property | Human | Weddell seal |
|---|---|---|
| Blood volume / (mL kg⁻¹) | about 70 | about 200 |
| Hematocrit | about 0.45 | about 0.6 (the spleen releases stored red cells into the circulation during a dive) |
| Muscle myoglobin / (mg g⁻¹) | about 5 | about 45 (sperm whale about 70) |
| $\ce{O2}$ store per unit body mass | about 20 mL kg⁻¹ | about 80–90 mL kg⁻¹ |
| Distribution of the $\ce{O2}$ store | lung about 1/3, blood about 1/2 | lung about 5%, blood about 70%, muscle about 25% |

That myoglobin can reach such a high concentration without precipitating is related to an increase in the net positive charge on its surface: molecules carrying like charges repel one another, raising solubility. Along the phylogeny of mammals, the net surface charge of myoglobin has risen independently in diving groups such as whales, seals, manatees, beavers and water shrews, and it can be used to infer the diving ability of extinct species (Mirceta et al., 2013). These $\ce{O2}$ stores work together with the **diving reflex** (bradycardia and peripheral vasoconstriction, Chapter 16): the $\ce{O2}$ in the blood is supplied mainly to the brain and heart, and muscle relies on its own myoglobin store before switching to anaerobic metabolism.

### 19.6.4. The Root Effect and Countercurrent Multiplication in the Swim Bladder

In the hemoglobin of many teleosts, a fall in pH not only lowers the affinity (Bohr effect) but also lowers the oxygen capacity itself: even at a very high $P_{\ce{O2}}$, some of the hemes cannot bind $\ce{O2}$.

**Definition 19.6.1 (Root Effect)** The decrease in the oxygen capacity (maximum saturation) of hemoglobin when blood pH falls is called the **Root effect** (Root, 1931).

The Root effect allows fish to "pump" $\ce{O2}$ to extremely high partial pressures. The gas gland of the swim bladder secretes lactate and $\ce{CO2}$, acidifying the blood that flows through it; hemoglobin releases $\ce{O2}$ through the Root effect, the local $P_{\ce{O2}}$ rises, and $\ce{O2}$ diffuses into the bladder. If this blood of high $P_{\ce{O2}}$ simply flowed away, the effect would be limited; but the vessels entering and leaving the gas gland are arranged as a well-developed countercurrent network (the **rete mirabile**), in which the outgoing veins lie close beside the incoming arteries, so that $\ce{O2}$ (and lactate and $\ce{CO2}$) in the venous blood diffuses into the arterial blood and is carried back to the gland, accumulating step by step along the network (**countercurrent multiplication**, the same principle as the mechanism in the renal medulla, Chapter 24). The longer the rete, the higher the partial pressure that can be reached: in deep-sea fish the rete can be several centimeters long, so that the bladder can still be filled with gas at depths of hundreds of meters, and the partial pressure of $\ce{O2}$ in the bladder can reach tens or even more than a hundred atmospheres. Behind the retina of many fish eyes there is a similar choroidal rete, which supplies the avascular retina with a high $P_{\ce{O2}}$. The Root effect is thus an example of turning a "defect" into a function by means of **countercurrent exchange** (Definition 17.3.1).

## Questions

1. Why is the transfer of $\ce{N2O}$ across the lung perfusion-limited and that of CO diffusion-limited, while that of $\ce{O2}$ is normally perfusion-limited? Under what conditions does $\ce{O2}$ become diffusion-limited?

   **Answer** $\ce{N2O}$ does not bind to hemoglobin, so the "capacity" of blood for it is only physical dissolution; a small amount of diffusion brings the plasma partial pressure up to that in the alveolus, after which no more is transferred, and the amount transferred depends only on how much fresh blood flows past, that is, perfusion limitation. CO binds hemoglobin extremely tightly, hemoglobin acts as a bottomless sink, the plasma CO partial pressure stays close to zero, the pressure difference is constant along the whole length, and the amount transferred depends only on the diffusing capacity of the membrane, that is, diffusion limitation. $\ce{O2}$ lies between the two: hemoglobin gives the blood a large capacity, but equilibration is still reached after about 0.25 s, and the remaining 0.5 s is reserve, so it is perfusion-limited under normal conditions. In strenuous exercise (shorter transit time), with thickening or loss of area of the respiratory membrane (lower $D_{\mathrm{L}}$), or at altitude (smaller initial pressure difference), blood has no time to equilibrate before leaving, and $\ce{O2}$ transfer becomes diffusion-limited (panel B).

2. Anemia lowers hemoglobin from 150 to 75 g/L; compare this with the binding of 50% of the hemoglobin by CO. Which affects tissue oxygen supply more seriously, and why?

   **Answer** In both cases the oxygen capacity is halved. In anemia the dissociation curve of the remaining hemoglobin is normal; when tissue $P_{\ce{O2}}$ falls to 40 mmHg, about 1/4 of the bound oxygen can still be unloaded, and more can be unloaded if it falls a little further; cardiac output can also increase to compensate. In CO poisoning the remaining hemoglobin has a higher affinity and the curve is shifted to the left, so almost no $\ce{O2}$ is released at 40 mmHg, and tissue $P_{\ce{O2}}$ must fall very low before $\ce{O2}$ can be obtained, so the brain and myocardium are damaged first. In addition, arterial $P_{\ce{O2}}$ is normal, the chemoreceptors are not stimulated, and there is no compensatory increase in ventilation. CO poisoning is therefore much more serious (panel B).

3. Why does tissue oxygen supply fall transiently after transfusion of a large volume of blood stored for 3 weeks? Explain with the oxygen dissociation curve.

   **Answer** Glycolysis in the red cells of stored blood nearly stops, and 2,3-BPG falls markedly within 1–3 weeks, so the affinity of hemoglobin increases, the curve shifts to the left and P₅₀ falls. After transfusion, this blood releases less $\ce{O2}$ at the same tissue $P_{\ce{O2}}$, and the oxygen utilization coefficient falls, until the red cells resynthesize 2,3-BPG in the body (about half in a day, essentially complete in two to three days).

4. Calculation: a person has a cardiac output of 5 L/min, hemoglobin 140 g/L, arterial saturation 97% and mixed venous saturation 70%. Find the oxygen content of arterial and venous blood (neglecting dissolved $\ce{O2}$), the oxygen consumption and the oxygen utilization coefficient.

   **Answer** Oxygen capacity $= 1.34 \times 14 = 18.8\,\mathrm{mL}/100\,\mathrm{mL}$. Arterial blood has $18.8 \times 0.97 = 18.2$ and venous blood $18.8 \times 0.70 = 13.1\,\mathrm{mL}/100\,\mathrm{mL}$. By the Fick principle, oxygen consumption $= 50\,\mathrm{dL\,min^{-1}} \times (18.2 - 13.1) = 255\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}}$; the oxygen utilization coefficient $= 5.1/18.2 \approx 28\%$.

5. Use the Bohr and Haldane effects to explain why hemoglobin is at once an efficient carrier of $\ce{O2}$, a carrier of $\ce{CO2}$ and a buffer of $\ce{H+}$. What happens to venous and tissue $P_{\ce{CO2}}$ if carbonic anhydrase is inhibited?

   **Answer** Binding of $\ce{O2}$ and binding of $\ce{H+}$ and $\ce{CO2}$ are antagonistic. In the tissues, $\ce{CO2}$ enters the red cell and carbonic anhydrase converts it into $\ce{H+}$ and $\ce{HCO3-}$; $\ce{H+}$ makes hemoglobin release $\ce{O2}$ (Bohr), and the deoxyhemoglobin so formed binds $\ce{H+}$ better and forms carbamate (Haldane), which both buffers the pH and allows more $\ce{CO2}$ to be transported as $\ce{HCO3-}$ and carbaminohemoglobin; in the lungs everything runs in reverse. When carbonic anhydrase is inhibited, hydration of $\ce{CO2}$ is slower, the red cell cannot convert $\ce{CO2}$ to $\ce{HCO3-}$ in time, and the same amount of $\ce{CO2}$ can be carried only in dissolved form, so the efficiency of $\ce{CO2}$ transport by blood falls and tissue and venous $P_{\ce{CO2}}$ rise; in the lungs $\ce{HCO3-}$ is likewise not converted back to $\ce{CO2}$ in time, and elimination is impeded. Acetazolamide also causes **metabolic acidosis** through the kidney, and can be used to prevent acute mountain sickness (Section 20.6.3).

6. Why have animals resident at altitude mostly evolved hemoglobin of high affinity, whereas small mammals at sea level have hemoglobin of low affinity? Is there a contradiction?

   **Answer** There is no contradiction; the optimal P₅₀ depends on whether uptake or unloading is the limiting end. At altitude the $P_{\ce{O2}}$ of inspired air is low and uptake in the lung is limiting; high affinity keeps the blood nearly saturated at a low alveolar $P_{\ce{O2}}$, and the gain exceeds the loss in unloading. At sea level the alveolar $P_{\ce{O2}}$ lies on the flat upper part of the curve, and arterial blood is nearly saturated even at a lower affinity; small mammals have a very high oxygen consumption per unit body mass, unloading is limiting, and a lower affinity lets the tissues obtain $\ce{O2}$ at a higher $P_{\ce{O2}}$, maintaining the diffusion gradient from capillary to mitochondrion.
