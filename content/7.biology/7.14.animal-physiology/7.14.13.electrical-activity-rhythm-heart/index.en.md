The human heart beats about 100,000 times a day and about 3 billion times in a lifetime, each beat initiated by an electrical signal generated by the heart itself and spreading through the atria and ventricles in a strict temporal order: atria first, then ventricles, with the parts of the ventricles activated almost simultaneously. The previous chapter treated the heart as a pump; this chapter asks where the electrical activity that drives the pump comes from, how it propagates and how it is regulated: the generation of rhythm (Section 13.1), the action potential and refractory period of working myocardial cells (Section 13.2), conduction of excitation in the heart (Section 13.3), the electrocardiogram recorded at the body surface (Section 13.4), the influence of the autonomic nerves and ions (Section 13.5), and the mechanisms of rhythm disturbance (Section 13.6). The ionic basis of the action potential, the **Goldman–Hodgkin–Katz equation**, the **cable equation** and the **Hodgkin–Huxley model**, with their derivation and simulation, are in [7.6.30](/7.6.30); the action potentials of neurons and skeletal muscle are in Chapter 4, and excitation–contraction coupling of cardiac cells and the structure of the intercalated disc are in Chapter 10. Here we give only the conclusions and how they are used in the heart.

## 13.1. Rhythmicity of the Heart and Autorhythmic Cells

### 13.1.1. Automaticity and Pacemakers

A heart removed from the body and placed in a suitable solution continues to beat rhythmically, which shows that the rhythm arises in the heart itself and not in the nerves.

**Definition 13.1.1 (Automaticity)** The property of a tissue or cell of generating rhythmic excitation spontaneously, in the absence of external stimulation, is called **automaticity**, measured by the number of spontaneous excitations per unit time (the automatic rate).

The cells of the heart fall into two classes: working cells (atrial and ventricular muscle) are contractile and normally lack automaticity; autorhythmic cells (the sinoatrial node, part of the atrioventricular node, the His bundle, bundle branches and Purkinje fibers) have automaticity but almost no contractile function. The automatic rate differs among sites:

| Site | Automatic rate (beats/min) |
|---|---|
| Sinoatrial node | About 100 (intrinsic rate when isolated or denervated; about 70 at rest in vivo, suppressed by the vagus) |
| Atrioventricular junction | 40–60 |
| His bundle and bundle branches | About 40 |
| Purkinje fibers | 15–40 |

**Definition 13.1.2 (Sinoatrial Node)** The flat, elliptical structure made of specialized cardiac cells, located in the wall of the right atrium near the entrance of the superior vena cava, is called the **sinoatrial node**; it is the normal pacemaker of the heart. The human sinoatrial node is about $15\,\mathrm{mm}$ long, $3\,\mathrm{mm}$ wide and $1\,\mathrm{mm}$ thick, and its cells have almost no contractile fibers and are only $3$–$5\,\mathrm{\mu m}$ in diameter.

A rhythm controlled by the sinoatrial node is called sinus rhythm. Autorhythmic tissue other than the sinoatrial node normally only conducts excitation and does not express its own rhythm, and is called latent pacemaker; when it takes over from the sinoatrial node in initiating the heartbeat it is called an ectopic pacemaker.

### 13.1.2. The Action Potential of Sinoatrial Node Cells

The action potential of sinoatrial node cells differs from that of nerve and working myocardium: it has no stable resting potential, and after each **repolarization** the membrane potential immediately begins a slow **depolarization**.

- Phase 4 (spontaneous diastolic depolarization): the maximum diastolic potential reached by repolarization is about $-60$ to $-65\,\mathrm{mV}$; the membrane potential then rises slowly at a few tens of millivolts per second and triggers phase 0 on reaching the **threshold potential** of about $-40\,\mathrm{mV}$. The rate of phase 4 depolarization determines the pacing frequency.
- Phase 0 (depolarization): caused by the opening of L-type calcium channels ($\mathrm{Ca_V1.3}$ and $\mathrm{Ca_V1.2}$) and $\ce{Ca^2+}$ influx; the depolarization is slow (about $10\,\mathrm{V\,s^{-1}}$, about 1/20 that of working myocardium) and of low amplitude (peak 0 to $+15\,\mathrm{mV}$). The maximum diastolic potential of sinoatrial node cells is around $-60\,\mathrm{mV}$, where most fast sodium channels are inactivated, so phase 0 depends on calcium current.
- Phase 3 (repolarization): there is no distinct phase 1 or plateau; calcium channels inactivate, delayed rectifier potassium channels activate, and $\ce{K+}$ efflux repolarizes the membrane to the maximum diastolic potential.

Cardiac cells whose phase 0 is produced by calcium current depolarize slowly and conduct slowly, and are called slow-response cells (the sinoatrial and atrioventricular nodes); those whose phase 0 is produced by sodium current are called fast-response cells (atrial muscle, ventricular muscle, the His bundle and Purkinje fibers). The two are compared in Section 13.2.2.

### 13.1.3. Membrane Clock and Calcium Clock

The spontaneous depolarization of phase 4 results from the waxing and waning of several currents, which can be grouped into two mutually coupled "clocks" (the coupled-clock model proposed by Lakatta and colleagues):

The *membrane clock*: the pacemaker current is the main component.

**Definition 13.1.3 (Funny Current)** The inward current carried by hyperpolarization-activated, cyclic nucleotide-gated cation channels (HCN channels, mainly HCN4 in the sinoatrial node) is called the **funny current** $I_{\mathrm{f}}$ (the "funny" current, because unlike most voltage-gated currents it activates on membrane hyperpolarization).

HCN channels are permeable to both $\ce{Na+}$ and $\ce{K+}$, with a reversal potential of about $-20$ to $-30\,\mathrm{mV}$. They activate gradually after each repolarization below $-60\,\mathrm{mV}$ and produce an inward $\ce{Na+}$ current at diastolic potentials that depolarizes the membrane; their activation curve is directly regulated by intracellular cAMP: cAMP binds the cyclic nucleotide-binding domain at the C-terminus of the channel and shifts the activation curve in the depolarizing direction by about $10\,\mathrm{mV}$. At the same time, the delayed rectifier potassium current activated in phase 3 gradually deactivates during diastole, and the decrease of outward current also depolarizes the membrane; in the second half of phase 4, T-type calcium channels ($\mathrm{Ca_V3.1}$, activating at about $-60\,\mathrm{mV}$) and L-type calcium channels begin to open and push the membrane to threshold.

The *calcium clock*: in the second half of diastole the sarcoplasmic reticulum of sinoatrial node cells spontaneously releases $\ce{Ca^2+}$ locally (mediated by the ryanodine receptor RyR2, in the form of "calcium sparks"), and the released $\ce{Ca^2+}$ is extruded by the membrane sodium–calcium exchanger (NCX), exchanging $3\ce{Na+}$ for $1\ce{Ca^2+}$; each $\ce{Ca^2+}$ extruded brings in a net positive charge, producing an inward current that accelerates the late depolarization of phase 4. The $\ce{Ca^2+}$ of the sarcoplasmic reticulum is taken back by SERCA, and the cycle of reuptake and release is itself an oscillator.

The two clocks are coupled to each other: through L-type calcium channels the membrane potential determines how much $\ce{Ca^2+}$ enters with each beat and how much the sarcoplasmic reticulum is filled; the moment of release of $\ce{Ca^2+}$ from the sarcoplasmic reticulum in turn influences the membrane potential through the NCX current. Both are regulated by cAMP–PKA (PKA phosphorylates the L-type calcium channel, phospholamban and RyR2), which lets the **sympathetic nervous system** speed both clocks at once (Section 13.5). A drug that blocks $I_{\mathrm{f}}$ (ivabradine) lowers heart rate by 10%–20% without stopping pacing completely, showing that pacing does not depend on a single current but has redundant safeguards.

### 13.1.4. The Hierarchy of Pacemakers

How does the sinoatrial node, with the highest automatic rate, control the other pacemakers? There are two mechanisms:

- Pre-emptive capture: the sinoatrial node has the highest frequency, and before the spontaneous phase 4 depolarization of a latent pacemaker reaches threshold, excitation arriving from the sinoatrial node has already made it fire an action potential, so its own rhythm has no time to appear.
- Overdrive suppression: when an autorhythmic cell is driven passively by stimuli at a rate higher than its own, once the driving stops its automatic activity resumes only after a quiescent period, and for some time after resumption it runs below its normal rate.

**Definition 13.1.4 (Overdrive Suppression)** The phenomenon in which the automaticity of an autorhythmic cell is inhibited when it is driven at a rate higher than its intrinsic rate, and takes some time to recover after the driving stops, is called **overdrive suppression**. The greater the difference between the driving rate and the intrinsic rate, the stronger the suppression and the slower the recovery.

The main cause of overdrive suppression is the $\ce{Na+}$/$\ce{K+}$-ATPase: during rapid firing every action potential lets $\ce{Na+}$ enter the cell, the increased intracellular $\ce{Na+}$ increases pump activity, and since the pump moves out 3 $\ce{Na+}$ for 2 $\ce{K+}$ moved in, the outward current produced hyperpolarizes the membrane and opposes the depolarization of phase 4; after the driving stops, it takes some time for the pump to restore $\ce{Na+}$ to normal before the outward pump current decreases. Clinically, when the sinoatrial node stops abruptly, latent pacemakers cannot take over at once and a period of cardiac arrest (which can cause syncope) results; when an artificial pacemaker must be replaced, the pacing rate also has to be lowered gradually. In disease of the sinoatrial node the rhythm of a latent pacemaker can become dominant (escape rhythm, at a lower rate); when the automaticity of a latent pacemaker is abnormally increased it may pre-empt the sinoatrial node and form an ectopic rhythm.

### 13.1.5. A Model of Pacing

The logic of pacing can be expressed by a minimal ionic current model. Let the membrane of a sinoatrial node cell (specific capacitance $C_{\mathrm{m}} = 1\,\mathrm{\mu F\,cm^{-2}}$) carry an L-type calcium current, a delayed rectifier potassium current, the pacemaker current, a background inward current and an acetylcholine-activated potassium current:

$$
C_{\mathrm{m}}\frac{\mathrm{d}V}{\mathrm{d}t} = -\bigl(I_{\mathrm{CaL}} + I_{\mathrm{K}} + I_{\mathrm{f}} + I_{\mathrm{b}} + I_{\mathrm{K,ACh}}\bigr),
$$

with each current written as conductance times driving force, $g\,x\,(V - E)$, and the gating variable $x$ approaching its steady-state value by first-order kinetics $\mathrm{d}x/\mathrm{d}t = (x_\infty(V) - x)/\tau_x$ (the same form as the Hodgkin–Huxley model, [7.6.30](/7.6.30#sec-30.6)). The activation $y_\infty(V) = 1/(1 + \mathrm{e}^{(V - V_{1/2})/k})$ of $I_{\mathrm{f}}$ increases with hyperpolarization; sympathetic transmitter shifts $V_{1/2}$ to the right through cAMP, and vagal transmitter shifts it to the left. The calcium clock is not modeled separately, and its effect is partly reflected in the size of $I_{\mathrm{CaL}}$.

A: membrane potential; spontaneous diastolic depolarization carries the membrane from the maximum diastolic potential to threshold (dotted line). Norepinephrine (activation curve of $I_{\mathrm{f}}$ shifted right by 8 mV, $I_{\mathrm{CaL}}$ increased by 50%) steepens phase 4 and raises the frequency; acetylcholine ($I_{\mathrm{f}}$ shifted left by 7 mV, and $I_{\mathrm{K,ACh}}$ activated) makes the maximum diastolic potential more negative, phase 4 shallower and the frequency lower. B: frequency when only one mechanism is changed at a time: each mechanism contributes, and together the effect is largest; blocking $I_{\mathrm{f}}$ lowers the frequency without stopping pacing](./plots/sa-node-pacemaker.py)

The model shows three ways in which the autonomic nerves change heart rate (Section 13.5): changing the slope of phase 4 depolarization (activation of $I_{\mathrm{f}}$), changing the maximum diastolic potential ($I_{\mathrm{K,ACh}}$), and changing the threshold or the current of phase 0 ($I_{\mathrm{CaL}}$).

## 13.2. The Action Potential of Ventricular Myocytes

### 13.2.1. The Resting Potential and the Phases of the Action Potential

The resting potential of ventricular myocytes is about $-90\,\mathrm{mV}$, close to the **equilibrium potential** of $\ce{K+}$ ($E_{\mathrm{K}} \approx -95\,\mathrm{mV}$ for $[\ce{K+}]_{\mathrm{o}} = 4$ and $[\ce{K+}]_{\mathrm{i}} = 140\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$). It is maintained mainly by the inward rectifier potassium channel ($I_{\mathrm{K1}}$, Kir2.1): this channel has a high conductance near the resting potential and closes on depolarization, blocked by intracellular $\mathrm{Mg^{2+}}$ and polyamines, so that it both stabilizes the resting potential near $E_{\mathrm{K}}$ and avoids wasting a large $\ce{K+}$ efflux during the plateau. A small background $\ce{Na+}$ influx makes the resting potential slightly more positive than $E_{\mathrm{K}}$; the general theory of the **resting potential** is in Chapter 4.

The **action potential** of ventricular muscle lasts $250$–$300\,\mathrm{ms}$ (about $2$–$5\,\mathrm{ms}$ in skeletal muscle) and is divided into five phases:

| Phase | Membrane potential | Main ionic currents (channels) | Features |
|---|---|---|---|
| Phase 0 (depolarization) | $-90 \to +30\,\mathrm{mV}$ | Fast sodium current $I_{\mathrm{Na}}$ ($\mathrm{Na_V1.5}$), inward | Lasts $1$–$2\,\mathrm{ms}$, maximum rate of depolarization $200$–$300\,\mathrm{V\,s^{-1}}$; sodium channels then inactivate |
| Phase 1 (early rapid repolarization) | $+30 \to$ about $0\,\mathrm{mV}$ | Transient outward potassium current $I_{\mathrm{to}}$ ($\mathrm{K_V4.2/4.3}$), outward | Lasts about $10\,\mathrm{ms}$, forming the "notch" (more evident on the epicardial side) |
| Phase 2 (plateau) | Stays near 0 mV | L-type calcium current $I_{\mathrm{CaL}}$ ($\mathrm{Ca_V1.2}$), inward, roughly balanced by delayed rectifier potassium currents ($I_{\mathrm{Kr}}$, $I_{\mathrm{Ks}}$), outward; inward rectifier potassium channels closed | Lasts $100$–$150\,\mathrm{ms}$; the entering $\ce{Ca^2+}$ triggers release of $\ce{Ca^2+}$ from the sarcoplasmic reticulum (**calcium-induced calcium release**, Chapter 10) |
| Phase 3 (late rapid repolarization) | $0 \to -90\,\mathrm{mV}$ | $I_{\mathrm{CaL}}$ inactivates; $I_{\mathrm{Kr}}$ (hERG), $I_{\mathrm{Ks}}$ and $I_{\mathrm{K1}}$, which reopens during repolarization, are outward | Regenerative repolarization: the more repolarized, the larger $I_{\mathrm{K1}}$ |
| Phase 4 (resting) | Stable at $-90\,\mathrm{mV}$ | $I_{\mathrm{K1}}$; $\ce{Na+}$/$\ce{K+}$-ATPase, NCX and the membrane calcium pump restore ion concentrations | $\ce{Ca^2+}$ entering with each beat is extruded mainly by NCX |

The plateau is the most important feature of the cardiac action potential: it lets a large amount of $\ce{Ca^2+}$ enter the cell to trigger contraction and makes the action potential and the contraction roughly overlap in time, which brings about the long refractory period (Section 13.2.3). The action potential of atrial muscle is similar to that of ventricular muscle, but the plateau is shorter and the whole action potential lasts only about $150\,\mathrm{ms}$. Many drugs block the hERG channel ($I_{\mathrm{Kr}}$), delaying repolarization and prolonging the action potential and the QT interval, which can cause fatal arrhythmias; new drugs must therefore be tested for their effect on hERG.

### 13.2.2. Fast-Response Cells and Slow-Response Cells

| | Fast-response cells (atrial muscle, ventricular muscle, His–Purkinje system) | Slow-response cells (sinoatrial node, atrioventricular node) |
|---|---|---|
| Resting potential or maximum diastolic potential | $-80$ to $-90\,\mathrm{mV}$ | $-60$ to $-65\,\mathrm{mV}$ |
| Current of phase 0 | $\ce{Na+}$ (fast sodium channels) | $\ce{Ca^2+}$ (L-type calcium channels) |
| Rate and amplitude of phase 0 | $200$–$300\,\mathrm{V\,s^{-1}}$, about $120\,\mathrm{mV}$ | About $10\,\mathrm{V\,s^{-1}}$, about $70\,\mathrm{mV}$ |
| Conduction velocity | Fast ($0.3$–$4\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$) | Slow ($0.02$–$0.05\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$) |
| Phase 4 | Stable in working cells; slow spontaneous depolarization in Purkinje cells (mainly $I_{\mathrm{f}}$) | Marked spontaneous depolarization |
| Refractory period | Determined mainly by recovery of sodium channels, comparable to the action potential duration | Calcium channels recover slowly, and the refractory period can continue after repolarization is complete (postrepolarization refractoriness) |

Under pathological conditions (ischemia, high potassium reducing the resting potential, sodium channel inactivation), fast-response cells can switch to slow-response electrical activity with slowed conduction, creating conditions for reentry (Section 13.6).

### 13.2.3. Cyclic Changes of Excitability

After each action potential of a cardiac cell, excitability goes through a cyclic change, based on the recovery of sodium channels (calcium channels in slow-response cells) from inactivation:

- Effective refractory period: from the beginning of phase 0 until repolarization in phase 3 reaches about $-55\,\mathrm{mV}$, all sodium channels are inactivated and a stimulus of any strength cannot evoke a response (**absolute refractory period**); from $-55$ to $-60\,\mathrm{mV}$, a few sodium channels begin to recover, and a strong stimulus can cause local depolarization but cannot produce a propagating action potential (local response period). Together the two are called the effective refractory period.
- **Relative refractory period**: from $-60$ to $-80\,\mathrm{mV}$, the recovered sodium channels gradually increase, and a suprathreshold stimulus can evoke an action potential, but its phase 0 is smaller and slower, conduction is slower, and the action potential duration is also shorter.
- Supernormal period: from $-80$ to $-90\,\mathrm{mV}$, sodium channels have essentially recovered, and since the membrane potential is close to threshold even a subthreshold stimulus can evoke an action potential (excitability higher than normal), although its phase 0 is still slightly smaller.

**Definition 13.2.1 (Effective Refractory Period)** The period from the beginning of phase 0 of the action potential until repolarization to about $-60\,\mathrm{mV}$, during which no stimulus can evoke a propagating action potential in a cardiac cell, is called the **effective refractory period**. The effective refractory period of ventricular muscle is about $200$–$250\,\mathrm{ms}$ and covers almost the whole of systole and early diastole.

The especially long effective refractory period of cardiac muscle has an important consequence: cardiac muscle does not undergo **tetanic contraction**. The refractory period of skeletal muscle is only a few milliseconds, far shorter than the duration of contraction (tens of milliseconds), so high-frequency stimulation can summate contractions into tetanus (Chapter 9); the effective refractory period of cardiac muscle lasts until the end of systole and beyond the beginning of diastole, no further excitation is possible during systole, and every contraction is necessarily followed by relaxation, which ensures that the ventricle can fill and allows the heart to pump.

If, after the effective refractory period and before the next sinoatrial excitation arrives, the ventricle receives an extra stimulus (from an ectopic pacemaker or artificial stimulation), an early excitation and contraction can result, called a *premature contraction* (in common speech an extrasystole). The premature excitation has its own effective refractory period; the sinoatrial excitation that arrives immediately afterward often falls within this effective refractory period and cannot excite the ventricle, which must wait until the next sinoatrial excitation to contract. Hence a premature contraction is often followed by a longer ventricular diastole, called a *compensatory pause*; the interval between the two normal beats before and after a ventricular premature beat equals exactly two normal **cardiac cycles** (a full compensatory pause), because the rhythm of the sinoatrial node has not been disturbed. A premature contraction has a short filling time and a small **stroke volume**; the beat after the compensatory pause has more filling and contracts more strongly, and patients often feel "the heart stopped for a moment and then thumped hard."

## 13.3. Conduction of Excitation

### 13.3.1. The Conduction System and Conduction Velocities at Each Site

Excitation from the sinoatrial node passes through atrial muscle and the preferential conduction pathways in the atrium (internodal tracts) to the atrioventricular node, then through the His bundle, the left and right bundle branches and the Purkinje fiber network to the subendocardial myocardium of the ventricles, and spreads from endocardium to epicardium through the whole ventricle.

| Site | Conduction velocity ($\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$) | Time of arrival of excitation (from the sinoatrial node, s) |
|---|---|---|
| Atrial muscle | $0.3$–$0.5$ | Left atrium about 0.08 |
| Internodal tracts (preferential pathways) | About 1 | Atrioventricular node about 0.03 |
| Atrioventricular node | $0.02$–$0.05$ | Leaving the atrioventricular node about 0.12 |
| His bundle and bundle branches | $1$–$2$ | About 0.16 |
| Purkinje fibers | $2$–$4$ | Subendocardial ventricle about 0.17–0.19 |
| Ventricular muscle | $0.3$–$1$ (faster longitudinally, slower transversely) | Epicardium last, about 0.22 |

This sequence of conduction has three functions: the atria contract before the ventricles (atrioventricular delay), and atrial contraction completes the final filling of the ventricles; the left and right ventricles are excited and contract almost simultaneously; and within the ventricle, excitation begins in the endocardium near the apex and spreads toward the base and the epicardium, so that the ventricle contracts from the apex toward the outflow tract like squeezing a tube of toothpaste.

### 13.3.2. The Atrioventricular Delay

**Definition 13.3.1 (Atrioventricular Delay)** The very slow conduction of excitation at the atrioventricular junction (the atrioventricular node), which delays conduction from atrium to ventricle by about $0.1\,\mathrm{s}$, is called the **atrioventricular delay**.

Conduction in the atrioventricular node is slow for several reasons: the node cells are small (about $5$–$10\,\mathrm{\mu m}$ in diameter), gap junctions between cells are few and mainly of connexin Cx45, whose single-channel conductance is small (atrial and ventricular muscle mainly have Cx43, and Purkinje fibers also have Cx40), so the axial resistance of the tissue is high; atrioventricular node cells are slow-response cells whose phase 0 is produced by calcium current and rises slowly, giving weak depolarizing drive to downstream cells; and transitional cells and nodal cells are interleaved in the node, making the path of excitation tortuous.

The significance of the atrioventricular delay is that atrial contraction is completed before that of the ventricles, ensuring ventricular filling; it also limits the maximum ventricular rate: the refractory period of the atrioventricular node is long, and when the atrial rate is too high (as in atrial fibrillation, atrial rate $350$–$600$ beats/min) only some of the atrial excitations can pass through the node, so the ventricular rate is limited to about $100$–$180$ beats/min, avoiding ventricular fibrillation. The atrioventricular node is one of the main sites of action of the vagus nerve; vagal excitation slows atrioventricular conduction and in severe cases blocks it (Section 13.6.2).

### 13.3.3. How Fast Must Ventricular Conduction Be

Synchronous contraction of ventricular muscle requires that excitation spread through the whole ventricle before mechanical contraction begins, that is, within the duration of the QRS complex of the ECG ($60$–$100\,\mathrm{ms}$). The conduction velocity the heart needs can be estimated from this.

**Example 13.3.1 (Estimating the Conduction Velocity)** In an adult the distance from apex to base of the ventricle is about $8$–$10\,\mathrm{cm}$ and the wall thickness is about $1.5\,\mathrm{cm}$ (left ventricle). The spread of excitation has two parts: along the endocardium from apex to base (longitudinal), and from endocardium across the wall to epicardium (transverse). If the transverse conduction velocity of ventricular muscle is $0.3$–$0.5\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, crossing a $1.5\,\mathrm{cm}$ wall takes $30$–$50\,\mathrm{ms}$; within a QRS duration of $80\,\mathrm{ms}$ only about $30$–$50\,\mathrm{ms}$ remains for longitudinal spread, requiring a longitudinal velocity of about

$$
v \approx \frac{9\,\mathrm{cm}}{40\,\mathrm{ms}} \approx 2.2\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}.
$$

:::

This is several times faster than the conduction velocity of ventricular muscle itself ($0.3$–$1\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$); if excitation were conducted by ventricular muscle alone, longitudinal spread would take $100$–$300\,\mathrm{ms}$, and contraction of apex and base would differ by more than half a systole. The heart's solution is to separate the two requirements: the longitudinal one is met by the Purkinje fiber network ($2$–$4\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$), which delivers excitation to the whole endocardium almost simultaneously; the transverse one is met by ventricular muscle cells, which reach $0.3$–$1\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ through the electrical coupling of the intercalated discs. In bundle branch block, one ventricle can be activated only slowly by ventricular muscle from the opposite side, the QRS complex widens to more than $120\,\mathrm{ms}$, the two ventricles contract asynchronously and pumping efficiency falls.

### 13.3.4. Intercalated Discs, Electrical Coupling and Cable Theory

Cardiac cells are branched cells about $100\,\mathrm{\mu m}$ long and $10$–$25\,\mathrm{\mu m}$ in diameter, joined to one another end to end through **intercalated discs**. The **gap junctions** in the discs are made of **connexins** (mainly Cx43 in ventricular muscle); each channel has a pore diameter of about $1.2\,\mathrm{nm}$ and lets ions and small molecules pass, connecting the cytoplasm of adjacent cells electrically; adherens junctions and desmosomes (together the area composita) provide the mechanical connection and are also the structural basis of electrical coupling (Chapter 10).

The classical understanding is described by the **cable equation** ([7.6.30](/7.6.30#sec-30.5)): depolarization of one cell causes local current to flow through gap junctions into the adjacent resting cell and depolarize it to threshold, and excitation propagates in this way as a regenerative wave. Treating the myocardium as a continuous cable, the relation between conduction velocity and tissue parameters is

$$
\theta \propto \frac{1}{\sqrt{r_{\mathrm{i}}}},
$$

that is, with membrane excitability (the size and kinetics of the ionic currents) unchanged, the conduction velocity is inversely proportional to the square root of the axial resistance per unit length (the sum of cytoplasmic resistance and gap junction resistance) and proportional to the square root of the fiber diameter ([7.6.30](/7.6.30#sec-30.6.4)). This is a relation that is often misstated: conduction velocity is not inversely proportional to the square of resistance but to its square root, and halving the gap junction conductance reduces the velocity only to about $1/\sqrt2 = 71\%$. Continuous cable theory also explains the anisotropy of cardiac conduction: cardiac cells are aligned along their long axis, the intercalated discs are mainly at the cell ends, the axial resistance is smaller longitudinally, and the longitudinal conduction velocity is 2–3 times the transverse one.

But cardiac muscle is not truly a continuous cable. The resistance is concentrated at the gap junctions between cells, and when coupling is weakened beyond a certain point excitation within each cell is almost instantaneous, the delay is concentrated at the junctions between cells, conduction becomes a step-by-step "jump," the velocity falls faster than the continuous theory predicts, and finally block occurs when the current delivered to the downstream cell is insufficient to bring it to threshold (source–sink mismatch). The simulation below shows this.

![Conduction of excitation along a one-dimensional chain of cardiac cells (Aliev–Panfilov cardiac excitation model, cell length 100 µm, conduction velocity set to 0.5 m/s at normal coupling). A: conduction velocity as a function of gap junction coupling strength (log–log axes). At stronger coupling the velocity is proportional to $1/\sqrt{r_{\mathrm{i}}}$, in agreement with the prediction of the continuous cable (dashed line); below about 10% of normal coupling the velocity falls faster, and conduction block occurs at about 0.7%. Heterozygous deletion of Cx43 (coupling roughly halved) lowers the velocity by only about 30% in this model. B: activation time along the chain of cells; at very weak coupling the delay is concentrated between cells (a staircase), and the velocity can be as low as a few centimeters per second without block](./plots/gap-junction-conduction.py)

The simulation illustrates an important property of cardiac conduction: conduction has a large safety margin against loss of gap junctions, excitation still propagates slowly when coupling is reduced to a few percent of normal, and very slow conduction is exactly the condition for reentry (Section 13.6.3).

In Cx43-knockout mice ventricular conduction velocity falls only partially, less than the simple cable model would predict, which prompted a reconsideration of a mode of coupling that does not require gap junctions. At the intercalated disc the membranes of two cells are separated only by a narrow cleft of a few tens of nanometers, where sodium channels $\mathrm{Na_V1.5}$ are densely distributed. When the upstream cell depolarizes, the sodium channels in its disc membrane open and rapidly draw $\ce{Na+}$ and positive charge out of the narrow cleft, making the extracellular potential in the cleft negative; for the downstream cell, a negative extracellular potential is equivalent to a positive transmembrane potential, so the sodium channels in the disc membrane of the downstream cell are activated. This coupling through the extracellular electric field rather than direct current is called *ephaptic coupling*, also known as electric-field coupling. Current models combine the direct electrical coupling of gap junctions with ephaptic coupling, the former dominating at normal coupling and the latter providing an additional safety margin when gap junctions are reduced. Defects of disc proteins are closely related to arrhythmia: mutations of desmosomal proteins (PKP2, DSG2, DSC2) cause arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy, and mutations of $\mathrm{Na_V1.5}$ cause Brugada syndrome and type 3 long QT syndrome; Cx43 is reduced in cardiac hypertrophy and heart failure and redistributes from the disc to the lateral cell surface, slowing conduction and aggravating anisotropy.

### 13.3.5. Specializations of Purkinje Cells

The Purkinje fiber network is the "highway" of the ventricle, and every specialization of its cells meets the requirements of cable theory for fast conduction:

- Large cell diameter ($30$–$70\,\mathrm{\mu m}$, about twice that of ventricular myocytes), so the axial resistance is small;
- High density of sodium channels, so phase 0 depolarization is fast and the local currents generated are large;
- Dense gap junctions between cells, rich in Cx40 of higher conductance, with cells connected mainly end to end;
- Few myofibrils, almost no contraction, and dedication to conduction; few or no transverse tubules.

The junction between Purkinje fibers and ventricular muscle has some unidirectionality (Purkinje cells are small and the ventricular muscle mass is large, so conduction from Purkinje fiber to ventricular muscle requires more current), and ventricular excitation does not readily travel backward into the conduction system. Purkinje cells have the longest action potential and the longest effective refractory period and act as a "gate," preventing premature excitation from entering the ventricle.

## 13.4. The Electrocardiogram

### 13.4.1. Principle of the Electrocardiogram

**Definition 13.4.1 (Electrocardiogram)** The curve of the potential changes produced when the heart is excited, conducted through body tissues to the body surface and recorded there with electrodes, is called the **electrocardiogram** (ECG).

The electrical activity of the heart can be regarded as a time-varying current dipole $\boldsymbol{p}(t)$ in a volume conductor: at each moment there are current sources and sinks at the interface between excited (depolarized) tissue and resting tissue, and seen from a distance their total effect is an **electric dipole**. The potential difference between two electrodes on the body surface is the projection of the dipole onto the lead vector determined by the electrode positions, and the potential is linearly related to the dipole ([7.6.30](/7.6.30#sec-30.9), which has the derivation and a simulation of a synthetic ECG). This explains several basic properties of the ECG:

- The ECG records the composite vector of the electrical activity of all cells of the heart, which differs from the shape of the action potential of a single cell and is not directly related to the mechanical contraction of the heart (in electromechanical dissociation the ECG can be normal while the heart ejects no blood).
- An upward deflection is recorded when the wave of excitation travels toward the positive electrode and a downward deflection when it travels away; when no wave is advancing (all depolarized or all resting) the dipole is zero and the baseline (isoelectric line) is recorded.
- The amplitude reflects how much myocardium takes part in excitation and how synchronous it is: the ventricular mass is large, so the QRS wave is largest; the electrical activity of the sinoatrial and atrioventricular nodes is too small to be recorded at the body surface.

### 13.4.2. Leads and Waveforms

The placement and connection of the electrodes for recording an ECG is called a lead, and 12 are routinely recorded clinically:

- Limb leads: standard leads I (left arm − right arm), II (left leg − right arm) and III (left leg − left arm), which satisfy Einthoven's law $\mathrm{II} = \mathrm{I} + \mathrm{III}$; and the augmented unipolar limb leads aVR, aVL and aVF (one limb electrode against the mean potential of the other two). The six limb leads form a hexaxial system in the frontal plane, used to determine the electrical axis.
- Chest leads V1–V6: electrodes are placed in turn at six positions across the chest from the right parasternal line to the left midaxillary line, reflecting the electrical activity of the heart in the horizontal plane (V1 and V2 face the right ventricle, V5 and V6 the left ventricle).

The waveforms and intervals of the normal ECG (taking standard lead II as an example):

| Wave or interval | Duration | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| P wave | $0.08$–$0.11\,\mathrm{s}$, amplitude not more than $0.25\,\mathrm{mV}$ | Depolarization of the left and right atria |
| PR interval (start of P wave to start of QRS) | $0.12$–$0.20\,\mathrm{s}$ | Conduction time of excitation from atrium to ventricle, mainly the atrioventricular delay |
| QRS complex | $0.06$–$0.10\,\mathrm{s}$ | Depolarization of the left and right ventricles; Q wave downward, R wave upward, S wave downward, not all of which appear in every lead |
| ST segment | Level with the baseline | The ventricles are entirely in the plateau and there is no potential difference between parts; elevated or depressed in myocardial ischemia or infarction |
| T wave | $0.05$–$0.25\,\mathrm{s}$, direction usually the same as the main QRS deflection | Repolarization of the ventricles |
| QT interval (start of QRS to end of T wave) | About $0.36$–$0.44\,\mathrm{s}$ at a heart rate of 75 beats/min | Total time of ventricular depolarization and repolarization, approximately the action potential duration of ventricular muscle |
| U wave | A small wave after the T wave, often inconspicuous | The mechanism is not fully understood and may be related to repolarization of Purkinje fibers or midmyocardium; prominent in hypokalemia |

The atrial repolarization wave (Ta wave) is very small and overlaps the QRS complex and is generally not seen. That the T wave has the same direction as the QRS seems to contradict the idea that "repolarization is opposite to depolarization": this is because the action potential of epicardial myocardium is shorter than that of endocardium, ventricular repolarization proceeds from epicardium to endocardium, opposite to the direction of depolarization, and two opposite processes produce dipoles in the same direction.

### 13.4.3. Reading Heart Rate and Rhythm

The ECG paper runs at $25\,\mathrm{mm\,s^{-1}}$; each small square $1\,\mathrm{mm} = 0.04\,\mathrm{s}$ and each large square (5 small squares) $= 0.2\,\mathrm{s}$; vertically every $10\,\mathrm{mm} = 1\,\mathrm{mV}$.

**Example 13.4.1 (Reading Heart Rate from the RR Interval)** The heart rate equals 60 divided by the interval between two adjacent R waves (the RR interval, in seconds). If the RR interval is 4 large squares, then $\mathrm{RR} = 0.8\,\mathrm{s}$ and the heart rate $= 60/0.8 = 75$ beats/min; a common quick method is heart rate $= 300/\text{number of large squares} = 1500/\text{number of small squares}$. The QT interval varies with heart rate, and for comparison the corrected $\mathrm{QT_c} = \mathrm{QT}/\sqrt{\mathrm{RR}}$ is used (Bazett's formula, RR in seconds): at a heart rate of 100 beats/min, with measured $\mathrm{QT} = 0.34\,\mathrm{s}$ and $\mathrm{RR} = 0.6\,\mathrm{s}$, $\mathrm{QT_c} = 0.34/0.775 = 0.44\,\mathrm{s}$, at the upper limit of normal.

:::

The criteria for sinus rhythm are: a P wave before every QRS, P waves upright in leads I, II and aVF and inverted in aVR (excitation propagates from the sinoatrial node at upper right toward lower left), and a PR interval fixed at $0.12$–$0.20\,\mathrm{s}$. In adults a sinus rate of 60–100 beats/min is normal; below 60 beats/min is sinus bradycardia (the resting heart rate of athletes can be as low as 40–50 beats/min, which is physiological) and above 100 beats/min is sinus tachycardia. When the RR interval of sinus rhythm varies regularly with respiration (shortening on inspiration and lengthening on expiration), it is called sinus arrhythmia; it is common in children and young adults, results from the variation of vagal tone with respiration, and is normal.

## 13.5. Effects of the Autonomic Nerves and Ions on Electrical Activity

### 13.5.1. Acetylcholine

Acetylcholine released by vagal nerve endings acts on $\mathrm{M}_2$ muscarinic receptors of the sinoatrial node, atrial muscle and atrioventricular node. The $\mathrm{M}_2$ receptor is a $\mathrm{G_i}$-coupled **G protein-coupled receptor** that acts through two routes:

- The $\beta\gamma$ subunit of the G protein directly activates the G protein-gated inward rectifier potassium channel (GIRK, Kir3.1/3.4), producing $I_{\mathrm{K,ACh}}$: the maximum diastolic potential of sinoatrial node cells becomes more negative and farther from threshold; the action potential of atrial muscle is shortened and the refractory period shortened.
- The $\alpha_{\mathrm{i}}$ subunit inhibits adenylyl cyclase and cAMP falls: the activation curve of $I_{\mathrm{f}}$ shifts left and phase 4 depolarization becomes slower; the L-type calcium current decreases, and phase 0 of the atrioventricular node becomes smaller and slower.

The result is a slowing of heart rate (negative chronotropy), slowing of atrioventricular conduction (negative dromotropy) and weakening of atrial contractility (negative inotropy); the direct effect on ventricular muscle is small because vagal innervation of the ventricle is sparse. Activation of $I_{\mathrm{K,ACh}}$ needs no second messenger and acts within a single beat, so the vagus nerve can regulate heart rate beat by beat (as in respiratory sinus arrhythmia and the fast component of the **baroreceptor reflex**).

### 13.5.2. Norepinephrine

Norepinephrine released by sympathetic nerve endings (and epinephrine in the blood) acts on $\beta_1$ receptors and through $\mathrm{G_s}$ activates adenylyl cyclase, increasing cAMP:

- cAMP binds HCN channels directly, shifting the activation curve of $I_{\mathrm{f}}$ to the right and speeding phase 4 depolarization; PKA phosphorylates the L-type calcium channel and the proteins of the calcium clock, and both clocks speed up: heart rate increases (positive chronotropy).
- The calcium current of the atrioventricular node increases, phase 0 speeds up, and conduction is faster (positive dromotropy).
- The L-type calcium current of ventricular muscle increases, both the entering $\ce{Ca^2+}$ and the $\ce{Ca^2+}$ released from the sarcoplasmic reticulum increase, and contraction is strengthened (positive inotropy); PKA phosphorylates phospholamban, relieving its inhibition of SERCA, and phosphorylates troponin I, lowering the sensitivity of the myofilaments to $\ce{Ca^2+}$, both of which speed relaxation (positive lusitropy); at the same time $I_{\mathrm{Ks}}$ is enhanced, so that the action potential shortens appropriately when heart rate rises, ensuring sufficient time for diastole.

### 13.5.3. Extracellular $\ce{K+}$ and $\ce{Ca^2+}$

| Change | Effect on electrical activity | ECG |
|---|---|---|
| Hyperkalemia | $E_{\mathrm{K}}$ becomes more positive and the resting potential is reduced (partial depolarization); excitability rises when mild, but with greater elevation more sodium channels inactivate, phase 0 becomes slower and smaller, and conduction slows; the conductance of $I_{\mathrm{Kr}}$ increases and repolarization is faster | Tall peaked T wave → flattened and absent P wave, widened QRS → sine-wave pattern, ventricular fibrillation or asystole |
| Hypokalemia | The resting potential is more negative, but the conductances of $I_{\mathrm{K1}}$ and $I_{\mathrm{Kr}}$ decrease and repolarization is delayed; automaticity of Purkinje fibers increases | Flattened T wave, prominent U wave, prolonged QT (QU); ectopic beats are likely |
| Hypercalcemia | Plateau $\ce{Ca^2+}$ influx increases, shortening the plateau; contraction strengthened | Shortened QT |
| Hypocalcemia | Plateau prolonged | Prolonged QT (prolonged ST segment) |

This influence of potassium shows that the resting potential of cardiac muscle is determined mainly by $\ce{K+}$, which in turn determines the availability of sodium channels, so extracellular $\ce{K+}$ is the ion to which the electrical activity of the heart is most sensitive; intravenous injection of concentrated potassium chloride can stop the heart in diastole, and the cardioplegic solutions of cardiac surgery make use of this.

## 13.6. Mechanisms of Arrhythmia

Arrhythmia is an abnormality of the origin, rate, rhythm or conduction of the cardiac impulse. By mechanism it can be divided into abnormalities of impulse formation and abnormalities of impulse conduction.

### 13.6.1. Abnormal Automaticity and Triggered Activity

- Abnormal automaticity: the automaticity of the sinoatrial node is too high or too low (sinus tachycardia or bradycardia, sick sinus syndrome); the automaticity of latent pacemakers is increased (sympathetic excitation, hypokalemia, ischemia accelerating phase 4 of Purkinje fibers), or working myocardium shows abnormal spontaneous depolarization when partially depolarized, forming ectopic beats.
- *Triggered activity*: oscillations of membrane potential after an action potential (afterdepolarizations) reach threshold and trigger a new action potential. Early afterdepolarizations occur during repolarization (phases 2 and 3), mostly when the action potential is prolonged (as with hERG blockade or long QT syndrome), when L-type calcium channels recover during the long plateau and reopen; delayed afterdepolarizations occur after repolarization is complete (phase 4), mostly with intracellular $\ce{Ca^2+}$ overload (digitalis toxicity, sympathetic excitation), when $\ce{Ca^2+}$ spontaneously released from the sarcoplasmic reticulum produces an inward current through NCX. Early afterdepolarizations can trigger torsades de pointes ventricular tachycardia.

### 13.6.2. Conduction Block

Conduction block is most common at the atrioventricular junction:

| Type | ECG appearance | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| First-degree AV block | Prolonged PR interval ($> 0.20\,\mathrm{s}$), a QRS after every P wave | Atrioventricular conduction slowed but not interrupted |
| Second-degree AV block | Some P waves not followed by a QRS (dropped beats); type I (Wenckebach): PR lengthens progressively until one beat is dropped; type II: PR fixed with sudden dropping | Partial block in the atrioventricular node (type I) or the His–Purkinje system (type II) |
| Third-degree (complete) AV block | P waves and QRS complexes independent; atria beat at the sinus rate and ventricles at the escape rate (30–40 beats/min) | Atrioventricular conduction completely interrupted, and a latent pacemaker takes over ventricular pacing; there is a period of asystole between the onset of block and the appearance of the escape rhythm (overdrive suppression), which can cause syncope |

Bundle branch block delays excitation of one ventricle and widens the QRS complex (Section 13.3.3).

### 13.6.3. Reentry and Wavelength

In the normal heart, excitation dies out after it has spread through the heart, because all the myocardium is refractory and there is no tissue left to excite. If excitation can travel around a circular path back to its starting point and that point has by then recovered from refractoriness, the excitation circulates continuously in the loop and excites the heart once more with each circuit; this is reentry.

**Definition 13.6.1 (Reentry)** The phenomenon in which excitation propagates around a circular pathway and, on returning to its starting site, finds that site recovered in excitability, so that it enters again and circulates repeatedly in the loop, is called **reentry**.

Reentry needs three conditions: a circular conduction pathway (anatomical, such as the dual pathways of the atrioventricular node or an accessory atrioventricular pathway; or functional, such as the border of an infarct scar); a unidirectional block in the loop, so that excitation can enter the loop only in one direction; and a time for excitation to travel once around the loop that is longer than the effective refractory period at every point of the loop. The third condition can be expressed by the wavelength:

$$
\lambda = \theta \times \mathrm{ERP},
$$

that is, the distance excitation travels in one effective refractory period. Only if the length $L$ of the loop is greater than $\lambda$ does the front of the reentrant wave not catch up with the refractory tissue at its own tail.

**Example 13.6.1 (Conditions for Reentry)** In normal ventricular muscle $\theta = 0.5\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ and $\mathrm{ERP} = 0.25\,\mathrm{s}$, so $\lambda = 12.5\,\mathrm{cm}$, longer than any small loop in the ventricle, and reentry does not occur readily in the normal ventricle. When myocardial ischemia lowers the conduction velocity to $0.05\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ (the case in the figure when coupling is reduced to about 2%) and the ERP is shortened to $0.2\,\mathrm{s}$, $\lambda = 1\,\mathrm{cm}$, and a loop a few centimeters in circumference around an infarct scar is enough to sustain reentry. Slowing conduction or shortening the refractory period therefore both promote reentry, while prolonging the refractory period (as with class III antiarrhythmic drugs that block potassium channels) increases the wavelength and terminates reentry; but excessive prolongation of repolarization may cause early afterdepolarizations, which is the dilemma of antiarrhythmic drugs.

:::

Reentry is the mechanism of most tachyarrhythmias: atrioventricular nodal reentrant tachycardia, atrioventricular reentrant tachycardia through an accessory pathway (pre-excitation syndrome), atrial flutter (a macroreentry around the tricuspid annulus), and ventricular tachycardia after myocardial infarction. In atrial and ventricular fibrillation, multiple unstable reentrant waves (spiral waves) are continually generated, collide and break up in the myocardium, and the myocardium loses coordinated contraction: in ventricular fibrillation the heart cannot eject blood and death follows within minutes; electrical defibrillation uses a strong current to depolarize all the myocardium simultaneously into refractoriness, extinguishing all the reentrant waves at once so that the sinoatrial node can resume pacing.

## 13.7. Evolution and Comparative Biology

### 13.7.1. Myogenic and Neurogenic Hearts

By the source of the rhythm, animal hearts fall into two classes:

- *Myogenic hearts*: the rhythm is generated by specialized cardiac cells themselves, and nerves only regulate its rate and force. The hearts of vertebrates, mollusks and most insects are myogenic. An isolated frog or snail heart continues to beat for a long time in physiological solution.
- *Neurogenic hearts*: the rhythm is generated by ganglia on the heart, the cardiac muscle itself cannot excite spontaneously, and every contraction is driven by a burst of impulses from the ganglion. The hearts of the horseshoe crab and of decapod crustaceans (lobsters, crabs) are neurogenic: Carlson found in 1904 that the heartbeat stops at once when the dorsal ganglion of the horseshoe crab heart is removed. The cardiac ganglion of the lobster has only 9 neurons (4 small pacemaker interneurons and 5 large motor neurons), which form a small **central pattern generator** that produces periodic bursts of discharge; the neurons of the ganglion are coupled to one another by **electrical synapses**, which synchronizes the bursts. This simple ganglion is a classical model for studying rhythmic circuits and the action of neuromodulators (Chapter 6).

Both kinds of heart can generate a stable rhythm; the difference is where the "metronome" is placed. The rhythm of a myogenic heart is more robust to nerve injury, and the heart can continue to work after denervation (heart transplantation depends on this); the rhythm of a neurogenic heart can be changed quickly and flexibly by neuromodulators and sensory feedback, but damage to the ganglion stops the heart.

### 13.7.2. Heartbeat Reversal in Tunicates

The heart of a tunicate (sea squirt) is a V- or U-shaped tube with a pacemaker region at each end. When the pacemaker region at one end dominates, the peristaltic wave passes from this end to the other and blood flows in one direction; usually after a few minutes (the interval varies with species, temperature and individual) the pacemaker region at the other end takes over, and the direction of the peristaltic wave and of blood flow reverses. The mechanism of reversal is thought to be competition between the two pacemaker regions: the dominant end drives the other and causes overdrive suppression of it (Section 13.1.4); as the driving end "fatigues" (its frequency gradually falls) and the suppressed end gradually recovers, the two alternate in leading in frequency. The dorsal vessel of many insects also shows similar periodic reversal (hemolymph flowing alternately forward and backward). In these animals the direction of blood in the circulatory system is variable, which suits their open or semi-open circulation.

### 13.7.3. Scaling of Heart Rate with Body Size

The resting heart rate of mammals falls systematically with increasing body mass. The fit of Stahl (1967) to mammalian data gives

$$
f_{\mathrm{H}} \approx 241\,M^{-0.25}\ \text{beats/min} \quad (M \text{ in kg}),
$$

with an exponent close to $-1/4$ (the fitting methods of **allometry** are in [3.3 Linear Regression Analysis](/3.3)).

| Animal | Body mass (approx.) | From the formula (beats/min) | Measured resting heart rate (approx., beats/min) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Shrew | 3 g | 1030 | 800–1000 (over 1000 when active) |
| Mouse | 25 g | 610 | 500–700 |
| Rat | 300 g | 325 | 300–400 |
| Human | 70 kg | 83 | 60–80 |
| Horse | 500 kg | 51 | 30–40 |
| Elephant | 4000 kg | 30 | 25–30 |

Why is the exponent $-1/4$? Resting metabolic rate increases as $M^{3/4}$ according to **Kleiber's law** (Chapter 30) and **cardiac output** is proportional to metabolic rate; the mass of the heart and the stroke volume are both roughly proportional to body mass ($\propto M^{1}$; the mammalian heart is about 0.6% of body mass). Hence

$$
f_{\mathrm{H}} = \frac{\mathrm{CO}}{\mathrm{SV}} \propto \frac{M^{3/4}}{M^{1}} = M^{-1/4}.
$$

The lifespan of mammals increases roughly as $M^{1/4}$, and the product of the two, the total number of heartbeats in a lifetime, is roughly independent of body mass, about $10^9$ (humans, with about $3 \times 10^9$, are a notable exception). The scaling of heart rate also holds in other vertebrates, but at the same body mass **ectotherms** have much lower heart rates (consistent with their lower metabolic rates) and depend strongly on temperature: for every $10$ °C rise in temperature the heart rate of fishes and reptiles increases about 2–3-fold ($Q_{10}$); birds have heart rates slightly higher than mammals of the same mass, and the heart rate of a hummingbird in flight can reach 1200 beats/min. The upper limit of frequency is constrained by the action potential duration of cardiac muscle and the speed of **excitation–contraction coupling**: the ventricular action potential of small mammals is very short (about $50\,\mathrm{ms}$ in the mouse, with almost no plateau), repolarizing rapidly mainly through $I_{\mathrm{to}}$, and $\ce{Ca^2+}$ cycling in cardiac cells depends more on the sarcoplasmic reticulum.

## Questions

1. Why is phase 0 of sinoatrial node cells produced by calcium current rather than sodium current? What might happen if the maximum diastolic potential of a sinoatrial node cell were hyperpolarized artificially to −90 mV?

   **Answer** The maximum diastolic potential of sinoatrial node cells is about −60 mV, at which most of the inactivation gates of fast sodium channels are closed (the midpoint of the steady-state inactivation curve is about −80 to −70 mV), few sodium channels are available, and phase 0 can rely only on the L-type calcium channels, which have a higher threshold and slower activation. If the membrane were hyperpolarized to −90 mV, sodium channels would recover and a fast sodium-dependent depolarization could be produced; but the membrane would at the same time be farther from threshold, phase 4 depolarization would take longer ($I_{\mathrm{f}}$ activates more at more negative potentials, but the background potassium current also increases), and pacing would slow or even stop. This is exactly the mechanism by which vagal activation of $I_{\mathrm{K,ACh}}$ slows the heart rate.

2. The effective refractory period of ventricular muscle is about 250 ms. At a heart rate of 150 beats/min can the effective refractory period still cover systole? How does the myocardium adapt to a high heart rate?

   **Answer** At 150 beats/min the cycle is 400 ms. If the action potential duration and effective refractory period stayed at 250 ms, only 150 ms would remain for diastole. In fact the action potential duration of the myocardium shortens as heart rate rises (sympathetic nerves strengthen $I_{\mathrm{Ks}}$; at high frequency the accumulation of intracellular $\ce{Ca^2+}$ and $\ce{Na+}$ alters the NCX current and pump current; $I_{\mathrm{CaL}}$ has no time to recover fully), and the effective refractory period shortens with it, as does systole. The refractory period therefore still roughly covers systole, the myocardium still does not undergo tetanus, and diastole is preserved (the QT interval shortens with heart rate, which is why $\mathrm{QT_c}$ correction is needed).

3. In a patient's ECG, P waves appear regularly at 80 beats/min and QRS complexes also appear regularly at 35 beats/min, with no fixed relation between them. What is the diagnosis? Why might the QRS complex be widened? Why did the patient faint at the onset?

   **Answer** Third-degree (complete) atrioventricular block: the atria are paced by the sinoatrial node and the ventricles by a latent pacemaker below the site of block, independently of each other. If the block is below the His bundle, the escape pacemaker is in the bundle branches or Purkinje fibers, excitation does not reach both ventricles simultaneously through the normal conduction system but spreads slowly through ventricular muscle from one side, and the QRS is widened. When the block occurs suddenly, latent pacemakers that have long been overdriven by the sinoatrial node undergo overdrive suppression and cannot take over at once, resulting in several seconds of ventricular asystole, interruption of cerebral blood supply and syncope (Adams–Stokes syndrome).

4. Using cable theory, estimate how many times faster than ventricular muscle a Purkinje fiber, whose diameter is about 2.5 times that of a ventricular myocyte, would conduct if all other parameters were the same. What other factors are needed to explain the actual ratio (about 4–8)?

   **Answer** In a continuous cable the conduction velocity is proportional to the square root of the diameter (the axial resistance per unit length $r_{\mathrm{i}} \propto 1/d^2$ and the membrane capacitance $\propto d$, which together give $\theta \propto \sqrt{d}$), so a diameter 2.5 times larger gives only $\sqrt{2.5} \approx 1.6$ times. The rest of the difference comes from the following: Purkinje cells have a higher density of sodium channels, so the phase 0 current is larger and rises faster; there are more gap junctions between cells, mainly Cx40 of higher conductance, and the cells are connected mainly end to end, so intercellular resistance is lower; and Purkinje fibers are arranged in bundles and are relatively electrically isolated from the surrounding myocardium, so current is not shunted into a large mass of ventricular muscle.

5. Why does the ECG in hyperkalemia first show tall peaked T waves, and in severe cases a widened QRS and finally asystole?

   **Answer** Raised extracellular $\ce{K+}$ increases the conductance of potassium channels such as $I_{\mathrm{Kr}}$ (the conductance of $I_{\mathrm{Kr}}$ increases with extracellular $\ce{K+}$), so phase 3 repolarization is faster and more synchronous and the T wave becomes tall and peaked; this appears while the resting potential has changed little. Further elevation of $\ce{K+}$ makes $E_{\mathrm{K}}$ and the resting potential more positive, more sodium channels inactivate, and the rate and amplitude of phase 0 fall and conduction slows: atrial conduction is affected first (the P wave flattens and disappears), and slowed intraventricular conduction widens the QRS; finally most sodium channels are inactivated, the myocardium loses excitability, and the heart stops in diastole (or ventricular fibrillation first occurs through reentry).

6. The heart rate of a mouse is about 600 beats/min. Using the scaling relation of heart rate and Kleiber's law, explain why a mouse must have such a high heart rate; what is distinctive about its ventricular action potential?

   **Answer** The metabolic rate per unit body mass of a mouse is about $(0.025/70)^{-1/4} \approx 7$ times that of a human, and the cardiac output required per unit body mass is also about 7 times higher; since stroke volume and heart mass are both proportional to body mass, the stroke volume per unit body mass is similar to that of a human, so the only way is to raise the heart rate about 7-fold ($83 \times 7 \approx 600$ beats/min). The cycle is only 100 ms, and the action potential must be very short (about 50 ms); the mouse ventricular muscle has almost no plateau, repolarization depends mainly on a strong $I_{\mathrm{to}}$ and ultrarapid delayed rectifier current, and $\ce{Ca^2+}$ reuptake depends mainly on SERCA (about 90%, compared with about 70% in humans). This makes the mouse ECG very different from the human one (no distinct ST segment), and care is needed in using the mouse to study human repolarization abnormalities (such as long QT syndrome).

7. (Open question) Design an experiment to decide whether the heart of an arthropod is myogenic or neurogenic.

   **Answer** Outline: (1) After removing the ganglion on the heart, or blocking it with tetrodotoxin or similar, observe whether the heart continues to contract spontaneously: a myogenic heart continues to beat and a neurogenic heart stops, in which case direct electrical stimulation of the muscle can still evoke contraction (excluding damage to the muscle). (2) Record the extracellular discharge of the ganglion and the electrical activity of the myocardium simultaneously: in a neurogenic heart every contraction is preceded by a burst of ganglion discharge, in one-to-one correspondence; in a myogenic heart the electrical activity of the muscle precedes or is independent of neural activity. (3) Block the neuromuscular junction with an antagonist of the appropriate transmitter receptor (the motor neurons of the crustacean cardiac ganglion release mainly glutamate, so a glutamate receptor antagonist rather than a cholinergic one should be used), and a neurogenic heart stops. (4) A developmental approach: observe whether the heart is already beating before the ganglion has formed in the embryo; a myogenic heart begins to beat before innervation is established (as in vertebrate embryos).
