The previous chapter showed that almost all of the body's energy ends up as heat ([7.14.30](/7.14.30)). Heat is a by-product of metabolism, and temperature in turn determines the rate of metabolism: the rate of nearly every physiological process varies with temperature, and the structure of macromolecules and the physical state of membranes also depend on it. Two basic questions follow. When body temperature changes, how do physiological processes cope? And what does it take to keep body temperature constant? The first problem faces all animals; the second is the solution adopted by birds and mammals. This chapter begins with human body temperature and the physics of heat exchange, then turns to the effects of temperature on molecules and cells, and then to the **negative feedback** system with which humans maintain body temperature (thermoregulation is the classic example of the feedback concept of Chapter 1, [7.14.1](/7.14.1#sec-1.6)). It ends with a long section comparing the thermal strategies of different animals, which concludes this part on digestion, metabolism and body temperature. For the physics of heat conduction, radiation and phase change see thermal physics ([5.2.2](/5.2.2)); for the molecular mechanism of heat production in brown fat see [7.1.23](/7.1.23#sec-23.5.1).

## 31.1. Body Temperature

### 31.1.1. Core Temperature and Surface Temperature

**Definition 31.1.1 (Core Temperature)** The temperature of the deep body (the organs of the cranial, thoracic and abdominal cavities) is called the **core temperature**, also the body-core temperature; the temperature of the skin, the subcutaneous tissue and the superficial layers of the limbs is called the surface (shell) temperature. By body temperature, physiology means the core temperature.

The core temperature of humans is remarkably constant. Except in fever it stays within $\pm0.6\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$ of $37\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$; during strenuous exercise or extreme cold it can fluctuate between $35.5$ and $40\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$. The temperatures of the individual core organs differ slightly: the liver, the most metabolically active, is at about $38\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$, and the brain is close to $38\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$. The circulating blood brings them toward a common value, so the temperature of deep blood represents the mean core temperature. Clinically, convenient sites are used as substitutes: rectal temperature (normally about $36.9$–$37.9\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$) is closest to the core temperature, the mouth (under the tongue) is about $0.3\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$ lower, and the axilla is a further $0.4\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$ lower; the temperature of the mid-esophagus is close to the blood temperature of the right atrium, and the tympanic temperature is close to the hypothalamic temperature.

Surface temperature varies much more. At an ambient temperature of $23\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$, the skin of the forehead is at about $33$–$34\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$, the trunk about $32\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$, the hand about $30\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$ and the foot about $27\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$; in the cold the hands and feet cool the most, which is why they are the first to suffer frostbite in winter. The boundary between "core" and "shell" is not fixed either: in the cold, peripheral vessels constrict, the core shrinks to the cranial cavity and trunk, and the limbs become an insulating shell with a steep temperature gradient; in the heat the core expands into the limbs.

### 31.1.2. Physiological Variations in Body Temperature

- Circadian rhythm: the temperature is lowest between 2 and 6 a.m. and highest between 2 and 6 p.m., fluctuating by about $0.5$–$0.7\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$ (generally no more than $1\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$). It is driven by the biological clock of the suprachiasmatic nucleus and persists in a subject kept in bed in a constant environment, so it is not a by-product of activity.
- Menstrual cycle: a woman's basal body temperature is low in the follicular phase and lowest on the day of ovulation; after ovulation it rises by $0.3$–$0.5\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$ and remains elevated until the next menstruation. The rise is caused by progesterone secreted by the corpus luteum acting on the hypothalamus ([7.14.37](/7.14.37)). The mean temperature of women is about $0.3\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$ higher than that of men.
- Age: children run slightly higher and the elderly slightly lower; newborns (especially premature infants) regulate temperature poorly and are easily affected by ambient temperature.
- Muscular activity and emotion: body temperature rises during exercise, and the core temperature of a marathon runner can reach $39$–$40\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$; emotional tension also raises it. Anesthetic drugs depress the thermoregulatory center and dilate skin vessels, so patients under surgery need to be kept warm.

### 31.1.3. The Balance of Heat Production and Heat Loss

Body temperature is determined by the balance between heat production and heat loss. Heat production is a by-product of metabolism, so every factor that affects the metabolic rate affects it ([7.14.30](/7.14.30#sec-30.2.4)); at rest the main heat-producing organs are the viscera (the liver most of all), and during exercise the skeletal muscles. Heat is lost mainly through the skin: blood first carries heat from the core to the skin, and the skin then gives it up to the environment, so the rate of loss depends on the speed of these two steps. The rate of change of the heat stored in the body is

**Formula 31.1.1 (Heat Balance Equation)**

$$
S = M - W - (R + C + K) - E_{\mathrm{v}},
\tag{31.1}
$$
where $M$ is metabolic heat production, $W$ the external mechanical work, $R$, $C$ and $K$ the heat lost by radiation, convection and conduction, respectively ("dry" heat exchange, which may be positive or negative), $E_{\mathrm{v}}$ the evaporative heat loss, and $S$ the rate of change of stored body heat; $S = mc\,\mathrm{d}T/\mathrm{d}t$, and the mean specific heat capacity of human tissue is $c \approx 3.5\,\mathrm{kJ\,kg^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$.

**Example 31.1.1** A person of 70 kg produces $80\,\mathrm{W}$ of heat at rest. If all routes of heat loss were blocked, by how much would body temperature rise per hour? What if he were running and produced $800\,\mathrm{W}$?

**Answer** $\mathrm{d}T/\mathrm{d}t = 80/(70\times3500) = 3.3\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{K\,s^{-1}} = 1.2\,\mathrm{K\,h^{-1}}$; while running, $12\,\mathrm{K\,h^{-1}}$, or about $1\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$ every 5 minutes. The regulation of heat loss therefore has to be fast and powerful: during exercise, sweating and skin blood flow must increase greatly within a few minutes.

## 31.2. The Physics of Heat Exchange

### 31.2.1. The Four Routes

There are four routes of heat exchange between the body and the environment:

- Conduction: heat passes from a hot place to a cold place through the motion and collisions of molecules within a solid or a stationary fluid, with no macroscopic movement of the material itself. By **Fourier's law** ([5.2.2](/5.2.2#sec-2.4.1)), the heat flow through a layer of material of area $A$ and thickness $L$ is

  $$
  \Phi = \lambda A\,\frac{\Delta T}{L},
  $$
  where $\lambda$ is the **thermal conductivity** of the material. For a given $\Delta T$, an animal can change the contact area (curling up, lying down), the thickness of the insulating layer (piloerection, fluffing of feathers) or the object in contact (lying on cool ground). Air has a very low thermal conductivity ($0.026\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$), so still air is a good insulator; water is about 23 times as conductive ($0.6\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-1}\,K^{-1}}$) and has a heat capacity per unit volume about 3500 times that of air, so heat is lost much faster in cold water than in air at the same temperature.
- Convection: heat is carried away by the macroscopic motion of a flowing fluid (air or water); in essence it is the combined effect of flow and conduction. Newton's law of cooling gives $\Phi = hA(T_{\mathrm{s}} - T_{\mathrm{f}})$, and the convective heat transfer coefficient $h$ depends on the properties and speed of the fluid: about $3$–$5\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}\,K^{-1}}$ in still air, tens in a wind, and hundreds in water. Fans and swimming both speed up convective heat loss; clothing traps a layer of unmoving air and reduces convective and conductive loss.
- Radiation: a body emits energy as electromagnetic waves (at body temperature mainly infrared with wavelengths of about $5$–$20\,\mathrm{\mu m}$) while absorbing the radiation of surrounding objects; it is the only mode of heat transfer that does not need a medium. By the **Stefan–Boltzmann law**, the net radiant heat flow is

  $$
  \Phi = A\varepsilon\sigma\left(T_{\mathrm{s}}^4 - T_{\mathrm{a}}^4\right),
  $$
  with $\sigma = 5.67\times10^{-8}\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}\,K^{-4}}$ and $T$ in K. Human skin is almost a black body in the infrared (emissivity $\varepsilon \approx 0.97$–$0.98$, independent of skin color, which affects only the absorption of visible light), and $A$ is the effective radiating area (about 75% of the body surface area when standing, less when curled up). For small temperature differences this can be linearized as $\Phi \approx h_{\mathrm{r}}A(T_{\mathrm{s}} - T_{\mathrm{a}})$ with $h_{\mathrm{r}} = 4\varepsilon\sigma\bar T^3 \approx 6\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}\,K^{-1}}$, comparable to the convective coefficient in still air.
- Evaporation: when water changes from liquid to vapor it absorbs the latent heat of vaporization from the body surface; the heat removed is $\Phi = \dot m L_{\mathrm{v}}$ with $L_{\mathrm{v}} \approx 2.43\,\mathrm{MJ\,kg^{-1}}$ (at $30\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$), so evaporating $1\,\mathrm{L\,h^{-1}}$ of sweat removes about $675\,\mathrm{W}$. Evaporation can only remove heat, never add it.

Even without sweating, water continually evaporates from the skin and the mucosa of the respiratory tract without forming visible droplets; this is **insensible water loss** ([7.14.21](/7.14.21)). It amounts to about $600$–$700\,\mathrm{mL}$ per day, removing about $20\,\mathrm{W}$, and is not under thermoregulatory control.

For a nude person sitting at rest at room temperature, about 60% of the heat is lost by radiation, about 15% by convection (and conduction to the air), about 3% by conduction to objects in contact, and about 22% by evaporation.

**Example 31.2.1** A nude person with a skin temperature of $33\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$ sits in a room whose walls are at $20\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$; the effective radiating area is $1.4\,\mathrm{m^2}$ and $\varepsilon = 0.98$. Find the radiative heat loss.

**Answer** $\Phi = 1.4\times0.98\times5.67\times10^{-8}\times(306.15^4 - 293.15^4) \approx 109\,\mathrm{W}$, which already exceeds the resting heat production (about $80\,\mathrm{W}$). A nude person sitting in a $20\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$ room therefore feels cold and needs to constrict skin vessels to lower the skin temperature, or to put on clothes. Even when the air of a winter room is heated to $22\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$, cold walls and windows still carry away a great deal of heat by radiation, which is why a seat next to the wall feels cold.

**Example 31.2.2 (Outdoors at $40\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$)** A person stands in the shade at $40\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$ with a skin temperature of $35\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$ and a body surface area of $1.8\,\mathrm{m^2}$; take $h_{\mathrm{c}} = 5$ and $h_{\mathrm{r}} = 6.2\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}\,K^{-1}}$, and a resting heat production of $100\,\mathrm{W}$. How does he lose heat, and how much sweat must evaporate?

**Answer** When the ambient temperature exceeds the skin temperature, radiation, convection and conduction all transfer heat from the environment to the body: $(5 + 6.2)\times1.8\times(40 - 35) \approx 100\,\mathrm{W}$. Adding the heat production, about $200\,\mathrm{W}$ must be lost, and the only route is evaporation: $200/(2.43\times10^6)\,\mathrm{kg\,s^{-1}} \approx 0.3\,\mathrm{L\,h^{-1}}$. In sunlight several hundred watts of solar radiation must be added; when the air is humid, sweat cannot evaporate effectively, and body temperature can no longer be maintained (Section 31.4.7).

The elephant has no sweat glands, and with its huge volume and small surface-to-volume ratio, losing heat is its main problem. Its two large pinnae make up about one-fifth of the body surface and are rich in blood vessels: when the weather is cool the elephant flaps its ears and increases blood flow to them, shedding a great deal of heat by convection and radiation; in the glare of the sun it holds the ears against the body and reduces the blood flow, avoiding the absorption of radiation. Elephants also wallow in mud and spray water on themselves, losing heat by evaporation and through the mud layer. Animals without sweat glands have other ways to evaporate water: dogs and many birds pant (birds also vibrate the throat rapidly, gular fluttering) to increase evaporation from the respiratory surfaces; rats and kangaroos spread saliva on the fur or forelimbs.

### 31.2.2. Surface-to-Volume Ratio and Insulation

Heat production is proportional to volume (mass) and heat loss to surface area, so the surface-to-volume ratio $A/V \propto 1/L$ determines how easy it is to stay warm: small animals lose heat fast and find it hard to keep warm, large animals the reverse. Animals can adjust the effective area for heat loss and the thermal resistance at several levels:

- Aggregation: small group-living mammals and penguins huddle together, reducing the exposed surface area ([7.14.30](/7.14.30#sec-30.6.2));
- Posture: curling into a ball, tucking in the head, limbs and tail;
- Insulation: fur, feathers, subcutaneous fat and blubber. The thermal resistance $R = L/(\lambda A)$ is proportional to thickness, and the insulating layer traps still air; piloerection ("goose bumps") and fluffing of feathers increase the thickness. Arctic animals have thick, well-insulating fur, whereas tropical animals have very thin fur (Section 31.6.3);
- Huge body size: very large ectotherms have a large thermal inertia and a small surface-to-volume ratio, which can keep body temperature stable and above the environment (gigantothermy, Section 31.6.1).

Insulation can also become a burden. The mane of the male lion is an example: it raises the surface temperature of the neck and head, and the darker and thicker the mane, the greater the warming. Males with dense, dark manes are in better nutritional condition and have higher testosterone levels, are preferred by lionesses, and are more readily given way to by other males; in hot seasons and regions the manes of male lions are shorter and paler. The mane has therefore been interpreted as an honest signal paid for with a heat burden.

## 31.3. Effects of Temperature on Physiological Processes

### 31.3.1. The Temperature Coefficient $Q_{10}$

Temperature affects every structure that depends on weak interactions (van der Waals forces, hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic interactions) and every process that must cross an energy barrier. The general measure of how a rate varies with temperature is the **temperature coefficient**:

**Formula 31.3.1 ($Q_{10}$)** Let the rates of a process at temperatures $T_1$ and $T_2$ be $K_1$ and $K_2$. Then

$$
Q_{10} = \left(\frac{K_2}{K_1}\right)^{10/(T_2 - T_1)},
\tag{31.2}
$$
that is, the factor by which the rate increases for each $10\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$ rise in temperature.

**Example 31.3.1** A reaction proceeds at $10$ units/min at $15\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$ and $20$ units/min at $25\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$. Find $Q_{10}$; if the temperature falls to $5\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$, what is the approximate rate?

**Answer** $Q_{10} = (20/10)^{10/10} = 2$. At $5\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$ the rate is about $10/2 = 5$ units/min. By the **Arrhenius equation**, $Q_{10} = \exp\!\big(10E_{\mathrm{a}}/(RT_1T_2)\big)$, and $Q_{10} = 2$ corresponds, over this temperature interval, to an **activation energy** of $E_{\mathrm{a}} = R\,T_1T_2\ln 2/10 = 8.314\times288.15\times298.15\times0.693/10 \approx 50\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$.

Different processes differ greatly in their sensitivity to temperature. Diffusion and ion migration in water have a $Q_{10}$ of only about $1.2$–$1.4$; most enzymatic reactions and the overall metabolic rate about $2$–$3$; heart rate and nerve conduction velocity about $2$–$3$. Processes that involve large conformational changes (gating of ion channels, protein denaturation) can be very high, and the $Q_{10}$ of temperature-sensitive TRP channels exceeds $10$ ([7.14.40](/7.14.40)), which is why they can serve as temperature sensors. In a system made of processes with different $Q_{10}$ values, a change in temperature uncouples the parts from one another: this, more than the slowing of any single reaction, is the root of the disturbance of physiological function when body temperature deviates.

### 31.3.2. Membrane Fluidity and Homeoviscous Adaptation

Temperature changes the physical state of membrane lipids. High temperature makes the membrane too fluid, lowering its mechanical strength, impairing its barrier function and increasing ion leakage; low temperature makes the membrane viscous or even gel-like, impeding conformational changes and lateral diffusion of proteins. Cells respond by changing the composition of the membrane:

**Definition 31.3.1 (Homeoviscous Adaptation)** The alteration by an organism of the composition of its membrane lipids at different growth or acclimation temperatures, so that the membrane keeps a similar fluidity (viscosity) at each temperature, is called **homeoviscous adaptation**.

The adjustments at low temperature include shorter fatty acid chains and greater unsaturation (double bonds bend the chain and loosen the packing), a change in the proportion of head groups (an increase of conical phosphatidylethanolamine), and an adjustment of the cholesterol content (cholesterol lowers fluidity at high temperature and prevents gelling at low temperature). The same rule is seen when species are compared: the fluidity of brain synaptic membranes of Antarctic fish, temperate fish and mammals, measured at their respective body temperatures, is similar, whereas compared at one and the same temperature it differs greatly.

### 31.3.3. Temperature Adaptation of Enzymes

Temperature can alter the kinetic parameters of an enzyme ($V_{\max}$, $K_{\mathrm{m}}$, and the $K_{\mathrm{a}}$ and $K_{\mathrm{i}}$ for allosteric activators and inhibitors) in several ways: changes in weak interactions alter the three-dimensional structure of the enzyme; temperature changes the ionization state of key amino acids at the active site; and temperature affects the ability of the enzyme to undergo the conformational changes needed for catalysis. Organisms respond at three levels:

1. Reducing demand: lowering the need for the product, for example, at low temperature muscle activity slows, the demand for ATP also falls, and a lower rate of ATP production can be tolerated;
2. Changing quantity: raising the expression of the enzyme in cold adaptation (mutations in the promoter region or altered regulation), compensating for the low activity of each enzyme molecule with numbers;
3. Changing quality: obtaining isozymes adapted to different temperatures by sequence change on an evolutionary time scale. The enzymes of cold-adapted species are usually more flexible in structure, with a higher $k_{\mathrm{cat}}$ and a lower thermal stability; moreover, $K_{\mathrm{m}}$ stays within a similar range at the physiological temperature of each species (for example, the $K_{\mathrm{m}}$ of lactate dehydrogenase for pyruvate in Antarctic and tropical fish), so that the enzyme has enough activity at the substrate concentrations in the body and still retains room for regulation.

Extreme temperatures cause proteins to denature and aggregate, and the cell's emergency response is the heat shock response: the induction of **heat shock proteins**, which help misfolded proteins to refold or be degraded.

## 31.4. Maintaining Body Temperature

### 31.4.1. Temperature Sensors and the Thermoregulatory Center

The thermoregulatory system has all the parts of a negative feedback loop ([7.14.1](/7.14.1#sec-1.6)): the controlled variable is the core temperature, the sensors are the central and peripheral temperature sensors, the integrating center is in the hypothalamus, and the effectors are skeletal muscle (shivering), brown fat, skin vessels, sweat glands and behavior.

- Peripheral temperature sensors: free nerve endings in the skin, divided into cold sensors and warm sensors, which fire at the highest frequency at skin temperatures of about $25$–$30\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$ and about $40$–$45\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$, respectively. There are about 10 times as many cold sensors as warm sensors, so the skin reports mainly cold to the center and triggers early the responses that prevent a fall in temperature. At the molecular level, cold is transduced by channels such as TRPM8 and heat by channels such as TRPV1 ([7.14.40](/7.14.40)). The spinal cord, the abdominal viscera and the great veins also contain temperature sensors.
- Central temperature sensors: the preoptic area–anterior hypothalamus (POA/AH) contains many warm-sensitive neurons (whose firing increases as temperature rises) and a few cold-sensitive neurons, and is very sensitive to small changes (a few tenths of a degree Celsius) in local temperature; heating this region directly causes sweating and skin vasodilation, and cooling it causes shivering.

The thermoregulatory center lies in the hypothalamus: as long as the hypothalamus and the neural structures below it are preserved, an animal can keep its body temperature constant, but after destruction of the hypothalamus it cannot. In the classical description the anterior hypothalamus (POA/AH) is responsible for heat loss and the posterior part for heat production; the POA is now regarded as the integrating center. It receives input from the peripheral and central temperature sensors, controls brown fat thermogenesis and cutaneous vasoconstriction through pathways via the dorsomedial hypothalamic nucleus and the raphe pallidus of the medulla, and drives the spinal motor neurons through the shivering center in the posterior hypothalamus. The output of the effectors can be summarized as follows:

| Body temperature above the set point | Body temperature below the set point |
|---|---|
| Skin vasodilation (inhibition of sympathetic vasoconstrictor tone) | Skin vasoconstriction (sympathetic excitation) |
| Sweating (sympathetic cholinergic fibers) | Piloerection (in humans only "goose bumps" remain) |
| Inhibition of heat production (less shivering and chemical thermogenesis) | Shivering and nonshivering thermogenesis, and in the long term more thyroid hormone |
| Behavior: undressing, seeking shade, reducing activity | Behavior: dressing, curling up, seeking a warm place, eating more |

The **feedback gain** of this system is high: for every $25$–$30\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$ change in ambient temperature, the core temperature of a person changes by only about $1\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$, a gain of about $-27$ to $-33$ (Example 1.6.1).

### 31.4.2. The Set Point

**Definition 31.4.1 (Set Point)** The reference value in the thermoregulatory center that determines the level at which body temperature is stabilized is called the **set point** of body temperature: when the core temperature is above it, the heat-loss responses are enhanced and heat production is reduced, and when it is below it, heat production increases and heat loss is reduced, so that body temperature stabilizes near it.

The human set point is about $37.1\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$: above it, when the hypothalamic temperature rises, sweating and skin vasodilation increase sharply; below it, shivering and chemical thermogenesis increase sharply. The set point is not a single number determined by the core temperature alone: skin temperature also takes part in the regulation. At a skin temperature above about $33\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$ the threshold for sweating is about $36.7\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$, and when the skin temperature falls to $29\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$ it rises to $37.4\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$; the threshold for shivering likewise rises as skin temperature falls. The effect is that as soon as the skin cools, the heat-producing responses begin, and sweating is inhibited, even before the core temperature has fallen, a kind of feedforward preadjustment. On the plane determined jointly by core and skin temperature there is an interthreshold zone of about $0.2$–$0.4\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$ between the sweating threshold and the shivering threshold, within which regulation depends only on the constriction and dilation of skin vessels.

The controller can be written as: drive of the effector $\propto \alpha T_{\mathrm{c}} + (1 - \alpha)T_{\mathrm{s}} - T_{\mathrm{set}}$, where the weight $\alpha$ of the core temperature is about $0.8$–$0.9$. The set point is a concept that describes the behavior of the system: the different effectors each have their own thresholds and gains and are driven by temperature signals relatively independently of one another, and body temperature stabilizes at the equilibrium point of their joint action; the "set point" can be understood as this equilibrium point, without assuming a separate structure in the hypothalamus that stores a reference value. The set point shifts physiologically (circadian rhythm, the rise after ovulation, a slight upward adjustment during exercise) or pathologically (fever, Section 31.5.1).

### 31.4.3. Heat Production: Shivering and Nonshivering

**Definition 31.4.2 (Shivering Thermogenesis)** The heat produced in the cold by involuntary, rhythmic contractions of skeletal muscle is called **shivering thermogenesis**.

Before shivering, muscle tone has already increased (cold-induced muscle tone) and the metabolic rate begins to rise; once shivering starts, heat production can increase to 4–5 times the resting level. Shivering obeys the **size principle**: the smallest motor neurons (innervating slow muscle fibers) are recruited first, and only later the larger neurons innervating fast fibers. The individual **motor units** contract asynchronously and antagonist muscles contract together, so the muscle as a whole makes hardly any large movement and does hardly any external work, and almost all the chemical energy becomes heat. Shivering consumes the fuel in muscle (glycogen and fat); it suits brief exposure to cold but not sustained cold stress.

**Definition 31.4.3 (Nonshivering Thermogenesis)** The heat produced by a rise in metabolic rate that does not depend on muscle contraction is called **nonshivering thermogenesis**, also chemical thermogenesis.

Nonshivering thermogenesis occurs mainly in **brown adipose tissue**: the sympathetic nerves release norepinephrine, which through the $\beta_3$ receptor and cAMP–PKA mobilizes fatty acids and activates **thermogenin** (UCP1) in the inner mitochondrial membrane, so that protons flow back without passing through ATP synthase and the energy of oxidation becomes heat directly ([7.1.23](/7.1.23#sec-23.5.1)). Brown fat is distributed between the scapulae, in the neck, in the axilla and around the great vessels; its cells contain many small lipid droplets and abundant mitochondria, and it is richly supplied with blood vessels and sympathetic nerves. Newborns have a considerable amount of brown fat, whose metabolism can more than double under maximal sympathetic stimulation; adults have little, but it can still be detected in the neck and supraclavicular region by PET–CT and increases after cold acclimation, and cold can also cause heat-producing "beige" adipocytes to appear in white fat. After several weeks of adaptation to cold, nonshivering thermogenesis in rodents can increase by 100%–500%, whereas in adults it generally does not exceed 10%–15%. Thyroid hormone is the most important humoral factor regulating heat production: through hypothalamic thyrotropin-releasing hormone (TRH), cold increases the secretion of thyroid hormone, which raises the metabolic rate within weeks, an action that is slow and lasting ([7.14.34](/7.14.34)); the actions of epinephrine and norepinephrine are rapid and brief.

### 31.4.4. Heat Loss: Sweating

Humans have two kinds of sweat glands. The eccrine glands are distributed all over the body (most densely on the palms and soles) and are innervated by sympathetic postganglionic fibers whose endings release acetylcholine (blockable by atropine); they are responsible for thermal sweating. The apocrine glands are confined to the axilla, the genital region and a few other places and open into hair follicles; they have little to do with thermoregulation. Besides thermal sweating there are emotional sweating (palms, soles and axillae, with its center in the cerebral cortex) and gustatory sweating (spicy foods cause sweating of the head and face).

The eccrine gland accomplishes two steps in two segments:

1. Secretion: the primary sweat produced by the secretory portion of the gland has a composition close to that of plasma, with $[\ce{Na+}] \approx 142\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$, $[\ce{Cl-}] \approx 104\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$ and low concentrations of other solutes;
2. Reabsorption: as the sweat flows through the duct, $\ce{Na+}$ is reabsorbed through the epithelial sodium channel and $\ce{Cl-}$ through CFTR, while the duct has a low permeability to water, so the sweat that emerges is hypotonic. At low sweating rates $\ce{Na+}$ and $\ce{Cl-}$ can fall below $5\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$, while solutes such as urea, lactate and $\ce{K+}$ are concentrated; at high sweating rates the duct has no time to reabsorb, and the $\ce{NaCl}$ concentration rises to $50$–$60\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$. In patients with cystic fibrosis the CFTR of the duct is defective and the $\ce{Cl-}$ of the sweat is high, which is the principle of the "sweat test."

Aldosterone promotes the reabsorption of $\ce{Na+}$ by the duct, adapting to the loss of salt during profuse sweating in the heat ([7.14.24](/7.14.24)). Acclimatization of humans to heat (1–6 weeks) raises the maximal sweat rate from about $1.5\,\mathrm{L\,h^{-1}}$ to $2$–$3\,\mathrm{L\,h^{-1}}$, which can dissipate more than 10 times the basal heat production; at the same time the salt content of the sweat falls, the threshold for sweating is lowered and sweating begins earlier, and the plasma volume increases. Sweat cools only by evaporating: sweat that is wiped off or runs down does nothing, so in a humid, windless environment profuse sweating may still fail to remove enough heat; profuse sweating with replacement of water only can cause hyponatremia, and sweating without replacing water causes hypertonic dehydration.

### 31.4.5. Skin Blood Flow

Skin blood flow ranges from nearly zero to about 30% of the cardiac output and is the main means of regulating the thermal resistance "from core to skin" ([7.14.16](/7.14.16#sec-16.6.4)). The arteriolar network of the subpapillary dermis connects through tortuous capillaries to a rich venous plexus, which is the store of warm blood; the skin of the hands, feet, ears, nose and lips also contains many arteriovenous anastomoses, which are under tonic control by sympathetic vasoconstrictor fibers and, when open, let a large volume of warm blood flow directly into the venous plexus. From full constriction to full dilation, the rate at which heat is delivered to the skin can increase about 8-fold. Local temperature also acts directly on the vessels: local heating to about $43\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$ causes marked vasodilation and cooling below about $26\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$ causes constriction (although in extreme cold, intermittent cold-induced vasodilation occurs in the fingers and elsewhere, preventing frostbite).

The deep veins of the limbs accompany the arteries and surround them, forming a countercurrent heat exchanger (Section 31.6.5): in the cold, the heat that arterial blood carries toward the extremity is carried back to the core by returning venous blood, the extremity cools and heat loss is reduced; in the heat, blood returns instead through the superficial veins and bypasses this exchanger.

### 31.4.6. Behavioral Thermoregulation

**Definition 31.4.4 (Behavioral Thermoregulation)** The regulation of heat production and heat loss by an animal through changes in behavior (choosing a microenvironment, changing posture and exposed area, adding or removing clothing, or building a nest) is called **behavioral thermoregulation**.

For humans, clothing, shelter and air conditioning are the most effective means of thermoregulation: the thermal resistance of a set of winter clothes can cut the heat loss of a nude person by more than half, far beyond any physiological regulation. Human physiological regulation (autonomic thermoregulation) can be regarded as a fine supplement to behavioral regulation. For ectotherms, behavior is almost the only mode of regulation (Section 31.6.4).

### 31.4.7. A Heat-Balance Model of Thermoregulation

Combining Eq. The model reproduces the relationship between human body temperature and ambient temperature: in dry air a nude person can keep the core temperature close to normal at ambient temperatures of about $11$–$54\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$ (measured range about $13$–$54\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$); at the two ends of the regulatory range, negative feedback fails, the $Q_{10}$ effect of metabolism becomes **positive feedback**, and body temperature deviates rapidly.

## 31.5. Disturbances of Thermoregulation

### 31.5.1. Fever

**Definition 31.5.1 (Fever)** A regulated rise in body temperature caused by an upward shift of the set point is called **fever**. It differs from hyperthermia caused by an environmental heat load that exceeds the capacity for heat loss: in fever the regulatory system is intact and only the reference value has changed.

**Definition 31.5.2 (Pyrogen)** A substance that shifts the set point upward and causes fever is called a **pyrogen**: exogenous pyrogens such as bacterial lipopolysaccharide stimulate immune cells to release endogenous pyrogens (cytokines such as interleukin-1, interleukin-6 and tumor necrosis factor α).

The cytokines act on sites that lack a blood–brain barrier, such as the organum vasculosum of the lamina terminalis, and on the vascular endothelium of the hypothalamus, inducing cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) and the synthesis of prostaglandin $\mathrm{E_2}$ ($\mathrm{PGE_2}$); $\mathrm{PGE_2}$ acts on the EP3 receptor of preoptic neurons and shifts the set point upward (here the **inflammation** response of [7.14.11](/7.14.11) is linked to thermoregulation). Antipyretics such as aspirin and ibuprofen inhibit cyclooxygenase and return the set point to normal, but they do not lower the body temperature of a normal person.

The course of a fever can be divided into three stages:

1. Rising phase (chill phase): the set point suddenly rises, and the body temperature is now below the new set point, so the body responds as if in the cold: skin vessels constrict, the skin is pale and cold, hair stands on end, shivering occurs, and the person feels cold and wants to put on more clothes or cover up, until body temperature reaches the new set point.
2. Plateau phase: body temperature is regulated at the level of the new set point, with neither shivering nor sweating.
3. Falling phase (defervescence): after the pyrogen has been cleared or an antipyretic given, the set point returns to normal; body temperature is now above the set point, skin vessels dilate, the skin is flushed, sweating is profuse and body temperature falls rapidly.

![Time course of a fever in the set-point model (controller as in the heat-balance model; room temperature 28 °C, subject lying in bed lightly covered, minimal conductance 7 W/K). At t = 1 h the set point rises from 37.0 °C to 39.5 °C within 20 min, and returns to 37 °C at t = 7 h. A: set point (dashed) and core temperature (solid): the core temperature catches up with the new set point in about 1 h. Fever itself may be an adaptation: the growth of many pathogens is inhibited at higher temperatures, and the proliferation and phagocytic activity of lymphocytes are enhanced at higher temperatures. Ectotherms also "develop fever": desert iguanas infected with bacteria deliberately choose warmer locations and raise their body temperature by about $2\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$, and individuals prevented from doing so have a higher mortality (Section 31.6.4). But an excessively high body temperature ($> 41\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$) is harmful in itself, and children may also develop febrile seizures.

### 31.5.2. Heatstroke and Hypothermia

**Definition 31.5.3 (Heatstroke)** The condition in which an environmental heat load exceeds the capacity for heat loss and the core temperature rises above about $40.5\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$, accompanied by dysfunction of the central nervous system (confusion, delirium, coma), is called **heatstroke**.

In dry air humans can tolerate very high air temperatures (above $54\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$ for short periods); in air near 100% humidity, however, evaporation almost stops above an air temperature of about $34\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$ and body temperature begins to rise, and during heavy labor the limit is lower. Once the core temperature is too high, the regulatory capacity of the hypothalamus declines and sweating may stop, while metabolism accelerates with rising temperature (by about 10% per $1\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$), forming positive feedback, and the core temperature can climb to $41$–$42\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$. High temperature damages cells directly, causing injury to brain, liver and kidney, coagulopathy and rhabdomyolysis, and often death after a few days. First aid for heatstroke is rapid cooling (immersion in cold water, or spraying with cold water and blowing air). The milder heat exhaustion is mainly due to the volume depletion caused by sweating and to peripheral vasodilation, and presents with weakness, dizziness and fainting; the core temperature is not necessarily very high.

**Definition 31.5.4 (Hypothermia)** A core temperature below $35\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$ is called **hypothermia**.

When the core temperature falls below about $34\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$ the regulatory capacity of the hypothalamus begins to weaken, and at about $29$–$30\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$ shivering stops and regulation is essentially lost; since heat production falls by more than half for every $10\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$ drop, a further fall in body temperature is again positive feedback. Below about $28\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$ ventricular fibrillation is likely, and drowsiness and even coma appear. Heat loss in cold water is extremely rapid, and a person who falls into water close to $0\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$ may lose consciousness within an hour. On the other hand, low temperature also reduces tissue metabolism and oxygen consumption ($Q_{10}$), and medicine uses controlled hypothermia for cardiac surgery or to protect the brain after cardiac arrest. Local freezing of the skin produces frostbite, in which ice crystals in the tissue and the subsequent disturbance of blood flow cause necrosis.

## 31.6. Evolution and Comparison

### 31.6.1. Classification of Thermal Strategies

Faced with changes in ambient temperature, an animal can spend energy to regulate its body temperature, or it can tolerate changes in body temperature; each strategy has its costs and benefits ([7.14.1](/7.14.1#sec-1.7)). There are two independent dimensions for describing a thermal strategy: where the heat comes from, and whether body temperature is stable.

**Definition 31.6.1 (Endotherms and Ectotherms)** An animal whose body temperature is determined mainly by its own metabolic heat production is called an **endotherm**; an animal whose body temperature is determined mainly by environmental heat (solar radiation, heat exchange with the environment) is called an **ectotherm**.

**Definition 31.6.2 (Homeotherms and Poikilotherms)** An animal whose body temperature is kept relatively constant, essentially independent of the ambient temperature, is called a **homeotherm**; an animal whose body temperature varies with the ambient temperature is called a **poikilotherm**.

**Definition 31.6.3 (Heterothermy)** A thermal strategy in which body temperature differs greatly in time (at different times of the day or season) or in space (in different parts of the body) is called **heterothermy**.

| | Homeothermic | Poikilothermic |
|---|---|---|
| Endothermic | Most mammals and birds | Temporal heterothermy: daily-torpor hummingbirds and small rodents, hibernating ground squirrels; spatial heterothermy: regional endothermy of tuna, mackerel sharks and billfish, moths and bumblebees warming up before flight |
| Ectothermic | Animals living in extremely stable thermal environments: deep-sea fish, fish in Antarctic seawater (about $-1.9\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$) | Most fish, amphibians, reptiles and invertebrates, whose body temperature follows a changing environment |

The two dimensions cannot replace each other: the same desert lizard is an ectotherm but keeps its body temperature quite stable during the day through behavioral regulation; a hibernating ground squirrel is an endotherm but its body temperature in winter is close to that of the environment. The terms "cold-blooded" and "warm-blooded" are therefore not accurate. The strategy of many animals contains several components; for example, the body temperature of monotremes (platypus, echidnas) is about $30$–$32\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$, lower than that of most mammals, and their range of regulation is narrower; the naked mole-rat lives in stable underground burrows, and the extent to which its body temperature follows the environment is rare among mammals. Nor is the constancy of body temperature in homeotherms absolute: in space the core temperature is constant while the surface temperature varies widely, and in time there are circadian rhythms, the menstrual cycle and fever.

Very large ectotherms can also keep body temperature above the environment and relatively stable by virtue of thermal inertia and a small surface-to-volume ratio, which is called *gigantothermy*: in cold water at about $8\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$ the core temperature of a leatherback turtle can be more than ten degrees Celsius above the water temperature, and the turtle relies at the same time on thick subcutaneous fat and countercurrent heat exchange in the flippers.

### 31.6.2. The Evolution of Endothermy

Endothermy has arisen independently many times in animal evolution: mammals (through the cynodonts among the synapsids) and birds (through theropod dinosaurs) each evolved whole-body endothermy; tuna, lamnid sharks, billfish and the opah evolved regional or whole-body endothermy; the flight muscles of many insects can warm up before flight; and some plants (the inflorescences of the Araceae) can also produce heat.

Indirect evidence from fossils: early mammal-like animals such as the cynodonts already had a secondary palate separating the oral and nasal cavities, which allowed the animal to breathe continuously while eating and suggests a need for a sustained high metabolic rate; the later-appearing nasal turbinates (respiratory turbinates) humidify the air on inhalation and recover water and heat on exhalation, are found among living animals only in endotherms, and are regarded as a sign of high ventilation. Early birds and many theropod dinosaurs had feathers with weak asymmetry that could not be used for flight, suggesting that feathers may first have served for insulation (and display), which shows that these animals already needed to maintain body temperature.

Why pay a 5–10-fold metabolic cost ([7.14.30](/7.14.30#sec-30.3.3))? The main hypotheses are the aerobic capacity hypothesis (selection acted first on the capacity for sustained activity, and a high resting metabolic rate is a by-product of a high maximal aerobic metabolic rate), the parental care hypothesis (a stable and higher body temperature speeds embryonic development and benefits the rearing of young), and the niche expansion hypothesis (independence of ambient temperature allows activity at night and in cold regions). These hypotheses are not mutually exclusive, and there is as yet no consensus.

### 31.6.3. The Thermoneutral Zone and the Scholander Curve

**Definition 31.6.4 (Thermoneutral Zone)** The range of ambient temperature in which the metabolic rate of an endotherm stays at the basal level and body temperature can be maintained only by adjusting thermal conductance (vasomotion, piloerection, posture) is called the **thermoneutral zone**; its lower limit is called the **lower critical temperature** and its upper limit the **upper critical temperature**.

When the ambient temperature is below the lower critical temperature, the animal must increase heat production. In the steady state heat production equals heat loss, $M = C(T_{\mathrm{b}} - T_{\mathrm{a}})$, where $C$ is the thermal conductance already adjusted by the animal to its minimum, so the metabolic rate increases linearly as the ambient temperature falls, and the straight line extrapolates to $M = 0$ exactly at $T_{\mathrm{a}} = T_{\mathrm{b}}$. Above the upper critical temperature the animal needs active evaporative cooling (panting, sweating), which itself consumes energy, and the metabolic rate rises again.

Scholander and colleagues (1950) compared mammals and birds of the Alaskan Arctic with those of tropical Panama. Tropical animals have lower critical temperatures of $20$–$30\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$, and must increase heat production when the ambient temperature is only slightly lower; at $0\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$ their metabolic rate is already several times the basal rate. The lower critical temperature of the Arctic fox is below $-40\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$, and at $-70\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$ its metabolic rate has increased by less than half. The basal metabolic rates of Arctic and tropical animals, scaled to body weight, differ little; the difference lies in insulation: thick fur makes $C$ much smaller, the slope of the line is gentle, and the lower critical temperature is low. From $C = \mathrm{BMR}/(T_{\mathrm{b}} - \mathrm{LCT})$, with basal metabolic rate $\propto M^{3/4}$ (Kleiber's law) and thermal conductance roughly $\propto M^{1/2}$, we get $T_{\mathrm{b}} - \mathrm{LCT} \propto M^{1/4}$: larger animals have lower critical temperatures.

### 31.6.4. Behavioral Thermoregulation in Ectotherms

Ectotherms do not have enough heat-producing capacity, but through behavior they can keep body temperature within a narrow range during most of the day. A lizard basks in the morning sun (with its body axis perpendicular to the sun's rays and its back darkened to absorb more radiation); once body temperature has risen into the preferred range it shuttles between sun and shade, and at noon it retreats into a burrow or raises its body off the scorching ground. Those that rely on solar radiation are called heliotherms, and those that rely on contact with warm surfaces (such as a snake lying on a warm rock at night) are called thigmotherms. Physiology also takes part in behavioral thermoregulation: the marine iguanas of the Galápagos have a higher heart rate and dilated skin vessels while warming, and a lower heart rate and constricted skin vessels while cooling, so that heat is gained quickly and lost slowly, and after foraging in cold water they can rewarm faster.

The locomotion and physiological performance of ectotherms vary with body temperature and can be described by a thermal performance curve: between the critical minimum and critical maximum temperatures, performance rises with temperature, peaks near the optimum temperature and then falls rapidly. The optimum temperature is usually close to the preferred body temperature selected by the animal in the wild. Ectotherms also show behavioral fever: after infection they select warmer locations (Section 31.5.1), which shows that fever is an ancient defensive response that existed before endothermy appeared.

### 31.6.5. Regional Endothermy and Countercurrent Heat Exchange

**Definition 31.6.5 (Countercurrent Heat Exchange)** The exchange of heat between two adjacent fluid streams flowing in opposite directions (such as arterial and venous blood), so that heat circulates between the two streams instead of leaving a region with the fluid, is called **countercurrent heat exchange**. A vascular exchanger made of many parallel small arteries and veins interwoven is called a *rete mirabile*.

For two streams of equal flow, the size of the exchanger is expressed by $\mathrm{NTU} = UA/(\dot m c)$ ($U$ is the heat transfer coefficient and $A$ the exchange area). In countercurrent flow, the fraction of the maximum possible heat that is exchanged (the effectiveness) is $\varepsilon = \mathrm{NTU}/(1 + \mathrm{NTU})$, which can approach 1 without limit; in concurrent (parallel) flow $\varepsilon = (1 - \mathrm{e}^{-2\,\mathrm{NTU}})/2$, which never exceeds $1/2$. The advantage of countercurrent flow is that a small, constant temperature difference is maintained along the whole exchanger, the same principle as gas exchange in fish gills and countercurrent multiplication in the kidney ([7.14.24](/7.14.24)). If the tissue inside the exchanger produces heat at a rate $Q$, the tissue is warmer than the water by $\Delta T = Q/\big(\dot m c(1 - \varepsilon)\big)$, that is, $1/(1 - \varepsilon)$ times the value without an exchanger.

Countercurrent heat exchange recurs throughout the animal kingdom:

- Tuna and mackerel sharks: in most fish, blood exchanges heat with the water in the gills, and body temperature equals water temperature. The red muscle of tuna (the aerobic swimming muscle) lies deep in the body near the spine and is supplied through a *rete mirabile* from arteries under the skin of the flanks, so the heat produced by the red muscle is kept inside the body: the red muscle of the bluefin tuna can be $10$–$20\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$ warmer than the water, enabling it to swim fast continuously in cold water and to migrate over wide areas. The lamnid sharks (porbeagle, great white) have independently evolved similar structures.
- The heater organs of billfish and swordfish: the superior rectus muscle of the eye is specialized into heat-producing tissue that has lost its ability to contract and is rich in mitochondria and sarcoplasmic reticulum; it produces heat by the energy-consuming cycling of $\ce{Ca^2+}$ between the sarcoplasmic reticulum and the cytosol, warming the eyes and brain and preserving their temporal resolution of vision in the cold water of the deep sea. The opah produces heat with its pectoral muscles and has countercurrent exchange in the gills; it is the only fish known to be endothermic throughout the body.
- The limbs of birds and mammals: in the legs of wading birds, the feet of Arctic foxes and the flippers of whales and seals, the arteries are surrounded by a network of veins, so the temperature of the extremity can approach that of the environment (the foot stands on ice without freezing) while very little heat is lost. The pampiniform venous plexus in the spermatic cord conversely cools the testis.
- Insects: the flight muscles of moths and bumblebees need about $30$–$35\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$ to produce enough power for takeoff; before takeoff they contract the antagonistic flight muscles simultaneously (a "warm-up" without beating the wings), and the thorax warms rapidly; the pattern of hemolymph flow between thorax and abdomen determines whether heat is retained in the thorax or shed in the abdomen. The **asynchronous flight muscles** of bumblebees also take part in heat production. A bee colony regulates temperature as a whole: honeybees keep the brood area at $34$–$36\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$, clustering and producing heat with the flight muscles when it is cold, and fanning and evaporating water droplets when it is hot; Asian honeybees surround an invading hornet in a "bee ball," raising the temperature at the center of the ball to about $45\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$, which the hornet cannot tolerate but the bees still can.

### 31.6.6. Daily Torpor and Hibernation

**Definition 31.6.6 (Torpor and Hibernation)** The active, controlled lowering of metabolic rate and body temperature by an endotherm is called **torpor**; torpor that lasts less than a day and recurs daily is called daily torpor, and multiday torpor that lasts from several days to several weeks and recurs within a season is called **hibernation**.

Torpor is not a failure of regulation: body temperature is regulated at a new, lower set point (below a critical value the animal increases heat production or wakes up), and both entry and exit are controlled. Hummingbirds and many small birds, bats and rodents enter daily torpor at night or when food is scarce; body temperature falls to $10$–$20\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$ and the metabolic rate can fall to a few percent of that in the awake resting state, and the energy saved is enough to make up the deficit from being unable to feed at night ([7.14.30](/7.14.30#sec-30.6.4)); the common poorwill of North America is the only bird known to hibernate.

The body temperature of hibernating ground squirrels falls close to the ambient temperature (about $0$–$5\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$, about $2\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$ above the environment), and the body temperature of Arctic ground squirrels is even below $0\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$ (about $-3\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$) in a state of **supercooling** without freezing; the metabolic rate is only 1%–5% of the basal metabolic rate, and the heart rate falls from several hundred to a few beats per minute. During hibernation they arouse spontaneously every one to three weeks, relying on **brown adipose tissue** and shivering, and body temperature returns to $37\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$ within a few hours and is maintained for less than a day before they go back to sleep; these periodic arousals occupy only a small fraction of the winter but consume most of the winter's energy, and their function (clearing metabolic products, restoring immune and neural function, sleep) is still unclear.

How much of the fall in metabolic rate is simply a $Q_{10}$ effect? If $Q_{10} = 2.5$ and body temperature falls from $37$ to $5\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$, the metabolic rate falls as $2.5^{-3.2} \approx 5\%$, close to what is observed in small hibernators, so their low metabolism can be explained to a large extent by low temperature; but the fall in metabolic rate on entering torpor precedes the fall in body temperature, which shows that there is also active metabolic suppression. Bears are the other extreme: in hibernation the body temperature of a black bear falls only to about $30$–$35\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$, yet the metabolic rate falls to about 25% of the basal level, far more than $Q_{10}$ can explain, so this is almost entirely active metabolic suppression. Small animals have a large surface-to-volume ratio, the cost of maintaining body temperature in the cold is high, and the benefit of deep torpor is also large; large animals cool and rewarm very slowly and are not suited to frequent deep torpor.

### 31.6.7. Freeze Avoidance and Freeze Tolerance

In environments below freezing, ectotherms must either avoid freezing of their body fluids or tolerate it.

- Avoiding freezing: Antarctic seawater is at about $-1.9\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$, whereas the blood of an ordinary marine teleost freezes at about $-0.7\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$. Antarctic fish (suborder Notothenioidei) contain large amounts of antifreeze glycoproteins in the blood (a class of **antifreeze proteins**), which adsorb to specific faces of tiny ice crystals and prevent crystal growth, lowering the freezing point below that of seawater while leaving the melting point essentially unchanged (thermal hysteresis). Arctic cod have independently evolved antifreeze glycoproteins of similar sequence, a molecular example of convergent evolution. Insects often supercool deeply with low-molecular-weight antifreezes such as glycerol and by removing ice nucleators.
- Tolerating freezing: in winter the North American wood frog can have about two-thirds of its body water frozen, with heartbeat and breathing stopped, and recovers on thawing in spring. When freezing begins, liver glycogen is rapidly broken down to glucose, and the blood glucose rises to hundreds of times normal and enters the cells, acting as an antifreeze so that ice forms only outside the cells and the water inside the cells is partly drawn out and does not freeze.

### 31.6.8. Bergmann's Rule and Allen's Rule

**Definition 31.6.7 (Bergmann's Rule and Allen's Rule)** Among endotherms of the same group, the tendency of species or populations living in colder regions to be larger in body size is called **Bergmann's rule**; the tendency of species or populations living in colder regions to have shorter appendages such as ears, tails and limbs is called **Allen's rule**.

Both rules can be explained by the surface-to-volume ratio: a large body and short appendages reduce the surface area for heat loss per unit mass. The Arctic fox has short, rounded ears, the red fox intermediate ones, and the fennec fox of North Africa enormous pinnae, a classic example of Allen's rule. These two rules are, however, statistical tendencies with many exceptions, and the explanation is not unique: by the estimate of Section 31.6.3, the effect of body size on the lower critical temperature ($\propto M^{1/4}$) is much smaller than that of a change in insulation, so the contribution of larger size to keeping warm is limited; a large body also brings larger fat reserves and a greater resistance to starvation, which is equally important for seasonal food shortage in cold regions. The opposite trend (smaller individuals in cold regions) is common in ectotherms, because small individuals warm up faster.

## Summary

- Body temperature: the core temperature (Definition 31.1.1) is constant at about $37\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$ $\pm0.6\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$, whereas the surface temperature varies widely; there are circadian (about $0.5$–$0.7\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$) and menstrual-cycle (a rise of $0.3$–$0.5\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$ after ovulation) variations, among others. Heat balance equation (Formula 31.1.1): $S = M - W - (R + C + K) - E_{\mathrm{v}}$.
- Heat exchange: conduction $\Phi = \lambda A\Delta T/L$, convection $\Phi = hA\Delta T$, radiation $\Phi = A\varepsilon\sigma(T_{\mathrm{s}}^4 - T_{\mathrm{a}}^4)$ (about 60% at rest), evaporation $\Phi = \dot mL_{\mathrm{v}}$ ($1\,\mathrm{L\,h^{-1}} \approx 675\,\mathrm{W}$); insensible water loss about $600$–$700\,\mathrm{mL\,d^{-1}}$; when the ambient temperature exceeds the skin temperature only evaporation can remove heat. Surface-to-volume ratio, aggregation, posture and insulation determine how well heat is retained.
- Temperature effects: $Q_{10}$ (Formula 31.3.1; $Q_{10} = 2$ corresponds to about $E_{\mathrm{a}} = 50\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$); homeoviscous adaptation (Definition 31.3.1); temperature adaptation of enzymes: reduced demand, changed quantity, changed quality ($K_{\mathrm{m}}$ conserved at the physiological temperature).
- Regulation: temperature sensors (cold sensors of the skin outnumber warm sensors 10 to 1, warm-sensitive neurons of the hypothalamic POA) and the hypothalamic thermoregulatory center; set point (Definition 31.4.1) about $37.1\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$, modulated by skin temperature; gain about $-30$. Heat production: shivering (Definition 31.4.2; up to 4–5-fold, recruited by the size principle) and nonshivering thermogenesis (Definition 31.4.3; brown fat and UCP1, thyroid hormone). Heat loss: sweat is formed from primary sweat ($\ce{Na+}$ 142, $\ce{Cl-}$ 104 mmol/L) by reabsorption in the duct into a hypotonic fluid, up to $2$–$3\,\mathrm{L\,h^{-1}}$ after acclimatization; skin blood flow and arteriovenous anastomoses change the conductance about 8-fold; behavioral thermoregulation (Definition 31.4.4). The heat-balance model reproduces the plateau of about $11$–$54\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$.
- Disturbances: fever (Definition 31.5.1) is an upward shift of the set point by a pyrogen (Definition 31.5.2) through $\mathrm{PGE_2}$, with a rising phase, a plateau phase and a falling phase; heatstroke (Definition 31.5.3) and hypothermia (Definition 31.5.4) are both positive feedback driven by $Q_{10}$ after the failure of negative feedback.
- Comparison: endotherm/ectotherm (Definition 31.6.1) and homeotherm/poikilotherm (Definition 31.6.2) are two independent dimensions, and heterothermy (Definition 31.6.3) combines both; endothermy arose independently many times; the thermoneutral zone (Definition 31.6.4) and the Scholander curve: insulation determines the lower critical temperature; behavioral thermoregulation and behavioral fever in ectotherms; countercurrent heat exchange (Definition 31.6.5, $\varepsilon = \mathrm{NTU}/(1 + \mathrm{NTU})$) and the regional endothermy of tuna, mackerel sharks, billfish and insects; $Q_{10}$ effect and active metabolic suppression in torpor and hibernation (Definition 31.6.6); antifreeze proteins and the freeze tolerance of the wood frog; Bergmann's and Allen's rules (Definition 31.6.7).

## Questions

1. A person is outdoors at $40\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$. What are his routes of heat loss? What happens if the air humidity reaches 100%?

   **Answer** When the ambient temperature is above the skin temperature (about $35\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$), radiation, convection and conduction all add heat to the body, and the only route of heat loss is evaporation of sweat (Example 31.2.2: about $0.3\,\mathrm{L\,h^{-1}}$ in the shade; much more in sunlight or during labor). At 100% humidity the water vapor pressure at the skin surface differs little from that of the air, evaporation almost stops, and sweat only runs off without removing heat. Heat produced and heat gained from the environment accumulate in the body; by the estimate of Example 31.1.1 body temperature rises by $1$–$2\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$ per hour or more, and heatstroke occurs within a few hours, so hot and humid conditions are much more dangerous than hot and dry ones.

2. Bare hands in winter first turn red, then white, and in prolonged extreme cold turn red again intermittently. Explain this course by the regulation of skin blood flow.

   **Answer** Cold first causes sympathetic constriction of skin vessels and closure of the arteriovenous anastomoses; the hands turn white and cold and heat loss is reduced, which protects the core temperature. If the temperature of the hands falls very low (below about $10\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$), the vascular smooth muscle is paralyzed by the cold and the release of sympathetic transmitter is inhibited, so the vessels dilate periodically (cold-induced vasodilation); warm blood rushes in, the hands turn red, and the tissue is rewarmed and spared from frostbite, after which the vessels constrict again, and so on. On coming from the cold into a warm room, the vessels dilate and local metabolites accumulate, and the hands become flushed and swollen.

3. A shrew (about 5 g) and a reindeer (about 100 kg): which has the lower lower critical temperature? Estimate the ratio of their $T_{\mathrm{b}} - \mathrm{LCT}$ from the allometric relationships of Section 31.6.3, and explain why real Arctic animals can do better still.

   **Answer** By $T_{\mathrm{b}} - \mathrm{LCT} \propto M^{1/4}$, the ratio is $(100/0.005)^{1/4} = (2\times10^4)^{1/4} \approx 12$: the $T_{\mathrm{b}} - \mathrm{LCT}$ of the shrew is only a few degrees Celsius (a lower critical temperature of above $30\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$), whereas that of the reindeer can reach several tens of degrees Celsius. On top of this, Arctic animals have evolved extremely thick fur that thickens seasonally (hollow hairs) and countercurrent heat exchange in the limbs, so that the thermal conductance is far smaller than the allometric prediction, and the lower critical temperatures of both the Arctic fox and the reindeer are below $-30\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$. Small Arctic mammals (lemmings) rely mainly on burrows and nests under the snow and live in a microenvironment much warmer than the air.

4. The heart rate of an ectotherm is 30 beats per minute at $20\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$ and 66 beats per minute at $30\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$. Find $Q_{10}$ and the corresponding apparent activation energy; what is its heart rate at $12\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$?

   **Answer** $Q_{10} = 66/30 = 2.2$. $E_{\mathrm{a}} = R\,T_1T_2\ln Q_{10}/10 = 8.314\times293.15\times303.15\times\ln 2.2/10 \approx 58\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$. The heart rate at $12\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$ is $\approx 30\times2.2^{-0.8} = 30/1.88 \approx 16$ beats/min.

5. Why does a patient in the rising phase of fever still shiver while wrapped in blankets, and sweat profusely during defervescence? If an ice pack is used to cool a feverish patient instead of an antipyretic, how will the thermoregulatory system respond?

   **Answer** In the rising phase the set point has already risen and the actual body temperature is below it; the thermoregulatory center treats this state as "cold": skin vessels constrict, shivering occurs and behavior seeks warmth, until body temperature reaches the new set point (in the figure, shivering reaches its maximum). During defervescence the set point returns to normal and the actual body temperature is above it, so the heat-loss mechanisms are fully activated: sweating and skin vasodilation. When cooling by ice pack alone without changing the set point, as soon as body temperature falls below the set point the center resists with shivering and vasoconstriction, increasing energy expenditure and discomfort, and the cooling effect is also limited; an antipyretic is therefore usually given first to lower the set point, with physical cooling as an adjunct. For heatstroke the reverse holds: the set point is normal but heat loss is insufficient, so physical cooling is the first measure and antipyretics are ineffective.

6. The red muscle of tuna can be more than ten degrees Celsius warmer than the water, whereas that of most fish cannot. Explain from the standpoint of heat exchange why the main difficulty in retaining heat in fish lies in the gills. If the *rete mirabile* of a tuna has $\mathrm{NTU} = 10$, by what factor does the temperature rise of the red muscle increase compared with having no exchanger?

   **Answer** The entire cardiac output of a fish flows through the gills, which have an enormous area and a very thin barrier for gas exchange; heat diffuses much faster than $\ce{O2}$, so the blood reaches almost complete thermal equilibrium with the water in the gills, any metabolic heat is lost there, and body temperature equals water temperature. To retain heat, it must be intercepted between the gills and the heat-producing tissue, which is exactly the role of the *rete mirabile*: it lies between the heat-producing red muscle and the cold blood from the gills, so the heat need not pass through the gills. At $\mathrm{NTU} = 10$, $\varepsilon = 10/11$ and the temperature rise is $1/(1 - \varepsilon) = 11$ times that without an exchanger. The *rete mirabile* could equally let $\ce{O2}$ diffuse directly from artery to vein and bypass the muscle, but the thermal diffusivity in tissue (about $1.4\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$) is several tens of times the diffusion coefficient of $\ce{O2}$, so the size of the exchanger is enough to retain heat while the shunting of $\ce{O2}$ is small; it can therefore retain heat selectively.

7. (Open question) A small rodent of 30 g has three choices on a winter night: keep its body temperature at $37\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$, enter daily torpor so that body temperature falls to $15\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$, or huddle with companions in the nest. Design an estimate to compare the energy cost of the three strategies; what parameters are needed?

   **Answer** Approach: use the Scholander–Irving model $M = \max\big(\mathrm{BMR}, C(T_{\mathrm{b}} - T_{\mathrm{a}})\big)$ to estimate the energy expenditure over the night (for example 12 h). The parameters needed are: the basal metabolic rate (about $0.25\,\mathrm{W}$ from Kleiber's law) and the thermal conductance $C$ (from the allometric relation, about $1.0\times30^{-0.5}\times30\,\mathrm{mL\,O_2\,h^{-1}\,{}^\circ C^{-1}}$, or about $0.03\,\mathrm{W/{}^\circ C}$); the ambient temperature at night (for example $0\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$); the metabolic rate in torpor (a $Q_{10}$ effect plus active suppression, about 10%–20% of BMR, regulated with the ambient temperature to keep body temperature from falling below $15\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$); the cost of entering and arousing (the heat needed for rewarming $mc\Delta T$, $0.03\times3500\times22 \approx 2.3\,\mathrm{kJ}$, plus the high metabolism during rewarming); and the reduction of $C$ by huddling (a nest and a group can more than halve the effective conductance). Example: keeping $37\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$ gives $M = 0.03\times37 \approx 1.1\,\mathrm{W}$, about $48\,\mathrm{kJ}$ over 12 h; huddling that halves the conductance gives about $24\,\mathrm{kJ}$; torpor for 10 h (about $0.05\,\mathrm{W}$, $1.8\,\mathrm{kJ}$) plus the cost of entering and about 2 h of rewarming (about $8\,\mathrm{kJ}$) gives about $10\,\mathrm{kJ}$ in all. The conclusion depends on the parameters, but torpor is usually the most economical when it is cold and food is short; the cost is that the animal cannot forage or defend itself during torpor, so when food or companions are plentiful the animal tends to huddle rather than enter torpor.
