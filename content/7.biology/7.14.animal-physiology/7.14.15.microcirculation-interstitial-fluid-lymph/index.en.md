The whole purpose of the circulation is fulfilled in the microcirculation: the heart and the large vessels merely deliver blood to this region, and the exchange of material between blood and tissue takes place in capillaries only a few micrometers in diameter whose wall is a single layer of endothelial cells. The pressure, resistance and velocity discussed in the previous chapter ([7.14.14](/7.14.14)) set the conditions of exchange here: a capillary pressure of about $17\,\mathrm{mmHg}$, a flow velocity of about $0.3\,\mathrm{mm\,s^{-1}}$ and a transit time of $1$–$3\,\mathrm{s}$. This chapter addresses four questions: the structure of the microcirculation and of the interstitium; how substances are exchanged by diffusion, filtration and transcytosis, with oxygen supply analyzed quantitatively by the Krogh cylinder model; how fluid and protein that are not reabsorbed return to the blood through the lymphatic system; and how a tissue regulates the blood flow it receives according to its own needs. The physics of diffusion is in [7.6.5](/7.6.5) and the viscoelastic models are in [7.6.22](/7.6.22#sec-22.7.1); the neural and humoral control of the circulation as a whole is the subject of the next chapter ([7.14.16](/7.14.16)).

## 15.1. Structure of the Microcirculation

### 15.1.1. Definition and Types

**Definition 15.1.1 (Microcirculation)** In a closed circulatory system, the circulation of blood between arterioles and venules is called the **microcirculation**. It is the interface between arteries and veins, the site where blood, interstitial fluid and cells exchange material, and the truly functional part of the circulatory system.

Functionally, the microcirculation falls into two classes. Nutritive microcirculation supports the metabolism of local tissue cells, as in the great majority of organs. Non-nutritive microcirculation serves special functions for the whole body: glomerular capillaries filter plasma ([7.14.22](/7.14.22)), subcutaneous arteriovenous anastomoses and venous plexuses dissipate heat ([7.14.31](/7.14.31)), and alveolar capillaries exchange gases ([7.14.18](/7.14.18)). The blood flow of these beds far exceeds local metabolic needs: per 100 g of tissue, renal blood flow is about 100 times that of resting skeletal muscle.

### 15.1.2. Components and Pathways

A typical microcirculatory unit consists of the following parts:

- Arterioles (diameter about $10$–$15\,\mu\mathrm{m}$): they have a regular, continuous layer of smooth muscle and act as the master gate of the microcirculation, controlled by the **sympathetic nervous system** and local factors.
- Metarterioles: their smooth muscle is discontinuous, present as scattered rings.
- Precapillary sphincters: a ring of smooth muscle at the origin of each true capillary, serving as a branch gate controlled mainly by local metabolites.
- True capillaries (diameter about $4$–$9\,\mu\mathrm{m}$): they have no smooth muscle, only endothelium, basement membrane and scattered pericytes; erythrocytes (diameter about $7\,\mu\mathrm{m}$, thickness about $2\,\mu\mathrm{m}$) must deform to pass.
- Thoroughfare channels: direct continuations of metarterioles, wider than true capillaries, leading straight to venules.
- Arteriovenous anastomoses: thick-walled shunts between arterioles and venules, innervated by sympathetic nerves.
- Venules: they collect blood from the capillaries and are the postcapillary resistance vessels.

Blood therefore flows from arteriole to venule by three pathways: the circuitous pathway (through the true capillary network; slow flow and a long path, the main site of exchange); the thoroughfare pathway (through thoroughfare channels, returning to the veins rapidly; common in skeletal muscle, where it ensures a certain **venous return**); and the arteriovenous shunt (through arteriovenous anastomoses; no exchange occurs, and the shunt serves mainly thermoregulation in the skin).

True capillaries are not all open at once: metarterioles and precapillary sphincters contract and relax alternately several times a minute (vasomotion), and the classical estimate is that at any moment only about 20%–25% of the capillaries in resting skeletal muscle carry flow (intravital microscopy suggests that a larger fraction may carry red cells, and recruitment also takes the form of increased and more uniform red-cell flux). When metabolism rises, more capillaries open (recruitment), enlarging the exchange area and shortening diffusion distances (Section 15.2.2). An adult has more than $10^{10}$ capillaries, with a total exchange area estimated at $500$–$1000\,\mathrm{m^2}$, several times that of a tennis court; any functioning cell is usually no more than $20$–$30\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ from the nearest capillary.

### 15.1.3. Types of Capillary Wall

The capillary wall consists of a single layer of endothelial cells (about $0.5\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ thick) and an outer basement membrane. The cleft between adjacent endothelial cells is about $6$–$7\,\mathrm{nm}$ wide and is partly sealed by **tight junctions**; the endothelial cells also contain many caveolae and vesicles, which form a transcellular route. By the continuity of the endothelium, capillaries fall into three types:

| Type | Structure | Distribution | Permeability |
|---|---|---|---|
| Continuous | Intact endothelium and continuous basement membrane; in the brain the tight junctions are especially dense | Muscle, skin, lung, connective tissue; brain (**blood–brain barrier**) | Small molecules pass through the intercellular clefts; macromolecules rarely |
| Fenestrated | Endothelium perforated by fenestrae $50$–$80\,\mathrm{nm}$ in diameter, often covered by a diaphragm | Glomeruli, gastrointestinal mucosa, endocrine glands, choroid plexus | High permeability to water and small molecules |
| Discontinuous (sinusoidal) | Large gaps between endothelial cells; basement membrane discontinuous or absent | Liver, spleen, bone marrow | Plasma proteins and even blood cells can pass |

The large pores of hepatic sinusoids let hepatocytes contact plasma proteins directly and secrete them ([7.14.29](/7.14.29)); the tight junctions of the brain prevent most water-soluble substances in the blood from entering the brain freely ([7.14.7](/7.14.7#def-7.2.1)); and the glomerular fenestrae, together with a specialized basement membrane and podocytes, form the filtration barrier ([7.14.22](/7.14.22)). Both the width of the intercellular clefts and the continuity of the tight junctions can be regulated: inflammatory mediators (histamine, bradykinin) cause endothelial cells to contract, widening the clefts and increasing permeability ([7.14.11](/7.14.11)).

### 15.1.4. The Interstitium

**Definition 15.1.2 (Interstitium)** The space between cells is called the **interstitium**. The fluid in it is the **interstitial fluid**, which makes up about one sixth of body weight, is derived from plasma by capillary filtration, and is the environment in which cells live directly.

The interstitium is not a water-filled cavity. Its solid network is made of collagen fiber bundles and proteoglycan filaments (extremely fine, coiled molecules of hyaluronan and protein). Most of the interstitial fluid is bound between the proteoglycan filaments and forms a gel; freely flowing fluid is usually less than 1%, present as small rivulets and vesicles, and increases greatly only in edema (Section 15.3.5). The gel has the following character: small molecules diffuse through it at rates barely affected (only a few percent slower than in free fluid), but it does not permit long-range directed fluid flow, so movement of substances through tissue relies mainly on diffusion.

Physically, the interstitium is a dynamic, multiphase, coupled material: the solid phase is the extracellular matrix network of collagen and proteoglycan, and the liquid phase is the interstitial fluid. It is **viscoelastic**: when a fixed deformation is imposed on the tissue, the mobile fluid inside gradually redistributes and bonds between macromolecules break and reform, so the stress decays roughly exponentially with time. Such behavior can be described by combinations of springs and dashpots ([7.6.22](/7.6.22#sec-22.7.1)): the Maxwell model, with spring and dashpot in series, describes stress relaxation ($\sigma = \sigma_0\mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau}$ at fixed strain), and the Voigt model, with the two in parallel, describes creep (strain increasing as $1 - \mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau}$ at fixed stress); the real behavior of tissue lies between the two, with a broad distribution of relaxation times. For example, the pit left by pressing a finger on an edematous leg takes tens of seconds to recover (pitting edema), which reflects the slow flow of fluid through the gel; the same viscoelasticity also protects cells when the tissue is loaded and provides a mechanical environment for cell migration and mechanical signaling.

Chemically, the interstitium is highly hydrated and strongly polar: the sulfate and carboxyl groups of glycosaminoglycans give it a high density of negative charge, which attracts cations such as $\ce{Na+}$ and $\ce{Ca^2+}$ and maintains the gel structure through electrostatic interaction and hydrogen bonds; its ions and weakly acidic and basic groups provide some pH buffering; and it is a medium for chemical signals, since growth factors, cytokines and the like can diffuse through it or be bound, stored and released by the matrix, thereby regulating cell behavior. Transport of macromolecules through it relies on slow, directed fluid flow driven by pressure, osmosis and cell activity, which finally drains into the initial lymphatics (Section 15.3).

## 15.2. Exchange of Substances

### 15.2.1. Diffusion

Diffusion is the main way small molecules are exchanged between blood and interstitial fluid, and the driving force is the concentration gradient. Lipid-soluble substances ($\ce{O2}$, $\ce{CO2}$, anesthetic gases) can cross the membranes of endothelial cells directly, so the entire capillary wall is their pathway, exchange is extremely fast, and it is limited mainly by blood flow. Water-soluble substances ($\ce{Na+}$, $\ce{Cl-}$, glucose, urea) can pass only through the intercellular clefts or fenestrae, which occupy only about 0.1% of the capillary surface area, yet diffusion of small molecules through them is still rapid: the diffusional flux of water molecules through the capillary wall is about 80 times the flow of plasma through the capillary.

The diffusional flux per unit area can be written in the form of **Fick's law** (compare the permeability coefficient of Chapter 3, [7.14.3](/7.14.3#def-3.2.1)):

$$
J_X = P_X\,(C_{\mathrm{c}} - C_{\mathrm{i}}), \qquad P_X = \frac{D_X}{a}, \qquad \text{total flux} = P_X\,S\,\Delta C, \tag{15.1}
$$

where $D_X$ is the effective diffusion coefficient of substance $X$ in the pathway (including the fraction of area occupied by pores and the hindrance the molecule experiences in the pore), $a$ is the thickness of the barrier, $S$ is the exchange area, and $\Delta C$ is the concentration difference across the vessel wall. Three groups of factors therefore determine exchange: the structure of the vessel (the thickness and selectivity of the barrier; the brain and the glomerulus are the two extremes); the working state (the number of open vessels and the surface area, especially high in exercising muscle and in the lung); and the concentration difference (maintained by blood flow and by tissue consumption).

The permeability of water-soluble molecules declines in steps with molecular size, reflecting sieving by the pore structure. Taking water as 1, the relative permeabilities of muscle capillaries to several molecules are approximately:

| Substance | Molecular weight | Relative permeability |
|---|---|---|
| Water | 18 | 1 |
| NaCl | 58 | 0.96 |
| Urea | 60 | 0.8 |
| Glucose | 180 | 0.6 |
| Sucrose | 342 | 0.4 |
| Inulin | about 5000 | 0.2 |
| Myoglobin | 17600 | 0.03 |
| Hemoglobin | 68000 | 0.01 |
| Albumin | 69000 | 0.001 |

Besides size, charge also matters: the **glycocalyx** on the endothelial surface is negatively charged, so the flux of negatively charged proteins (such as albumin) is much smaller than that of neutral macromolecules of the same size, and positively charged macromolecules have the highest apparent permeability. The charge selectivity of the glomerular filtration barrier rests on the same principle ([7.14.22](/7.14.22)).

Macromolecules can also cross the endothelium by **transcytosis**: they are taken up into caveolae or vesicles on one side and released on the other. This process does not obey the law of diffusion and has some specificity (for example albumin through the gp60 receptor, insulin and transferrin through their own receptors); it is important in the transport of macromolecules across continuous capillaries, and is strictly limited in the blood–brain barrier.

### 15.2.2. The Krogh Cylinder Model

Can $\ce{O2}$ be delivered from the capillary to every cell in the tissue? Krogh (1919) gave the first quantitative answer, one of the earliest mathematical models in physiology.

**Definition 15.2.1 (Krogh Cylinder Model)** Regard the tissue as composed of many identical cylindrical units: each unit has on its axis a capillary of radius $r_{\mathrm{c}}$, surrounded by a tissue cylinder of radius $R$; the tissue consumes $\ce{O2}$ at a uniform rate $M$ (volume of $\ce{O2}$ consumed per unit volume of tissue per unit time), $\ce{O2}$ diffuses only radially, and there is no exchange between adjacent units. This is the **Krogh cylinder model**.

The key parameters are: the free $\ce{O2}$ concentration at the capillary inlet (expressed as $P_{\ce{O2}}$) and the total $\ce{O2}$ content (free plus hemoglobin-bound); the capillary radius $r_{\mathrm{c}}$ and blood flow $F$; the diffusion coefficient $D$ and solubility $\alpha$ of $\ce{O2}$ in tissue; the radius $R$ of the tissue cylinder (determined by capillary density: for $n$ capillaries per square millimeter, $R = 1/\sqrt{\pi n}$) and the oxygen consumption rate $M$; and the distance $r$ from a point in the tissue to the capillary.

In the steady state, the tissue $P_{\ce{O2}}$ satisfies a radial diffusion equation with a consumption term (the cylindrical form of the steady-state diffusion equation of [2.5](/2.5)):

$$
K\,\frac{1}{r}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}r}\left(r\frac{\mathrm{d}P}{\mathrm{d}r}\right) = M, \qquad P(r_{\mathrm{c}}) = P_{\mathrm{c}}, \qquad \left.\frac{\mathrm{d}P}{\mathrm{d}r}\right|_{r = R} = 0, \tag{15.2}
$$

where $K = D\alpha$ is called the Krogh diffusion constant, and the second boundary condition expresses that no $\ce{O2}$ crosses the outer boundary of the unit (symmetry). Integrating twice gives

$$
P(r) = P_{\mathrm{c}} + \frac{M}{4K}\left(r^2 - r_{\mathrm{c}}^2\right) - \frac{MR^2}{2K}\ln\frac{r}{r_{\mathrm{c}}}. \tag{15.3}
$$

$P$ decreases monotonically outward from the capillary wall and is lowest at the outer boundary $r = R$ of the unit. The difference between the minimum and $P_{\mathrm{c}}$ is proportional to $M R^2/K$: doubling oxygen consumption doubles the pressure drop, and doubling the capillary spacing increases the drop about fourfold.

Along the capillary, $\ce{O2}$ is continuously removed from the blood, and $P_{\mathrm{c}}$ falls from about $95\,\mathrm{mmHg}$ at the arterial end along the **oxygen dissociation curve** ([7.1.7](/7.1.7)) to its venous-end value. The place with the lowest $P_{\ce{O2}}$ in the whole tissue is therefore the corner at the "venous end, farthest from the capillary", the so-called "lethal corner".

![The Krogh cylinder model (Eq. (15.3); Krogh diffusion constant K = Dα, D = 2000 μm²/s, α = 3×10⁻⁵ mL O₂/(mL·mmHg), capillary radius 3 μm). The model shows two means of supplying oxygen to tissue during exercise: increasing blood flow (raising the mean value of $P_{\mathrm{c}}$ along the capillary; metabolic regulation, Section 15.4), and recruiting capillaries (decreasing $R$). The effect of the latter scales as $R^2$ and is especially important in skeletal muscle; long-term training increases capillary density through angiogenesis (Section 15.4.6). The capillary density in the leg muscles of endurance athletes is about 50% higher than in ordinary people. Cardiac muscle has a capillary density of about $3000$–$4000$ per mm² ($R \approx 9$–$10\,\mu\mathrm{m}$), with a capillary next to almost every myocardial fiber, so even though cardiac muscle consumes a great deal of oxygen at rest (about 20 times resting skeletal muscle), tissue $P_{\ce{O2}}$ is maintained.

The Krogh model involves several simplifications: oxygen consumption in tissue is not uniform; myoglobin provides facilitated diffusion, making $\ce{O2}$ transport in muscle faster than by diffusion alone ([7.14.19](/7.14.19)); the blood flow of adjacent capillaries is often in opposite directions, so the "lethal corners" compensate each other; and the capillary wall and plasma themselves offer some resistance. Nevertheless, it captures the basic geometry of tissue oxygen supply and can be extended to glucose, $\ce{CO2}$ and any other substance supplied by diffusion, adding other processes (facilitated diffusion, convection) where necessary.

### 15.2.3. Oxygen Extraction and the Fick Principle

The $\ce{O2}$ taken up by an organ from the blood equals the difference between inflow and outflow; this is conservation of mass and is called the Fick principle in physiology.

**Definition 15.2.2 (Fick Principle and Oxygen Extraction Ratio)** Let the blood flow of an organ be $F$ and the $\ce{O2}$ contents of arterial and venous blood be $C_{\mathrm{a}}$ and $C_{\mathrm{v}}$. The oxygen consumption rate of the organ

$$
\dot V_{\ce{O2}} = F\,(C_{\mathrm{a}} - C_{\mathrm{v}}), \tag{15.4}
$$
is called the **Fick principle**; the arteriovenous content difference as a fraction of the arterial content

$$
E_{\ce{O2}} = \frac{C_{\mathrm{a}} - C_{\mathrm{v}}}{C_{\mathrm{a}}} = \frac{\dot V_{\ce{O2}}}{F\,C_{\mathrm{a}}} \tag{15.5}
$$
is called the **oxygen extraction ratio**.

Content is usually expressed in mL $\ce{O2}$ per 100 mL blood (about $20\,\mathrm{mL\,dL^{-1}}$ in arterial blood). For the whole body at rest, $\dot V_{\ce{O2}} \approx 250\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}}$, **cardiac output** is $5\,\mathrm{L\,min^{-1}}$, the arteriovenous difference is about $5\,\mathrm{mL\,dL^{-1}}$, and the extraction ratio is about 25%: about three quarters of the $\ce{O2}$ remains in venous blood, which is the body's oxygen reserve. Organs differ greatly: the kidney's blood flow far exceeds its metabolic needs, and its extraction ratio is below 10%; the extraction ratio of the myocardium is already 70%–80% at rest, so when the myocardium needs more $\ce{O2}$ it can do so almost only by increasing coronary blood flow ([7.14.16](/7.14.16)); in strenuously exercising skeletal muscle the extraction ratio can reach 80%–90%.

Equation (15.5) answers a question: what happens if both the oxygen consumption rate $\dot V_{\ce{O2}}$ and the blood flow $F$ are large? The extraction ratio depends only on their ratio. In exercise $\dot V_{\ce{O2}}$ increases faster than $F$, the extraction ratio rises, venous $P_{\ce{O2}}$ falls, and the "lethal corner" of the tissue becomes dangerous (panel C); what the Krogh model provides is how far the extraction ratio can still rise at a given $F$ without hypoxia. The same approach applies to $\ce{CO2}$ output and glucose uptake, and it is the classical method of measuring organ blood flow: Fick used it to measure cardiac output (measuring whole-body oxygen consumption and the difference in oxygen content between pulmonary arterial and arterial blood), and **renal clearance** in kidney physiology is one of its applications ([7.14.22](/7.14.22)).

**Example 15.2.1 (Oxygen supply to the leg during exercise)** At rest, the blood flow of the muscles of one leg is about $0.3\,\mathrm{L\,min^{-1}}$, the extraction ratio 25% and the arterial $\ce{O2}$ content $20\,\mathrm{mL\,dL^{-1}}$, so the oxygen consumption rate is $3\,\mathrm{dL\,min^{-1}} \times 20 \times 0.25 = 15\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}}$. In strenuous exercise blood flow rises to $6\,\mathrm{L\,min^{-1}}$ (20-fold) and the extraction ratio to 85%, giving $60 \times 20 \times 0.85 \approx 1000\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}}$, about 70 times the resting value. Of this 70-fold increase, about 20-fold comes from blood flow and about 3.4-fold from the extraction ratio; the latter depends on capillary recruitment and myoglobin, and on the rightward shift of the oxygen dissociation curve caused by the increased acidity and temperature in exercising muscle (the **Bohr effect**).

### 15.2.4. Filtration and Reabsorption: Starling Forces

Besides diffusion, fluid also crosses the capillary wall by bulk flow, carrying the dissolved substances with it (convection). Compared with diffusion, the amount of this fluid is small, but it determines the distribution of volume between plasma and interstitial fluid.

**Definition 15.2.3 (Starling Forces and Net Filtration Pressure)** The four pressures that drive fluid across the capillary wall are the capillary hydrostatic pressure $P_{\mathrm{c}}$, the interstitial hydrostatic pressure $P_{\mathrm{i}}$, the plasma colloid osmotic pressure $\pi_{\mathrm{c}}$ and the interstitial colloid osmotic pressure $\pi_{\mathrm{i}}$, together called the **Starling forces**. Taking outward as positive,

$$
\mathrm{NFP} = (P_{\mathrm{c}} - P_{\mathrm{i}}) - \sigma(\pi_{\mathrm{c}} - \pi_{\mathrm{i}}), \qquad J_{\mathrm{v}} = K_{\mathrm{f}}\cdot\mathrm{NFP}, \tag{15.6}
$$
$\mathrm{NFP}$ is called the **net filtration pressure** and $J_{\mathrm{v}}$ is the transmural fluid flow. Here $\sigma$ is the reflection coefficient of the protein (close to 1 for albumin), and the proportionality coefficient $K_{\mathrm{f}}$ is called the **capillary filtration coefficient**. It characterizes the permeability of the capillary wall to water and is determined jointly by the number (area) of open capillaries in the tissue and the size and number of endothelial clefts.

The **colloid osmotic pressure** arises from the plasma proteins that cannot pass freely through the capillary wall, of which about 80% comes from albumin ([7.14.11](/7.14.11#def-11.1.2)); small molecules and ions pass freely and exert no osmotic effect on fluid movement across the capillary. With the classical values of Guyton (in mmHg):

| | Arterial end | Venous end | Mean over all capillaries |
|---|---|---|---|
| Capillary pressure $P_{\mathrm{c}}$ | 30 | 10 | 17.3 |
| Interstitial hydrostatic pressure $P_{\mathrm{i}}$ | $-3$ | $-3$ | $-3$ |
| Interstitial colloid osmotic pressure $\pi_{\mathrm{i}}$ | 8 | 8 | 8 |
| Sum of outward forces $P_{\mathrm{c}} - P_{\mathrm{i}} + \pi_{\mathrm{i}}$ | 41 | 21 | 28.3 |
| Plasma colloid osmotic pressure $\pi_{\mathrm{c}}$ (inward) | 28 | 28 | 28 |
| Net filtration pressure | $+13$ | $-7$ | $+0.3$ |

The interstitial hydrostatic pressure in loose tissue is slightly below atmospheric (about $-3\,\mathrm{mmHg}$), a result of the continuous suction of the lymphatic system (Section 15.3). In this classical picture, fluid is filtered at the arterial end of the capillary and reabsorbed at the venous end; about 90% of the filtrate returns to the blood at the venous end, and the remaining 10% or so is recovered by the lymph. The whole-body mean net filtration pressure is only about $0.3\,\mathrm{mmHg}$ and the whole-body $K_{\mathrm{f}}$ is about $6.67\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}\,mmHg^{-1}}$, so the net filtration is about $2\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}}$, or about $3\,\mathrm{L}$ per day, finally returned to the blood by the lymphatic system. The $K_{\mathrm{f}}$ of glomerular capillaries is much higher and the filtration pressure is positive throughout, so they only filter and never reabsorb ([7.14.22](/7.14.22)).

### 15.2.5. The Glycocalyx and the Revised Starling Principle

The classical "filtration at the arterial end, reabsorption at the venous end" was challenged by experiments in the late twentieth century: measured directly in most tissues, sustained reabsorption at the venous end is rare, and the main route by which interstitial fluid returns to the blood is the lymph. The revised Starling principle of Levick and Michel (2010) explains this.

The luminal surface of endothelial cells is covered by a layer of **glycocalyx** (the endothelial surface layer), about $0.5\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ thick and made of proteoglycans and glycoproteins; it is the true protein molecular sieve. The colloid osmotic pressure difference that governs fluid flow is not that between plasma and the interstitial fluid as a whole, but that between plasma and the thin layer of fluid beneath the glycocalyx at the exit of the intercellular cleft. During filtration, the continuous outflow of fluid dilutes the protein in this layer, so that its colloid osmotic pressure is much lower than the bulk $\pi_{\mathrm{i}}$; the inward osmotic pressure difference is therefore larger than the classical value, and the filtration is smaller than classically calculated. Once pressure falls to the point where reabsorption could occur, fluid flows backward, protein in the interstitial fluid quickly diffuses into this layer, and the osmotic pressure difference disappears, so reabsorption can occur only transiently. In the steady state, therefore, most capillaries filter at a low level (or near zero) along their whole length, with no sustained reabsorption, and the filtrate is returned almost entirely by the lymph.

The revised principle still uses Eq. (15.6), but with $\pi_{\mathrm{i}}$ replaced by the value beneath the glycocalyx. It has two important corollaries. One is the "autotransfusion" after blood loss: when capillary pressure falls suddenly, there is indeed a brief reabsorption that replenishes plasma volume ([7.14.16](/7.14.16)), but it is transient. The other concerns the effect of infusions: when the glycocalyx is damaged (sepsis, surgical trauma, hyperglycemia) the filtration barrier fails, and infused colloid solutions also leak out. The same balance of forces determines the conditions for **pulmonary edema** in the alveoli: pulmonary capillary pressure is only about $7\,\mathrm{mmHg}$ and the interstitial pressure is more negative, so the alveoli normally remain dry ([7.14.18](/7.14.18)).

## 15.3. The Lymphatic System

### 15.3.1. Structure and Functions

**Definition 15.3.1 (Lymph and the Lymphatic System)** Interstitial fluid that has entered the initial lymphatics is called **lymph**; the system of vessels that returns interstitial fluid to the veins, composed of initial lymphatics, collecting lymphatics, lymph nodes, lymph trunks and lymph ducts, is called the **lymphatic system**.

Almost all tissues have initial lymphatics, except a few sites (epidermis, the parenchyma of the central nervous system, bone). Lymph from the lower body, the left side of the head and neck, the left upper limb and the left chest drains into the thoracic duct, which empties into the veins at the junction of the left internal jugular and subclavian veins; lymph from the right side of the head and neck, the right upper limb and the right chest enters the veins at the right venous angle through the right lymphatic duct. Lymph nodes contain lymphocytes and macrophages, filtering pathogens and particles out of the lymph; their immunological functions belong to [7.15](/7.15).

The physiological functions of the lymphatic system are:

- Returning interstitial fluid: the classical estimate is that about 10% of the filtered fluid returns via the lymph (the revised Starling principle holds that it is more); human lymph flow is about $2$–$4\,\mathrm{L}$ per day, about $100$–$120\,\mathrm{mL\,h^{-1}}$ at rest, of which about $100\,\mathrm{mL\,h^{-1}}$ passes through the thoracic duct.
- Returning protein: a small amount of plasma protein continuously leaks from the capillaries, each day about a quarter to a half of the total plasma protein; it cannot be reabsorbed into the capillaries and can return to the blood only through the lymph. If lymph return stopped completely, the protein concentration in interstitial fluid would keep rising, the colloid osmotic pressure difference would vanish, plasma would be continuously lost into the tissues, and a person would die within 24 hours.
- Transporting fat: fat absorbed in the small intestine enters the lacteals as **chylomicrons** and is carried by the lymph; after a meal the thoracic duct lymph is milky (chyle) and contains 1%–2% fat ([7.14.28](/7.14.28)).
- Immune defense: carrying antigens and immune cells from the tissues to the lymph nodes.

### 15.3.2. Initial Lymphatics and the Lymph Pump

Initial lymphatics begin as blind ends in the interstitium; their wall is a single layer of endothelial cells without a continuous basement membrane. The edges of adjacent endothelial cells overlap like a row of flap valves opening into the lumen: when the interstitial pressure exceeds that inside the vessel, the overlapping edges are pushed apart and fluid, protein and even cells and particles can enter; when the pressure inside is higher, the edges are pressed together and prevent backflow. Outside, the endothelial cells are connected to the surrounding connective tissue by anchoring filaments: when interstitial fluid increases and the tissue swells, the filaments pull on the endothelial cells, keeping the initial lymphatic open and dilated and promoting fluid entry. This design makes the lymphatic entrance far more permeable to fluid and macromolecules than a blood capillary.

From the initial lymphatics, lymph enters collecting lymphatics, which have valves. The segment between two valves (a lymphangion) has smooth muscle in its wall: when distended by lymph, the smooth muscle is stretched and contracts, pushing the lymph past the next valve; the next segment is then distended and contracts, and so on, advancing the lymph segment by segment. This is called the lymph pump. External compression also propels lymph: the contraction of skeletal muscle, body movement, pulsation of adjacent arteries and external compression of the tissue all push lymph centrally in valved vessels. In exercise, lymph flow can increase 10–30-fold. The lymphatic pump is quite powerful: when the thoracic duct is occluded, the pressure in it can rise to $50$–$100\,\mathrm{mmHg}$.

### 15.3.3. Interstitial Pressure, Tissue Compliance and Lymph Flow

Lymph flow is determined mainly by the interstitial hydrostatic pressure. When interstitial pressure rises from about $-6\,\mathrm{mmHg}$ to about $0\,\mathrm{mmHg}$ (normal is about $-3\,\mathrm{mmHg}$), lymph flow increases about 20-fold; with a further rise, fluid accumulated in the interstitium compresses the larger lymph vessels and flow no longer increases (panel B). Any factor that raises interstitial pressure increases lymph flow: raised capillary pressure, lowered plasma colloid osmotic pressure, raised interstitial colloid osmotic pressure, and increased capillary permeability.

The mechanical properties of the tissue itself cooperate with this. At negative interstitial pressure, the compliance of the gel is very low: a small increase in volume raises the pressure markedly, which immediately increases lymph flow and carries the excess fluid away. Once interstitial pressure exceeds zero, a large amount of free fluid appears in the gel, the compliance suddenly becomes large, volume can increase by tens of percent with almost no change in pressure, and edema appears. The lymphatic system thus regulates the interstitial fluid in three ways: maintaining its protein concentration, maintaining its volume and pressure, and establishing its negative pressure, which holds the components of the tissue tightly together and maintains the integrity of tissue structure.

Viewed as a whole, effective transport of material in the circulation relies on a combination of directed flow and diffusion: small molecules are exchanged between blood and tissue mainly by diffusion, and proteins and lipids are distributed and recovered mainly by directed flow (the blood circulation and lymph return).

### 15.3.4. The Glymphatic System

There are no lymphatic vessels in brain parenchyma, and how metabolic waste is cleared was long a mystery. Nedergaard and colleagues (2012) proposed the glymphatic system: cerebrospinal fluid enters the brain parenchyma along the perivascular spaces around arteries, exchanges with the interstitial fluid through the water channel aquaporin AQP4 on astrocytic endfeet, and flows out along the perivascular spaces around veins, carrying away soluble metabolites (including β-amyloid). Its driving force is thought to come from arterial pulsation and respiration. In mice the extracellular space of the brain expands by about 60% during sleep and clearance is markedly increased, which offers a hypothesis for one function of sleep. In 2015 true lymphatic vessels (meningeal lymphatics) were found in the dura mater, draining macromolecules and immune cells from the cerebrospinal fluid to the cervical lymph nodes. The existence of the glymphatic system and the role of convection in it (as opposed to diffusion) remain debated and need more direct measurements.

### 15.3.5. Edema

**Definition 15.3.2 (Edema)** Excessive accumulation of interstitial fluid in the interstitium is called **edema**.

From Eq. (15.6), edema has four classes of cause:

| Cause | Examples |
|---|---|
| Raised capillary pressure | Heart failure (raised venous pressure); venous obstruction; salt and water retention in renal failure; prolonged standing ([7.14.14](/7.14.14#sec-14.6.3)) |
| Lowered plasma colloid osmotic pressure | Cirrhosis (reduced albumin synthesis); nephrotic syndrome (protein loss in urine); malnutrition (ascites in kwashiorkor) |
| Increased capillary permeability | Inflammation and allergy (histamine, bradykinin); burns; destruction of the glycocalyx in sepsis |
| Obstructed lymph return | Lymphedema after malignant tumors or surgical removal of lymph nodes; filariasis (elephantiasis) |

Tissue has a safety margin before edema appears: capillary pressure can rise by about $17\,\mathrm{mmHg}$ without causing obvious edema. By Guyton's estimate there are three safety factors: the low compliance of tissue at negative interstitial pressure (about $3\,\mathrm{mmHg}$); the increase in lymph flow (about $7\,\mathrm{mmHg}$); and the "washout" of protein from the interstitial fluid by the increased lymph flow, which lowers $\pi_{\mathrm{i}}$ (about $7\,\mathrm{mmHg}$).

## 15.4. Regulation of Local Blood Flow

### 15.4.1. Tissue Demand Determines Local Blood Flow

Blood is a limited resource and cardiac output is limited, so each tissue receives just the blood flow that meets its needs: matching supply to demand minimizes the load on the heart. Demand is determined by tissue metabolism: the supply of $\ce{O2}$, glucose, amino acids and fat, the removal of $\ce{CO2}$ and $\ce{H+}$, the maintenance of ions, and the delivery of hormones. The blood flow of the organs at rest is approximately as follows (adult, cardiac output about $5\,\mathrm{L\,min^{-1}}$; data of Guyton):

| Organ | Blood flow / $\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}}$ | Fraction of cardiac output | Per 100 g tissue / $\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}}$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Brain | 700 | 14% | 50 |
| Heart (coronary) | 200 | 4% | 70 |
| Liver and gastrointestinal tract (portal vein and hepatic artery) | 1350 | 27% | 95 |
| Kidney | 1100 | 22% | 360 |
| Skeletal muscle (resting) | 750 | 15% | 4 |
| Skin (warm environment) | 300 | 6% | 3 |
| Thyroid, adrenal gland, etc. | 75 | 1.5% | 160–300 |
| Bronchi, bone and others | about 525 | about 10% | |

The blood flow per unit mass of the thyroid and adrenal glands is very high because their metabolic rate is high and they must deliver hormones into the blood rapidly; the blood flow of the kidney and liver mainly serves their function of processing the blood. Regulation of local blood flow is of two kinds: acute regulation changes blood flow within seconds to minutes by contraction and relaxation of small arteries, metarterioles and precapillary sphincters, with no change in vascular structure; long-term regulation changes the number and size of vessels over days, weeks or months (Section 15.4.6).

### 15.4.2. Acute Regulation: Metabolic Mechanisms

When tissue metabolic rate rises or $\ce{O2}$ supply falls, local blood flow increases. Tissue blood flow also increases when the oxygen saturation of arterial blood decreases (high altitude, pneumonia, CO poisoning, anemia). There are two theories of the mechanism, and in fact both act together.

The vasodilator theory: when metabolism is enhanced or oxygen is lacking, the tissue releases vasodilator substances that diffuse to nearby arterioles, metarterioles and precapillary sphincters and relax them. The main candidates are adenosine, $\ce{CO2}$, $\ce{H+}$ (lactate), $\ce{K+}$, histamine, phosphate and others. Adenosine is a breakdown product of ATP and is especially prominent in cardiac muscle: in myocardial hypoxia, ATP is broken down to AMP and adenosine, and adenosine released into the extracellular space dilates the coronary arteries ([7.14.16](/7.14.16)). When skeletal muscle contracts, every action potential causes $\ce{K+}$ efflux, interstitial $\ce{K+}$ rises, and vascular smooth muscle is hyperpolarized and relaxes.

The oxygen-demand theory: $\ce{O2}$ itself is a nutrient required to maintain contraction of vascular smooth muscle, and when local $\ce{O2}$ falls, the smooth muscle cannot sustain contraction and relaxes naturally. When metabolic rate rises or oxygen transport is impaired, tissue $P_{\ce{O2}}$ falls, microvessels and sphincters relax, more capillaries open and tissue blood flow increases; when $\ce{O2}$ supply is restored they close again. This mechanism can explain vasomotion: the periodic opening and closing of precapillary sphincters makes the mean oxygen supply of the tissue just meet its needs.

The two typical manifestations of metabolic regulation are:

- Active hyperemia: blood flow increases when tissue activity increases. Blood flow in exercising skeletal muscle can increase about 20-fold, and the same is true of the active digestive tract, secreting glands and excited brain regions.
- Reactive hyperemia: when blood flow to a tissue, blocked for seconds to hours, is restored, flow exceeds normal for a time, increasing 4–7-fold; the longer the occlusion, the greater the excess, and the excess flow roughly repays the oxygen debt accrued during occlusion.

### 15.4.3. Myogenic Autoregulation

When arterial pressure rises suddenly, the blood flow of many organs first increases with it but within a minute returns close to its former level. This is **autoregulation** (defined in [7.14.1](/7.14.1)) in the vessels: over a range of about $70$–$175\,\mathrm{mmHg}$, when arterial pressure increases more than twofold, blood flow increases by only about 20%–30%. Autoregulation is especially marked in the kidney, brain, heart and skeletal muscle; the kidney also has a dedicated mechanism, **tubuloglomerular feedback** ([7.14.22](/7.14.22)).

Autoregulation has two sources. A metabolic one: increased pressure raises flow, which brings excess $\ce{O2}$ and washes away vasodilator substances, so the vessels constrict. A myogenic one: Bayliss (1902) found that when a small artery is suddenly stretched, its smooth muscle contracts actively; this is called the myogenic response (Bayliss effect) and is independent of nerves and metabolism. Its molecular basis is that stretch activates nonselective cation channels in the wall smooth muscle (**mechanosensitive channels** of the TRP family and others), depolarizing the membrane and opening L-type $\ce{Ca^2+}$ channels, and $\ce{Ca^2+}$ influx causes contraction ([7.14.10](/7.14.10#sec-10.4.1)). The myogenic response also has a protective role: it prevents a rise in arterial pressure from being transmitted directly to the capillaries, keeping capillary pressure and filtration stable. It is therefore strongest in the kidney, where filtration must be kept constant, and in the brain, where edema must be prevented. By **Laplace's law**, if the vessel diameter decreases after a pressure rise, the wall tension $T = Pr$ also returns partly to its original value, so smooth muscle may be regulating wall tension.

### 15.4.4. Endothelial Regulation

Endothelial cells are not merely a passive barrier: they sense chemical and physical signals in the lumen and regulate the underlying smooth muscle by releasing paracrine factors:

- Nitric oxide (NO): the shear stress of blood flow pulls on endothelial cells and, through mechanosensitive ion channels (such as Piezo1, [7.6.25](/7.6.25)) and kinases, phosphorylates and activates endothelial **nitric oxide synthase** (eNOS); acetylcholine, bradykinin, histamine and others raise endothelial $\ce{Ca^2+}$ through their own receptors and also activate eNOS. NO diffuses into smooth muscle, activates soluble guanylyl cyclase, raises cGMP and relaxes the smooth muscle. One of its physiological roles is flow-mediated vasodilation: dilation of the microvessels increases flow in the upstream arterioles and medium arteries, raising shear stress, and NO relaxes these larger vessels as well; otherwise the dilation of small vessels would have a limited effect because of upstream resistance. NO acts mainly as a regulator in the larger vessels. Nitroglycerin treats angina by acting as an NO donor.
- Endothelin (endothelin-1): a peptide of 21 amino acids and one of the most powerful vasoconstrictors known, released in large amounts at vascular injury; its action is opposite to that of NO and prevents excessive bleeding from the injured vessel; it also takes part in vascular remodeling in hypertension.
- Prostacyclin ($\mathrm{PGI}_2$, a **prostaglandin**): dilates vessels and inhibits platelet aggregation ([7.14.11](/7.14.11)).

The endothelium also responds to chemical factors (such as angiotensin II) and physical factors (shear stress, stretch), and through the extracellular matrix regulates the mechanical environment of the smooth muscle.

### 15.4.5. Humoral Factors

Hormones and chemicals in the blood also regulate vessels, but most act on the whole body (Chapter 16). By action they fall into three classes:

| Class | Substance | Key points |
|---|---|---|
| Vasoconstrictors | Norepinephrine, **epinephrine** | Constrict most vessels through $\alpha_1$ receptors; at low concentrations epinephrine acts on $\beta_2$ receptors and dilates the vessels of skeletal muscle and liver |
| | Angiotensin II | Strongly constricts arterioles and raises **total peripheral resistance** ([7.14.24](/7.14.24)) |
| | **Vasopressin** (antidiuretic hormone) | One of the most powerful vasoconstrictor hormones, but at normal concentrations it acts mainly on the kidney; it raises pressure appreciably only in severe hemorrhage |
| Vasodilators | Bradykinin | Generated by kallikrein acting on plasma kininogen; strongly dilates arterioles and increases capillary permeability; acts in inflammation, sweat glands and salivary glands |
| | Histamine | Released by mast cells and basophils in tissue injury, inflammation or allergy; dilates arterioles, increases capillary permeability and causes edema |
| Ions and others | Raised $\ce{Ca^2+}$ | Constricts (promotes **excitation–contraction coupling** in smooth muscle) |
| | Raised $\ce{K+}$, $\ce{Mg^2+}$, $\ce{H+}$, anions (acetate, citrate), raised $\ce{CO2}$ | Dilate; $\ce{CO2}$ has its strongest dilating effect on cerebral vessels, and through the central nervous system indirectly constricts vessels of the whole body |

### 15.4.6. Long-Term Regulation: Angiogenesis and Remodeling

When the metabolic demand of a tissue changes for a long time, the capacity of acute regulation is no longer enough: acute regulation can restore blood flow to only about three quarters of normal at most, and the remaining gap is made up by structural change.

The number of vessels changes: when a tissue is chronically hypoxic or its metabolism is chronically enhanced (repeatedly trained muscle, life at high altitude, growing tissue, tumors), hypoxia-inducible factor HIF rises and prompts cells to secrete vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), fibroblast growth factor (FGF), angiopoietins and others, causing **angiogenesis**: the endothelium of existing venules or capillaries sprouts, proliferates, forms a lumen and connects with other vessels. In a hyperoxic environment the reverse occurs and vessels regress: in premature infants in a hyperoxic incubator retinal vessel growth is suppressed, and vessels proliferate excessively after return to air, causing retinopathy of prematurity. The developmental mechanisms of angiogenesis are in [7.5.29](/7.5.29). The formation of collateral circulation is also long-term regulation: after an artery is occluded, the small collaterals connected to it enlarge over days to months, gradually restoring part of the flow, which is of great significance for the myocardium after coronary occlusion.

Morphological and structural remodeling of vessels: chronic changes in blood pressure and flow cause existing vessels to change their configuration.

| Stimulus | Mainly occurs in | Result of remodeling |
|---|---|---|
| Chronically raised blood pressure | Pressure-sensitive small vessels | Smooth muscle cells and endothelial cells rearrange inward, the lumen narrows, and the wall-to-lumen ratio increases (inward eutrophic remodeling) |
| Chronically raised blood pressure | Large vessels | The wall thickens and stiffens, extracellular matrix increases, smooth muscle cells lengthen or hypertrophy |
| Chronically increased blood flow | Large vessels | The lumen enlarges with unchanged wall thickness and increased cross-section (shear stress returns to its set value, [7.14.14](/7.14.14#sec-14.7.3)) |
| Both pressure and flow increased | | The lumen enlarges and the wall thickens |

These rules can be understood in terms of two "set values": the endothelium regulates the lumen so that the shear stress $\tau \propto F/r^3$ returns to its set value; the smooth muscle and matrix regulate the wall so that the wall stress $\sigma = Pr/h$ (Laplace's law) returns to its set value.

## 15.5. Evolution and Comparative Aspects

### 15.5.1. Open Circulation: Hemolymph Is Interstitial Fluid

In an **open circulatory system** ([7.14.12](/7.14.12#def-12.1.1)), hemolymph flows from the vessels into the hemocoel, bathes the tissues directly, and returns to the heart through the ostia, so blood, interstitial fluid and lymph are the same fluid; the "microcirculation" and the "lymphatic system" do not exist here, and there is no Starling balance across a capillary wall. The volume of hemolymph is far greater than the blood volume of vertebrates (about 7% of body weight in vertebrates): in insects and mollusks hemolymph often makes up 20%–40% of body volume or even more. Hemolymph pressure is very low, generally only $0.7$–$1.3\,\mathrm{kPa}$ ($5$–$10\,\mathrm{mmHg}$), and most of the flow depends on pressures produced by body movement. In many mollusks and arthropods hemolymph is also part of a **hydrostatic skeleton**: after molting, insects expand the new exoskeleton and wings with hemolymph pressure, and spiders, whose legs have no extensor muscles, extend them by hemolymph pressure (in a jumping spider's leg the pressure can transiently rise to about $60\,\mathrm{kPa}$ during a jump).

### 15.5.2. Insects: Circulation Does Not Carry Oxygen

The open circulation of insects is very inefficient, yet it supports flight, one of the activities with the highest mass-specific oxygen consumption in the animal kingdom, because their $\ce{O2}$ does not pass through the circulation at all: the **tracheal system** branches from the spiracles on the body surface throughout the body, and the finest tracheoles, less than $1\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ in diameter, reach right up to the cells and even into flight muscle cells; $\ce{O2}$ diffuses in the gas phase, about $10^4$ times faster than in water ([7.14.17](/7.14.17)). Insect hemolymph therefore generally contains no respiratory pigment and transports only nutrients, hormones, metabolic wastes and immune cells. A few exceptions confirm the rule: the larvae of chironomid midges living in oxygen-poor mud have hemoglobin in their hemolymph, and some stoneflies have hemocyanin. The insect's Krogh problem is thus transferred to the geometry of the tracheae: the distance of gas-phase diffusion limits insect body size, and when atmospheric oxygen was higher in the Paleozoic, giant griffinflies with a wingspan of about $70\,\mathrm{cm}$ appeared.

### 15.5.3. Cephalopods: A High-Pressure Closed Circulation

Cephalopods (squid, octopus) are the only group of mollusks that have evolved a high-pressure closed circulation, with a complete capillary network. Their blood carries oxygen with hemocyanin dissolved in the plasma, with an oxygen-carrying capacity only about a quarter that of human blood, so the tissues must have a very high extraction ratio and venous blood is almost devoid of oxygen. To supply a high metabolic rate with such blood, they need high flow and pressure: the pressure in the octopus aorta can reach $4$–$6\,\mathrm{kPa}$ ($30$–$45\,\mathrm{mmHg}$), an order of magnitude higher than in open-circulation mollusks of comparable size. Venous blood returning from the body is at low pressure, and two branchial hearts at the entrance of the gills pressurize it to drive it through the gill capillaries; after the gills the pressure falls greatly again, and the systemic heart then pumps arterial blood to the whole body; this is another solution to the same problem as the low post-gill pressure in the single circulation of fishes ([7.14.16](/7.14.16)). Filtration across cephalopod capillaries also obeys Starling forces, and the appendages of the branchial hearts take part in filtering the primary urine.

### 15.5.4. Lymph Hearts of Amphibians

Amphibians and reptiles (and some birds, mostly in the embryo) have specialized lymph vessels with a wide lumen and a thick muscle layer in the wall that contract rhythmically, called lymph hearts; the situation in fishes is debated: teleosts have a secondary vascular system arising from arteries whose relation to the lymphatic system of tetrapods is still discussed, and some species (such as eels) have a pulsating caudal heart in the tail; mammals have no lymph hearts, and the rhythmic contraction of the collecting lymphatics themselves (Section 15.3.2) propels the lymph. The lymph hearts of amphibians are especially important: frogs and toads have highly permeable capillaries and a water-permeable skin, and must stand upright or jump on land, so they produce far more interstitial fluid than mammals; lymph flow per body weight is about an order of magnitude higher than in mammals, and the lymph returned each day can amount to several times the plasma volume. A frog usually has two pairs of lymph hearts, driven by motor neurons of the spinal cord and beating at several tens of beats per minute; after destruction of the lymph hearts or their nerves, the plasma volume of a frog falls rapidly and it dies soon after. Toads also "pump" lymph back to the lymph hearts with lung expansion and special muscle actions, and the more terrestrial the species, the greater its capacity for lymph return. This shows that lymph return is not a minor channel but an essential part of how the closed circulation maintains blood volume.

## Summary

- Structure: the microcirculation (Definition 15.1.1) consists of arterioles (master gate), metarterioles, precapillary sphincters (branch gates), true capillaries, thoroughfare channels, arteriovenous anastomoses and venules, with three pathways (circuitous, thoroughfare, arteriovenous shunt); the capillary wall has three types (continuous, fenestrated, discontinuous). The interstitium (Definition 15.1.2) is a viscoelastic gel rich in negative charge, with less than 1% free fluid, in which substances move mainly by diffusion.
- Diffusion: $J = P\,S\,\Delta C$, $P = D/a$; lipid-soluble substances cross the whole endothelium and water-soluble substances pass through the intercellular clefts, with permeability declining in steps with molecular size and showing charge selectivity; macromolecules can cross by transcytosis.
- Krogh cylinder model (Definition 15.2.1): $P(r) = P_{\mathrm{c}} + \frac{M}{4K}(r^2 - r_{\mathrm{c}}^2) - \frac{MR^2}{2K}\ln\frac{r}{r_{\mathrm{c}}}$, with a pressure drop $\propto MR^2/K$ and the "lethal corner" at the venous end farthest from the capillary; capillary recruitment and angiogenesis reduce $R$.
- Fick principle (Definition 15.2.2): $\dot V_{\ce{O2}} = F(C_{\mathrm{a}} - C_{\mathrm{v}})$; the extraction ratio is about 25% for the whole body, 70%–80% in the myocardium, up to 90% in exercising muscle and below 10% in the kidney.
- Filtration: $J_{\mathrm{v}} = K_{\mathrm{f}}[(P_{\mathrm{c}} - P_{\mathrm{i}}) - \sigma(\pi_{\mathrm{c}} - \pi_{\mathrm{i}})]$ (Definition 15.2.3); with the classical values $+13$ at the arterial end and $-7\,\mathrm{mmHg}$ at the venous end, a whole-body mean of about $0.3\,\mathrm{mmHg}$ and a net filtration of about $2\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}}$. Revised Starling principle: what matters is the colloid osmotic pressure beneath the glycocalyx; in the steady state there is almost no sustained reabsorption, and the filtrate is returned mainly by the lymph.
- Lymph (Definition 15.3.1): $2$–$4\,\mathrm{L}$ per day; returns protein (24 h fatal if stopped), transports chylomicrons, and serves immunity; the overlapping endothelium and anchoring filaments of the initial lymphatics, the lymph pump of lymphatic smooth muscle and external compression; lymph flow increases about 20-fold as interstitial pressure goes from $-6$ to $0\,\mathrm{mmHg}$. The glymphatic system clears brain metabolites along perivascular spaces. Edema (Definition 15.3.2) results from raised $P_{\mathrm{c}}$, lowered $\pi_{\mathrm{c}}$, increased permeability or lymphatic obstruction; the safety factor is about $17\,\mathrm{mmHg}$.
- Local regulation: acute (metabolic vasodilators: adenosine, $\ce{CO2}$, $\ce{H+}$, $\ce{K+}$; oxygen demand; active and reactive hyperemia; myogenic autoregulation over about $70$–$175\,\mathrm{mmHg}$; endothelial NO and endothelin; humoral factors) and long-term (VEGF- and FGF-mediated angiogenesis, collateral circulation; remodeling to set values of shear stress and wall stress).
- Evolution: in open circulation hemolymph is interstitial fluid; insects supply oxygen directly by tracheae and hemolymph does not carry oxygen; cephalopods compensate for the low oxygen-carrying capacity of hemocyanin with a high-pressure closed circulation and two branchial hearts; the lymph hearts of amphibians return large amounts of interstitial fluid.

## Questions

1. Using Guyton's classical values, calculate the whole-body mean net filtration pressure and net filtration for a person whose plasma colloid osmotic pressure falls from $28$ to $18\,\mathrm{mmHg}$ because of hypoproteinemia ($K_{\mathrm{f}} = 6.67\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}\,mmHg^{-1}}$, other conditions unchanged). Why is actual edema often not so severe?

   **Answer** Mean net filtration pressure $= 17.3 - (-3) - (18 - 8) = 10.3\,\mathrm{mmHg}$ and net filtration $\approx 69\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}}$, more than thirty times the normal $2\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}}$ and far beyond the maximal return capacity of the lymph. The actual situation is milder because the safety factors operate: interstitial pressure rises (from $-3$ toward $0$), lymph flow increases about 20-fold, and protein in the interstitial fluid is washed out so that $\pi_{\mathrm{i}}$ falls; by the revised Starling principle, the colloid osmotic pressure beneath the glycocalyx is low to begin with, so the actual filtration is also smaller than the classical calculation. Only when interstitial pressure exceeds zero and compliance becomes large are these factors exhausted, and obvious edema appears.

2. Use the Krogh cylinder model to estimate, for a tissue with oxygen consumption rate $M = 2\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{mL\,O_2\,mL^{-1}\,s^{-1}}$, capillary $P_{\ce{O2}} = 30\,\mathrm{mmHg}$, $r_{\mathrm{c}} = 3\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ and $K = 0.06\,\mu\mathrm{m^2\,mL\,O_2\,mL^{-1}\,s^{-1}\,mmHg^{-1}}$ (obtained from $D = 2000\,\mu\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$ and $\alpha = 3\times10^{-5}$), the maximal tissue cylinder radius $R$ such that the lowest tissue $P_{\ce{O2}}$ is not below $5\,\mathrm{mmHg}$. What is the corresponding capillary density?

   **Answer** From Eq. (15.3), the pressure drop at $r = R$ is $\Delta P = \frac{MR^2}{2K}\ln\frac{R}{r_{\mathrm{c}}} - \frac{M}{4K}(R^2 - r_{\mathrm{c}}^2)$, and we require $\Delta P \le 25\,\mathrm{mmHg}$. $M/K = 2\times10^{-3}/0.06 = 1/30\,\mathrm{mmHg\,\mu m^{-2}}$. Try $R = 28\,\mu\mathrm{m}$: $\frac{784}{60}\ln 9.33 - \frac{775}{120} = 13.07\times2.23 - 6.46 = 22.7$; $R = 30$: $15\times2.30 - 7.43 = 27.1$. So $R \approx 29\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ and the density is $n = 1/(\pi R^2) \approx 1/(\pi\times8.4\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{mm^2}) \approx 380$ capillaries/mm². If only half of the capillaries are open, $R$ increases to about $41\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ and a hypoxic zone appears; this is why capillaries must be recruited in exercise.

3. At rest a person has a cardiac output of $5\,\mathrm{L\,min^{-1}}$, an arterial oxygen content of $20\,\mathrm{mL\,dL^{-1}}$ and a mixed venous oxygen content of $15\,\mathrm{mL\,dL^{-1}}$. Find the whole-body oxygen consumption rate and extraction ratio. In exercise the oxygen consumption rises to $3\,\mathrm{L\,min^{-1}}$ and cardiac output to $20\,\mathrm{L\,min^{-1}}$; what is the mixed venous oxygen content?

   **Answer** At rest: $\dot V_{\ce{O2}} = 50\,\mathrm{dL\,min^{-1}}\times5\,\mathrm{mL\,dL^{-1}} = 250\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}}$ and the extraction ratio is $5/20 = 25\%$. In exercise: the arteriovenous difference is $3000/200 = 15\,\mathrm{mL\,dL^{-1}}$, the mixed venous oxygen content is $20 - 15 = 5\,\mathrm{mL\,dL^{-1}}$, and the extraction ratio is 75% (the local extraction ratio is higher in exercising muscle and lower in non-exercising organs).

4. Explain the following: (a) patients with heart failure often develop edema of the lower limbs and pulmonary edema; (b) patients with cirrhosis develop ascites; (c) local redness and swelling after an insect bite; (d) edema of the ipsilateral arm after axillary lymph node dissection for breast cancer.

   **Answer** (a) Right heart failure raises systemic venous pressure and thus capillary pressure, especially in the lower limbs under gravity; left heart failure raises pulmonary venous and pulmonary capillary pressure, and when this exceeds the plasma colloid osmotic pressure, fluid enters the pulmonary interstitium and alveoli. (b) The liver synthesizes less albumin, so plasma colloid osmotic pressure falls; portal venous pressure rises, raising splanchnic capillary pressure; and the large pores of the hepatic sinusoids let protein-rich fluid leak into the peritoneal cavity. (c) Histamine and other inflammatory mediators are released locally, arterioles dilate (redness, warmth), the permeability of capillaries and venules increases (protein leaks out and $\pi_{\mathrm{i}}$ rises), and fluid exudes (swelling). (d) Lymph return is obstructed, so filtered fluid and protein cannot be recovered, protein accumulates in the tissue, a protein-rich lymphedema forms, and fibrosis may follow.

5. Explain why the myocardium can meet an increased demand for $\ce{O2}$ only by increasing coronary blood flow, whereas skeletal muscle can increase both blood flow and extraction ratio. What role does adenosine play?

   **Answer** The extraction ratio of the myocardium is already 70%–80% at rest, venous $P_{\ce{O2}}$ is only about $20\,\mathrm{mmHg}$, and there is little room to increase extraction; by the Fick principle, an increase in oxygen consumption can come only from an increase in $F$. Skeletal muscle at rest has an extraction ratio of about 25% and only part of its capillaries are open, so in exercise it can increase both blood flow (about 20-fold) and extraction (to 80%–90%). When myocardial oxygen consumption rises or supply is insufficient, adenosine produced from ATP breakdown is released into the interstitial fluid and dilates the coronary arterioles, matching flow to metabolism; adenosine also opens $\ce{K+}$ channels in the myocardium, weakening contraction and lowering oxygen consumption.

6. Design an experiment to distinguish myogenic autoregulation from metabolic autoregulation.

   **Answer** One design: take an isolated segment of small artery, cannulate both ends, and, under conditions of no blood flow (equal pressures at the two ends, the lumen filled with physiological salt solution free of metabolic substrates, no tissue), suddenly raise the intraluminal pressure and observe the diameter. If the diameter first expands passively and then actively decreases (below the original diameter), a myogenic response independent of blood flow and metabolites exists; if the response persists after removal of the endothelium and disappears when L-type calcium channels are blocked or smooth muscle depolarization is prevented, it resides in the smooth muscle. For contrast, in the intact organ, keep the perfusion pressure constant while changing the $P_{\ce{O2}}$ of the perfusate or adding an adenosine receptor antagonist and observe the change in flow, to isolate the metabolic mechanism. One can also compare in the intact organ an arterial pressure rise (which affects both) with a venous pressure rise (which raises capillary pressure while reducing flow: the myogenic mechanism predicts constriction, the metabolic mechanism predicts dilation).

7. (Open question) What is the fundamental difference in the regulation of interstitial fluid between open and closed circulation? Why does a closed circulation need a lymphatic system, while an open circulation does not?

   **Answer** In a closed circulation, blood and interstitial fluid are separated by the capillary wall, the two fluids have different protein concentrations, and their volumes are determined by Starling forces; because capillaries inevitably leak a little fluid and protein, there must be another pathway to return them, otherwise plasma volume and colloid osmotic pressure cannot be maintained, and this is the lymphatic system. The benefits of separation are that high blood pressure and flow can be maintained, the composition of blood and interstitial fluid can be controlled separately, and blood flow can be distributed finely among the organs. In an open circulation hemolymph bathes the tissues directly, blood is the interstitial fluid, there is no "leak" to recover, and hence no need for a lymphatic system; the price is low pressure, no fine distribution of blood flow, and a large volume of circulating fluid. It follows that every group that has evolved a high-pressure closed circulation (vertebrates, cephalopods) needs some mechanism to recover the filtrate.
