The preceding two chapters used skeletal muscle as the example to show how muscle is activated ([Chapter 8](/7.14.8)) and how it produces force and work ([Chapter 9](/7.14.9)). All muscles generate force by actin–myosin sliding and all use $\ce{Ca^2+}$ as the switch, but different functional demands have led them in different directions in cellular organization, mode of activation and regulation. This chapter first compares skeletal, cardiac and smooth muscle within a unified framework, then treats the excitation–contraction coupling and mechanics of cardiac and smooth muscle in turn, and finally compares the muscles of invertebrates: nematodes, earthworms, molluscs and the flight muscles of insects show that "where the rhythm is generated" and "how force is coordinated" can be assigned separately to nerves, to the muscle itself or to the mechanical structure of the body. The electrical activity, pacemaker and conduction system of the heart are in [Chapter 13](/7.14.13), the pumping mechanics of the heart in [Chapter 12](/7.14.12), and the slow waves and interstitial cells of Cajal of gastrointestinal smooth muscle in [Chapter 26](/7.14.26).

## 10.1. Comparison of the Three Kinds of Muscle

### 10.1.1. Five Functional Dimensions and Three Coordination Strategies

The macroscopic function of a muscle arises from the coordinated contraction of a large number of muscle cells in a particular spatiotemporal pattern. Muscles can be compared along five dimensions:

- Force: achieved by many contractile units in parallel and their synchronous activation;
- Speed: at the molecular level by the cycling rate of myosin, and at the structural level by contractile units in series;
- Automaticity: whether pacemaker cells generate rhythm spontaneously, with coupling between cells then synchronizing the whole;
- Precision: the accuracy of control over the strength, extent and timing of contraction. It trades off against force: great force requires many cells to be activated at once, whereas fine control requires that a few cells can be controlled individually;
- Extensibility: the greatest degree to which the muscle remains structurally intact when stretched. It is greatest in smooth muscle (adapted to the distension of hollow organs), intermediate in skeletal muscle and least in cardiac muscle (which maintains the shape of the ventricle).

There are three strategies for achieving coordination:

1. Syncytium: cells fuse into a single multinucleate cell and intracellular signals are synchronized globally (skeletal muscle, the asynchronous flight muscle of insects);
2. Direct coupling between cells: mechanical coupling (**adherens junctions**, **desmosomes**) transmits force and electrical coupling (**gap junctions**) transmits excitation, forming a "functional syncytium" (cardiac muscle, single-unit smooth muscle);
3. Indirect coordination through other tissues: the same motor neuron synchronously activates a group of fibers (the motor unit), and connective tissue integrates and transmits force (skeletal muscle, [Chapter 9](/7.14.9)).

## 10.2. Cardiac Muscle

### 10.2.1. Functional Demands and the Intercalated Disc

The sole function of cardiac muscle is to power the pump through rhythmic contraction, which requires each contraction to be fast, strong and highly synchronous, and the whole ventricle to contract in a strict sequence. Several orders of magnitude illustrate these demands:

- The maximal rate of rise of left ventricular pressure, $\mathrm{d}P/\mathrm{d}t_{\max}$, generally exceeds $1200\,\mathrm{mmHg\,s^{-1}}$ in normal people ($160\,\mathrm{kPa\,s^{-1}}$; $1\,\mathrm{mmHg} = 0.133\,\mathrm{kPa}$); in about $0.05\,\mathrm{s}$ it raises the intraventricular pressure from a few mmHg to near aortic pressure.
- Treating the left ventricle as a thick-walled spherical shell, the wall stress by Laplace's law is $\sigma = Pr/(2h)$. With a systolic pressure of $16\,\mathrm{kPa}$ ($120\,\mathrm{mmHg}$), a cavity radius of $2.5\,\mathrm{cm}$ and a wall thickness of $1\,\mathrm{cm}$, $\sigma \approx 16\times2.5/2\,\mathrm{kPa} = 20\,\mathrm{kPa}$, of the order of $10^4$–$10^5\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-2}}$.
- The stroke work of the left ventricle is approximately the stroke volume times the mean ejection pressure: $70\,\mathrm{mL}\times13\,\mathrm{kPa} \approx 0.9\,\mathrm{J}$, that is, about $1\,\mathrm{J}$ per beat (for the external work and internal losses of the heart see [Chapter 12](/7.14.12)).
- Excitation spreads through the whole ventricle within $60$–$100\,\mathrm{ms}$ (the duration of the QRS complex of the electrocardiogram), which requires the longitudinal specialized conduction system (Purkinje fibers, $2$–$4\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$) and the working myocardium crossing the ventricular wall transversely ($0.3$–$1\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$) to act together ([Chapter 13](/7.14.13)).

Cardiomyocytes are not structurally a syncytium but a "functional syncytium" joined end to end by intercalated discs.

**Definition 10.2.1 (Intercalated Disc)** The specialized stepwise junctional region between the ends of adjacent cardiomyocytes is called the **intercalated disc**. It consists of gap junctions, adherens junctions and desmosomes: gap junctions let excitation spread directly between cells, and adherens junctions and desmosomes (mixed in mammals into a "composite area") join the myofibrils and intermediate filaments of adjacent cells into one and transmit contractile force.

The intercalated disc is also enriched in $\mathrm{Na_V1.5}$ channels and many scaffold proteins. The gap junctions of working myocardium consist mainly of connexin Cx43, the atria and Purkinje fibers also have Cx40, and the cells of the nodal region have mainly the slowly conducting Cx45. The **cadherin** of the adherens junction senses the mechanical force acting on it and, through α-catenin and vinculin, initiates downstream signals that change sarcomere structure and cell stiffness; the cardiomyocyte membrane also has stretch-sensitive ion channels, so that the mechanical state in turn influences electrical activity (mechano-electrical feedback). Mutations of genes encoding desmosomal proteins (plakophilin-2, desmoglein-2, desmocollin-2 and others) cause arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy: weakness of the mechanical connection eventually appears as disorder of electrical conduction. The contribution to conduction of gap junctions and of another possible mechanism of intercellular coupling (ephaptic coupling mediated by the extracellular electric field) is discussed in [Chapter 13](/7.14.13).

### 10.2.2. Excitation–Contraction Coupling in Cardiac Muscle

**Definition 10.2.2 (Calcium-Induced Calcium Release)** The positive-feedback process in which a local rise of cytosolic $\ce{Ca^2+}$ concentration opens the $\ce{Ca^2+}$ release channels (RyR, or $\mathrm{IP_3}$ receptor) of the sarcoplasmic (endoplasmic) reticulum, releasing stored $\ce{Ca^2+}$ and raising cytosolic $\ce{Ca^2+}$ further, is called **calcium-induced calcium release** (CICR).

During the plateau of the ventricular action potential, the L-type $\ce{Ca^2+}$ channels on the transverse tubule membrane ($\mathrm{Ca_V1.2}$, the DHPR of cardiac muscle) open, and the small amount of "trigger calcium" that enters opens the adjacent RyR2; the $\ce{Ca^2+}$ released from the sarcoplasmic reticulum is several times the amount that enters. This coupling depends on extracellular $\ce{Ca^2+}$: in 1883 Ringer found that an isolated frog heart soon stopped beating when the perfusate was made up with distilled water rather than London tap water, thereby discovering the necessity of $\ce{Ca^2+}$ for cardiac contraction; skeletal muscle, by contrast, can still contract in calcium-free solution ([Chapter 8](/7.14.8)). The transverse tubules of cardiac muscle are much thicker than those of skeletal muscle, and their lumen is rich in negatively charged glycocalyx that can bind $\ce{Ca^2+}$, providing a reserve of trigger calcium close to the channels.

In cardiac muscle the transverse tubule is apposed to only one terminal cisterna of the sarcoplasmic reticulum, forming a dyad: on the two sides of a gap $12$–$15\,\mathrm{nm}$ wide, a cluster of L-type channels lies opposite a cluster of RyR2, the ratio of their numbers being about 1 : 4 (to 1 : 10). One such unit is called a couplon, and a ventricular myocyte has about $10^4$ of them.

Why does the positive feedback not make all the RyRs fire at a touch and release in an all-or-none manner? The answer is local control (Stern, 1992). When an L-type channel opens, only the $[\ce{Ca^2+}]$ in the dyad cleft where it sits rises to tens of $\mu\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$, sufficient to open the RyRs of the same cluster; the released $\ce{Ca^2+}$ has been diluted and buffered by the time it diffuses to the next cluster about $1$–$2\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ away, and generally cannot trigger it. One release from one cluster of RyRs appears under the confocal microscope as a Ca²⁺ spark (Cheng, Lederer and Cannell, 1993): a local $\ce{Ca^2+}$ rise lasting about $20$–$30\,\mathrm{ms}$ with a half-width of about $2\,\mu\mathrm{m}$. The $\ce{Ca^2+}$ transient of the whole cell is the temporal superposition of thousands of synchronous sparks, and its total amount is graded with the number of couplons triggered, which in turn depends on the size of the $\ce{Ca^2+}$ current and on the $\ce{Ca^2+}$ load of the sarcoplasmic reticulum. Positive feedback confined to nanometer-scale compartments becomes, at the scale of the cell, an amplifier that can be graded.

The role of the transverse tubules is to let every couplon receive the trigger signal almost simultaneously: the action potential travels down the tubules to the depth of the cell in less than $1\,\mathrm{ms}$, much faster than $\ce{Ca^2+}$ could diffuse in from the cell surface (Example 8.5.1). The protein BIN1 bends the sarcolemma inward to form the tubules and delivers $\mathrm{Ca_V1.2}$ to them. In myocardial infarction and heart failure the transverse tubules become sparse and disorganized, and some RyR2 lose the L-type channels opposite them and become "orphan RyRs" that can be activated only late by $\ce{Ca^2+}$ diffusing from neighbors: $\ce{Ca^2+}$ release becomes asynchronous and contraction weakens, which is thought to be an important cause of the dysregulation of $\ce{Ca^2+}$ signaling in the failing heart.

During relaxation $\ce{Ca^2+}$ is removed as follows: SERCA2a pumps it back into the sarcoplasmic reticulum (about 70% in the human ventricle), the $\mathrm{Na^+}$–$\ce{Ca^2+}$ exchanger (NCX) on the sarcolemma extrudes it (about 28%), and the rest is handled by the plasma membrane $\ce{Ca^2+}$ pump and mitochondria; in the steady state the $\ce{Ca^2+}$ entering through L-type channels in each beat must be extruded in equal amount by NCX. SERCA2a is inhibited by phospholamban, and the sympathetic nerves activate **protein kinase A** through $\beta_1$ receptors, which phosphorylates phospholamban and relieves the inhibition while also increasing the L-type current: contraction is stronger and relaxation is also faster.

**Example 10.2.1 (The Direction of NCX)** For each $\ce{Ca^2+}$ extruded, NCX exchanges in 3 $\mathrm{Na^+}$, with a net movement of one positive charge inward, and its reversal potential is $E_{\mathrm{NCX}} = 3E_{\mathrm{Na}} - 2E_{\mathrm{Ca}}$. Take $37\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$, $[\mathrm{Na^+}]_{\mathrm{o}}/[\mathrm{Na^+}]_{\mathrm{i}} = 145/10$ and, in diastole, $[\ce{Ca^2+}]_{\mathrm{o}}/[\ce{Ca^2+}]_{\mathrm{i}} = 2\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}/0.1\,\mu\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$; the Nernst equation gives $E_{\mathrm{Na}} = 26.7\ln14.5 \approx 71\,\mathrm{mV}$, $E_{\mathrm{Ca}} = 13.4\ln(2\times10^4) \approx 132\,\mathrm{mV}$ and $E_{\mathrm{NCX}} \approx 213 - 264 = -51\,\mathrm{mV}$. The diastolic membrane potential ($-85\,\mathrm{mV}$) is below it, so the exchanger runs in the forward mode and extrudes $\ce{Ca^2+}$; during the upstroke and early plateau of the action potential the membrane potential is above it (cytosolic $\ce{Ca^2+}$ has not yet risen), and the exchanger briefly reverses and brings in a little $\ce{Ca^2+}$. Cardiac glycosides of the digitalis type inhibit the **sodium pump**, raising $[\mathrm{Na^+}]_{\mathrm{i}}$ and lowering $E_{\mathrm{NCX}}$, so that less $\ce{Ca^2+}$ is extruded, the $\ce{Ca^2+}$ load of the sarcoplasmic reticulum increases and contraction is strengthened.

### 10.2.3. Mechanical Features of Cardiac Muscle

The mechanics of cardiac muscle differ from those of skeletal muscle in three important ways. First, cardiac muscle works in the body on the ascending limb of the length–tension curve (sarcomeres about $1.8$–$2.3\,\mu\mathrm{m}$), and the ascending limb is much steeper than in skeletal muscle: stretch not only increases the effective filament overlap but also raises the sensitivity of the myofilaments to $\ce{Ca^2+}$ (length-dependent activation). The more the ventricle fills, the stronger the next contraction; this is the Frank–Starling mechanism ([Chapter 12](/7.14.12)). Second, cardiac muscle expresses a short, stiff titin isoform (N2B), and passive tension rises steeply above a sarcomere length of about $2.2\,\mu\mathrm{m}$, making it difficult for sarcomeres to be stretched onto the descending limb and preventing overdistension of the ventricle. Third, in every heartbeat all cardiomyocytes contract, and there are no "motor units" to recruit; the effective refractory period lasts until the end of contraction, so tetanus is impossible ([Chapter 9](/7.14.9), Question 3). The contractile force of cardiac muscle can therefore be regulated in only two ways: by changing the initial length, or by changing the size of the $\ce{Ca^2+}$ transient and the $\ce{Ca^2+}$ sensitivity of the myofilaments (contractility).

## 10.3. Structure and Types of Smooth Muscle

### 10.3.1. Cell Structure

Smooth muscle forms the walls of blood vessels, airways, the digestive tract and the urinary and reproductive tracts; it both maintains the shape of the organ and resists loads by sustained tonic contraction, and propels contents by rhythmic or phasic contraction. Smooth muscle cells are spindle-shaped, about $2$–$5\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ in diameter, $20$–$500\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ long (longer in the pregnant uterus) and uninucleate. They have no sarcomeres and therefore no striations:

- Thin filaments are numerous, with a thin-to-thick filament ratio of about $10$–$15 : 1$ (about $2 : 1$ in skeletal muscle). The thin filaments are anchored to dense bodies in the cytoplasm and dense plaques on the inner side of the cell membrane. Dense bodies contain α-actinin and correspond structurally and mechanically to Z lines; intermediate filaments (desmin, vimentin) link the dense bodies into a network, and the dense plaques of adjacent cells are joined by protein bridges, through which force passes from one cell to the next.
- The thick filaments are "side-polar" (Craig and Megerman, 1977): on one side of the filament the myosin heads point in one direction along its whole length and on the other side in the opposite direction, with no central bare zone. The thin filaments can therefore slide along the whole length of the thick filament, smooth muscle can shorten to 1/3 of its original length or even less, and it produces nearly maximal force over a very wide range of lengths.
- There are no transverse tubules, and the plasma membrane has many caveolae enriched in receptors, ion channels and signaling molecules; the sarcoplasmic reticulum is poorly developed and lies mostly beneath the membrane.

### 10.3.2. Single-Unit and Multi-Unit Smooth Muscle

**Definition 10.3.1 (Single-Unit and Multi-Unit Smooth Muscle)** Smooth muscle in which the cells have many gap junctions between them, are electrically coupled into a functional syncytium and contract as a whole is called **single-unit smooth muscle** (also called visceral smooth muscle); smooth muscle in which the cells have almost no gap junctions between them and each cell is innervated individually and contracts independently is called **multi-unit smooth muscle**.

| | Single-unit smooth muscle | Multi-unit smooth muscle |
|---|---|---|
| Coupling between cells | Many gap junctions; excitation spreads through the cell population | Almost no gap junctions |
| Innervation | Sparse; modulates spontaneous activity only | Almost every cell is innervated |
| Automaticity | Present: pacemaker cells, slow waves, sensitive to stretch (myogenic contraction) | Absent |
| Precision | Low, contracts synchronously as a whole | High, can be controlled finely and independently |
| Examples | Gastrointestinal tract, uterus, ureter, bladder (very strongly coupled), small blood vessels (more weakly coupled) | Iris and ciliary muscle, arrector pili, vas deferens, part of the smooth muscle of large arteries |

The two are the ends of a continuum: smooth muscle in the same organ often has features of both kinds, and the strength of coupling is adapted to the function of the tissue.

The neuromuscular junction of smooth muscle is quite different from that of skeletal muscle. The axons of autonomic nerves run among the smooth muscle with many bead-like swellings along their course, called varicosities, which contain transmitter vesicles; the transmitter is released from the varicosities and diffuses tens of nanometers to several micrometers to the smooth muscle cells (diffuse junctions), so that one cell can receive the action of several varicosities and one varicosity acts on several cells. Contacts in multi-unit smooth muscle are closer (about $30$–$40\,\mathrm{nm}$). The transmitter is not limited to ACh: sympathetic postganglionic fibers release norepinephrine, as well as ATP, nitric oxide and various neuropeptides ([Chapter 7](/7.14.7)).

## 10.4. Excitation–Contraction Coupling in Smooth Muscle

### 10.4.1. Sources of Ca²⁺: Electromechanical and Pharmacomechanical Coupling

The key signal for smooth muscle contraction is a rise of cytosolic $\ce{Ca^2+}$; neither an action potential nor even a change in membrane potential is necessary.

- Electromechanical coupling: an upstream signal opens ion channels in the membrane, causing an action potential (mostly a $\ce{Ca^2+}$ spike, which in visceral smooth muscle often fires in bursts on the peaks of slow waves) or a graded local depolarization; voltage-gated L-type $\ce{Ca^2+}$ channels open, external calcium enters, and the signal can be amplified by CICR.
- Pharmacomechanical coupling: a transmitter or hormone acts on **G protein-coupled receptors** and through $\mathrm{G_q}$–phospholipase C produces **inositol 1,4,5-trisphosphate**, which opens the $\mathrm{IP_3}$ receptors of the sarcoplasmic reticulum to release internal calcium, with no change of membrane potential at all; the diacylglycerol produced by the same pathway and the Rho pathway also raise the $\ce{Ca^2+}$ sensitivity of the contractile apparatus (Section 10.4.3).

The factors regulating smooth muscle contraction are therefore particularly diverse: autonomic transmitters; hormones (epinephrine, angiotensin II, vasopressin, oxytocin, endothelin); local chemical factors ($\ce{O2}$, $\ce{CO2}$, $\mathrm{pH}$, lactate, $\mathrm{K^+}$, adenosine; local blood flow regulation is in [Chapter 15](/7.14.15)); and stretch itself.

### 10.4.2. Thick-Filament Regulation: MLCK and MLCP

Smooth muscle has no troponin, and the $\ce{Ca^2+}$ sensor is **calmodulin**, acting on myosin ([7.3.13](/7.3.13#sec-13.5.4)):

1. Four $\ce{Ca^2+}$ bind calmodulin, and $\ce{Ca^2+}$–calmodulin activates myosin light-chain kinase (MLCK);
2. MLCK phosphorylates Ser19 of the myosin regulatory light chain, the myosin ATPase is activated, and the cross-bridges begin to cycle with actin;
3. Myosin light-chain phosphatase (MLCP) dephosphorylates the light chain; after $\ce{Ca^2+}$ falls and MLCK is inactivated, the cross-bridge cycle stops and the smooth muscle relaxes.

Caldesmon and calponin on the thin filament also inhibit the actomyosin interaction and play an auxiliary regulatory role. In the steady state the degree of light-chain phosphorylation is determined by the ratio of the activities of the two enzymes, $\phi = k_{\mathrm{MLCK}}/(k_{\mathrm{MLCK}} + k_{\mathrm{MLCP}})$, where $k_{\mathrm{MLCK}}$ rises with $[\ce{Ca^2+}]$ according to a Hill relation.

### 10.4.3. Ca²⁺ Sensitization and Desensitization

Since contraction depends on the balance between kinase and phosphatase, changing the activity of MLCP alone, without changing $\ce{Ca^2+}$, can equally change contraction:

- $\ce{Ca^2+}$ sensitization: agonists activate RhoA and Rho kinase (ROCK) through $\mathrm{G_{12/13}}$, and ROCK phosphorylates the regulatory subunit of MLCP, MYPT1, inhibiting MLCP; protein kinase C activated by diacylglycerol phosphorylates the inhibitory protein CPI-17, which likewise inhibits MLCP. The same $\ce{Ca^2+}$ then produces more force. So-called "$\ce{Ca^2+}$-independent contraction" is actually contraction caused by sensitization at basal $\ce{Ca^2+}$ levels.
- $\ce{Ca^2+}$ desensitization and relaxation: nitric oxide released by the endothelium activates soluble guanylyl cyclase, and cGMP activates protein kinase G, which activates MLCP and lowers cytosolic $\ce{Ca^2+}$; nitroglycerin and phosphodiesterase 5 inhibitors (sildenafil) both use this pathway. $\beta_2$ receptors relax airway smooth muscle through **cyclic AMP** and **protein kinase A**, which is the principle of $\beta_2$ agonists in the treatment of asthma.

## 10.5. Mechanics of Smooth Muscle

### 10.5.1. Slow, Strong and Economical

The cross-bridge cycle of smooth muscle is 10 to 300 times slower than that of skeletal muscle, but each cross-bridge stays attached for a long time, so the maximal tension per unit cross-sectional area can equal or even exceed that of skeletal muscle ($40$–$60\,\mathrm{N\,cm^{-2}}$, compared with about $30$–$40\,\mathrm{N\,cm^{-2}}$ in skeletal muscle). A contraction begins only $50$–$100\,\mathrm{ms}$ after stimulation, reaches its maximum in about $0.5\,\mathrm{s}$ and lasts $1$–$3\,\mathrm{s}$, about 30 times longer than a skeletal muscle twitch. To maintain the same tension, smooth muscle consumes only 1/10 to 1/300 of the energy of skeletal muscle. This economy is important for the energy budget of the whole body: the smooth muscle of blood vessels, the digestive tract and the bladder maintains a certain tension almost throughout life.

### 10.5.2. The Latch State

After smooth muscle is stimulated, light-chain phosphorylation and the cross-bridge cycling rate first rise, and then $\ce{Ca^2+}$ and phosphorylation fall to lower levels, while the force hardly falls, and at that point the shortening velocity and ATP consumption are both very low.

**Definition 10.5.1 (Latch State)** The state in which smooth muscle continues to maintain most of its tension with very little energy expenditure after the degree of light-chain phosphorylation and the cross-bridge cycling rate have fallen is called the **latch state**.

Hai and Murphy (1988) explained latch with a four-state model: myosin can be in the free unphosphorylated state M, the free phosphorylated state $\mathrm{M_p}$, the attached phosphorylated state $\mathrm{AM_p}$ and the attached dephosphorylated state AM:

$$
\mathrm{M} \underset{K_2}{\overset{K_1}{\rightleftharpoons}} \mathrm{M_p} \underset{K_4}{\overset{K_3}{\rightleftharpoons}} \mathrm{AM_p} \underset{K_6}{\overset{K_5}{\rightleftharpoons}} \mathrm{AM} \overset{K_7}{\longrightarrow} \mathrm{M}, \tag{10.1}
$$
where $K_1 = K_6$ is the activity of MLCK (varying with $\ce{Ca^2+}$), $K_2 = K_5$ is the activity of MLCP, $K_3$ and $K_4$ are the attachment and detachment of phosphorylated cross-bridges, and $K_7$ is the detachment of dephosphorylated cross-bridges. The key assumption is that an attached cross-bridge, once dephosphorylated by MLCP (AM, the "latch bridge"), no longer cycles but remains attached and bears force, detaching only at the very slow rate $K_7$. Force is proportional to the attached cross-bridges $\mathrm{AM_p} + \mathrm{AM}$; ATP is consumed at each phosphorylation and at each cycle of a phosphorylated cross-bridge. The fractions of the states satisfy a set of linear ordinary differential equations, and the steady-state solution is the solution of a four-variable linear system (linear systems of [2.4](/2.4)).

The latch allows smooth muscle to maintain tension for hours with very little $\ce{Ca^2+}$ and very little ATP, which is exactly what vascular tone and sphincters require. Latch is a completely different strategy from the tetanus of skeletal muscle: tetanus maintains force by sustained high $\ce{Ca^2+}$ and rapid cycling, whereas latch maintains it by letting the cross-bridges "stick" and stay still.

### 10.5.3. Stress Relaxation and Plasticity

Single-unit smooth muscle, especially that of hollow organs, can bring its tension roughly back to its original level within seconds to minutes after being stretched or shortened: when the volume of the bladder suddenly increases, the pressure first rises sharply and then returns almost to its original value within 15 seconds to 1 minute; when the volume suddenly decreases the reverse happens. This is called stress relaxation and reverse stress relaxation, and it keeps the intraluminal pressure of a hollow organ roughly constant as its volume changes greatly (for the micturition reflex see [Chapter 22](/7.14.22)). Its basis is the slow rearrangement of cross-bridges and the length adaptation of the contractile apparatus itself: the actin and myosin filaments in smooth muscle cells can depolymerize and reassemble at a new length.

Mechanical coupling among smooth muscle cells is also influenced by the extracellular matrix. In culture experiments with airway smooth muscle, cells on a soft matrix simulating the stiffness of a healthy airway ($E \approx 300\,\mathrm{Pa}$) are joined to one another by **adherens junctions** formed by N-cadherin–β-catenin, and force is transmitted between cells; when the matrix stiffens to about $13\,\mathrm{kPa}$, **focal adhesions** begin to appear at the cell borders; at $40\,\mathrm{kPa}$ the intercellular adherens junctions disappear completely, and each cell is connected to the matrix through **integrins** and exerts force on the matrix independently. A rigid matrix lets the integrin–focal adhesion complex bear tension better than intercellular junctions, and focal adhesions also promote actin polymerization through signals such as FAK and paxillin, strengthening the contractile force of single cells while tissue-level coordination declines. Fibrosis of the airway wall in asthma stiffens the matrix, which may be one of the mechanisms of airway hyperresponsiveness.

## 10.6. Evolution and Comparative Aspects

### 10.6.1. The Origin of Muscle

The force-generating actin–myosin system is much older than animals: myosin II and the phosphorylation of its regulatory light chain exist in amoebae and *Dictyostelium* ([7.3.13](/7.3.13)). Smooth-muscle-type thick-filament regulation therefore probably represents the ancestral state. Sponges have no muscle cells, but their epithelial cells can contract slowly; cnidarians have epitheliomuscular cells (both epithelial and contractile cells), and the swimming muscle of jellyfish is already striated. Comparative genomics shows that the striated muscles of cnidarians and bilaterians result from **convergent evolution** (Steinmetz et al., 2012): both use a "striated-type" myosin heavy chain that had already diverged before the origin of animals, but the striated muscle of jellyfish lacks core components of bilaterian striated muscle such as troponin and titin. The regular arrangement of striations is a design that evolved repeatedly for fast contraction.

### 10.6.2. Nematodes: Longitudinal Muscle, Hydrostatic Skeleton and Nerve-Dominated Timing

Nematodes live in an environment of low **Reynolds number** ([7.6.22](/7.6.22)), and their bodies are filled with incompressible, high-pressure coelomic fluid, forming a hydrostatic skeleton: the body volume is constant and the length is difficult to change, so muscle contraction can appear only as local bending, propagating along the body axis to form a propulsive wave.

**Definition 10.6.1 (Hydrostatic Skeleton)** A supporting system consisting of an incompressible fluid-filled cavity surrounded by layers of muscle is called a **hydrostatic skeleton**. Muscle contraction changes the shape of the fluid cavity without changing its volume, thereby producing movement or support; antagonistic muscles interact through the fluid.

The body wall muscle of *Caenorhabditis elegans* has only longitudinal muscle (95 uninucleate muscle cells arranged in two dorsal and two ventral rows) and no circular muscle, so alternating dorsal and ventral contraction can only bend the body from side to side (in fact dorsoventrally). The myofibrils of a muscle cell lie only at the base of the cell, in an oblique striated arrangement (Section 10.6.4), the cell itself is mostly cytoplasm, and it extends "muscle arms" to the nerve cords to receive synapses ([Chapter 8](/7.14.8)). Each muscle cell is an independent unit of execution with a very small individual output; although there are gap junctions between muscle cells, coordination relies mainly on the temporally alternating activation of muscle at different positions by motor neurons in the dorsal and ventral nerve cords, and the muscle itself has no automaticity. Nematodes have no voltage-gated $\mathrm{Na^+}$ channels; the body wall muscle depolarizes through L-type $\ce{Ca^2+}$ channels (EGL-19) and repolarizes through $\mathrm{K^+}$ channels, producing $\ce{Ca^2+}$ action potentials: the input from motor neurons is a graded continuous signal, which the muscle turns into discrete "contract or not contract" events whose frequency varies with the input. Nematodes are only millimeters long, so even a nerve impulse that propagates slowly as a graded potential arrives within milliseconds, and fast conduction is not needed.

### 10.6.3. Annelids: Antagonistic Muscles, Segmentation and Peristalsis

The body wall of each segment of an earthworm has an outer layer of circular muscle and an inner layer of longitudinal muscle, and the coelom is divided by septa into segment-by-segment hydrostatic skeletons. Because the volume is constant, contraction of the circular muscle makes the segment thinner and longer and contraction of the longitudinal muscle makes it thicker and shorter, with the setae protruding and gripping the soil. In locomotion these two states propagate along the body with a fixed phase difference, forming a peristaltic wave: the thickened, shortened segments act as anchors that stay fixed, and the thinned, lengthened segments extend forward; the contraction wave propagates from head to tail, opposite to the direction of progression.

![Kinematic model of earthworm peristaltic crawling (after the kinematic description of Quillin, 1999; schematic parameters). Each segment alternates between an anchoring phase (longitudinal muscle contracted, thick and short, setae out, stationary relative to the ground, 0.6 s) and a forward phase (circular muscle contracted, thin and long, moving forward, 0.4 s), with a period of 1 s and an advance of 0.4 segment lengths per step; the forward phase of each segment is 0.12 s later than that of the segment before it. In this kinematics the forward speed equals the step length times the frequency; the number of segments simultaneously in the anchoring phase determines the ability of the body to resist backward slip, and in soil with high resistance the earthworm prolongs the anchoring phase and increases the number of segments anchored at once. Neural control is distributed: each segment has its own ganglion, which can generate local rhythms and reflexes; the segments coordinate their phase through neural connections and sensory feedback from mechanical stretch. Classical experiments of the late 19th century showed that when an earthworm is cut into two pieces and the pieces are tied together with a thread, the peristaltic wave still propagates across the cut: the posterior segments begin to contract when they sense the stretch from the anterior segments. There are also giant axons running through the whole body that carry the rapid whole-body escape reflex (withdrawal into the burrow), coexisting with local rhythmic control. The nervous system no longer controls the details of each contraction but generates a temporal pattern with spatial structure, which muscles and the hydrostatic skeleton turn into movement.

### 10.6.4. Molluscs: Catch Muscle and Obliquely Striated Muscle

The smooth muscle in the adductor muscles of bivalves and the anterior byssus retractor muscle of the mussel can, after stimulation has ceased and cytosolic $\ce{Ca^2+}$ has fallen, still maintain tension for hours with very little energy, keeping the shell tightly closed at low tide.

**Definition 10.6.2 (Catch Muscle)** A molluscan muscle that, after $\ce{Ca^2+}$ has fallen back to the resting level, maintains tension for a long time in a "catch" state with almost no consumption of ATP is called a **catch muscle**.

The molecular switch of catch is twitchin, a giant protein of the titin family: dephosphorylated twitchin connects the thick filaments (with paramyosin as their core) to the thin filaments and locks the tension; the signal that releases catch is serotonin, which activates protein kinase A through cAMP and phosphorylates twitchin, unlocking it. ACh causes contraction, and when $\ce{Ca^2+}$ falls after contraction the muscle enters the catch state. Catch differs in mechanism from the latch of vertebrate smooth muscle (Section 10.5.2) but solves the same problem: using a phosphorylation switch in place of continuous cross-bridge cycling to "lock" force is another example of convergent evolution.

Many muscles of nematodes, annelids and the mantle of cephalopods are obliquely striated muscle: the filaments are arranged as regularly as in striated muscle, but the filament columns of neighboring sarcomeres are staggered, so that the stripes run obliquely in a spiral direction. The oblique arrangement allows thick filaments to slide past one another, and the muscle can produce force over a very wide range of lengths ("supercontraction"), which suits hydrostatic-skeleton animals that need large deformations.

### 10.6.5. Insect Flight Muscle: Synchronous and Asynchronous

The frequency demands on insect flight muscle are the most extreme. The flight muscles of locusts, dragonflies and butterflies are synchronous flight muscles: each motor nerve impulse causes one contraction, and the wingbeat frequency (about $10$–$30\,\mathrm{Hz}$) is limited by the speed at which $\ce{Ca^2+}$ can be released and taken up in every contraction; these muscles have a well-developed sarcoplasmic reticulum. The wingbeat frequency of flies, mosquitoes, bees and beetles reaches $100$–$1000\,\mathrm{Hz}$ (about $1000\,\mathrm{Hz}$ in midges), far beyond the range that nerves and the $\ce{Ca^2+}$ cycle could drive impulse by impulse.

**Definition 10.6.3 (Asynchronous Flight Muscle)** An insect flight muscle whose contraction frequency does not correspond one to one to the frequency of motor nerve impulses and which oscillates by itself through delayed stretch activation and the mechanical resonance of the thorax is called **asynchronous flight muscle**; one in which contraction corresponds one to one to nerve impulses is called synchronous flight muscle.

The key property of asynchronous flight muscle is delayed stretch activation: when $\ce{Ca^2+}$ is held at an intermediate level, after the muscle is stretched rapidly, additional tension is produced only after a delay; when it is shortened, tension falls with a delay. The indirect flight muscles of flies (the dorsal longitudinal muscles and the dorsoventral muscles) are a pair of antagonists, both attached to the thoracic exoskeleton rather than to the wings: contraction of the dorsal longitudinal muscles deforms the thorax and stretches the dorsoventral muscles, which contract after a delay and in turn stretch the dorsal longitudinal muscles, and so on. The thorax and wings constitute an elastic resonant system, the frequency of oscillation is determined by this mechanical system, and the motor nerve need only fire at a few to a dozen times per second to maintain the level of cytosolic $\ce{Ca^2+}$. Asynchronous flight muscle therefore needs almost no sarcoplasmic reticulum, and its volume is given mainly to myofibrils and mitochondria; special proteins between its thick and thin filaments (flightin, the F1 isoform of troponin C) are thought to take part in stretch activation.

![Delayed stretch-activation oscillator of asynchronous flight muscle (minimal model, schematic parameters). The thorax and wings are simplified to a damped mass–spring system m x″ + c x′ + k x = −K(A_DLM − A_DVM), with x > 0 stretching the dorsal longitudinal muscle (DLM) and shortening the dorsoventral muscle (DVM); the active force A of each muscle follows its own degree of stretch with a first-order lag of 1 ms, τA′ = s(±x) − A, s(u) = max(0, tanh u); K is the degree of activation set by the cytosolic Ca²⁺ level. The physical meaning of this model can be illustrated by the work done in one cycle. Let $x = X\sin\omega t$; if the muscle force $F = -KA$ lags the displacement by a phase $\varphi$ ($0 < \varphi < \pi$), the work done by the muscle on the thorax in one cycle is $W = \oint F\,\mathrm{d}x = \pi KA_0X\sin\varphi > 0$: when force lags length, the muscle puts energy into the system in each cycle, equivalent to negative damping. When it exceeds the dissipation by aerodynamic and internal damping, the resting state becomes unstable (**Hopf bifurcation**, [2.4.11](/2.4.11)) and the system enters a stable **limit cycle**. The "work loop" method invented by Josephson measures the net work done by a muscle per cycle in just this way, by periodic stretching: synchronous muscle does positive work only when the phase of the nerve impulse is suitable, whereas asynchronous muscle does positive work automatically while $\ce{Ca^2+}$ is maintained. Asynchronous flight muscle has originated independently about 7–10 times in insects.

### 10.6.6. Rhythm Moves from Nerve to Mechanics

Putting several kinds of muscle together, we can see how the location of rhythm generation and the degree of involvement of structure vary:

| System | Where the rhythm arises | Role of structure | Mode of coordination |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nematode body wall muscle | Nerve (alternating activation of dorsal and ventral motor neurons) | The hydrostatic skeleton turns local contraction into bending | Nerve-dominated temporal coordination |
| Earthworm body wall muscle | Ganglia of the segments + intersegmental sensory feedback | Segmented hydrostatic skeleton, friction of setae with soil | Phase coordination (peristaltic wave) |
| Insect asynchronous flight muscle | Mechanical resonance of muscle and exoskeleton | Thoracic elasticity determines frequency | Mechanical self-sustained oscillation, nerve maintains only the activation level |
| Vertebrate cardiac muscle | Myogenic pacemaker cells ([Chapter 13](/7.14.13)) | Functional syncytium | Electrical coupling spreads excitation, nerve only modulates |
| Gastrointestinal smooth muscle | Slow waves of interstitial cells of Cajal ([Chapter 26](/7.14.26)) | Syncytium of smooth muscle and interstitial cells | Electrical coupling + regulation by the enteric nervous system |

The coordination of movement is not concentrated at any single level but distributed among nerve, muscle cell and body mechanics. "Rhythm moves from nerve to mechanics" is not a one-way evolutionary route but different divisions of labor of the same components in different groups: when the mechanical structure of the body can reliably determine timing (asynchronous flight muscle, the heart), the nervous system retreats to the role of regulator.

The Venus flytrap provides a control without muscle ([7.13.19](/7.13.19)). Its leaf closes in about $0.1\,\mathrm{s}$, much faster than changes in cell turgor can directly drive: slow changes of turgor make the hyperbolic leaf accumulate elastic energy, and after a critical point is passed the curvature of the leaf suddenly inverts (elastic instability). This is the same principle as the catapult of the flea ([Chapter 9](/7.14.9)): a slow actuator plus elastic energy storage can achieve speeds beyond the capacity of the actuator itself.

## Questions

1. Why can $\ce{Ca^2+}$ release in cardiac muscle depend on positive feedback (CICR) and yet still be graded, rather than being fully released once triggered?

   **Answer** The positive feedback of CICR is confined to nanometer-scale local compartments such as the dyad cleft: the opening of one L-type channel raises $[\ce{Ca^2+}]$ only in the couplon where it sits to a level able to open that cluster of RyR2, and the released $\ce{Ca^2+}$ has been diluted and buffered by the time it diffuses to the next cluster about $1$–$2\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ away, and generally cannot trigger it again (local control). The release of each couplon is one calcium spark, and the $\ce{Ca^2+}$ transient of the whole cell is the sum of the triggered sparks, so the total release is graded with the size of the L-type current (the number of couplons triggered) and the load of the sarcoplasmic reticulum. Only when the load of the sarcoplasmic reticulum is too high can sparks trigger one another and form spontaneously propagating $\ce{Ca^2+}$ waves, which is the cellular mechanism of some arrhythmias.

2. Use the Hai–Murphy model (Eq. (10.1)) to explain why vascular smooth muscle can still maintain tension after $\ce{Ca^2+}$ and light-chain phosphorylation have both fallen. If a drug increased $K_7$ to the same value as $K_4$, how would the ATP needed to maintain the same tension change?

   **Answer** An attached phosphorylated cross-bridge, after dephosphorylation by MLCP, becomes a latch bridge AM, which no longer cycles (consumes no ATP) but remains attached and bears force, detaching only at the very slow rate $K_7 = 0.01\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}$; while MLCK retains some activity, part of the AM is rephosphorylated. At a low level of phosphorylation the total number of attached cross-bridges (the force) therefore remains large. If $K_7$ increases to $K_4$, dephosphorylated cross-bridges detach as fast as phosphorylated ones and force is roughly proportional to phosphorylation; to maintain the same force a higher level of phosphorylation and faster cross-bridge cycling must be maintained, and ATP consumption increases several-fold (panel C).

3. Calculate: the thorax and wings of an insect can be regarded as a spring oscillator with $m = 2\,\mathrm{mg}$ and $k = 2\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}$. What is its approximate wingbeat frequency? If the wings are clipped so that the effective mass falls to $1.5\,\mathrm{mg}$, what does the frequency become? Would the same treatment of a synchronously flying locust change its wingbeat frequency noticeably? Why?

   **Answer** $f = \sqrt{k/m}/(2\pi) = \sqrt{2/(2\times10^{-6})}/(2\pi) = 1000/(2\pi) \approx 159\,\mathrm{Hz}$; after the mass is reduced to $1.5\,\mathrm{mg}$, $f \approx 159\times\sqrt{2/1.5} \approx 184\,\mathrm{Hz}$. For asynchronous flight muscle the oscillation frequency is determined by mechanical resonance and therefore changes with mass. The synchronous flight muscle of the locust is triggered by a nerve impulse in every contraction, and the wingbeat frequency is determined mainly by the rhythm of the central pattern generator, so clipping the wings has only a small effect (the mechanical properties of the wings may slightly change the rhythm through sensory feedback).

4. Compare the responses of skeletal, cardiac and smooth muscle when extracellular $\ce{Ca^2+}$ is removed, and explain the reason for each.

   **Answer** Skeletal muscle: it can still contract normally to stimulation for some time, because the DHPR is mechanically coupled to RyR1 and all the $\ce{Ca^2+}$ comes from the sarcoplasmic reticulum, until the stores are exhausted by slow loss. Cardiac muscle: it stops contracting within a few beats (Ringer's experiment), because the opening of RyR2 needs trigger calcium entering through L-type channels, with no external calcium there is no CICR, and the sarcoplasmic reticulum also gradually loses its replenishment. Smooth muscle: electromechanically coupled contraction (such as that caused by depolarization with high $\mathrm{K^+}$) soon disappears, because it depends on external calcium entering through L-type channels; pharmacomechanical coupling can mobilize internal calcium from the sarcoplasmic reticulum through $\mathrm{IP_3}$ receptors and cause a brief contraction, but the sarcoplasmic reticulum is small and the response disappears after repeated stimulation.

5. (Open question) Starting from the "five functional dimensions", compare a system that needs fast, fine movement (such as the human extraocular muscles) with a system that needs sustained tension (such as blood vessels or the adductor muscle of the mussel), and explain why they have adopted different coordination strategies and molecular mechanisms.

   **Answer** Key points: the extraocular muscles demand the highest speed and precision: the motor units are extremely small (about 10 fibers), the fibers are fast, the sarcoplasmic reticulum and SERCA are extremely well developed, the twitch lasts only a few milliseconds, and force is graded by recruiting a large number of small motor units and by very high firing frequencies; they also have multiply innervated tonic fibers used to maintain eye position (Chapter 8). The price of all this is high energy use and low economy of tension per unit. Vascular smooth muscle and the mussel adductor need to maintain tension for a long time at minimum energy cost, and precision and speed are unimportant: cross-bridge cycling is slow, and a phosphorylation switch (latch, or catch by twitchin) is used to "lock" the force, with regulation by $\ce{Ca^2+}$ and $\ce{Ca^2+}$ sensitivity; vascular smooth muscle coordinates by electrical coupling and local factors (single-unit), and the adductor is controlled by neurotransmitters (ACh and serotonin) governing entry into and release from catch. The same actin–myosin machine has differentiated at the levels of activation, regulation and organization according to functional demands.
