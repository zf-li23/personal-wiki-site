The $[\ce{H+}]$ of arterial blood is about $40\,\mathrm{nmol\,L^{-1}}$, only about one three-millionth of $[\ce{Na+}]$, and yet it is held within $\pm 5\,\mathrm{nmol\,L^{-1}}$. This is because almost all proteins carry ionizable groups, so changes in $[\ce{H+}]$ alter the charge, conformation and function of proteins: the activity of enzymes, the gating of ion channels and the binding of oxygen to hemoglobin all depend on pH. Yet the body produces acid at every moment: metabolism generates about $15\,000$–$20\,000\,\mathrm{mmol}$ of $\ce{CO2}$ and about $50$–$100\,\mathrm{mmol}$ of nonvolatile acid per day. This chapter, the last on the urinary system, explains how the body deals with these acids: the basic concepts of $\ce{H+}$ **homeostasis** (Section 25.1), the sources of acid and the three lines of defense (Section 25.2), chemical buffering (Section 25.3), respiratory regulation (Section 25.4) and renal regulation (Section 25.5), followed by the analysis of acid–base disturbances (Section 25.6). Finally, after comparing acid–base regulation in different animals, we give a systematic summary of excretion and osmoregulation in Chapters 21–25 (Section 25.7). For the quantitative basis of weak acids, $\mathrm{p}K_{\mathrm{a}}$ and buffers, see [7.1.2](/7.1.2); for the transport of $\ce{CO2}$ in blood, see [Chapter 19](/7.14.19); and for the chemosensory regulation of breathing, see [Chapter 20](/7.14.20).

## 25.1. $\ce{H+}$ Homeostasis

### 25.1.1. $[\ce{H+}]$ and pH

**pH** is the negative logarithm of the $\ce{H+}$ **activity**, $\mathrm{pH} = -\lg a_{\ce{H+}}$, and in physiological solutions concentration ($\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$) can be used as an approximation for activity. For arterial blood $[\ce{H+}] = 4.0 \times 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$,

$$
\mathrm{pH} = -\lg(4.0 \times 10^{-8}) = 8 - \lg 4.0 = 7.40 .
$$

The normal range $[\ce{H+}] = 35$–$45\,\mathrm{nmol\,L^{-1}}$ corresponds to pH $7.35$–$7.45$. pH is a logarithmic scale: a decrease of $0.3$ in pH doubles $[\ce{H+}]$. Between pH $7.2$ and $7.5$ there is a convenient approximation for mental arithmetic between $[\ce{H+}]$ (nmol/L) and pH: for each $0.01$ by which pH deviates from $7.40$, $[\ce{H+}]$ changes by about $1\,\mathrm{nmol\,L^{-1}}$ in the opposite direction. The range of arterial pH compatible with survival in humans is about $6.8$–$8.0$, that is, $[\ce{H+}]$ of about $160$–$10\,\mathrm{nmol\,L^{-1}}$.

The pH of the various body fluids differs widely, reflecting the transport of the epithelia that produce them.

| Fluid | pH | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Arterial blood | $7.40$ | Venous blood and **interstitial fluid** about $7.35$, containing more $\ce{CO2}$ |
| Cerebrospinal fluid | about $7.32$ | Little protein and weak buffering, so changes in $\ce{CO2}$ have a greater effect (Chapter 20) |
| **Intracellular fluid** | about $7.0$–$7.2$ | Can fall to about $6.4$ in vigorously exercising skeletal muscle; about $4.5$–$5$ in **lysosomes** |
| Gastric juice | $1$–$3$ | The acidic extreme in the body (Chapter 27) |
| Pancreatic juice | about $8$ | Contains about $145\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$ $\ce{HCO3-}$ |
| Urine | $4.5$–$8.0$ | Varies with the needs of acid–base balance |

### 25.1.2. Acids and Bases

According to the **Brønsted–Lowry acid–base theory**, a molecule or ion that can release $\ce{H+}$ (a proton) is an acid and one that can bind $\ce{H+}$ is a base. A hydrogen atom that has lost its electron is just a proton, so $\ce{H+}$ is a proton. $\ce{HCl}$ dissociates almost completely in solution and is a strong acid; $\ce{H2CO3}$ dissociates only partly and is a weak acid. Bases include $\ce{HCO3-}$, $\ce{HPO4^2-}$ and $\ce{OH-}$, of which $\ce{OH-}$ is a strong base and $\ce{HCO3-}$ and $\ce{HPO4^2-}$ are weak bases. The imidazole groups of histidine and the terminal amino groups of proteins can all bind $\ce{H+}$ and are important bases, the most abundant being hemoglobin in the erythrocytes. Almost all the substances in the body fluids that take part in acid–base balance are weak acids and weak bases (**conjugate acid–base pairs**), and they form **buffer solutions**.

**Acidosis** is an excess of $\ce{H+}$ in the body (blood pH below $7.35$), and **alkalosis** a deficit of $\ce{H+}$ (pH above $7.45$); either can be caused by an accumulation of acid or a loss of base (or the reverse) ([7.1.2](/7.1.2)). Acidosis mainly depresses the central nervous system (coma in severe cases) and reduces the contractility of the heart and the response of blood vessels to catecholamines; alkalosis makes nerves and muscles hyperexcitable (tetany and convulsions resembling those of hypocalcemia, because in alkalosis more $\ce{Ca^2+}$ is bound to plasma proteins and free $\ce{Ca^2+}$ falls).

## 25.2. Sources of Acid and the Three Lines of Defense

### 25.2.1. Volatile and Fixed Acids

- Volatile acid: the complete oxidation of carbohydrate, fat and protein produces $\ce{CO2}$, about $15\,000$–$20\,000\,\mathrm{mmol}$ per day. $\ce{CO2}$ dissolves in water to form carbonic acid, but it can be excreted by the lungs and does not accumulate as long as ventilation keeps pace with production.
- Fixed (nonvolatile) acid: the oxidation of sulfur-containing amino acids (methionine, cysteine) produces sulfuric acid, the hydrolysis of phosphorus-containing proteins, nucleic acids and phospholipids produces phosphoric acid, and the incomplete oxidation of carbohydrate and fat produces organic acids such as lactic acid and **ketone bodies**. An adult on a mixed diet produces a net of about $1\,\mathrm{mmol\,kg^{-1}}$ of fixed acid per day, about $50$–$100\,\mathrm{mmol}$ (we take $80\,\mathrm{mmol}$ below). The organic acid salts in vegetables and fruit (citrate and others) are metabolized to $\ce{HCO3-}$ and are a source of base, so vegetarians have a smaller net acid load and a more alkaline urine. Fixed acid can only be excreted by the kidney.

### 25.2.2. The Three Lines of Defense

| Line of defense | Speed | Action | Capacity |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chemical buffering in body fluids | Seconds | Weak bases bind $\ce{H+}$ (or weak acids release $\ce{H+}$), reducing the change in free $[\ce{H+}]$ | Only moderates the change and does not remove acid from the body; the buffers are consumed |
| Respiratory regulation | Minutes | Alters alveolar ventilation and so $P_{\ce{CO2}}$ and the concentration of carbonic acid | Can correct about $50\%$–$75\%$ of a pH deviation; cannot dispose of fixed acid itself |
| Renal regulation | Hours to days | Excretes acidic or alkaline urine, reabsorbs and regenerates $\ce{HCO3-}$ | The strongest; can fully restore acid–base balance and buffer reserves |

The three act in series: buffers first take up a sudden increase of $\ce{H+}$, respiration then lowers $P_{\ce{CO2}}$ to bring pH close to normal, and the kidney finally excretes the excess acid and replaces the $\ce{HCO3-}$ that has been consumed.

## 25.3. Chemical Buffering

### 25.3.1. How Large Is the Effect of Buffering?

**Example 25.3.1** A fixed acid load of $80\,\mathrm{mmol}$ is produced each day. Distributed unbuffered in about $42\,\mathrm{L}$ of body fluid, it would raise $[\ce{H+}]$ by $80/42 \approx 1.9\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$ and lower pH to about $2.7$, 50 000 times the normal $40\,\mathrm{nmol\,L^{-1}}$. In reality almost all of this $\ce{H+}$ is bound by buffers, and free $[\ce{H+}]$ stays near $40\,\mathrm{nmol\,L^{-1}}$. But buffers are finite: the $\ce{HCO3-}$ in the **extracellular fluid** amounts to only about $14\,\mathrm{L} \times 24\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}} \approx 340\,\mathrm{mmol}$ in all, and if the kidney did not excrete acid and replenish $\ce{HCO3-}$ it would be exhausted in a little over four days.

:::

There are three main classes of buffer systems in body fluids: bicarbonate, phosphate and proteins. All buffer pairs in the same solution are in equilibrium with the same $[\ce{H+}]$ (the isohydric principle), so knowing the ratio for one pair gives the pH and therefore the state of every buffer pair. Clinically it is the bicarbonate pair that is measured.

### 25.3.2. The Bicarbonate Buffer System

The **bicarbonate buffer system** is the most important buffer system of the extracellular fluid ([7.1.2](/7.1.2)):

$$
\ce{CO2 + H2O <=>[\text{carbonic anhydrase}] H2CO3 <=> H+ + HCO3-}.
$$

The hydration of $\ce{CO2}$ is slow without an enzyme (half-time of several seconds to more than ten seconds), and **carbonic anhydrase** in erythrocytes, renal tubules and gastric mucosa accelerates it about $10^6$-fold; the enzyme catalyzes both directions, helping to form carbonic acid in the tissues and to release $\ce{CO2}$ in the lungs. The concentration of $\ce{H2CO3}$ is extremely low (at 37 °C only about $1/400$–$1/800$ of that of dissolved $\ce{CO2}$, with some variation between measurements), so combining the first two steps and using dissolved $\ce{CO2}$ in place of $\ce{H2CO3}$ gives the **Henderson–Hasselbalch equation** with an apparent $\mathrm{p}K' = 6.1$:

$$
\mathrm{pH} = 6.1 + \lg\frac{[\ce{HCO3-}]}{0.03\,P_{\ce{CO2}}},
$$

where $P_{\ce{CO2}}$ is in $\mathrm{mmHg}$ ($1\,\mathrm{mmHg} = 0.1333\,\mathrm{kPa}$) and $0.03\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}\,mmHg^{-1}}$ (that is, $0.23\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}\,kPa^{-1}}$) is the **solubility** of $\ce{CO2}$ in plasma. For normal arterial blood, $[\ce{HCO3-}] = 24\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$ and $P_{\ce{CO2}} = 40\,\mathrm{mmHg}$ ($5.3\,\mathrm{kPa}$):

$$
\mathrm{pH} = 6.1 + \lg\frac{24}{1.2} = 6.1 + \lg 20 = 7.40 .
$$

pH is determined only by the ratio of $[\ce{HCO3-}]$ to $P_{\ce{CO2}}$, the former regulated by the kidney and the latter by the lungs; this equation is the starting point for the analysis of every acid–base problem.

Chemically speaking, bicarbonate is not a good buffer pair: its $\mathrm{p}K' = 6.1$ lies $1.3$ units from blood pH, and in a closed solution its buffering power is very weak. It is the most important because it is an open system: $\ce{CO2}$ is held constant by the lungs (the $\ce{CO2}$ formed when added $\ce{H+}$ combines with $\ce{HCO3-}$ is exhaled) and $\ce{HCO3-}$ is regulated by the kidney, so the two components can change independently. The **buffer capacity** of an open system is about 20 times that of a closed system, and [7.1.2](/7.1.2) compares the two quantitatively with a figure. When a strong acid is added,

$$
\ce{HCl + NaHCO3 -> NaCl + H2CO3 -> NaCl + CO2 ^ + H2O},
$$

the strong acid is converted into $\ce{CO2}$, which can be exhaled; the increased $\ce{CO2}$ stimulates breathing and is excreted still more. When a strong base is added,

$$
\ce{NaOH + H2CO3 -> NaHCO3 + H2O},
$$

the strong base is converted to the weak base $\ce{NaHCO3}$; $\ce{CO2}$ is consumed, reduced breathing lets $\ce{CO2}$ rise again, and the excess $\ce{HCO3-}$ is excreted by the kidney. Both cases show the cooperation of the respiratory system.

### 25.3.3. Phosphate and Proteins

The phosphate buffer pair $\ce{H2PO4- <=> H+ + HPO4^2-}$ has a $\mathrm{p}K = 6.8$, close to the pH of body fluids, and buffers efficiently; but its concentration in the **extracellular fluid** is only about $1\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$ and its contribution is small. It is important in two places: the **intracellular fluid** (where the concentrations of organic and inorganic phosphates are high) and the tubular fluid (where phosphate is concentrated along the tubule and the pH is lowered, Section 25.5.4).

Proteins are the most important buffers inside cells and account for about $60\%$–$70\%$ of the chemical buffering power of the whole body. Among them the imidazole group of histidine ($\mathrm{p}K$ about $6$–$7$, [7.1.2](/7.1.2)) buffers best near physiological pH. The most important nonbicarbonate buffer in blood is hemoglobin (concentration about $150\,\mathrm{g\,L^{-1}}$, 38 histidine residues per tetramer): deoxyhemoglobin binds $\ce{H+}$ more readily than oxyhemoglobin (the **Haldane effect**, the mirror image of the **Bohr effect**), so that blood unloading oxygen in the tissues is just able to take up more of the $\ce{H+}$ generated from $\ce{CO2}$ (Chapter 19).

For changes caused by $\ce{CO2}$ (respiratory changes), the bicarbonate system cannot buffer by itself ($\ce{CO2}$ and $\ce{HCO3-}$ are the two ends of the same pair), and they can be buffered only by nonbicarbonate buffers (mainly hemoglobin and plasma proteins). The nonbicarbonate buffer value of whole blood is about $25$–$30\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$ per pH unit.

### 25.3.4. Intracellular and Bone Buffering

In an acid load, about half of the $\ce{H+}$ is eventually buffered inside cells: $\ce{H+}$ and $\ce{CO2}$ enter the cells and, in exchange, $\ce{K+}$ and $\ce{Na+}$ leave them, which is one of the reasons why metabolic acidosis is often accompanied by hyperkalemia (alkalosis is the reverse, with hypokalemia). Bone is a huge reserve of base (carbonate and phosphate): in chronic acidosis the mineral of bone dissolves to buffer $\ce{H+}$, at the cost of bone loss and increased urinary calcium.

## 25.4. Respiratory Regulation

Respiratory regulation is a **negative feedback**: an increase in $\ce{H+}$ (metabolic acidosis) stimulates the **peripheral chemoreceptors** (carotid bodies) and the **central chemoreceptors** (Chapter 20), alveolar ventilation increases, $P_{\ce{CO2}}$ falls and pH rises again; when pH rises, ventilation is reduced, $P_{\ce{CO2}}$ rises and the alkaline change is moderated. From the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation, if $[\ce{HCO3-}]$ is unchanged, doubling alveolar ventilation halves $P_{\ce{CO2}}$ and raises pH by $\lg 2 = 0.3$; in practice the release of $\ce{H+}$ by nonbicarbonate buffers lowers $[\ce{HCO3-}]$ slightly and the rise is about $0.2$–$0.25$. Lowering ventilation to one quarter of normal lowers pH by about $0.4$–$0.5$.

The **feedback gain** of respiratory regulation is about $1$–$3$, and it can correct only $50\%$–$75\%$ of a pH deviation: once pH approaches normal, the stimulus driving the increase in ventilation also weakens; and ventilation must also serve the need for oxygen supply, so lowering ventilation to compensate for alkalosis lowers arterial $P_{\ce{O2}}$, and hypoxia in turn limits any further reduction in ventilation. Respiratory regulation also cannot excrete fixed acid from the body: it merely adjusts the ratio of $\ce{CO2}$ to $\ce{HCO3-}$ back toward normal, and the $\ce{HCO3-}$ that has been consumed can only be replenished by the kidney.

## 25.5. Renal Regulation

### 25.5.1. The Two Tasks of the Kidney

The kidney has two tasks in acid–base balance: (1) to recover all of the filtered $\ce{HCO3-}$, since otherwise this would amount to losing base; and (2) to excrete about $80\,\mathrm{mmol}$ of fixed acid per day and at the same time generate an equal amount of new $\ce{HCO3-}$ to add to the blood, replacing the amount consumed in buffering. The $\ce{HCO3-}$ filtered per day is about $180\,\mathrm{L} \times 24\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}} = 4320\,\mathrm{mmol}$. Both tasks are accomplished by tubular secretion of $\ce{H+}$, and about $4320 + 80 = 4400\,\mathrm{mmol}$ of $\ce{H+}$ must be secreted per day in all:

- When the body fluids are too acidic: more $\ce{H+}$ is secreted, all the $\ce{HCO3-}$ is reabsorbed and new $\ce{HCO3-}$ is produced; the urine becomes acidic and the acidity of the body fluids is reduced.
- When the body fluids are too alkaline: $\ce{H+}$ secretion decreases, the reabsorption of $\ce{HCO3-}$ is incomplete, and type B **intercalated cells** actively secrete $\ce{HCO3-}$; the urine becomes alkaline and the alkalinity of the body fluids is reduced.

### 25.5.2. Reabsorption of $\ce{HCO3-}$

$\ce{HCO3-}$ cannot cross the apical membrane directly, and its reabsorption depends on the secretion of $\ce{H+}$.

1. Intracellular $\ce{CO2}$ and $\ce{H2O}$ form $\ce{H+}$ and $\ce{HCO3-}$ under the catalysis of carbonic anhydrase II.
2. $\ce{H+}$ is secreted across the apical membrane into the tubular fluid: in the proximal tubule mainly through the $\ce{Na+}/\ce{H+}$ exchanger NHE3 (secondary active transport driven by the $\ce{Na+}$ gradient established by the **sodium pump**), and a little through the $\ce{H+}$-ATPase.
3. In the tubular fluid $\ce{H+}$ combines with filtered $\ce{HCO3-}$ to form $\ce{H2CO3}$, which brush-border carbonic anhydrase IV breaks down into $\ce{CO2}$ and $\ce{H2O}$; $\ce{CO2}$ diffuses into the cell and takes part in step 1 again.
4. The $\ce{HCO3-}$ formed in the cell enters the interstitial fluid across the basolateral membrane: in the proximal tubule through the $\ce{Na+}$–$\ce{HCO3-}$ **cotransporter** (NBCe1, $1\,\ce{Na+}:3\,\ce{HCO3-}$) and elsewhere through the $\ce{Cl-}/\ce{HCO3-}$ exchanger (AE1 and others).

For each $\ce{H+}$ secreted, one $\ce{HCO3-}$ returns to the blood, but this $\ce{H+}$ has not been excreted: in the lumen it turns, together with $\ce{HCO3-}$, into $\ce{CO2}$ and water, so this process is only recovery and not acid excretion. Of the filtered $\ce{HCO3-}$, the proximal tubule reabsorbs $80\%$–$90\%$, the thick ascending limb about $10\%$, and the distal convoluted tubule and collecting duct recover the rest; normal urine contains almost no $\ce{HCO3-}$. Carbonic anhydrase inhibitors (acetazolamide) block this process and $\ce{HCO3-}$ is lost in the urine (Chapter 23).

### 25.5.3. $\ce{H+}$ and $\ce{HCO3-}$ Secretion by Intercalated Cells

Type A **intercalated cells** of the late distal convoluted tubule and collecting duct (Chapter 23) secrete $\ce{H+}$ actively through the apical $\ce{H+}$-ATPase (V-type proton pump) and the $\ce{H+}/\ce{K+}$-ATPase, and the $\ce{HCO3-}$ formed in the cell at the same time enters the blood through basolateral AE1. The secretion here is **primary active transport** and can proceed against a very large gradient: the pH of the tubular fluid can fall as low as about $4.5$, with $[\ce{H+}]$ about $800$ times that of blood. The proximal tubule secretes a large amount of $\ce{H+}$ (about $80\%$–$90\%$ of the total) but against a small gradient (minimum tubular fluid pH about $6.7$); the collecting duct secretes a small amount of $\ce{H+}$ but can build a large gradient, and determines the final acidity of the urine. Type B intercalated cells have the opposite polarity: apical pendrin (a $\ce{Cl-}/\ce{HCO3-}$ exchanger) secretes $\ce{HCO3-}$ and the basolateral $\ce{H+}$-ATPase returns $\ce{H+}$ to the blood; the activity of type B cells increases in alkalosis.

Even if urine pH falls to $4.5$, free $\ce{H+}$ is only $10^{-4.5} \approx 0.03\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$, and $1.5\,\mathrm{L}$ of urine per day can excrete only about $0.05\,\mathrm{mmol}$ of $\ce{H+}$ in the free form, less than one thousandth of what is required. $\ce{H+}$ must therefore be excreted bound to buffers in the tubular fluid, and these are titratable acid and $\ce{NH4+}$.

### 25.5.4. Titratable Acid

**Definition 25.5.1 (Titratable Acid)** The $\ce{H+}$ excreted in the urine bound to buffers (mainly $\ce{HPO4^2-}$, also creatinine, uric acid and others) can be measured by the amount of strong base needed to titrate the urine back to blood pH ($7.4$), and is called **titratable acid**.

At plasma pH $7.4$, $[\ce{HPO4^2-}]/[\ce{H2PO4-}] = 10^{7.4 - 6.8} = 4$, so $80\%$ of the filtered phosphate is in the base form; when the tubular fluid has been acidified to pH $5.8$, the ratio falls to $10^{-1} = 0.1$ and only about $9\%$ remains in the base form. If $30\,\mathrm{mmol}$ of phosphate is excreted per day, it carries away about $(0.80 - 0.09) \times 30 \approx 21\,\mathrm{mmol}$ of $\ce{H+}$. Each $\ce{H+}$ excreted bound to $\ce{HPO4^2-}$ corresponds to one new $\ce{HCO3-}$ formed in the cell and added to the blood. Titratable acid is normally about $20$–$30\,\mathrm{mmol\,d^{-1}}$, is limited by the amount of phosphate excreted, and can increase only to a limited extent in acidosis (in **diabetic ketoacidosis** ketone anions can also act as titratable buffers).

### 25.5.5. $\ce{NH4+}$ and New $\ce{HCO3-}$

A larger and more adjustable route of acid excretion is $\ce{NH4+}$.

1. Proximal tubule cells take up glutamine and, through glutaminase and glutamate dehydrogenase, form 2 $\ce{NH4+}$ and $\alpha$-ketoglutarate, which by **gluconeogenesis** or oxidation yields 2 $\ce{HCO3-}$ that enter the blood.
2. $\ce{NH4+}$ is secreted into the tubular fluid through apical NHE3 (with $\ce{NH4+}$ in place of $\ce{H+}$).
3. In the thick ascending limb, $\ce{NH4+}$ is reabsorbed through NKCC2 (occupying the $\ce{K+}$ site), accumulates in the medullary interstitium, and is concentrated in the medulla in a countercurrent fashion.
4. In the collecting duct, type A intercalated cells secrete $\ce{H+}$ and acidify the tubular fluid, and $\ce{NH3}$ from the medulla diffuses into the lumen through Rh glycoproteins (RhCG, RhBG), where it combines with $\ce{H+}$ to form $\ce{NH4+}$, which cannot cross the cell membrane and is "trapped" and excreted in the urine (diffusion trapping; the $\mathrm{p}K$ of $\ce{NH3/NH4+}$ is about $9.2$, and the more acidic the tubular fluid, the more effective the trapping).

For each $\ce{NH4+}$ excreted, one new $\ce{HCO3-}$ enters the blood. Note that this $\ce{HCO3-}$ is "new" only if the $\ce{NH4+}$ is actually excreted in the urine: if $\ce{NH4+}$ returns to the blood and goes to the liver to be used for urea synthesis (the **urea cycle** consumes two $\ce{HCO3-}$ equivalents for each urea synthesized), the newly generated base is canceled.

Normally $\ce{NH4+}$ excretion is about $30$–$50\,\mathrm{mmol\,d^{-1}}$; in chronic acidosis the enzymes and transporters of glutamine metabolism in the proximal tubule are induced, and over several days $\ce{NH4+}$ excretion can increase to more than $300\,\mathrm{mmol\,d^{-1}}$, which is the main way in which the kidney compensates for chronic acidosis. In renal failure the production of ammonia is reduced, which is the main cause of acidosis.

### 25.5.6. Net Acid Excretion

**Definition 25.5.2 (Net Acid Excretion)** The sum of the titratable acid and $\ce{NH4+}$ excreted by the kidney per day, minus the $\ce{HCO3-}$ lost in the urine, is called **net acid excretion**:

$$
\mathrm{NAE} = U_{\mathrm{TA}}\dot V + U_{\ce{NH4+}}\dot V - U_{\ce{HCO3-}}\dot V .
$$

It equals the amount of new $\ce{HCO3-}$ generated by the kidney and added to the blood per day. In the steady state net acid excretion equals the daily net production of fixed acid (about $80\,\mathrm{mmol}$, for example $25\,\mathrm{mmol}$ of titratable acid plus $55\,\mathrm{mmol}$ of $\ce{NH4+}$).

$\ce{H+}$ secretion is regulated by the following factors: a rise in extracellular $P_{\ce{CO2}}$ or a fall in pH directly promotes $\ce{H+}$ secretion and ammonia production by tubular cells (the cells also become acidified); a reduction of extracellular fluid volume (angiotensin II stimulating NHE3 and **aldosterone** stimulating the $\ce{H+}$-ATPase of intercalated cells) promotes $\ce{H+}$ secretion and $\ce{HCO3-}$ reabsorption; hypokalemia acidifies the tubular cells and promotes $\ce{H+}$ secretion and ammonia production. The last two explain why the volume depletion and hypokalemia caused by vomiting and **diuretics** perpetuate metabolic alkalosis: the kidney then, far from excreting the excess $\ce{HCO3-}$, goes on reabsorbing it.

## 25.6. Acid–Base Disturbances

### 25.6.1. The Four Primary Disturbances and Their Compensation

**Definition 25.6.1 (Respiratory and Metabolic Acid–Base Disturbances)** Acidosis that originates in a rise in $P_{\ce{CO2}}$ (hypoventilation) is called **respiratory acidosis**, and alkalosis that originates in a fall in $P_{\ce{CO2}}$ (hyperventilation) **respiratory alkalosis**; acidosis that originates in a fall in $[\ce{HCO3-}]$ (an increase of fixed acid or a loss of base) is called **metabolic acidosis**, and alkalosis that originates in a rise in $[\ce{HCO3-}]$ (a loss of acid or an increase of base) **metabolic alkalosis**.

From the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation, pH is determined by $[\ce{HCO3-}]/P_{\ce{CO2}}$, so a primary change is always compensated by a change of the other in the same direction: respiratory disturbances are compensated by the kidney (changing $[\ce{HCO3-}]$, which takes days) and metabolic disturbances by respiration (changing $P_{\ce{CO2}}$, within minutes to hours). Compensation brings pH close to normal but does not restore it to normal, and never overcompensates; if the results show "overcompensation", two disturbances coexist. The expected compensations commonly used in clinical practice (empirical rules) are as follows.

| Disturbance | Primary change | Compensation | Expected degree of compensation | Common causes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Metabolic acidosis | $[\ce{HCO3-}]$ ↓ | Hyperventilation, $P_{\ce{CO2}}$ ↓ | $P_{\ce{CO2}} \approx 1.5[\ce{HCO3-}] + 8 \pm 2$ (Winter's formula) | **Ketoacidosis**, lactic acidosis, renal failure, diarrhea |
| Metabolic alkalosis | $[\ce{HCO3-}]$ ↑ | Reduced ventilation, $P_{\ce{CO2}}$ ↑ | $P_{\ce{CO2}}$ rises about $0.7\,\mathrm{mmHg}$ for each $1\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$ rise in $[\ce{HCO3-}]$ | Vomiting, diuretics, aldosterone excess |
| Respiratory acidosis | $P_{\ce{CO2}}$ ↑ | Renal retention of $\ce{HCO3-}$ | For each $10\,\mathrm{mmHg}$ rise in $P_{\ce{CO2}}$, $[\ce{HCO3-}]$ rises about $1$ acutely and about $3.5\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$ chronically | Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, respiratory depression |
| Respiratory alkalosis | $P_{\ce{CO2}}$ ↓ | Renal excretion of $\ce{HCO3-}$ | For each $10\,\mathrm{mmHg}$ fall in $P_{\ce{CO2}}$, $[\ce{HCO3-}]$ falls about $2$ acutely and about $5\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$ chronically | Anxiety, high altitude, fever, pregnancy |

The small changes in $[\ce{HCO3-}]$ in acute respiratory disturbances come from nonbicarbonate buffers (Section 25.3.3) and the large chronic changes from the kidney. These relations are clear at a glance in pH–$[\ce{HCO3-}]$ coordinates.

**Definition 25.6.2 (Davenport Diagram)** The diagram used to represent and analyze acid–base status, with plasma pH on the abscissa and $[\ce{HCO3-}]$ on the ordinate, in which the $P_{\ce{CO2}}$ isobars determined by the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation and the buffer line of blood are drawn, is called the **Davenport diagram**.

![Davenport diagram. ### 25.6.2. The Anion Gap

**Definition 25.6.3 (Anion Gap)** The difference between the concentrations of the main cation and the main anions in plasma,

$$
\mathrm{AG} = [\ce{Na+}] - [\ce{Cl-}] - [\ce{HCO3-}]
$$
is called the **anion gap**, normally about $12 \pm 4\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$ (for example $142 - 106 - 24 = 12$).

Plasma is of course electrically neutral; the anion gap represents the unmeasured anions (mainly albumin, then phosphate, sulfate and organic anions) minus the unmeasured cations ($\ce{K+}$, $\ce{Ca^2+}$, $\ce{Mg^2+}$). It is used to distinguish the causes of metabolic acidosis.

- Raised anion gap: the added acid brings an unmeasured anion with it. $\ce{H+}$ consumes $\ce{HCO3-}$ and leaves lactate, $\beta$-hydroxybutyrate, sulfate and the like; the decrease in $\ce{HCO3-}$ is made up by unmeasured anions, and $[\ce{Cl-}]$ is unchanged. Examples are lactic acidosis, ketoacidosis, renal failure (retention of sulfuric and phosphoric acid) and methanol or ethylene glycol poisoning.
- Normal anion gap (hyperchloremic): what is lost is $\ce{HCO3-}$ itself, replaced by $\ce{Cl-}$. Examples are diarrhea (loss of the $\ce{HCO3-}$ in intestinal fluid), renal tubular acidosis (the kidney cannot reabsorb $\ce{HCO3-}$ or cannot secrete $\ce{H+}$) and the use of carbonic anhydrase inhibitors.

Albumin is the main component of the anion gap, and hypoalbuminemia masks a raised anion gap: for each decrease of $10\,\mathrm{g\,L^{-1}}$ in albumin the anion gap falls by about $2.5\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$, and a correction must be made in the analysis.

### 25.6.3. Steps of the Analysis

**Example 25.6.1** A patient with type 1 **diabetes mellitus** presents with vomiting and confusion. Arterial blood has pH $7.18$, $P_{\ce{CO2}} = 20\,\mathrm{mmHg}$ and $[\ce{HCO3-}] = 7.5\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$; plasma $[\ce{Na+}] = 135$ and $[\ce{Cl-}] = 95\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$.

1. Check the data: $6.1 + \lg(7.5/(0.03 \times 20)) = 6.1 + \lg 12.5 = 7.20$, in reasonable agreement with the measured $7.18$.
2. The pH is low, so this is acidosis; $[\ce{HCO3-}]$ is lowered and $P_{\ce{CO2}}$ is also lowered, so the primary disturbance is metabolic acidosis (if it were primarily respiratory, $P_{\ce{CO2}}$ would be raised).
3. Expected compensation: $P_{\ce{CO2}} = 1.5 \times 7.5 + 8 = 19.3 \pm 2\,\mathrm{mmHg}$; the measured value is $20$, so the respiratory compensation is appropriate and there is no coexisting respiratory disturbance.
4. Anion gap $= 135 - 95 - 7.5 = 32.5\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$, markedly raised: consistent with ketoacidosis (the unmeasured anions are $\beta$-hydroxybutyrate and acetoacetate).
5. Further: the anion gap has risen by about $20.5$ and $[\ce{HCO3-}]$ has fallen by $16.5$; the ratio of the two is about $1.2$, which is roughly consistent and suggests no marked coexisting metabolic disturbance (if the rise in the gap were much larger than the fall in $\ce{HCO3-}$, a coexisting metabolic alkalosis would be indicated, and in this case vomiting would tend to cause it).

## 25.7. Evolution and Comparison

### 25.7.1. Body Temperature and pH: Alpha-Stat

In humans blood pH is held at $7.40$; but when the blood of **poikilotherms** is measured, pH falls with rising body temperature, by about $0.017$ per $1\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$ (Robin 1962; Reeves 1972; Rahn 1975). In frogs, turtles and fish the blood pH is about $7.9$–$8.0$ at $5\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$ and about $7.4$–$7.5$ at $35\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$; their $[\ce{HCO3-}]$ is essentially unchanged, and the change in pH comes from the rise of $P_{\ce{CO2}}$ with temperature (the ratio of ventilation to metabolism falls). This is not a failure of regulation but the regulation of a different quantity.

![Blood pH and body temperature: alpha-stat. A: the pH of neutral water (pN = pK_w/2, calculated from the temperature dependence of K_w) falls with rising temperature; the blood pH of poikilotherms varies along a line parallel to pN and about 0.6 units above it, with a slope of about −0.018 per °C, keeping [OH⁻]/[H⁺] at about 16; the pH 7.40 of mammals at 37 °C (blue dot) also lies on this line. B: the fraction deprotonated, α, of the histidine imidazole group (pK 6.8 at 37 °C, enthalpy of dissociation 30 kJ/mol): when pH varies along the line in A (alpha-stat), α is almost constant from 0 to 40 °C; if pH is held at 7.40 at all temperatures (pH-stat), α is markedly lower at low temperature and the charge state of proteins changes accordingly](./plots/alpha-stat.py)

**Definition 25.7.1 (Alpha-Stat)** The mode of acid–base regulation in which, when body temperature changes, blood pH is made to vary in parallel with the neutral point of water (about $-0.017$ per ${}^\circ\mathrm{C}$) and thereby the degree of dissociation $\alpha$ of the protein histidine imidazole groups (and the net charge of proteins) is kept constant, is called **alpha-stat** regulation; in contrast, the mode that maintains the same pH at all temperatures is called pH-stat.

The temperature dependence of the $\mathrm{p}K$ of imidazole is almost the same as that of $\mathrm{p}K_{\mathrm{w}}/2$ of water, and imidazole is the main buffering group inside cells and in plasma and also the active center of many enzymes; keeping $\alpha$ constant keeps the charge state and function of enzymes, so that metabolism can operate at different body temperatures. The pH of $7.40$ of mammals at $37\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$ also fits this rule, so **endotherms** and **ectotherms** evidently use the same setting for "relative alkalinity" ($[\ce{OH-}]/[\ce{H+}]$). Hibernating mammals, by contrast, use pH-stat when body temperature falls (a respiratory acidosis relative to alpha-stat), which is thought to help depress metabolism. In cardiac surgery with hypothermic cardiopulmonary bypass, which mode of managing the patient's blood gases (alpha-stat or pH-stat) is best remains a question of clinical research, and alpha-stat is mostly used in adults.

### 25.7.2. Water Breathers and Air Breathers

Breathing in water and breathing in air differ sharply in the way acid–base balance is regulated. The solubility of $\ce{O2}$ in water is very low, whereas that of $\ce{CO2}$ is about 30 times that of $\ce{O2}$; to obtain enough $\ce{O2}$, fish pass large volumes of water over the gills, and as a result $\ce{CO2}$ is washed out very thoroughly: arterial $P_{\ce{CO2}}$ is only about $2$–$4\,\mathrm{mmHg}$ and plasma $[\ce{HCO3-}]$ only about $5$–$10\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$. This has two consequences.

- Fish cannot use ventilation to regulate pH: ventilation is controlled by the need for oxygen supply, and $P_{\ce{CO2}}$ is already close to zero, so further increases in ventilation can hardly lower it.
- Fish regulate acid–base status mainly by ion exchange at the gills: the ionocytes of the gill secrete $\ce{H+}$ through $\ce{Na+}/\ce{H+}$ exchange (or a combination of $\ce{H+}$-ATPase and $\ce{Na+}$ channels) and $\ce{HCO3-}$ through $\ce{Cl-}/\ce{HCO3-}$ exchange, and the excretion of $\ce{NH4+}$ also carries away acid (Chapter 21); the contribution of the kidney is usually less than $10\%$. When environmental $\ce{CO2}$ is raised (hypercapnia), fish within one to three days reduce the secretion of $\ce{HCO3-}$ and increase the excretion of $\ce{H+}$, raising plasma $\ce{HCO3-}$ above $30\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$ and restoring pH (at the cost of loss of $\ce{Cl-}$); some fish (such as the white sturgeon) even regulate pH preferentially inside cells.

After vertebrates moved onto land, ventilation of the lungs needed only to meet the abundant $\ce{O2}$ of air, ventilation fell greatly relative to metabolism, and $P_{\ce{CO2}}$ rose to about $40\,\mathrm{mmHg}$ (mammals) and plasma $[\ce{HCO3-}]$ correspondingly to about $24$–$30\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$, which maintained the same pH. This made ventilation, for the first time, a powerful means of regulating pH, and once gill ion exchange was lost the kidney became the main organ excreting fixed acid. Amphibians lie between the two: tadpoles use gills, and in adults most $\ce{CO2}$ is excreted through the skin, with $P_{\ce{CO2}}$ between that of fish and that of mammals (about $10$–$15\,\mathrm{mmHg}$). Birds undergo respiratory alkalosis during prolonged panting to lose heat, while laying hens produce large amounts of $\ce{H+}$ at night when the eggshell is calcified ($\ce{Ca^2+ + HCO3- -> CaCO3 + H+}$) and develop a metabolic acidosis that is compensated by respiration and the kidney.

### 25.7.3. A Systematic Comparison of Excretion and Osmoregulation

Chapters 21–25 discussed **osmoregulation**, nitrogenous wastes, filtration and transport in the nephron, urine concentration and acid–base balance in turn. Putting them together shows how different animal groups combine the same set of tools.

| Group | Body fluids and environment | Excretory organ and formation of primary urine | Urine compared with body fluids | Main nitrogenous waste | Extrarenal regulatory organ | Main route for acid–base regulation |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Marine invertebrates | Osmoconformers, isosmotic | Metanephridia, antennal glands; ultrafiltration | Isosmotic | Ammonia | Gills (ionic regulation) | Ion exchange at the gills |

| Birds | Land and sea | Metanephros, two types of nephron | Up to about 2–2.5 times | Uric acid | Salt glands, cloaca and gut | Lungs and kidney |

Several regularities run through it.

- There are only two ways to form primary fluid (ultrafiltration or secretion), after which a stretch of tubule alters the composition as required; hyperosmotic urine appears only in mammals and birds, which have the **countercurrent multiplication** structure, and the other groups make up for it with extrarenal organs (salt glands, rectal glands, rectal pads, cloaca).
- The form of the nitrogenous waste is related to the availability of water and to the mode of reproduction: ammonia is excreted in water, urea or uric acid on land, and the embryo in the amniotic egg excretes uric acid.
- The gill is the "lung plus kidney" of aquatic animals: it exchanges gases, regulates ions, excretes ammonia and regulates acid–base status at the same time; the move onto land separated these functions, with the lung responsible for $\ce{CO2}$ and the kidney for ions, nitrogen and fixed acid, which also redistributed the division of acid–base regulation between respiration and the kidney.
- The cellular mechanisms of all these organs are shared: the **sodium pump** or proton pumps build the gradients, cotransporters, channels and **aquaporins** use them, **tight junctions** determine paracellular permeability, and hormones (ADH-type peptides, corticosteroids, natriuretic peptides, prolactin and others) regulate the number and activity of transport proteins.

## Questions

1. Calculate $[\ce{H+}]$ for pH $7.10$ and $7.70$, and explain why, for the same deviation of $0.3$ in pH, the change in $[\ce{H+}]$ is much larger on the acidotic side.

   **Answer** At pH $7.10$, $[\ce{H+}] = 10^{-7.1} = 79\,\mathrm{nmol\,L^{-1}}$; at pH $7.70$, $20\,\mathrm{nmol\,L^{-1}}$. Compared with $40$, the former is an increase of $39$ and the latter a decrease of $20$. pH is a logarithmic scale, and equal changes in pH correspond to equal factors (about 2-fold), while the absolute change is larger when the factor increases. The acid and alkaline sides therefore look symmetric when expressed as pH and are not symmetric when expressed as $[\ce{H+}]$.
2. A patient with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease has an arterial $P_{\ce{CO2}} = 60\,\mathrm{mmHg}$ and $[\ce{HCO3-}] = 31\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$. Calculate the pH and decide the type of disturbance and whether the compensation is appropriate. If pneumonia raises his $P_{\ce{CO2}}$ to $80\,\mathrm{mmHg}$ within a day, what is the approximate pH?

   **Answer** $\mathrm{pH} = 6.1 + \lg(31/1.8) = 6.1 + 1.24 = 7.34$. $P_{\ce{CO2}}$ is raised by $20$, the chronic expectation is a rise of $[\ce{HCO3-}]$ of $7$ to $31$, which agrees with the measured value: chronic respiratory acidosis with appropriate compensation. With a further acute rise of $20\,\mathrm{mmHg}$, $[\ce{HCO3-}]$ rises acutely by only about $2$ to $33$: $\mathrm{pH} = 6.1 + \lg(33/2.4) = 6.1 + 1.14 = 7.24$. Chronic compensation cannot keep up with the acute change, so acute exacerbations in patients with chronic respiratory failure are often accompanied by marked acidemia.
3. Why is the titratable acid that the kidney can excrete limited, whereas the excretion of $\ce{NH4+}$ can increase greatly? Explain in terms of the $\mathrm{p}K$ and the origin of the buffers.

   **Answer** Titratable acid depends mainly on phosphate ($\mathrm{p}K$ 6.8), whose amount is limited by diet and by the amount of phosphate excreted, and when urine pH has fallen to about 5 it is already almost entirely protonated, so a further fall in pH cannot carry away more $\ce{H+}$. The $\mathrm{p}K$ of $\ce{NH3/NH4+}$ is about $9.2$, so at any urine pH it exists almost entirely as $\ce{NH4+}$, and each $\ce{NH3}$ can carry away one $\ce{H+}$; moreover $\ce{NH3}$ is produced by the kidney itself from glutamine, and in acidosis its production can be induced to increase several-fold. $\ce{NH4+}$ is therefore the "adjustable part" of renal acid excretion.
4. A patient with repeated vomiting has pH $7.52$, $P_{\ce{CO2}} = 48\,\mathrm{mmHg}$, $[\ce{HCO3-}] = 38\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$, blood $[\ce{K+}] = 2.9\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$ and urine pH $5.5$. Explain why his urine is nevertheless acidic ("paradoxical aciduria"), and why replacement with normal saline and KCl can correct the alkalosis.

   **Answer** Vomiting loses HCl from the stomach and generates $\ce{HCO3-}$ (each $\ce{H+}$ secreted by a parietal cell releases one $\ce{HCO3-}$ into the blood), causing metabolic alkalosis; the rise of $P_{\ce{CO2}}$ of $8$ agrees with the expectation ($0.7 \times 14 \approx 10$). Normally the kidney would excrete the excess $\ce{HCO3-}$, but vomiting also causes volume depletion and hypokalemia: volume depletion activates the RAAS, and angiotensin II and aldosterone promote $\ce{Na+}$ reabsorption and $\ce{H+}$ secretion; a deficit of $\ce{Cl-}$ means that $\ce{Na+}$ can be reabsorbed only together with $\ce{HCO3-}$; hypokalemia acidifies the tubular cells and further promotes $\ce{H+}$ secretion. The kidney therefore goes on reabsorbing all the $\ce{HCO3-}$ and excretes acidic urine, and the alkalosis is maintained. Replacing NaCl and KCl corrects the volume, $\ce{Cl-}$ and $\ce{K+}$ deficits and removes these "maintenance factors", and the kidney then excretes the excess $\ce{HCO3-}$.
5. If a fish is moved from water at $10\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$ to water at $25\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$, how should its blood pH change according to alpha-stat? How can it bring this change about?

   **Answer** At $-0.017$ per ${}^\circ\mathrm{C}$, pH should fall by about $0.26$ (for example from $7.95$ to about $7.70$). As to how: air-breathing poikilotherms mainly adjust the ratio of ventilation to metabolism so that $P_{\ce{CO2}}$ rises with temperature; in fish ventilation is controlled by oxygen supply, $P_{\ce{CO2}}$ is already low and there is little room for adjustment, and they mainly lower plasma $[\ce{HCO3-}]$ by gill ion exchange (excreting $\ce{HCO3-}$ or reducing the excretion of $\ce{H+}$), which takes some hours. In many fish the measured slope is smaller than $-0.017$, showing that alpha-stat is only an approximate rule.
6. From the standpoint of acid–base regulation and osmoregulation, explain why the gill is said to be the "lung plus kidney" of aquatic animals, and how these functions were redistributed when vertebrates moved onto land.

   **Answer** The gill performs at once gas exchange ($\ce{O2}$ and $\ce{CO2}$), ionic regulation (marine fish excrete NaCl, freshwater fish take up NaCl), excretion of nitrogenous waste ($\ce{NH3}$ diffuses out and is trapped as $\ce{NH4+}$) and acid–base regulation ($\ce{H+}/\ce{Na+}$ and $\ce{HCO3-}/\ce{Cl-}$ exchange), and in land vertebrates these are carried out by the lung and the kidney separately. After the move onto land, the abundant $\ce{O2}$ of air greatly reduced ventilation, $P_{\ce{CO2}}$ and $[\ce{HCO3-}]$ rose, ventilation became a means of regulating pH and the lung handled volatile acid; with the loss of ion exchange and ammonia diffusion in the aquatic environment, the kidney took over ionic regulation, the excretion of fixed acid and the excretion of nitrogen as urea or uric acid, and in mammals and birds developed the ability to concentrate urine to conserve water.
