The previous chapter showed that the renal tubules reabsorb more than $99\%$ of the filtered water and $\ce{Na+}$; but "how much is reabsorbed" must itself change with the needs of the body. When deprived of water we excrete a small volume of concentrated urine, and after drinking a lot we excrete a large volume of dilute urine, while the excretion of $\ce{Na+}$ and that of water can be regulated separately. This allows the body to use two almost independent control systems to hold the osmolality of the extracellular fluid (determined by water) and its volume (determined by $\ce{Na+}$, Chapter 21). We begin with water in this chapter: how urine osmolality varies (Section 24.1), how dilute and concentrated urine are formed (Section 24.2), how medullary hypertonicity is built by countercurrent multiplication (Section 24.3), and the antidiuretic hormone (Section 24.4) and thirst (Section 24.5) that control it all. We then turn to $\ce{Na+}$: how the extracellular fluid volume is regulated by the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system and several other pathways, and how the kidney determines long-term blood pressure through pressure natriuresis (Section 24.6). Finally we compare the ability of different animals to concentrate urine (Section 24.7). Short-term neural regulation of blood pressure is treated in [Chapter 16](/7.14.16), and the regulation of adrenocortical hormone secretion in Chapter 35.

## 24.1. Urine Osmolality and Water Balance

### 24.1.1. Dilute and Concentrated Urine

The osmolality of urine can vary between about $50$ and $1200\,\mathrm{mOsm\,L^{-1}}$, that is, from $1/6$ to $4$ times that of plasma (about $300$), while the excretion of the other urinary solutes changes little. When the body has excess water, the secretion of antidiuretic hormone (ADH) falls, water reabsorption in the distal tubule and collecting duct decreases, and dilute urine is excreted; when water is short, ADH rises, water reabsorption increases and concentrated urine is excreted.

### 24.1.2. The Urinary Response to Drinking

After $1\,\mathrm{L}$ of water is drunk, urine flow begins to increase after about $30$–$45\,\mathrm{min}$ and peaks at about $1\,\mathrm{h}$: urine flow rises from about $1$ to about $6\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}}$ and urine osmolality falls from about $600$ to about $100\,\mathrm{mOsm\,L^{-1}}$, while the excretion of urinary solutes rises by only about $10\%$; after about $2$–$3\,\mathrm{h}$ the excess water has been excreted and the urine returns to its former state. The fall in plasma osmolality is very small ($1\,\mathrm{L}$ of water distributed in about $42\,\mathrm{L}$ of body fluid lowers osmolality by only about $2\%$–$3\%$), but this is enough to suppress ADH secretion almost completely.

**Definition 24.1.1 (Water Diuresis)** The marked increase in urine volume caused, after the drinking of a large amount of plain water, by a lower plasma osmolality and a reduced secretion of antidiuretic hormone is called **water diuresis**.

Drinking the same volume of isotonic saline does not cause water diuresis: saline does not change osmolality and only increases the extracellular fluid volume, and its excretion is much slower, relying on the volume regulation of Section 24.6.

### 24.1.3. Free-Water Clearance

Urine can be regarded as the sum of two parts: one is urine isosmotic with plasma that contains all the urinary solutes (its volume being the osmolar clearance $C_{\mathrm{osm}}$), and the other is solute-free "free water".

**Definition 24.1.2 (Free-Water Clearance)** The difference between urine flow and osmolar clearance,

$$
C_{\ce{H2O}} = \dot V - C_{\mathrm{osm}} = \dot V\left(1 - \frac{U_{\mathrm{osm}}}{P_{\mathrm{osm}}}\right)
$$
is called the **free-water clearance**. $C_{\ce{H2O}} > 0$ means that the kidney is excreting solute-free water (hypotonic urine), and $C_{\ce{H2O}} < 0$ means that the kidney is retaining water (hypertonic urine).

**Example 24.1.1** In water diuresis, $\dot V = 6\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}}$, $U_{\mathrm{osm}} = 100$ and $P_{\mathrm{osm}} = 290\,\mathrm{mOsm\,L^{-1}}$: $C_{\mathrm{osm}} = 100 \times 6/290 = 2.1\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}}$ and $C_{\ce{H2O}} = +3.9\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}}$, so the kidney excretes about $4\,\mathrm{mL}$ of pure water per minute. In water deprivation, $\dot V = 0.5\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}}$ and $U_{\mathrm{osm}} = 1200$: $C_{\mathrm{osm}} = 2.1\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}}$ and $C_{\ce{H2O}} = -1.6\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}}$, so the kidney "saves" about $1.6\,\mathrm{mL}$ of water per minute for the body.

## 24.2. Formation of Dilute and Concentrated Urine

The two kinds of urine have the same history in the proximal tubule and the loop of Henle and differ only in the late distal convoluted tubule and the collecting duct.

| Segment | Water | Solutes | Osmolality of tubular fluid ($\mathrm{mOsm\,L^{-1}}$) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Proximal tubule | Highly permeable | NaCl and others reabsorbed actively | Isosmotic, about $300$ |
| Thin descending limb | Highly permeable (AQP1) | Almost impermeable | Water is drawn out by the hypertonic medulla and osmolality rises to about $1200$ at the tip of the loop (in water deprivation) |
| Ascending limb (thin and thick) | Impermeable | NaCl reabsorbed passively in the thin and actively in the thick limb | Falls to about $100$–$150$ |
| Distal convoluted tubule | Impermeable | NaCl reabsorption | Falls further |
| Collecting duct, without ADH | Impermeable | Solute reabsorption continues | Falls to about $50$: dilute urine |
| Collecting duct, with ADH | Permeable (AQP2) | Water is drawn out by the hypertonic medulla | First returns to about $300$ in the cortex, then rises in the medulla to about $1200$, the same as the medullary interstitium: concentrated urine |

The tubular fluid leaving the thick ascending limb is therefore always hypotonic, and dilute urine simply lets this hypotonic fluid be diluted further and excreted. Producing concentrated urine requires two conditions: (1) a hypertonic medullary interstitium, which provides the osmotic gradient that draws water out of the collecting duct; and (2) water permeability of the collecting duct, which is controlled by ADH. We discuss them in turn.

## 24.3. Medullary Hypertonicity

### 24.3.1. Countercurrent Multiplication

The osmolality of the renal cortical interstitium equals that of plasma (about $300\,\mathrm{mOsm\,kg^{-1}}$) and rises progressively from the outer to the inner medulla, reaching about $1200\,\mathrm{mOsm\,kg^{-1}}$ at the human renal papilla. This gradient is built by the hairpin structure of the loop of Henle.

**Definition 24.3.1 (Countercurrent Multiplication)** In two adjacent tubes with flows in opposite directions, one tube transports solute actively into the other (or into the surrounding interstitium) and establishes only a small transverse concentration difference at each level (the single effect), but because the flows are in opposite directions these single effects add up along the tubes into a large longitudinal concentration gradient; this mechanism is called **countercurrent multiplication**.

In the loop of Henle, the thick ascending limb transports NaCl actively into the interstitium and is itself impermeable to water, so that at each level the interstitium is about $200\,\mathrm{mOsm\,kg^{-1}}$ more concentrated than the ascending-limb fluid (the single effect); the descending limb is permeable to water and equilibrates with the interstitium at each level. As the tubular fluid flows downward it carries the higher osmolality of the descending limb to greater depth, and the ascending limb pumps out solute on a still higher base; the two steps alternate and the gradient is "multiplied" again and again. The classic stepwise model divides the loop into several levels and alternates a "pump" step and a "flow" step:

![Stepwise model of countercurrent multiplication in the loop of Henle (schematic). The loop is divided into 10 levels: in the pump step the ascending limb at each level becomes lower than the descending limb and the interstitium by a single effect D = 200 mOsm/kg (the mean of the two is unchanged); in the flow step the fluid in the descending limb moves down one level (fresh fluid of 300 mOsm/kg enters at the top) and at the tip passes into the ascending limb and moves up one level. In each cycle the vasa recta remove 3% of the solute above 300 at each level, so that the profile reaches a steady state. A: the osmolality profiles of the interstitium (solid line, the same as the descending limb) and the ascending limb (dashed line) build up with the number of cycles; at steady state the tip of the loop is at about 1200 mOsm/kg and the fluid leaving the ascending limb is hypotonic (about 150). B: the steady-state osmolality at the tip increases approximately linearly with the single effect, and is higher the longer the loop (the more levels): the transverse difference of 200 mOsm/kg is multiplied into a longitudinal difference of about 1000 mOsm/kg](./plots/countercurrent-multiplier.py)

Several conclusions of the model agree with observation.

- The size of the gradient depends on the single effect (the pump of the thick ascending limb) and the length of the loop, so loop diuretics that inhibit NKCC2 abolish the medullary gradient and the urine can only approach isosmolality; the juxtamedullary nephrons with long loops and a thicker medulla are the structural basis of strong concentrating ability (Section 24.7).
- The fluid leaving the ascending limb is always hypotonic, even in water deprivation.
- Continuous energy supply is required to maintain it: the gradient is an energy-consuming steady state, and as soon as the pumps stop it is washed away by blood flow within tens of minutes.

This model applies only to the outer medulla: the thick ascending limb exists only in the outer medulla, and the inner medulla has only thin ascending limbs that cannot transport actively, so how the osmotic gradient of the inner medulla is established has not been fully resolved. The passive model proposed by Kokko and Rector (1972) and by Stephenson holds that urea reabsorbed by the inner medullary collecting duct accumulates in the inner medullary interstitium and draws water out of the thin descending limb, raising the NaCl concentration in the descending limb; on reaching the thin ascending limb, which is permeable to NaCl but not very permeable to urea, NaCl then diffuses out passively down its concentration gradient. However, the measured permeabilities of the thin limbs do not fully support this model, and other possible mechanisms (such as contraction of the renal pelvic wall squeezing the medulla, and the production of solutes such as lactate) are still under study.

### 24.3.2. Urea Recycling

About half of the hypertonicity of the inner medullary interstitium comes from urea. The route of urea in the kidney is as follows: about half is reabsorbed in the proximal tubule; the distal convoluted tubule and the cortical and outer medullary collecting ducts are impermeable to urea, so urea is concentrated in the tubular fluid as water is reabsorbed; in the inner medullary collecting duct, ADH increases the number and activity of the urea transporters UT-A1 and UT-A3, and urea enters the inner medullary interstitium down its concentration gradient; part of it then enters the thin limb of the loop through UT-A2 and flows with the tubular fluid back to the collecting duct, forming a recycling loop. UT-B in the endothelium of the vasa recta lets urea exchange between the ascending and descending vessels and remain in the medulla.

The role of urea is to allow the collecting duct to produce highly concentrated urine without consuming large amounts of extra water: because the inner medullary interstitium already contains a high concentration of urea, the urea in the tubular fluid does not osmotically "hold on" to water, and water can be drawn out by the NaCl gradient of the inner medulla. A low-protein diet reduces urea production and concentrating ability falls; a high-protein diet strengthens it.

### 24.3.3. Vasa Recta

The medulla needs a blood supply for oxygen and nutrients, but ordinary capillaries would quickly wash away the solutes of the medulla. The vasa recta are arranged in parallel with the loops of Henle as U-shaped vessels. As the blood in the descending vessels flows toward the depths, solute enters and water leaves, and the osmolality rises together with that of the interstitium; on the return flow in the ascending vessels, solute leaves and water enters, and the osmolality falls back to near $300$. This passive exchange between the two limbs is **countercurrent exchange** (by the same principle as countercurrent exchange in fish gills, [Chapter 17](/7.14.17)), and it means that blood passing through the medulla carries away very little solute. The vasa recta have two further features: the blood flow is small (less than $1\%$–$2\%$ of renal blood flow), and the ascending vessels carry away somewhat more water than the descending vessels (equivalent to removing the water reabsorbed from the loops and collecting ducts). The vasa recta therefore preserve the gradient but do not build it; an increase in medullary blood flow (as in vasodilation or **osmotic diuresis**) washes out medullary solutes and weakens concentrating ability.

### 24.3.4. Minimal Urine Volume

A human produces about $600\,\mathrm{mOsm}$ of solute per day that must be excreted (urea, NaCl, $\ce{K+}$ salts, phosphate and others, varying with diet). The maximal urine osmolality is limited, so the minimal urine volume is

$$
\dot V_{\min} = \frac{\text{daily solute excretion}}{\text{maximal urine osmolality}} = \frac{600\,\mathrm{mOsm\,d^{-1}}}{1200\,\mathrm{mOsm\,L^{-1}}} = 0.5\,\mathrm{L\,d^{-1}}.
$$

This is the urine volume a human must lose each day (the obligatory urine volume); adding **insensible water loss** (about $0.7\,\mathrm{L\,d^{-1}}$, Chapter 21), a person who drinks no water at all loses at least about $1.2\,\mathrm{L}$ of water a day. For the same solute load, the beaver (maximal urine concentration about $500\,\mathrm{mOsm\,kg^{-1}}$) needs $1.2\,\mathrm{L}$ of urine, whereas the Australian hopping mouse (*Notomys alexis*, about $9400\,\mathrm{mOsm\,kg^{-1}}$) needs less than $0.07\,\mathrm{L}$.

**Example 24.3.1 (Can Seawater Quench Thirst?)** The osmolality of seawater is about $1000\,\mathrm{mOsm\,kg^{-1}}$ (Chapter 21), most of it from NaCl, so it can be approximated as a NaCl solution of about $1000\,\mathrm{mOsm\,L^{-1}}$. The maximal osmolality of human urine is about $1200\,\mathrm{mOsm\,L^{-1}}$, but in water deprivation about half of it is urea, so NaCl can account for at most about $600\,\mathrm{mOsm\,L^{-1}}$. To excrete the roughly $1000\,\mathrm{mOsm}$ of NaCl in $1\,\mathrm{L}$ of seawater requires about $1000/600 \approx 1.7\,\mathrm{L}$ of urine, $0.7\,\mathrm{L}$ more than was drunk. Drinking seawater therefore dehydrates a person faster. Seabirds excrete salt through salt glands (Chapter 21) and can drink seawater.

## 24.4. Antidiuretic Hormone

### 24.4.1. Synthesis and Release

**Definition 24.4.1 (Antidiuretic Hormone)** The nonapeptide hormone that is synthesized by the magnocellular neurons of the supraoptic and paraventricular nuclei of the hypothalamus, carried along the hypothalamo-hypophyseal tract to the neurohypophysis, where it is stored and released into the blood, and that increases the reabsorption of water by the renal collecting duct is called **antidiuretic hormone** (ADH); in humans residue 8 of ADH is arginine, and the hormone is also called arginine vasopressin (AVP).

ADH is synthesized as a larger precursor and transported in the axons together with the carrier protein (neurophysin II) and copeptin. When the action potentials of the neurons reach the terminals in the neurohypophysis, ADH is released into the blood by **exocytosis**. Its plasma half-life is about $10$–$20\,\mathrm{min}$, so the plasma level can change rapidly with secretion. This is a typical example of **neurosecretion**, in which hormone secretion is directly controlled by the firing frequency of neurons (Chapter 34).

### 24.4.2. Regulation of Secretion

**Definition 24.4.2 (Osmoreceptor)** A receptor that senses changes in the osmolality of the extracellular fluid is called an **osmoreceptor**.

The osmoreceptors that regulate ADH secretion are located in the organum vasculosum of the lamina terminalis and the subfornical organ of the anterior hypothalamus; these two circumventricular organs lack a **blood–brain barrier** and can directly "see" the plasma osmolality, and the magnocellular neurons of the supraoptic nucleus can also sense osmolality themselves. When the osmolality of the extracellular fluid rises, these cells lose water and shrink, mechanosensitive cation channels (an N-terminally truncated variant of TRPV1) open, and the cells depolarize and fire more. ADH secretion is extremely sensitive to osmolality.

- The threshold is about $280\,\mathrm{mOsm\,kg^{-1}}$, below which ADH is almost undetectable.
- Above the threshold, each rise of plasma osmolality by $1\,\mathrm{mOsm\,kg^{-1}}$ (about $0.35\%$) increases plasma ADH by about $0.4\,\mathrm{pg\,mL^{-1}}$.
- At about $5\,\mathrm{pg\,mL^{-1}}$ the urine is close to maximally concentrated, corresponding to a plasma osmolality only about $10$–$15\,\mathrm{mOsm\,kg^{-1}}$ above the threshold.

A change of $1\%$ in plasma osmolality can cause a marked change in urine osmolality, so this is a **negative feedback** of high gain: water deficit → rise in extracellular osmolality → ADH secretion → rise in plasma ADH → increased water permeability of the collecting duct → increased water reabsorption and reduced excretion → osmolality falls again.

Changes in blood volume and blood pressure also affect ADH: volume receptors (low-pressure receptors) in the atria and the great thoracic veins and baroreceptors in the carotid sinus and aortic arch send afferent signals through the vagus and glossopharyngeal nerves to the nucleus of the solitary tract, which projects to the supraoptic and paraventricular nuclei. An increase in blood volume and atrial stretch inhibits ADH, and a fall in blood volume or blood pressure promotes it. This pathway is far less sensitive than osmotic regulation, and blood volume must fall by about $5\%$–$10\%$ before it acts; with larger falls, however, ADH rises exponentially, to several times the level that osmotic stimulation can cause, and the vasoconstrictor action of ADH (the $\mathrm{V_1}$ receptor, which gives "vasopressin" its name) then also helps to maintain blood pressure. Reduced blood volume also shifts the threshold of osmotic regulation downward and increases its slope: in severe hemorrhage the body prefers to let osmolality fall in order to preserve volume. Other factors: pain, nausea, stress and angiotensin II promote ADH secretion, and ethanol and atrial natriuretic peptide inhibit it (one of the reasons for the large urine volume after drinking alcohol).

### 24.4.3. Mechanism of Action

The process by which ADH increases the water permeability of the principal cells of the collecting duct has five steps.

1. ADH binds to the $\mathrm{V_2}$ receptor in the basolateral membrane of the principal cell (a **G protein-coupled receptor**).
2. Through $\mathrm{G_s}$ it activates **adenylyl cyclase**, **cyclic AMP** increases, and **protein kinase A** is activated.
3. Protein kinase A phosphorylates AQP2 in intracellular vesicles containing the aquaporin AQP2; the vesicles move along microtubules to the apical membrane and fuse with it, and AQP2 is inserted into the apical membrane (within a few minutes).
4. Water enters the cell through AQP2 in the apical membrane.
5. Water leaves the cell through the constitutively expressed AQP3 and AQP4 in the basolateral membrane, enters the medullary interstitium, and is carried away by the vasa recta.

When ADH decreases, AQP2 is endocytosed back into intracellular vesicles and the apical membrane again becomes impermeable to water. Long-term ADH stimulation also increases the transcription of the AQP2 gene through cAMP–CREB (over hours to days). In addition, ADH increases the urea transporters UT-A1/A3 of the inner medullary collecting duct (Section 24.3.2) and promotes NKCC2 in the thick ascending limb and ENaC in the collecting duct; all these increase medullary solute and raise concentrating ability.

Diseases of the ADH system illustrate its action. When ADH secretion is insufficient (central diabetes insipidus) or the kidney does not respond to ADH (nephrogenic diabetes insipidus, mostly due to mutations of the $\mathrm{V_2}$ receptor or AQP2), $10$–$20\,\mathrm{L}$ of dilute urine can be excreted per day, balanced by large water intake; when ADH secretion is inappropriately increased (as in ectopic secretion by lung cancer), water is retained, plasma osmolality and $[\ce{Na+}]$ fall, and hyponatremia results, while the extracellular fluid volume increases only slightly (volume regulation causes the excess $\ce{Na+}$ and water to be excreted).

## 24.5. Osmolality and $\ce{Na+}$

### 24.5.1. Estimating Osmolality from $\ce{Na+}$

The $[\ce{Na+}]$ of the extracellular fluid is about $142\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$ and normally fluctuates by less than $3\%$. $\ce{Na+}$ and its accompanying anions (mainly $\ce{Cl-}$ and $\ce{HCO3-}$) account for about $94\%$ of the osmolality of the extracellular fluid, so that

$$
P_{\mathrm{osm}} \approx 2\,[\ce{Na+}] = 2 \times 142 = 284\,\mathrm{mOsm\,L^{-1}};
$$

clinically one adds the concentrations of glucose and urea ($\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$). The plasma osmolality obtained by adding the solute concentrations is about $300\,\mathrm{mOsm\,L^{-1}}$, but interionic attraction makes the actual osmotic activity of the solution about $7\%$ lower than the ideal value (osmotic coefficient about $0.93$); after correction it is about $282\,\mathrm{mOsm\,L^{-1}}$, in agreement with the value measured by freezing-point depression.

Since $[\ce{Na+}]$ almost determines the osmolality of the extracellular fluid, and osmolality is determined by the water balance, plasma $[\ce{Na+}]$ reflects the balance of water rather than of $\ce{Na+}$: hyponatremia usually means a relative excess of water (as in inappropriate ADH secretion) and hypernatremia usually means a deficit of water, rather than how much total $\ce{Na+}$ there is in the body. The direction of $\ce{Na+}$ movement determines where the water goes, and the total amount of $\ce{Na+}$ determines the volume of the extracellular fluid (Section 24.6).

### 24.5.2. Thirst

ADH can only reduce water loss; replenishing water depends on drinking. Thirst is caused by the same two kinds of signals as ADH: a rise in plasma osmolality (the osmoreceptors of the organum vasculosum of the lamina terminalis, with a threshold of about $290$–$295\,\mathrm{mOsm\,kg^{-1}}$, slightly higher than that of ADH) and a reduction in blood volume (through baroreceptors, and through angiotensin II acting on the subfornical organ). Ordinarily, therefore, ADH does the fine regulation, and thirst arises only when ADH is not enough to correct the disturbance.

The satisfaction of thirst has the character of a **feedforward control**: within a few minutes after drinking, long before the water has been absorbed and has lowered plasma osmolality, the sensation of thirst disappears and ADH begins to fall. Sensory signals from the oropharynx and the digestive tract "meter" the water drunk in advance, which prevents overdrinking before the water is absorbed. In $\ce{Na+}$ deficiency, animals also show a specific craving for salt, which is caused by angiotensin II and aldosterone acting together on the brain.

## 24.6. $\ce{Na+}$ Balance and Extracellular Fluid Volume

### 24.6.1. Volume Is Determined by Total $\ce{Na+}$

Because osmolality is held precisely constant by ADH and thirst, each additional $140\,\mathrm{mmol}$ of $\ce{Na+}$ in the **extracellular fluid** retains about $1\,\mathrm{L}$ more water; extracellular fluid volume and blood volume are therefore determined by the total amount of $\ce{Na+}$ in the body. Regulating $\ce{Na+}$ balance means regulating extracellular fluid volume and hence blood pressure. $\ce{Na+}$ intake varies greatly with diet, loss in sweat and feces is usually small, and excretion is almost entirely determined by the kidney: about $25\,600\,\mathrm{mmol}$ is filtered each day and only a small adjustable part of the filtered load is excreted (Chapter 23).

The body has no receptor that measures total $\ce{Na+}$ directly, but senses the related "effective circulating volume": the low-pressure receptors of the atria and great veins, the high-pressure receptors of the carotid sinus and aortic arch ([Chapter 16](/7.14.16)), and the kidney itself (the perfusion pressure of the afferent arteriole and the NaCl at the macula densa). At least four pathways regulate the volume of the circulating fluid: the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system, the sympathetic nerves, antidiuretic hormone and atrial natriuretic peptide; the first three conserve $\ce{Na+}$ when volume is reduced, and only atrial natriuretic peptide promotes $\ce{Na+}$ excretion when volume is increased.

### 24.6.2. The Renin–Angiotensin–Aldosterone System

**Definition 24.6.1 (Renin–Angiotensin–Aldosterone System)** The hormone system that is initiated by the protease renin secreted by juxtaglomerular cells, generates active peptides in a cascade from angiotensinogen → angiotensin I → angiotensin II, and in which angiotensin II promotes the secretion of aldosterone by the adrenal cortex, so that together they increase the retention of $\ce{Na+}$ and water and constrict blood vessels, is called the **renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system** (RAAS).

The RAAS begins and ends in the kidney.

- Input: renin secretion is promoted by three signals: (1) a fall in the perfusion pressure of the afferent arteriole (the juxtaglomerular cells themselves are baroreceptors); (2) stimulation by the **sympathetic nervous system** (through $\beta_1$ receptors, for example in stress, standing and hemorrhage); (3) a decrease in the NaCl flowing past the macula densa (through prostaglandin $\mathrm{E_2}$ and other mediators generated by the macula densa). Angiotensin II inhibits renin secretion by feedback.
- Cascade: angiotensinogen synthesized in the liver (452 residues in the mature protein) is cleaved in plasma by renin to release the N-terminal decapeptide angiotensin I; angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE, mainly on the endothelium of the pulmonary vessels) then removes two residues to produce the octapeptide angiotensin II. Renin secretion is the rate-limiting step of this cascade. ACE also degrades bradykinin, which is why ACE inhibitors cause a dry cough; ACE2 converts angiotensin II into the vasodilator angiotensin (1–7) and acts as a counterweight.
- Output: angiotensin II acts through the $\mathrm{AT_1}$ receptor on several targets: (1) it constricts blood vessels, and in the kidney preferentially constricts the efferent arteriole, which maintains GFR and lowers the hydrostatic pressure of the peritubular capillaries, promoting reabsorption (Chapter 22); (2) it directly stimulates NHE3 of the proximal tubule and increases $\ce{Na+}$ reabsorption; (3) it stimulates the secretion of aldosterone by the zona glomerulosa of the adrenal cortex; (4) it acts on the brain to promote ADH secretion, thirst and salt craving, and to enhance sympathetic activity.

**Definition 24.6.2 (Aldosterone)** The steroid hormone secreted by the zona glomerulosa of the adrenal cortex that acts on the mineralocorticoid receptor of the principal cells of the distal nephron and collecting duct and promotes the reabsorption of $\ce{Na+}$ and the secretion of $\ce{K+}$ and $\ce{H+}$ is called **aldosterone**.

Aldosterone secretion is stimulated mainly by angiotensin II and by a rise in plasma $[\ce{K+}]$ (Chapter 23). Aldosterone enters the principal cell, binds to the intracellular mineralocorticoid receptor and, acting as a transcription factor, induces proteins such as SGK1, which increase the number and opening of ENaC and the number of sodium–potassium pumps, so there is a latent period of about $1\,\mathrm{h}$. The plasma concentration of **cortisol** is about 1000 times higher than that of aldosterone and its affinity for the mineralocorticoid receptor is the same; the principal cell converts cortisol into inactive cortisone by 11β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase 2, protecting the receptor so that it responds only to aldosterone; eating large amounts of licorice (which inhibits this enzyme) can cause hypertension and hypokalemia.

The action of the RAAS can be seen in an example: with salt restriction, renin and aldosterone rise several-fold within a few days and urinary $\ce{Na+}$ falls to near zero; with a high salt intake they are suppressed and urinary $\ce{Na+}$ rises to equal intake. Between these two extremes the RAAS allows the intake of $\ce{Na+}$ to vary about 6-fold while extracellular fluid volume and blood pressure change only slightly.

### 24.6.3. Sympathetic Nerves and Antidiuretic Hormone

Norepinephrine released from sympathetic nerve endings lowers renal blood flow and GFR, thereby reducing the filtration and excretion of $\ce{Na+}$; directly activates NHE3 and the sodium–potassium pump of the proximal tubule, promoting $\ce{Na+}$ reabsorption; and promotes renin secretion. Besides promoting water reabsorption, antidiuretic hormone also promotes $\ce{Na+}$ reabsorption (NKCC2 of the thick ascending limb, ENaC of the collecting duct). Both increase when blood volume is reduced.

### 24.6.4. Atrial Natriuretic Peptide

**Definition 24.6.3 (Atrial Natriuretic Peptide)** The 28-residue hormone that is secreted by atrial myocytes when they are stretched and that promotes renal excretion of $\ce{Na+}$ and water and dilates blood vessels is called **atrial natriuretic peptide** (ANP).

ANP binds to natriuretic peptide receptor A on the membrane of target cells, which is itself a **guanylyl cyclase** and produces cGMP. Its main actions are to dilate the afferent arteriole and constrict the efferent arteriole, increasing renal blood flow and GFR; to inhibit $\ce{Na+}$ reabsorption in the collecting duct; and to inhibit the secretion of renin, aldosterone and ADH. Among the four pathways that regulate the volume of the circulating fluid, ANP is the only one that promotes $\ce{Na+}$ excretion. The ventricles, when stretched, secrete the structurally similar brain natriuretic peptide (BNP); plasma BNP is markedly raised in patients with heart failure and is an index for the diagnosis and evaluation of heart failure; in heart failure, however, the natriuretic action of the natriuretic peptides is not enough to offset the sodium-retaining action of the RAAS and the sympathetic nerves.

### 24.6.5. Other Regulatory Factors

There are three further classes of humoral factors in the kidney and plasma that regulate $\ce{Na+}$ reabsorption.

- Endogenous inhibitors of the sodium–potassium pump: steroids similar to ouabain or bufadienolides are present in plasma; their concentration rises when body $\ce{Na+}$ increases, and they inhibit the **sodium pump** of the renal tubules and promote $\ce{Na+}$ excretion; but they also inhibit the pump of vascular smooth muscle and raise blood pressure, and their physiological role and origin remain controversial.
- **Prostaglandins** and bradykinin: synthesized in the kidney, they dilate renal vessels and inhibit NKCC2 of the thick ascending limb and ENaC of the collecting duct, reducing $\ce{Na+}$ reabsorption.
- Dopamine: proximal tubule cells convert filtered L-DOPA into dopamine, which acts as a **paracrine** signal; its production increases with high $\ce{Na+}$ intake and decreases with low intake; through $\mathrm{D_1}$-like receptors it inhibits NHE3 and the sodium–potassium pump, reducing $\ce{Na+}$ reabsorption, and dilates renal vessels, promoting $\ce{Na+}$ excretion. About half of the increase in proximal $\ce{Na+}$ excretion on a high-salt diet can be attributed to dopamine.

### 24.6.6. Pressure Natriuresis and Long-Term Blood Pressure Regulation

**Definition 24.6.4 (Pressure Natriuresis)** The increase in renal excretion of $\ce{Na+}$ and water when arterial pressure rises is called **pressure natriuresis** (also called pressure diuresis).

Pressure natriuresis has a component independent of nerves and hormones: when blood pressure rises, despite **autoregulation**, medullary blood flow (which is not subject to autoregulation) and renal interstitial hydrostatic pressure still increase, apical NHE3 and NaPi of the proximal tubule are endocytosed, and $\ce{Na+}$ reabsorption decreases. In the whole body, a rise in blood pressure also suppresses the RAAS and the sympathetic nerves and promotes the natriuretic peptides, which further amplifies the diuresis.

From this Guyton proposed that long-term arterial pressure is determined by renal pressure natriuresis. Let the extracellular fluid volume be $V$, the intake rate of $\ce{Na+}$ (and water) be $I$, and the renal excretion rate $E(P)$ be an increasing function of arterial pressure $P$ (the renal function curve), while $P$ increases with $V$:

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}V}{\mathrm{d}t} = I - E(P), \qquad P = P(V).
$$

As long as $E(P) \neq I$, $V$ changes and $P$ changes with it until $E(P^*) = I$. The long-term equilibrium pressure $P^*$ is therefore determined only by the renal function curve and the intake, and not by the heart, peripheral resistance or other factors: for example, an increase in peripheral resistance raises blood pressure temporarily, and pressure natriuresis excretes $\ce{Na+}$ and water until the pressure returns to $P^*$. This is a form of integral control ($V$ integrates the error $I - E$), so that the error is zero at steady state ("infinite gain"). In healthy people, because the RAAS and other mechanisms make the renal function curve very steep in the long term, an increase of $\ce{Na+}$ intake of several-fold raises **mean arterial pressure** by only a few $\mathrm{mmHg}$; if the renal function curve is shifted to the right (kidney disease, aldosterone excess) or flattened (salt-sensitive hypertension), the same intake requires a higher pressure to be excreted, and hypertension results. This view emphasizes the central role of the kidney in long-term blood pressure regulation; the arterial **baroreceptor reflex** acts within minutes to hours but resets and can hardly determine long-term blood pressure ([Chapter 16](/7.14.16)), the two being responsible for different time scales. The central nervous system (especially the action of the sympathetic nerves on the kidney) can shift the renal function curve and also has some role in long-term regulation.

## 24.7. Evolution and Comparison

### 24.7.1. Relative Medullary Thickness and Concentrating Ability

The ability to produce urine more concentrated than plasma is a feature of birds and mammals; the kidneys of fishes, amphibians and reptiles have no loops of Henle and their urine is at most isosmotic with plasma. Among mammals the maximal urine concentration ranges from about $500\,\mathrm{mOsm\,kg^{-1}}$ in the beaver to nearly $10\,000\,\mathrm{mOsm\,kg^{-1}}$ in desert rodents, a difference of about 20-fold. This difference arises mainly from the relative thickness of the medulla:

**Definition 24.7.1 (Relative Medullary Thickness)** The thickness of the renal medulla divided by the size of the kidney (the geometric mean of its three dimensions, or another linear measure), multiplied by 10, is called the **relative medullary thickness** (RMT). It reflects the length of the loops of Henle relative to the kidney and removes the effect of body size.

This relation agrees with the countercurrent multiplication model: the longer the loop, the more the same single effect is multiplied. But relative medullary thickness does not explain all the differences: small species have a high metabolic rate and may also have a larger single effect, which was taken into account in later analyses by Beuchat; the shape of the renal papilla and the arrangement of collecting ducts and vessels in the medulla (the fine arrangement of vascular bundles and loops in gerbils) also matter.

### 24.7.2. Desert Mammals

- Kangaroo rats (*Dipodomys*) and hopping mice: urine can reach $5000$–$9000\,\mathrm{mOsm\,kg^{-1}}$; together with dry feces, water recovery in the nasal passages (Chapter 21), nocturnal activity and daytime refuge in humid burrows, they can live entirely on **metabolic water** and the water in their food.
- Camel: urine concentration is not especially high (about $3000\,\mathrm{mOsm\,kg^{-1}}$), and its strategy is mainly tolerance: it can withstand the loss of $25\%$–$30\%$ of body weight as water (a human is in danger of death after losing about $12\%$), and the water lost comes mainly from the interstitial and intracellular fluid, so that plasma volume is better maintained and the blood does not become too viscous; body temperature is allowed to rise by several degrees during the day and is dissipated at night, which reduces sweating (Chapter 31); in water deprivation both GFR and urine volume fall markedly, the excretion of urea is reduced and urea is recycled for protein synthesis by rumen microbes. A dehydrated camel can drink the equivalent of $30\%$ of its body weight in ten minutes, and its erythrocytes can withstand the resulting hypotonicity without bursting.

### 24.7.3. Birds and Marine Mammals

The avian kidney contains both a large number of loopless "reptilian-type" nephrons and a small number of "mammalian-type" nephrons with loops of Henle, the latter concentrated in the medullary cones (Chapter 22). Avian urine is at most only about $2$–$2.5$ times as concentrated as plasma, and the only solute used for concentration is NaCl: birds excrete uric acid, which is insoluble and contributes nothing to osmolality, so there is no urea recycling. The strategies of birds are postrenal processing (water reabsorption in the cloaca and gut, Chapter 23) and salt glands (Chapter 21).

Marine mammals live in seawater but face a shortage of fresh water. The kidneys of whales and seals consist of many lobules (reniculi), each with a complete cortex and medulla; the maximal urine concentration is about $1500$–$2500\,\mathrm{mOsm\,kg^{-1}}$, higher than seawater but not much higher than that of terrestrial mammals. They seldom drink seawater, and their main sources of water are food (the water in fish and invertebrates, the former being approximately isosmotic with their own body fluids) and metabolic water from fat oxidation. Species that eat invertebrates (isosmotic with seawater) must excrete more salt and need stronger concentrating ability. The sea otter eats a great deal and also drinks some seawater, excreting the salt by means of its stronger concentrating ability.

Taken together, the ability to concentrate urine is one element of the scheme of water balance, accompanied by the form of the excreta, extrarenal salt-excreting organs, behavior and thermoregulation; Chapter 25 will give a systematic summary of excretion and osmoregulation in its comparison of acid–base balance.

## Questions

1. A subject has a urine flow of $2\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}}$, a urine osmolality of $900\,\mathrm{mOsm\,L^{-1}}$ and a plasma osmolality of $300\,\mathrm{mOsm\,L^{-1}}$. Find the osmolar clearance and the free-water clearance. Is his plasma ADH level likely to be high or low?

   **Answer** $C_{\mathrm{osm}} = 900 \times 2/300 = 6\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}}$ and $C_{\ce{H2O}} = 2 - 6 = -4\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}}$: the kidney is retaining water, the urine is hypertonic, and the ADH level is high. A urine flow of $2\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}}$ that is at the same time so concentrated implies a very large solute excretion ($1.8\,\mathrm{mOsm\,min^{-1}}$, about 4 times normal), so a solute load (such as urea after a high-protein diet, or osmotic diuresis) may be present.
2. Why do loop diuretics inhibit both the concentration and the dilution of urine? Why do thiazide diuretics inhibit only dilution and not concentration?

   **Answer** NKCC2 of the thick ascending limb accomplishes two things at once: it establishes the medullary hypertonicity (needed for concentration) and it makes the tubular fluid hypotonic (needed for dilution). Loop diuretics inhibit NKCC2, so both are impaired and the urine tends toward isosmolality. Thiazides act on the cortical distal convoluted tubule, where NaCl reabsorption dilutes the tubular fluid further but does not take part in building the medullary gradient, so they weaken dilution (which is why thiazides readily cause hyponatremia) without affecting concentration.
3. Use the countercurrent multiplication model to explain why an animal with only short-looped nephrons cannot produce very concentrated urine even if the pumps of its thick ascending limbs are strong.

   **Answer** The longitudinal gradient is the accumulation of the single effect along the loop; when the loop is short (few levels), the single effect can be added up fewer times; in addition, the gradient needs a medulla thick enough to contain it, and washout by the vasa recta has a relatively larger effect on a short medulla. The maximal urine concentration is therefore limited by loop length, which is exactly why relative medullary thickness correlates with concentrating ability. In the model, doubling the single effect can partly compensate, but the actual single effect is limited by the transepithelial concentration difference the epithelium can maintain (about $200\,\mathrm{mOsm\,kg^{-1}}$).
4. A patient with ectopic ADH secretion from lung cancer has a plasma $[\ce{Na+}]$ of $120\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$. Why is his extracellular fluid volume only slightly increased, and why may his total body $\ce{Na+}$ even be reduced? Why is water restriction an effective treatment?

   **Answer** Excess ADH retains water and osmolality falls; the water is distributed between the intracellular and extracellular compartments (two thirds enters the cells). The increase in extracellular fluid volume is detected by volume receptors, the RAAS is suppressed, ANP increases and pressure natriuresis occurs, and the kidney excretes more $\ce{Na+}$ and water, bringing the volume back to near normal. The result is a relative excess of water, with total $\ce{Na+}$ unchanged or even reduced, and the blood sodium falls. With water restriction, continuing insensible loss and obligatory urine volume put water in negative balance, and osmolality rises again; there is no need to replace large amounts of $\ce{Na+}$, because the problem lies in water and not in $\ce{Na+}$.
5. According to Guyton's model, can the long-term use of a vasodilator that lowers peripheral resistance, without acting on the kidney, lower blood pressure in the long term? Why are diuretics first-line drugs for hypertension in clinical practice?

   **Answer** A reduction in peripheral resistance lowers blood pressure temporarily, $E(P) < I$, body $\ce{Na+}$ and water are retained, extracellular fluid volume and cardiac output increase, until the pressure returns to $P^*$; so a vasodilator that does not change the renal function curve has a limited long-term hypotensive effect (in clinical practice a vasodilator alone is often accompanied by edema and reflex tachycardia). Diuretics shift the renal function curve to the left: the same intake can be excreted at a lower pressure, the equilibrium pressure $P^*$ falls, and so they can lower pressure in the long term. ACE inhibitors and $\mathrm{AT_1}$ receptor blockers also act mainly by shifting the renal function curve to the left.
6. Birds concentrate urine with NaCl only, not urea. From the standpoint of countercurrent multiplication and urea recycling, explain what this means for the maximal urine concentration of birds.

   **Answer** About half of the inner medullary hypertonicity of mammals comes from urea, and urea recycling allows the collecting duct to concentrate urine to a very high level without consuming extra water. Birds excrete uric acid, which is insoluble and contributes almost nothing to osmolality, so the medullary hypertonicity can rely only on NaCl transported actively by the ascending limb; moreover only a few nephrons have loops of Henle, and the loops are short, so the maximal urine concentration is only about 2–2.5 times that of plasma. Birds make up for the limited concentrating ability by precipitating uric acid (which carries away almost no water) and by reabsorption in the cloaca and gut.
