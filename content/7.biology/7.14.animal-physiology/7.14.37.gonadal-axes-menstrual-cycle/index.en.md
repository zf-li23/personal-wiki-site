The reproductive system differs fundamentally from the other systems: it does not maintain the homeostasis of the individual but starts, runs and shuts down on a strict schedule over the lifetime, and in females runs cyclically. It is controlled by the hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal axis, a three-level endocrine system that works mainly by negative feedback and switches to positive feedback at particular moments. This chapter first treats the male: the structure of the reproductive organs, the spatial organization of spermatogenesis, the actions of the two gonadotropins on two cell types, and testosterone (Section 37.1), and the axis that regulates them (Section 37.2). It then treats the female: the development of follicles (Section 37.3), the ovarian and endometrial cycles (Section 37.4), the feedback switch that allows the cycle to run and the selection of the dominant follicle (Section 37.5), and combines these in two models (Section 37.6). We then turn to the actions of sex hormones (Section 37.7), the beginning and end of reproductive function (Section 37.8), and finally compare other animals (Section 37.9).

The cell biology of gametogenesis and fertilization (arrest and resumption of meiosis, formation of sperm, the acrosome reaction) is in [7.5.27](/7.5.27) and [7.5.4](/7.5.4), and the differentiation of the gonads and reproductive tract is in [7.5.26](/7.5.26); the synthesis of steroid hormones and nuclear receptors are in Chapter 35 and [7.1.15](/7.1.15); frequency coding of hormone pulses is in [Chapter 33](/7.14.33). Pregnancy, parturition and lactation are in Chapter 38.

## 37.1. The Male Reproductive System

### 37.1.1. Structure

The testis consists of about 900 coiled seminiferous tubules, each more than $0.5\,\mathrm{m}$ long, hundreds of meters in total length, the site of sperm formation; in the interstitium between the tubules are blood vessels and the Leydig cells that secrete testosterone. Sperm pass from the seminiferous tubules through the rete testis and efferent ductules into the epididymis, a coiled duct about $6\,\mathrm{m}$ long in which sperm travel for about two weeks and acquire motility and fertilizing ability; they then pass through the vas deferens and ejaculatory duct into the urethra. Sperm can be stored in the tail of the epididymis and in the vas deferens for more than a month, during which epididymal fluid holds them in an inhibited state, and they begin to move actively only after ejaculation.

The secretions of the accessory glands make up most of the semen:

| Gland | Share of semen volume | Secretion and function |
|---|---|---|
| Seminal vesicle | about $60\%$ | Fructose (the energy source of sperm), prostaglandins, proteins that promote coagulation |
| Prostate | about $30\%$ | Thin alkaline fluid containing citrate, zinc and prostate-specific antigen (a protease that liquefies the coagulated ejaculate within 15–30 min) |
| Bulbourethral gland | Small | Mucus that lubricates the urethra before ejaculation |

One ejaculate is about $2$–$5\,\mathrm{mL}$ and contains tens of millions to over a hundred million sperm per milliliter (the World Health Organization reference lower limit is $1.6\times10^7\,\mathrm{mL^{-1}}$). The alkaline semen neutralizes the acidity of the vagina, favoring sperm survival.

The testes lie outside the body in the scrotum, at a temperature about $2$–$3\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$ below that of the abdominal cavity, which is required for normal spermatogenesis: cryptorchidism (undescended testis) causes infertility. The testicular artery is wound by the pampiniform plexus of veins, and **countercurrent exchange** between them (Chapters 17 and 31) precools the arterial blood entering the testis; the cremaster muscle and the dartos of the scrotum change the distance of the testis from the body with ambient temperature.

### 37.1.2. Spatial Organization of Spermatogenesis

The cellular process of **spermatogenesis** is in [7.5.27](/7.5.27); from the physiological viewpoint, what matters is its spatial organization and timing:

- The epithelium of the seminiferous tubule consists of germ cells and Sertoli cells (supporting cells). Sertoli cells extend from the basement membrane to the lumen, and germ cells move from the basement membrane toward the lumen as they develop: spermatogonium → primary spermatocyte → secondary spermatocyte → spermatid → spermatozoon, which is finally released into the lumen.
- Tight junctions between neighboring Sertoli cells form the blood–testis barrier, dividing the epithelium into a basal compartment (spermatogonia, in communication with the blood) and an adluminal compartment (cells in meiosis and later). The barrier provides a special microenvironment for meiosis and keeps spermatocytes and spermatids, which appear only at puberty and carry new antigens, from being recognized by the immune system. For spermatocytes to cross the barrier, new junctions form above them first and the old junctions below are opened afterward.
- Sertoli cells provide germ cells with nutrition (lactate), phagocytose the residual cytoplasm shed when spermatids transform, and secrete luminal fluid, androgen-binding protein, inhibin and anti-Müllerian hormone (in the embryo). Each Sertoli cell can support only a certain number of germ cells, so the number of Sertoli cells (fixed in the fetal period and before puberty) determines sperm output in adulthood.
- Cells derived from the same spermatogonium are linked by cytoplasmic bridges and develop synchronously. In humans it takes about $64$–$74$ days from spermatogonium to sperm; at a given position in the tubule, cells of different stages of development appear in sequence in fixed combinations (the cycle of spermatogenesis), so sperm are produced continuously and not in batches. Humans produce about $10^8$ sperm per day.

### 37.1.3. LH, FSH and the Two Cell Types

The two gonadotropins of the adenohypophysis, luteinizing hormone (LH) and follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), are both glycoproteins composed of a common α subunit and a specific β subunit each (TSH and human chorionic gonadotropin also share this α subunit). In the testis the two hormones act on two cell types:

| | Leydig cell (interstitial) | Sertoli cell (within the tubule) |
|---|---|---|
| Receptor | LH receptor ($\mathrm{G_s}$–cAMP–PKA) | FSH receptor ($\mathrm{G_s}$–cAMP) and androgen receptor |
| Product | Testosterone (through StAR and CYP11A1, CYP17 and others, Chapter 35) | Androgen-binding protein (ABP), inhibin B, aromatase (converts testosterone to estradiol), growth factors |
| Action | Testosterone diffuses to the tubules and the blood | ABP binds testosterone and keeps testosterone in the tubule at about 100 times the plasma level; converts the hormone signal into support for germ cells |

Germ cells themselves have no FSH or androgen receptors, and the hormones act through Sertoli cells. A high intratesticular testosterone concentration is required for spermatogenesis; FSH mainly starts spermatogenesis at puberty and fixes the number of Sertoli cells and has a reinforcing role in adulthood; growth hormone and IGF-1 are also needed for normal testicular function, and when they are lacking puberty is delayed and spermatogenesis is weak. A corollary is that exogenous testosterone (for example abuse of anabolic androgenic steroids) actually reduces spermatogenesis, because through negative feedback it suppresses LH, intratesticular testosterone falls, and plasma testosterone concentrations come nowhere near the levels needed inside the testis; this is also the principle of research on male hormonal contraception.

### 37.1.4. Testosterone

The testes secrete about $95\%$ of the body's testosterone, about $5$–$7\,\mathrm{mg}$ per day in an adult male; the adrenal and peripheral tissues (conversion from androstenedione in skin and fat) provide the rest. About $44\%$–$65\%$ of plasma testosterone is tightly bound to sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG), $33\%$–$50\%$ is loosely bound to albumin, and only about $2\%$ is free; the albumin-bound part dissociates readily as blood flows through tissues and, together with the free fraction, constitutes "bioavailable" testosterone ([Chapter 33](/7.14.33)).

Testosterone enters cells by diffusion and binds the intracellular androgen receptor (a **nuclear receptor**), and the complex acts as a transcription factor that regulates a large number of target genes. In some tissues it is first converted into a more active product or a product with a different action:

- 5α-Reductase (prostate, external genitalia, skin and hair follicles) converts it to dihydrotestosterone (DHT), which has a higher affinity for the receptor. Masculinization of the fetal external genitalia, prostate growth at puberty, beard growth and male-pattern baldness all depend on DHT. A male fetus with 5α-reductase 2 deficiency has female-appearing external genitalia at birth and is only partly masculinized after testosterone rises greatly at puberty.
- Aromatase (fat, bone, brain) converts it to estradiol. Epiphyseal closure and maintenance of bone mass in males, part of the negative feedback and sexual behavior all need estradiol: males lacking aromatase or the estrogen receptor keep growing taller and have osteoporosis.

The actions of testosterone:

| Period and tissue | Action |
|---|---|
| Fetus | Causes the **Wolffian duct** to differentiate into epididymis, vas deferens and seminal vesicle; masculinizes the external genitalia through DHT; promotes descent of the testis (together with INSL3) |
| Secondary sex characteristics at puberty | Enlargement of the larynx and thickening of the vocal cords (voice change), beard, pubic and body hair, thickening of the skin, increased sebum secretion (acne) |
| Muscle and bone | Promotes protein synthesis and increases muscle mass; promotes bone growth and bone mass, pubertal growth spurt (together with growth hormone), and finally, through estrogen, epiphyseal closure |
| Blood | Promotes secretion of **erythropoietin**; hemoglobin in men is about $10\%$–$15\%$ higher than in women |
| Brain and behavior | Maintains libido and sexual behavior (androgens are needed in both sexes), affects aggression and mood |
| Reproduction | Maintains spermatogenesis and the function of the accessory glands |

Inactivating mutations of the androgen receptor cause **androgen insensitivity syndrome**: a 46,XY individual has testes and very high testosterone but a female external appearance (the Wolffian ducts and external genitalia cannot respond to androgen, while anti-Müllerian hormone secreted by the testes still causes the Müllerian ducts to regress, so there is no uterus).

### 37.1.5. Erection and Ejaculation

Erection is a neurovascular reflex: tactile and psychological stimuli, through the parasympathetic nerves of the sacral cord (pelvic nerve), relax the smooth muscle of the cavernous arteries of the penis and of the trabeculae of the corpora cavernosa, the main transmitter being nitric oxide acting through cGMP; blood fills the sinusoidal spaces of the corpora cavernosa and compresses the veins under the tunica albuginea, preventing outflow, and the pressure in the corpora cavernosa approaches arterial pressure. Phosphodiesterase 5 inhibitors (sildenafil) inhibit the breakdown of cGMP and thereby enhance erection.

Ejaculation has two steps: emission is caused by the sympathetic nerves of the lumbar cord, the smooth muscle of the vas deferens, seminal vesicles and prostate contracts and moves sperm and secretions into the posterior urethra, while the bladder neck contracts to prevent retrograde flow; expulsion is a spinal reflex in which striated muscles such as the bulbospongiosus contract rhythmically and expel the semen. That is, "the parasympathetic causes erection and the sympathetic causes ejaculation."

## 37.2. The Hypothalamic–Pituitary–Gonadal Axis

**Definition 37.2.1 (Hypothalamic–Pituitary–Gonadal Axis)** The regulatory system composed of gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) from the hypothalamus, luteinizing hormone and follicle-stimulating hormone from the adenohypophysis, and sex hormones and inhibin from the gonads, linked by feedback of the gonadal hormones on the two upstream levels, is called the **hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal axis** (HPG axis).

### 37.2.1. GnRH Neurons and the Pulse Generator

GnRH is a decapeptide. The neurons that synthesize it do not form a nucleus: humans have only about $1000$–$2000$ GnRH neurons, scattered in the medial basal hypothalamus (infundibular region, around the arcuate nucleus) and the preoptic area, whereas in rodents they are mainly in the preoptic area; their axons end in the median eminence and release GnRH into the hypophyseal portal system. GnRH neurons originate in the embryo from the olfactory placode and migrate along the olfactory nerve to the hypothalamus; failure of migration (such as *ANOS1* mutations) causes gonadotropin deficiency and loss of smell (Kallmann syndrome).

GnRH is secreted in pulses, and the rhythm is generated by a group of KNDy neurons in the arcuate nucleus: they co-express kisspeptin, neurokinin B (which synchronously excites the population) and dynorphin (which then inhibits the population), forming an oscillator that alternates excitation and inhibition, and each synchronous discharge makes the GnRH neurons release one GnRH pulse. Kisspeptin acts through its receptor KISS1R on GnRH neurons; people with inactivating KISS1R mutations cannot enter puberty (a discovery of 2003), which shows that kisspeptin is the "master switch" of the axis.

GnRH acts on the GnRH receptor ($\mathrm{G_q}$) on pituitary gonadotrophs and causes the release and synthesis of LH and FSH through the $\mathrm{IP_3}$–$\ce{Ca^2+}$ and DAG–PKC pathways. The frequency of the pulses carries information in itself ([Chapter 33](/7.14.33) discussed models of receptor desensitization and frequency decoding):

- A physiological frequency (about once every $1$–$2\,\mathrm{h}$) maintains the secretion of LH and FSH; faster frequencies favor transcription of the LH β subunit and slower frequencies favor FSH.
- Continuous GnRH desensitizes and internalizes the receptor, and gonadotropin secretion is instead suppressed. This apparent paradox is used in therapy: after a brief initial stimulation, long-acting GnRH agonists produce "medical castration," used in prostate cancer, endometriosis and central precocious puberty.

The experiments on rhesus monkeys by Knobil and colleagues (1978–1980) showed the necessity of pulses most clearly: after the region of the arcuate nucleus was destroyed in female monkeys, gonadotropins disappeared; infusing GnRH pulses once an hour restored LH and FSH, and the menstrual cycle, including the preovulatory LH surge, was fully restored; when the same GnRH was infused continuously, gonadotropins were suppressed again. That is, in primates a GnRH pulse signal of fixed frequency is enough to let the feedback between pituitary and ovary generate the whole cycle itself; GnRH is a "permissive" signal.

### 37.2.2. Feedback in the Male

The HPG axis of the male is a simple negative feedback system:

- Testosterone (and the estradiol made from it by aromatization) inhibits the frequency of GnRH pulses in the hypothalamus (through KNDy neurons) and reduces the response to GnRH in the pituitary, mainly suppressing LH.
- Inhibin B secreted by Sertoli cells selectively inhibits FSH, and it reflects the state of spermatogenesis: when spermatogenesis is impaired, inhibin B falls and FSH rises.

LH and testosterone therefore form one loop and FSH and inhibin another; the former regulates endocrine function and the latter reflects spermatogenic function. Clinically, primary testicular failure (such as Klinefelter syndrome, 47,XXY) shows low testosterone with high LH and FSH; lesions of the hypothalamus or pituitary make all three low.

## 37.3. The Female Reproductive System and Follicle Development

### 37.3.1. Structure

The female internal genitalia comprise a pair of ovaries, a pair of oviducts, the uterus and the vagina. The ovary is divided into a cortex (containing follicles of all stages) and a medulla (blood vessels and nerves). The fimbriated end of the oviduct picks up the ovulated egg, fertilization usually occurs in the ampulla of the oviduct, and the fertilized egg reaches the uterus after about 3–4 days. The uterus is a pear-shaped muscular organ suspended by ligaments; from outside inward, the uterine wall consists of the serosa, a thick smooth muscle layer (myometrium) and the endometrium. The endometrium consists of glands and stroma and is divided into a functional layer that is shed periodically and a basal layer that is retained and responsible for regeneration; its blood is supplied by spiral arteries that pass through the myometrium. The uterus communicates with the vagina through the narrow cervical canal, and cervical mucus is the "gate" for sperm entering the uterus.

### 37.3.2. Oogenesis and the Follicle Reserve

**Oogenesis** is entirely different in timing from spermatogenesis ([7.5.27](/7.5.27)):

- Mitosis of oogonia occurs only in the fetal period and reaches about 7 million at about 20 weeks of gestation; they enter meiosis and arrest in the diplotene (dictyate) stage of prophase of the first meiotic division, becoming primary oocytes, each surrounded by a layer of flat granulosa cells, forming a primordial follicle.
- Thereafter oocytes only decrease and never increase: about $1$–$2$ million at birth and about $300\,000$–$400\,000$ at puberty. Only about $400$–$500$ are ovulated in a lifetime, and all the rest are lost by atresia. When the follicle reserve falls below about $1000$ (at an average of about 51 years), menstruation stops (Section 37.8.2).
- Oocytes arrested in prophase I can remain so for decades, until the preovulatory LH surge resumes meiosis, the first polar body is extruded, and the oocyte arrests again at metaphase of the second meiotic division and completes meiosis only at fertilization. Long arrest of the oocyte is thought to be one reason for the increase in aneuploid eggs in older mothers.

### 37.3.3. Stages of Follicle Development

The follicle is the basic unit of ovarian function and consists of the oocyte, the granulosa cells surrounding it, and the theca cells outside. Every day a cohort of primordial follicles is activated and begins irreversible growth; from activation to just before ovulation takes about a year, of which the first several months are independent of gonadotropins and the last two weeks or so (late antral follicle) depend on FSH.

| Stage | Diameter | Features | Gonadotropin dependence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Primordial follicle | about $30$–$50\,\mathrm{\mu m}$ | A layer of flat granulosa cells and a basement membrane around the oocyte; the great majority of follicles are in this state | No |
| Primary follicle | about $0.1\,\mathrm{mm}$ | Granulosa cells become cuboidal, still in a single layer; the oocyte enlarges and secretes the **zona pellucida** | No |
| Secondary follicle (preantral follicle) | about $0.2\,\mathrm{mm}$ | Granulosa cells in several layers; theca cells appear outside the basement membrane (the inner layer expresses LH receptors); the oocyte grows to about $120\,\mathrm{\mu m}$ and growth is essentially complete; granulosa cells express FSH, androgen and estrogen receptors | Weak |
| Antral follicle (tertiary follicle) | $2$–$10\,\mathrm{mm}$ | Granulosa cells secrete fluid, forming the follicular cavity (antrum); gap junctions among granulosa cells and between granulosa cells and the oocyte; begins to produce steroid hormones | FSH-dependent (from about $2$–$5\,\mathrm{mm}$) |
| Preovulatory follicle (Graafian follicle) | about $18$–$25\,\mathrm{mm}$ | Very large antrum, the oocyte on the cumulus; granulosa cells differentiate into three types: outer mural granulosa cells (metabolically active, acquire LH receptors, the main body of the corpus luteum after ovulation), cumulus granulosa cells surrounding the oocyte and forming a unit with it (nourish the oocyte and are ovulated with the egg, the corona radiata), and granulosa cells lining the antrum | FSH and LH |

FSH and estrogen together promote the proliferation of granulosa cells and the formation of the antrum and induce granulosa cells to produce LH receptors; FSH, LH and estrogen together promote the production of progesterone; LH inhibits granulosa cell division and promotes their differentiation. The timing of the appearance of the three hormones determines the fate of the follicle.

### 37.3.4. The Two-Cell, Two-Gonadotropin Model

**Definition 37.3.1 (Two-Cell, Two-Gonadotropin Model)** Synthesis of estradiol by the follicle requires a division of labor between two cell types and two gonadotropins: under the action of LH, theca cells synthesize androgen (androstenedione) from cholesterol, and the androgen diffuses to the granulosa cells, which convert it to estradiol by FSH-induced aromatase. This scheme is called the **two-cell, two-gonadotropin model**.

The reason for the division of labor is the distribution of enzymes: theca cells have a blood supply (and can obtain LDL cholesterol) and express CYP17 but have little aromatase; granulosa cells lie inside the avascular basement membrane and have little CYP17 but are induced by FSH to make large amounts of aromatase (CYP19). This arrangement is the same as the division of labor between Leydig cells and Sertoli cells in the testis. In the late phase of follicle development granulosa cells also acquire LH receptors; after the LH surge they turn to synthesizing large amounts of progesterone (luteinization).

## 37.4. The Ovarian and Endometrial Cycles

### 37.4.1. Stages of the Cycle

**Definition 37.4.1 (Menstrual Cycle)** In a woman during the reproductive years, the growth of ovarian follicles, ovulation and the formation and regression of the corpus luteum, together with the accompanying proliferation, secretion and shedding with bleeding of the endometrium, recur with a period of about one month; this process is called the **menstrual cycle**; the cycle takes the first day of menstruation as day 1.

Ovulation in the human female is spontaneous: it does not need an external stimulus such as mating but arises from the periodic interaction of signals among the hypothalamus, adenohypophysis and ovary. Periodic changes in cervical and uterine function are controlled by the concentrations of circulating ovarian hormones (estrogen and progesterone). All these changes serve to let the reproductive tract accomplish the transport of sperm and egg, fertilization, implantation and pregnancy.

Taking a 28-day cycle as an example:

| Day | Ovarian cycle | Endometrial cycle | Main hormones |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1–5 | Early follicular phase: a cohort of antral follicles begins to grow under raised FSH | Menstrual phase: the functional layer is shed | Estrogen and progesterone low, FSH raised (the intercycle FSH rise) |
| 6–13 | Mid to late follicular phase: one dominant follicle is selected and the rest undergo atresia | Proliferative phase: the endometrium grows rapidly under estrogen (4–7 days), $3$–$5\,\mathrm{mm}$ thick at ovulation; spiral arteries lengthen; the cervix secretes thin, clear, stretchy mucus | Estradiol rises continuously, FSH falls |
| 14 | Ovulation | — | LH surge |
| 15–28 | Luteal phase: the corpus luteum secretes progesterone and estrogen and, if pregnancy has not occurred, regresses after about 14 days | Secretory phase: glands become tortuous and secrete glycogen-rich fluid, the stroma becomes edematous, and a week after ovulation the endometrium is $5$–$6\,\mathrm{mm}$ thick, preparing for implantation; cervical mucus becomes viscous and blocks the passage of sperm | Progesterone peaks around day 21, estrogen shows a second peak; inhibin A rises |

Individual variation in cycle length ($21$–$35$ days are all normal) comes mainly from the follicular phase; the luteal phase is relatively constant at about $14$ days.

Menstruation is the result of progesterone withdrawal: after the corpus luteum regresses, progesterone and estrogen fall abruptly, the endometrium releases prostaglandins, the spiral arteries constrict strongly, and the functional layer becomes ischemic, necrotic and is shed; one menstrual period loses about $40\,\mathrm{mL}$ of blood plus about $35\,\mathrm{mL}$ of serous fluid. Menstrual blood contains plasminogen activator released by the endometrium, and fibrin is dissolved (**fibrinolysis**), so menstrual blood usually does not clot. Progesterone raises the hypothalamic temperature set point, and basal body temperature rises by $0.3$–$0.5\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$ after ovulation ([Chapter 31](/7.14.31)).

### 37.4.2. Negative and Positive Feedback

For most of the cycle ovarian steroids exert negative feedback on the hypothalamus and adenohypophysis:

- Early to mid follicular phase: moderate levels of estradiol and inhibin B suppress FSH (and LH).
- Luteal phase: progesterone slows the frequency of GnRH pulses (from about once every $60$–$90\,\mathrm{min}$ in the follicular phase to about once every $3$–$4\,\mathrm{h}$) and, together with estradiol and inhibin A, holds LH and FSH very low.

But at the end of the follicular phase the action of estradiol reverses:

**Definition 37.4.2 (LH Surge)** At the end of the follicular phase, when the estradiol secreted by the dominant follicle rises to and is maintained above a threshold (about $700$–$1000\,\mathrm{pmol\,L^{-1}}$, that is about $200$–$300\,\mathrm{pg\,mL^{-1}}$, for about $36$–$48\,\mathrm{h}$), its action on gonadotropins switches from negative to positive feedback, causing a massive release in which plasma LH rises about 10-fold within about a day and lasts about $48\,\mathrm{h}$; this is called the **LH surge**. FSH shows a smaller peak at the same time.

The mechanism of the LH surge has two levels:

1. Pituitary: high estradiol increases the number of GnRH receptors on gonadotrophs and the synthesis of LH, so that LH is stored in large amounts in the cell; at the same time GnRH itself has a "self-priming" effect, in which an earlier pulse strengthens the effect of the next. For the same GnRH pulse, the release of LH is therefore multiplied. The small amount of progesterone that the preovulatory follicle begins to secrete further promotes this action.
2. Hypothalamus: in rodents, estradiol acts on kisspeptin neurons of the anteroventral periventricular nucleus and causes massive release of GnRH (the "GnRH surge"); in primates, Knobil's experiments showed that GnRH pulses of fixed frequency are already enough to produce an LH surge, so positive feedback is mainly in the pituitary and the role of the medial basal hypothalamus is auxiliary.

Positive feedback is unstable, and the LH surge must terminate itself (Chapter 1 discussed the termination of positive feedback): the LH stored in the pituitary is exhausted and GnRH receptors are desensitized; after the LH surge the follicle stops secreting estradiol and secretes progesterone instead, and progesterone in the luteal phase inhibits the recurrence of positive feedback.

Ovulation occurs about $34$–$36\,\mathrm{h}$ after the start of the LH surge (about $10$–$12\,\mathrm{h}$ after the peak). The LH surge also causes the following: resumption of meiosis in the oocyte (LH lowers cGMP in granulosa cells, cGMP passing through gap junctions to the oocyte falls, and the inhibition of meiosis is released); expansion of the cumulus cells, which secrete hyaluronic acid; luteinization of granulosa cells, which begin to synthesize progesterone; thinning and rupture of the follicle wall under prostaglandins and proteases, so that the oocyte and cumulus are expelled together. Blocking prostaglandin synthesis (for example high doses of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs) can prevent follicles from rupturing.

### 37.4.3. The Corpus Luteum

**Definition 37.4.3 (Corpus Luteum)** After ovulation, the granulosa cells and theca cells of the ruptured follicle proliferate under LH, accumulate lipid, and form a richly vascularized endocrine gland that secretes large amounts of progesterone and estradiol; it is called the **corpus luteum**.

The human corpus luteum reaches peak function about $7$–$8$ days after ovulation and, if pregnancy has not occurred, regresses after about $12$–$14$ days (luteolysis) and is gradually replaced by connective tissue to become the corpus albicans. Maintenance of the corpus luteum needs LH pulses, but LH in the luteal phase does not fall to a level insufficient to maintain the corpus luteum, so the mechanism of luteolysis in humans is not fully understood and may involve local prostaglandin $\mathrm{F_{2\alpha}}$ within the corpus luteum and a decline in sensitivity to LH. If pregnancy occurs, human chorionic gonadotropin secreted by the embryo "rescues" the corpus luteum (Chapter 38).

In ruminants and pigs the signal for luteolysis comes from the uterus: late in the luteal phase the endometrium secretes prostaglandin $\mathrm{F_{2\alpha}}$, which leaves by the uterine vein; the utero-ovarian vein and the ovarian artery lie close together, and $\mathrm{PGF_{2\alpha}}$ passes directly from vein to artery by **countercurrent exchange** and reaches the ipsilateral ovary without passing through the lungs (which would degrade most $\mathrm{PGF_{2\alpha}}$ in a single circulation). When the uterus of a sheep is removed the corpus luteum persists for months; when only one uterine horn is removed, only the ipsilateral corpus luteum is maintained. This is another example of local vascular architecture determining the range of action of a hormone ([Chapter 33](/7.14.33)).

### 37.4.4. Inhibin, Activin and Follistatin

Besides steroids the ovary secretes peptides of the transforming growth factor β family:

- Inhibin consists of an α subunit and a β subunit and selectively inhibits FSH secretion by the pituitary. In the follicular phase it is mainly inhibin B secreted by small antral follicles, and in the luteal phase mainly inhibin A secreted by the corpus luteum.
- Activin consists of two β subunits and promotes the synthesis and secretion of FSH. It is produced mainly within the pituitary by the gonadotrophs themselves and acts in an autocrine–paracrine manner, rather than entering the circulation as a feedback signal from the ovary; in the ovary activin mainly regulates granulosa cells locally within the follicle.
- Follistatin is a protein that binds and neutralizes activin and is also produced locally in the pituitary.

When the corpus luteum regresses, inhibin A falls together with progesterone and estradiol, releasing the inhibition of FSH and producing the "intercycle FSH rise," which recruits a new cohort of antral follicles for the next cycle.

## 37.5. Selection of the Dominant Follicle

At the start of each cycle about ten antral follicles of $2$–$5\,\mathrm{mm}$ in the two ovaries begin to grow under raised FSH, but by the middle of the follicular phase (days 6–8) only one continues to grow and the rest undergo atresia, and finally usually only one egg is ovulated. How is this achieved?

At first all follicles grow; as FSH falls the smaller follicles undergo atresia in turn, and by about day 7 only one follicle (red) continues to grow, reaching about 17 mm on day 14. B: Plasma FSH (blue) and the FSH thresholds of the dominant follicle (red dashed) and the second-largest follicle (gray dotted). The threshold of the second-largest follicle is crossed by FSH at about day 7, so the "FSH window" closes for it, whereas the threshold of the dominant follicle keeps falling as it grows and always stays below FSH](./plots/dominant-follicle.py)

This mechanism also explains clinical phenomena: when exogenous FSH is used so that FSH does not fall in the middle of the follicular phase (the "window is kept open"), several follicles can grow large, which is the principle of controlled ovarian hyperstimulation in in vitro fertilization; that only one follicle is selected, in monovulatory species (human, horse, cow), is due to a narrow window, while in polyovulatory species (rodents, pigs) the window is wider and a dozen or so eggs are ovulated at a time.

## 37.6. Dynamical Model of the Menstrual Cycle

Combining the elements above gives a model that generates the cycle by itself. The structure of the model below follows the literature models of Selgrade, Harris-Clark and others, but is greatly simplified:

- FSH secretion is under negative feedback by estradiol and inhibin;
- LH is first synthesized and stored in the pituitary, synthesis being promoted by estradiol and inhibited by progesterone; the release rate of stored LH increases sharply once estradiol exceeds a steep threshold (negative feedback switches to positive feedback), and progesterone inhibits this release;
- the follicle grows when FSH exceeds a threshold, and its FSH sensitivity increases as it grows; when LH reaches the ovulation threshold the follicle is converted to a corpus luteum within about a day;
- the corpus luteum has a lifespan of about $12$–$14$ days (represented by three compartments in series), first secreting progesterone and estradiol and then regressing;
- estradiol comes from the follicle and the mature corpus luteum, progesterone from the corpus luteum, and inhibin from both.

![Dynamical model of the menstrual cycle (structure follows the model of Selgrade, Harris-Clark and others, simplified; parameters and units are illustrative; see the equations in the script). The model needs no external clock and generates by itself oscillations with a period of about 28 days; two cycles are shown, with day 0 the start of menstruation (progesterone withdrawal). A: Gonadotropins. When the corpus luteum regresses FSH rises (the intercycle rise) and recruits follicles; in the follicular phase FSH is held down by estradiol and inhibin; the LH surge appears at the end of the follicular phase (the surge in the model is shorter than the real one of about 48 h). B: Ovarian steroids. Estradiol rises faster at the end of the follicular phase and, after crossing the threshold (dashed, the level at the start of the LH surge), causes the LH surge; after ovulation progesterone rises and peaks about 5 days after ovulation (about 7 days in reality), and estradiol shows a second, lower peak; after the corpus luteum regresses both fall, causing menstruation and the next FSH rise. C: Tissue mass of the growing follicle and the corpus luteum. The timing of the cycle is determined mainly by two processes: the time for the follicle from recruitment until estradiol reaches the threshold (the follicular phase), and the lifespan of the corpus luteum (the luteal phase)](./plots/menstrual-cycle-model.py)

The model illustrates several important conclusions:

1. The cycle is generated by feedback between ovary and pituitary, and the hypothalamus need only supply stable GnRH pulses (consistent with Knobil's experiments); the time of ovulation is determined by the ovary, that is, the dominant follicle, which is called "the ovary is the clock of the cycle."
2. The LH surge needs two conditions: during the follicular phase estradiol, while below the threshold, has already caused the pituitary to store a large amount of LH; estradiol then crosses the threshold and opens the gate for release. Termination of the positive feedback comes from depletion of stores and conversion of the follicle to a corpus luteum.
3. The length of the luteal phase is determined by the lifespan of the corpus luteum and the length of the follicular phase by the rate of follicle growth, so variation in cycle length comes mainly from the follicular phase.
4. If exogenous estrogen and progesterone are held continuously at luteal-phase levels (combined oral contraceptives), FSH cannot rise, follicles are not recruited, estradiol cannot cross the threshold, and no LH surge or ovulation occurs.

## 37.7. Actions of Estrogen and Progesterone

| Target organ | Estrogen (mainly estradiol) | Progestogen (progesterone) |
|---|---|---|
| Endometrium | Promotes proliferation, induces progesterone receptors | Converts the proliferative endometrium to the secretory phase; maintains the endometrium in early pregnancy (decidualization) |
| Myometrium | Increases myometrial excitability, oxytocin receptors and gap junctions | Lowers excitability and oxytocin receptors, keeping the uterus quiescent ("progesterone block") |
| Cervix and vagina | Cervical mucus thin, abundant and stretchy (favoring passage of sperm); vaginal epithelium proliferates and cornifies | Cervical mucus viscous and scant, blocking sperm and microorganisms |
| Oviduct | Promotes epithelial proliferation, ciliary beating and smooth muscle contraction | Weakens contraction |
| Mammary gland | Promotes growth of the duct system and fat deposition | Promotes development of alveoli (lobules) |
| Bone | Inhibits bone resorption (lowers RANKL/OPG, Chapter 36); at puberty promotes the growth spurt and epiphyseal closure | — |
| Liver and metabolism | Increases synthesis of SHBG, thyroxine-binding globulin, clotting factors and angiotensinogen; raises HDL, lowers LDL | — |
| Cardiovascular | Promotes endothelial nitric oxide production, dilating vessels | — |
| Brain and other | Mediates the positive feedback at the end of the follicular phase and sexual behavior; secondary sex characteristics: female fat distribution, widening of the pelvis | Raises the temperature set point; stimulates respiration (Chapter 20, the increased ventilation in pregnancy); antagonizes aldosterone, with a mild natriuretic effect |

Estrogen and progesterone often act together: estrogen first induces progesterone receptors, and only then can progesterone act; progesterone in turn reduces estrogen receptors, limiting the proliferative action of estrogen. Long-term action of estrogen without cyclic progesterone (for example anovulatory cycles, estrogen-only replacement therapy) causes continued proliferation of the endometrium and raises the risk of endometrial cancer.

## 37.8. Puberty and Menopause

### 37.8.1. Puberty

The HPG axis is active in the fetal period and in the first months after birth ("minipuberty"), and is then strongly suppressed throughout childhood until it is reactivated at puberty. The suppression is central: in childhood the gonads can respond to gonadotropins and the pituitary can respond to GnRH, but the GnRH pulse generator is suppressed. At the onset of puberty GnRH pulses first appear and strengthen during sleep at night and then extend to the whole day.

The start of puberty integrates multiple signals: maturation of the kisspeptin system, and permissive signals reflecting body condition, especially leptin secreted by adipose tissue ([Chapter 30](/7.14.30)). With too little body fat (athletes, anorexia nervosa) puberty is delayed or menstruation stops, and people with congenital leptin deficiency do not enter puberty but can recover with leptin replacement. In girls breast development begins at about 10–11 years of age and menarche at about 12–13 years; in boys testicular enlargement begins at about 11–12 years. Over the past century and a half the age at menarche in industrialized countries has advanced by several years, mainly related to improved nutrition.

### 37.8.2. Menopause

When the follicle reserve of a woman is exhausted, the ovary no longer has follicles that respond to gonadotropins and menstruation stops (menopause, at an average of about 51 years). In the several years before menopause inhibin B falls first as follicles decline, FSH rises, and cycles become irregular; after menopause estradiol and inhibin are both very low, negative feedback is lost, and FSH and LH rise markedly (FSH more, up to more than 10 times the reproductive-years level).

The consequences of estrogen deficiency include hot flashes (KNDy neurons lose the inhibition by estrogen and become overactive, and neurokinin B acting on the thermoregulatory center of the hypothalamus causes sudden cutaneous vasodilation and sweating; neurokinin 3 receptor antagonists relieve hot flashes, which is direct evidence for this mechanism), urogenital atrophy, accelerated bone resorption and osteoporosis (Chapter 36), and increased cardiovascular risk.

Men have no corresponding abrupt change: testosterone falls by about $1\%$ per year after the age of 40, SHBG increases, and free testosterone falls more; spermatogenesis can be maintained into old age.

## 37.9. Evolution and Comparison

### 37.9.1. Spontaneous and Induced Ovulation

**Definition 37.9.1 (Induced Ovulation)** The mode in which ovulation occurs only after mating (or a similar stimulus) has caused an LH surge is called **induced ovulation**; in contrast, humans and most domestic animals ovulate spontaneously according to the ovarian cycle.

Rabbits, cats, ferrets, camels and alpacas, mink and many voles are induced ovulators. In the rabbit, mechanical stimulation of the reproductive tract during mating is relayed through the spinal cord to the hypothalamus and causes massive release of GnRH and an LH surge, and ovulation follows about $10\,\mathrm{h}$ later. The situation in camelids is more special: semen contains an "ovulation-inducing factor," β-nerve growth factor, which after absorption into the blood acts directly on the hypothalamus and causes an LH surge; injecting seminal plasma of a male camel into the muscle of a female camel can also cause ovulation. Induced ovulation synchronizes ovulation precisely with mating and suits solitary species in which the chances of the sexes meeting are few.

Spontaneous and induced ovulation are not sharply separated: the rat ovulates spontaneously, but the stimulus of mating (through twice-daily prolactin secretion) is needed to keep the corpus luteum functional long enough, otherwise the corpus luteum functions for only about two days and the estrous cycle is only 4–5 days; infertile mating causes "pseudopregnancy." In many species considered spontaneous ovulators, mating can also advance or enhance ovulation.

### 37.9.2. Estrous Cycles and Menstrual Cycles

**Definition 37.9.2 (Estrous Cycle)** The reproductive cycle of female mammals marked by the periodic appearance of a behavioral period in which they accept mating by males (estrus) is called the **estrous cycle**; if pregnancy does not occur, the thickened endometrium is reabsorbed instead of being shed with bleeding.

Most mammals have estrous cycles: the female accepts mating only during estrus around ovulation, and sexual behavior is induced in the hypothalamus by estrogen (in most species with prior action of progesterone as well), thereby confining mating to the time when fertilization is possible. The sexual receptivity of the human female is not confined to the time around ovulation, which is rare among mammals.

Few species menstruate: humans and other apes, Old World monkeys, some bats, elephant shrews, and the spiny mouse (*Acomys*), a rodent found to menstruate only in 2016. What they have in common is that decidualization of the endometrium does not depend on a signal from the embryo but occurs spontaneously during the luteal phase of each cycle; when there is no pregnancy, the decidualized endometrium can only be shed. One explanation is that the placentas of these species are strongly invasive (hemochorial placenta, Chapter 38), and the mother needs an endometrium prepared in advance of the embryo's arrival that can "test" the embryo; menstruation is the price of this strategy.

Cycle length and structure differ greatly among species:

| Species | Cycle | Features |
|---|---|---|
| Rat, mouse | 4–5 days | Luteal phase very short (prolonged only after mating); estrus at night |
| Sheep | about 17 days | Seasonal (breeds under short days); luteolysis by uterine $\mathrm{PGF_{2\alpha}}$ |
| Cow | about 21 days | Year round; luteolysis by uterine $\mathrm{PGF_{2\alpha}}$ |
| Horse | about 21 days | Seasonal (breeds under long days); estrus lasts up to a week |
| Dog | 1–2 times a year | Single estrus; the luteal phase lasts about two months whether or not pregnancy occurs |
| Human | about 28 days | Spontaneous ovulation, menstruation, no marked estrus |

### 37.9.3. Seasonal Breeding and Photoperiod

At middle and high latitudes many animals breed only in a particular season of the year so that the young are born in the season of greatest food abundance. Day length is the most reliable seasonal signal (**photoperiodism**). Mammals read day length through melatonin from the pineal gland: melatonin is secreted only in darkness, and the longer the night, the longer the duration of secretion (melatonin secretion is controlled jointly by the **circadian rhythm** of the suprachiasmatic nucleus and by light).

- Long-day breeders (hamster, horse, ferret) enter the breeding season in spring when day length increases and the duration of melatonin secretion shortens; short-day breeders (sheep, goat, deer) enter the breeding season in autumn when day length shortens, because their gestation is about five months (about seven months in deer), so that the young are all born in spring.
- How does the melatonin signal reach GnRH neurons? Yoshimura and colleagues (from 2003) found in Japanese quail, and it was then confirmed in mammals, that long days cause the pars tuberalis of the pituitary to secrete TSH, which acts on tanycytes in the wall of the third ventricle, inducing type 2 deiodinase (DIO2) and suppressing type 3 deiodinase (DIO3), so that local T3 in the medial basal hypothalamus increases; T3 changes the structural relation between GnRH nerve terminals and glia in the median eminence and promotes the release of GnRH. Thyroid hormone is here a local seasonal signal (local activation, [Chapter 33](/7.14.33)).
- Birds do not need the pineal gland or the eyes to sense photoperiod: light penetrates the skull and is sensed directly by deep brain photoreceptors in the hypothalamus (such as OPN5).

### 37.9.4. Birds, Amphibians and Fishes

- The single avian ovary: in most birds only the left ovary and oviduct develop and function, and the right regresses in the embryo (in some raptors both sides are present); one explanation is reduction of body weight for flight and adaptation to large, hard-shelled eggs. A laying hen ovulates almost once a day: the follicles in the ovary are arranged in a size hierarchy (F1, F2, …), and the largest follicle matures each day. The preovulatory LH surge in birds is caused mainly by progesterone secreted by the largest follicle (whereas in mammals it is mainly estradiol), and ovulation occurs about $4$–$6\,\mathrm{h}$ after the LH surge; within about $24$–$26\,\mathrm{h}$ in the oviduct the egg is coated with albumen and shell (Chapter 36).
- Vitellogenin: the eggs of birds, reptiles, amphibians and fishes contain large amounts of yolk, whose main precursor vitellogenin is synthesized in the liver under the action of estrogen, carried by the blood to the ovary, and taken up by receptor-mediated endocytosis through receptors on the oocyte membrane (members of the low-density lipoprotein receptor family), and in the oocyte is cleaved into phosvitin and lipovitellin. Vitellogenin binds large amounts of calcium, phosphate and lipid, so plasma total calcium in egg-laying females rises markedly. Normal male fish have almost no vitellogenin in the blood; in rivers polluted with estrogenic contaminants (such as ethinylestradiol from contraceptives), vitellogenin appears in the blood of male fish and even intersex occurs, which has become a standard indicator of environmental endocrine disruptors.
- Gonadal steroids and sex change in fishes: the main androgen of teleosts is 11-ketotestosterone, and the hormone that induces final maturation of the oocyte is the progestin 17α,20β-dihydroxyprogesterone (not a direct action of LH). Many teleosts can change sex in adulthood: in protogynous wrasses, after the male disappears from a group the largest female changes into a male in behavior within days and in the gonad within weeks; protandrous clownfish do the reverse. These transitions are closely tied to the rise and fall of aromatase expression (a change in the ratio of estrogen to androgen) and show that gonadal differentiation in vertebrates is reversible in some groups (for sex determination see [7.5.26](/7.5.26)).

## Questions

1. A bodybuilder who has injected high doses of testosterone for a long time finds after several months that his sperm concentration is very low and his testes have shrunk. Explain the mechanism. Why does recovery of spermatogenesis take several months after he stops?

   **Answer** Exogenous testosterone suppresses hypothalamic GnRH pulses and pituitary LH and FSH secretion by negative feedback; Leydig cells lose LH stimulation and atrophy, and intratesticular testosterone falls greatly; although plasma testosterone is high, it is far below the intratesticular concentration needed for spermatogenesis (about 100 times plasma, maintained by ABP), and lack of FSH also weakens support by Sertoli cells, so spermatogenesis stops and the seminiferous tubules and testes shrink. After withdrawal, clearance of the exogenous testosterone and release of the suppression of the axis take several weeks; spermatogenesis must then start again from spermatogonia, a full cycle taking about 64–74 days, plus about two weeks of transit in the epididymis, so at least three months are needed, and in long-term abusers more than a year may be needed.
2. Why can a long-acting GnRH agonist treat prostate cancer, while the same molecule given in pulses once an hour can treat hypothalamic amenorrhea?

   **Answer** The GnRH receptor of pituitary gonadotrophs responds only to intermittent signals: a continuously present agonist desensitizes and internalizes the receptor, signal transduction is downregulated, and after a brief initial rise the secretion of LH and FSH is suppressed, testosterone falls to the castrate level, and androgen-dependent prostate cancer is therefore suppressed. The cause of hypothalamic amenorrhea is that the GnRH pulse generator stops working (as with very low body weight or excessive exercise), while the pituitary and ovary themselves are normal; giving GnRH pulses at physiological frequency can replace the missing signal and restore LH and FSH secretion, follicle development and ovulation. The difference between the two is precisely the temporal pattern of the hormone (frequency coding).
3. Using the FSH threshold and window model of Section 37.5, explain why giving exogenous FSH in in vitro fertilization can yield a dozen or so mature follicles. What would happen if one follicle had an activating mutation of the FSH receptor?

   **Answer** In a normal follicular phase, the estradiol and inhibin B secreted by growing follicles lower endogenous FSH, and only the follicle with the lowest threshold (the most sensitive) remains above threshold, while the rest undergo atresia. Exogenous FSH keeps FSH from falling in the mid to late follicular phase and holds it above the thresholds of many follicles (the window stays open), so all these follicles can continue to grow to preovulatory size. A follicle with an activating mutation of the FSH receptor has enough signal even at low FSH, equivalent to an extremely low threshold; if it is a somatic mutation affecting only one follicle, that follicle would become the dominant follicle earlier; if it is a germline mutation affecting all follicles (reported in actual cases of ovarian hyperstimulation in pregnancy), several follicles respond excessively to FSH (or to cross-reacting hCG) at once and may cause multiple follicle development.
4. Cycle length in women varies from 25 to 35 days, while the luteal phase is almost always about 14 days. According to the menstrual cycle model, what determines cycle length? If a woman's cycle suddenly lengthens to 45 days, which phase is most likely prolonged? What are the possible causes?

   **Answer** In the model the cycle consists of two segments: the time for a follicle from recruitment by the intercycle FSH rise until estradiol crosses the threshold (the follicular phase), and the intrinsic lifespan of the corpus luteum (the luteal phase, about 14 days). The latter is set by the lifespan of luteal cells and varies little; the former depends on the FSH level, the sensitivity of follicles to FSH and the growth rate, and is the main source of variation in the cycle. When the cycle lengthens to 45 days, the follicular phase (about 31 days) is most likely prolonged, that is, follicle growth is slow or selection of the dominant follicle is delayed; possible causes include weakened GnRH pulses (stress, weight loss, strenuous exercise), hyperprolactinemia, thyroid dysfunction, polycystic ovary syndrome (follicular arrest, androgen excess), or a reduced follicle reserve in the menopausal transition; of course pregnancy must be excluded first.
5. Why can a combined oral contraceptive (estrogen plus progestogen) prevent ovulation? Why does stopping it for a week after taking it for a time produce "menstruation"?

   **Answer** Exogenous estrogen and progestogen stay continuously at levels like those of the luteal phase and suppress GnRH pulses (progestogen slows the frequency) and pituitary FSH and LH by negative feedback: FSH cannot rise, follicles are not recruited, and not enough estradiol is produced to cross the threshold; at the same time progestogen inhibits the positive feedback action of estradiol, so that even if estradiol rises no LH surge occurs, and therefore there is no ovulation. Progestogen also makes cervical mucus viscous and the endometrium unfavorable for implantation. When the drug is stopped for a week, exogenous hormone levels fall abruptly, equivalent to the progesterone withdrawal after regression of the corpus luteum, and the endometrium maintained by the drug is shed with bleeding, called "withdrawal bleeding," which is not the result of a true menstrual cycle.
6. (Open question) The rabbit is an induced ovulator and the human a spontaneous one. Design a set of experiments to distinguish whether ovulation in a newly discovered mammal is spontaneous or induced, and, if induced, whether the triggering signal comes from mechanical stimulation or from chemicals in semen.

   **Answer** Outline: (1) House females separately from males and observe the ovaries for several consecutive weeks by ultrasound or laparoscopy, measuring blood progesterone (secreted by the corpus luteum after ovulation) and LH; if ovulation and progesterone rise appear periodically without mating, ovulation is spontaneous. (2) If there is no ovulation in isolation, divide into groups: mating with a vasectomized male (mechanical stimulation, no sperm but some seminal plasma), mating with a male whose accessory glands have been removed (mechanical stimulation, reduced seminal plasma), artificial mechanical stimulation of the reproductive tract (a glass rod), and intramuscular or intrauterine injection of male seminal plasma (no mechanical stimulation), and measure LH and whether ovulation occurs in the following hours. If mechanical stimulation alone can cause an LH surge while injection of seminal plasma is ineffective, ovulation is induced by a neural reflex (as in the rabbit); if injection of seminal plasma alone can cause it, seminal plasma contains a chemical signal (such as β-NGF in camels), and the active component of the seminal plasma can be further isolated and purified. (3) Give a GnRH antagonist in each group to test whether the triggering signal acts through hypothalamic GnRH.
