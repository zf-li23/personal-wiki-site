The alveolus is where gas exchange really takes place. To fit as large an exchange surface as possible into the limited volume of the thorax, the lung divides the exchange surface into about 300 million small sacs a few tenths of a millimeter in diameter, and makes the barrier between them and the blood vessels less than 1 micrometer thick. This design raises two physical difficulties: an extremely thin exchange surface must be covered by a layer of liquid, which hinders diffusion if too thick and dries out if too thin, so its amount must be precisely controlled; and the **surface tension** of this liquid layer makes these small sacs inherently unstable and inclined to collapse. This chapter first describes the structure of the alveolus (Section 18.1), then how the alveolus solves the two difficulties: supply and clearance of the liquid film (Section 18.2), and the maintenance of stability by surface tension, surfactant and the tissue network (Section 18.3). Next it turns to the other side of the alveolus, the pulmonary circulation: its low pressure and resistance, fluid balance and response to hypoxia (Section 18.4), the uneven distribution of blood flow caused by gravity (Section 18.5), and how the matching of ventilation and blood flow determines the composition of alveolar gas and arterial blood (Section 18.6). Finally it compares fish gills, bird lungs and reptilian lungs (Section 18.7). The mechanics of ventilation and the alveolar gas equation are in Chapter 17, diffusion of gases across the respiratory membrane is in Chapter 19, and lung development and the production of surfactant are in [7.5.30](/7.5.30).

## 18.1. Structure of the Alveolus

### 18.1.1. The Branching Respiratory Tree

**Definition 18.1.1 (Alveolus)** The polyhedral small sacs that open from the walls of the respiratory bronchioles, alveolar ducts and alveolar sacs are called **alveoli**; they are the basic units of gas exchange in the lung.

The two lungs of an adult contain about 300 million alveoli (the classical estimate; stereological counts average about 480 million and vary greatly among individuals, from about 270 to 790 million, Ochs and colleagues, 2004), $200$–$300\,\mathrm{\mu m}$ in diameter, with a total surface area of about $70\,\mathrm{m^2}$ ($50$–$100\,\mathrm{m^2}$), about 40 times the body surface area. The 23 generations of dichotomous branching from trachea to alveolar sacs (Chapter 17, Section 17.4.1) form a tree that almost fills space, and a volume of only about $5\,\mathrm{L}$ holds this large area; the fractal dimension of such a space-filling structure is close to 3. Alveoli are not spheres but close to tightly packed polyhedra (tetradecahedra such as the truncated octahedron are often used as models), and the alveolar septum between adjacent alveoli contains only a single capillary network and a little connective tissue, reducing the interstitium that takes no part in exchange.

The alveolar septa have pores $10$–$15\,\mathrm{\mu m}$ in diameter (pores of Kohn) that connect adjacent alveoli and provide collateral ventilation when small airways are obstructed. In emphysema the alveolar septa are destroyed, many alveoli fuse into a few large sacs, and the exchange area is greatly reduced.

### 18.1.2. Cells of the Alveolus

| Cell | Morphology and number | Function |
|---|---|---|
| Type I alveolar epithelial cell (AT1) | Extremely flat, only $0.1$–$0.2\,\mathrm{\mu m}$ thick except around the nucleus; only about half as numerous as AT2 but covering more than 90%–95% of the alveolar surface | Forms the main interface of gas exchange; takes part in ion and fluid transport (Section 18.2.2) |
| Type II alveolar epithelial cell (AT2) | Cuboidal, mostly at the corners of alveoli, with lamellar bodies in the cytoplasm | Synthesizes, secretes and recycles surfactant; secretes a little $\ce{Cl-}$ and fluid; as a stem cell of the alveolar epithelium, proliferates after injury and differentiates into AT1 |
| Alveolar macrophage | Wanders in the alveolar lumen and liquid film | Phagocytoses inhaled particles and pathogens, clears excess surfactant |
| Capillary endothelial cell | Continuous endothelium, with extremely thin cytoplasm facing AT1 | Together with AT1 forms the blood–gas barrier; metabolizes vasoactive substances (converts angiotensin I to II, inactivates bradykinin) |

The nucleus and organelles of AT1 are concentrated in one small region, and the rest of the cell is only an extremely thin layer of cytoplasm, like a spread-out film. This form makes the exchange surface as thin as possible, at the cost that AT1 itself is fragile and almost unable to divide, and must be replenished from AT2 after injury.

### 18.1.3. The Blood–Gas Barrier

The tissue between alveolar gas and capillary blood is called the **blood–gas barrier**; it consists of the alveolar epithelium (AT1), two basement membranes (fused into one over much of the area) and the capillary endothelium, with a liquid film and a surfactant layer on the surface (a complete list of the layers is in Chapter 19, Section 19.1.1). The arithmetic mean thickness of the barrier is about $2\,\mathrm{\mu m}$, but the harmonic mean thickness that is decisive (diffusion resistance averages as the reciprocal of thickness) is only about $0.6\,\mathrm{\mu m}$, and the thinnest part is about $0.2\,\mathrm{\mu m}$.

The barrier is constrained in two directions: it must be thin to favor diffusion, and strong enough to withstand the pressure in the capillaries and the stretch of lung expansion. The stress is borne mainly by type IV collagen in the basement membrane, whose strength is sufficient to resist the normal capillary transmural pressure (about $10\,\mathrm{mmHg}$). When pressure is too high (racehorses in strenuous exercise, left heart failure, high-altitude pulmonary edema) the barrier undergoes "stress failure", and erythrocytes and protein leak into the alveoli. Pulmonary hemorrhage is common in racehorses during races precisely because their **cardiac output** is enormous and pulmonary capillary pressure can exceed $70\,\mathrm{mmHg}$ (West and colleagues).

## 18.2. The Alveolar Liquid Film: Maintaining Efficiency

### 18.2.1. Thickness and Supply of the Liquid Film

The surface of the alveolar epithelium is covered by a very thin layer of liquid, the alveolar epithelial lining fluid, with an average thickness of only $0.07$–$0.2\,\mathrm{\mu m}$, composed of water, electrolytes, protein and surfactant. It is necessary: the surface of epithelial cells must be kept moist, and gas must first dissolve in the liquid phase before it can diffuse; but the thicker the liquid layer, the greater the diffusion distance, and too much liquid would fill the alveoli (pulmonary edema). The amount of the film must therefore be neither too much nor too little, and this is maintained by a balance between supply from three sources and active clearance:

- Capillary filtration: the hydrostatic pressure of the pulmonary capillaries causes a very small amount of fluid to filter continuously into the interstitium (Section 18.4.3), and a small part of it passes through the epithelium into the alveoli. The tight junctions of the epithelium are very tight, and the amount from this source is small but persistent.
- Secretion by AT2: while secreting surfactant, AT2 secretes $\ce{Cl-}$, which draws a little $\ce{Na+}$ and water into the alveoli.
- Spreading of surfactant: surfactant lowers surface tension and makes the liquid spread into a continuous, uniform thin film instead of gathering into droplets and leaving local areas dry.

### 18.2.2. Clearance of Alveolar Fluid

**Definition 18.2.1 (Alveolar Fluid Clearance)** The process in which the alveolar epithelium actively absorbs $\ce{Na+}$, establishes an osmotic gradient across the epithelium, and so causes water to move passively from the alveolar lumen into the interstitium and blood is called **alveolar fluid clearance**.

The directionality of clearance comes from the polar distribution of transport proteins in the epithelial cells (as in other absorptive epithelia, Chapter 3):

1. The epithelial sodium channel (ENaC, amiloride-sensitive) in the apical membrane is the main route for $\ce{Na+}$ entry into the cell. ENaC consists of three subunits, $\alpha$, $\beta$ and $\gamma$, of which the $\alpha$ subunit is indispensable: mice lacking $\alpha$ENaC cannot clear fetal lung liquid after birth and die within 40 hours (Hummler and colleagues, 1996). Nonselective cation channels and cyclic nucleotide-gated channels also take part.
2. The **Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase** (ouabain-sensitive) in the basolateral membrane pumps $\ce{Na+}$ into the interstitium, maintaining a low intracellular $\ce{Na+}$ and providing the driving force for apical $\ce{Na+}$ entry.
3. $\ce{Cl-}$ follows through CFTR and other channels, across the cell or by the paracellular route, maintaining electroneutrality.
4. Water follows passively down the osmotic gradient, mainly by the **paracellular pathway** and through **aquaporin** 5 (AQP5), which is abundant in AT1. AQP5 does not drive clearance: in AQP5 knockout mice the water permeability of the alveolar epithelium is greatly reduced, but the rate of alveolar fluid clearance is essentially unchanged (Ma and colleagues, 2000). The rate-limiting step of clearance is the active transport of $\ce{Na+}$, not water permeability.
5. Fluid entering the interstitium is returned to the circulation by the lymphatics, with a small part entering the pleural cavity through the visceral pleura; the driving force that moves fluid toward the lymphatics and the hilum is the negative interstitial pressure.

A former paradox was that AT2 occupies only a few percent of the alveolar surface yet was thought to carry out most of the $\ce{Na+}$ transport. It was later found that AT1 also expresses the $\alpha$ subunit of ENaC and the $\alpha_1$ and $\beta_1$ subunits of the Na⁺/K⁺ pump (Borok and colleagues, 2002), i.e., AT1, which covers most of the surface, also takes part in active transport, which explained the high rate of clearance.

Clearance is regulated. Catecholamines, through the $\beta_2$ receptor–cAMP pathway, increase the open probability and the membrane number of ENaC and strengthen the Na⁺/K⁺ pump; **glucocorticoids**, mineralocorticoids and **thyroid hormone** upregulate the expression of these proteins. The surge of fetal **epinephrine** at birth and the rise of glucocorticoid levels before delivery convert the lung within hours from an organ that secretes fluid into one that absorbs fluid, clearing the fetal lung liquid, which is a prerequisite for the newborn to begin breathing. Conversely, inflammatory factors (TNF-$\alpha$, IL-1$\beta$) inhibit these transporters, and in lung injury the clearance capacity falls and aggravates pulmonary edema.

### 18.2.3. Self-Limitation of Clearance

Clearance does not drain the liquid film dry, because there are two layers of **negative feedback**:

- Self-limitation by the osmotic gradient: the osmotic gradient established by $\ce{Na+}$ transport drives the movement of water; as fluid decreases, the solute concentration in the alveolar lumen rises relatively, the osmotic gradient weakens, and the water flux falls automatically.
- Regulation of channel number: the number of ENaC in the membrane is determined by the balance of insertion and degradation. The E3 **ubiquitin** ligase Nedd4-2 recognizes the PPxY motif on ENaC subunits, causing their ubiquitination, endocytosis and degradation. The kinase SGK1, induced by glucocorticoids and **aldosterone**, phosphorylates Nedd4-2, which then binds 14-3-3 proteins and loses its action on ENaC, so more ENaC accumulates in the membrane; whereas the MAPK pathway activated during sustained hormone action phosphorylates ENaC and strengthens its binding to Nedd4-2, partly offsetting the effect of SGK1. The same machinery also regulates $\ce{Na+}$ reabsorption by the renal collecting duct (Chapter 24), and Liddle syndrome is caused by mutations in the PPxY motif of ENaC that let the channel escape Nedd4-2 degradation.

## 18.3. Surface Tension and the Stability of Alveoli

### 18.3.1. Laplace Pressure and the Instability of Alveoli

An alveolus covered by a liquid film can be approximated as a small sphere whose inner surface is a gas–liquid interface. The pressure difference across a curved interface is given by the **Young–Laplace equation** (**excess pressure**, [6.3.19](/6.3.19); for the lung case see [Formula 30.7.1 of 7.5.30](/7.5.30#fml-30.7.1)):

**Formula 18.3.1 (Laplace Pressure of an Alveolus)** For a spherical alveolus of radius $r$ and inner surface tension $\gamma$, the pressure difference between inside and outside needed to keep it open is

$$
\Delta P = \frac{2\gamma}{r}.
\tag{18.1}
$$

In physiology this relation is often called **Laplace's law**.

At constant $\gamma$, $\Delta P$ increases as $r$ decreases. Of two alveoli of different sizes connected through the same airway, the pressure in the smaller alveolus is higher than in the larger, gas flows from the small alveolus to the large one, the small one gets smaller until it collapses, and the large one gets larger. Alveoli are never of the same size at any moment (morphometry shows that alveolar diameters are continuously distributed over about $200$–$500\,\mathrm{\mu m}$, and they also deform continuously during breathing), so this instability is a real physical threat.

**Example 18.3.1 (Size of the Laplace pressure difference)** Take the surface tension of pure water, $\gamma = 0.072\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}$. For two alveoli of diameter $200\,\mathrm{\mu m}$ ($r = 100\,\mathrm{\mu m}$) and $300\,\mathrm{\mu m}$ ($r = 150\,\mathrm{\mu m}$),

$$
\Delta P_1 = \frac{2 \times 0.072}{1.0 \times 10^{-4}} = 1440\,\mathrm{Pa},\qquad \Delta P_2 = \frac{2 \times 0.072}{1.5 \times 10^{-4}} = 960\,\mathrm{Pa},
$$

which differ by about $480\,\mathrm{Pa}$ ($4.9\,\mathrm{cmH_2O}$). For comparison, the transpulmonary pressure at the functional residual capacity in quiet breathing is about $500\,\mathrm{Pa}$ ($5\,\mathrm{cmH_2O}$), and the changes of alveolar pressure that drive airflow are only about $\pm 100\,\mathrm{Pa}$. With the surface tension of pure water alone, the driving force for a small alveolus to vent into a large one is therefore comparable to the pressure of breathing itself, enough to trigger a chain of collapse; and merely keeping all alveoli open would need a transpulmonary pressure of $10$–$15\,\mathrm{cmH_2O}$, far above the actual value. If diameter is mistakenly substituted for radius ($r = 200$ and $300\,\mathrm{\mu m}$), the pressure difference obtained is $240\,\mathrm{Pa}$, underestimated by half.

Actual alveoli do not collapse, which shows that there must be mechanisms stabilizing them: either lowering and regulating surface tension, or providing support from tissue. These are accomplished by surfactant (Section 18.3.2) and by the fiber network of the lung parenchyma (Section 18.3.4), respectively.

### 18.3.2. Pulmonary Surfactant

**Pulmonary surfactant** ([7.5.30](/7.5.30)) is synthesized by AT2, secreted into the alveolar lumen as lamellar bodies, unfolds into tubular myelin, and then forms a film at the gas–liquid interface. Its composition is about 90% lipid and 10% protein:

| Component | Content (by mass) | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Dipalmitoylphosphatidylcholine (DPPC) | About 40% | Its two saturated palmitic acid chains can pack closely, forming an ordered solid film on compression that brings $\gamma$ close to zero |
| Other phosphatidylcholines (mostly unsaturated) | About 30% | Keep the film fluid so that it spreads rapidly on expansion |
| Anionic phospholipids such as phosphatidylglycerol | About 10% | Interact with SP-B and SP-C |
| Cholesterol | About 5%–10% | Regulates membrane fluidity |
| SP-A, SP-D (hydrophilic collectins) | — | Innate immunity: bind pathogens and promote phagocytosis by macrophages; SP-A takes part in forming tubular myelin |
| SP-B, SP-C (small and hydrophobic) | — | Promote adsorption of lipid to the interface and formation of a multilayer reservoir beneath the interface; absence of SP-B is fatal in newborns |

Surfactant molecules insert into the gas–liquid interface with polar heads toward the water and hydrophobic tails toward the air, replacing water molecules at the interface and weakening the cohesion between water molecules, thereby lowering surface tension. Its most important property is that $\gamma$ varies with interfacial area. In the classical squeeze-out model: when the interfacial area decreases (expiration), unsaturated lipids and other components that are hard to compress are squeezed out of the monolayer into a multilayer reservoir beneath the interface, the interface is enriched in DPPC and forms a dense film, and $\gamma$ falls to about $1\,\mathrm{mN\,m^{-1}}$; when the area increases (inspiration), lipids in the reservoir spread again onto the interface, keeping the film intact, and $\gamma$ rises to about $25$–$30\,\mathrm{mN\,m^{-1}}$. Ordered and disordered phases coexist in the interfacial film, which is rigid enough to resist compression and can still reorganize as it deforms.

Four actions of surfactant:

1. Lowering surface tension, which raises **lung compliance** and reduces the work of breathing (Chapter 17, Section 17.4.4).
2. Making $\gamma$ fall as the alveolus shrinks, so that $2\gamma/r$ no longer increases as $r$ decreases and the internal pressures of alveoli of different sizes tend to equalize, preventing collapse of small alveoli (figure in Section 18.3.4).
3. Reducing the "suction" of surface tension on the interstitium: the surface tension of a curved liquid surface lowers the pressure beneath the liquid film and draws fluid from the capillaries toward the alveolus; surfactant weakens this effect and helps keep the alveoli dry.
4. SP-A and SP-D take part in innate immunity of the lung.

The consequences of surfactant deficiency are most obvious in premature infants: in babies born before about 34 weeks of gestation AT2 is not yet mature, and neonatal respiratory distress syndrome occurs, with collapsed alveoli (atelectasis), extremely low compliance and severe hypoxia; glucocorticoids given to the mother before birth and exogenous surfactant instilled into the trachea of the newborn are effective treatments ([7.5.30](/7.5.30)). In acute respiratory distress syndrome in adults, protein leaking into the alveoli inactivates surfactant, which is also one cause of alveolar collapse.

### 18.3.3. The Pressure–Volume Curve and Hysteresis

Both features of the air-filling curve arise from surface tension:

- The inflation limb needs a high opening pressure. Collapsed or nearly collapsed lung units must first be "blown open"; their radius is very small, and by Eq. (18.1) a very large pressure is needed; once open, the radius increases and the pressure requirement falls, many units open in succession, and the curve steepens. The first breath of a newborn must overcome this opening pressure and needs a transpulmonary pressure of $30$–$40\,\mathrm{cmH_2O}$; later breaths are much easier.
- The deflation limb lies above the inflation limb. In deflation the surfactant film is compressed and the surface tension is low (solid line in panel B), so the lung keeps a larger volume at a lower pressure; this favors stability at the end of expiration.

### 18.3.4. The Fiber Network, Interdependence and Division of Labor by Volume

The lung parenchyma contains a continuous network of **elastic fibers** and collagen fibers: the fibers run along the edges of the alveolar septa and the entrances of the alveoli and form ring-shaped load-bearing structures around the alveolar ducts. Alveoli are subject to two kinds of force: inward forces (surface tension and elastic recoil of tissue) that make them shrink or even collapse, and outward forces (the pressure difference between the pleural cavity and the alveolus, transmitted through the fiber network) that make them expand.

**Definition 18.3.1 (Alveolar Interdependence)** Adjacent alveoli share alveolar septa and are connected to one another through the fiber network; the collapse of one alveolus pulls on the walls of the surrounding alveoli and meets their opposing traction, so that it tends to be pulled open again. This property of alveoli stabilizing one another through shared structure is called **alveolar interdependence** (Mead and colleagues, 1970).

Interdependence shows that alveoli are not a string of independent "soap bubbles" but a continuous sponge; together with surfactant, it makes it hard for a single alveolus to collapse on its own.

The contributions of the two stabilizing mechanisms shift with lung volume:

| Lung volume | Main threat | Dominant mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| Low (end of expiration) | Small radius and large curvature, strongest tendency to collapse by Eq. (18.1) | Surfactant: the film is compressed and $\gamma$ falls steeply as radius decreases, offsetting the increase in curvature |
| High (end of inspiration) | Overdistension | Fiber network: elastic fibers and collagen are straightened and recoil increases rapidly, limiting further expansion |

### 18.3.5. The Identity of AT1 Is Maintained Mechanically

Breathing periodically stretches the alveolar wall. This mechanical signal not only affects structure but also helps maintain cell identity: AT1 senses the tension of the extracellular matrix through integrins and focal adhesions, the tension is transmitted through the actin cytoskeleton to the nucleus and brings the transcriptional coactivator **YAP** ([7.3.19](/7.3.19)) into the nucleus, where together with TEAD it maintains the gene expression of AT1; in mice, after YAP/TAZ are deleted in AT1, AT1 reprograms into AT2-like cells. The nucleus itself is also a force sensor: when the nuclear envelope is stretched the nuclear pores widen and YAP enters the nucleus faster (Elosegui-Artola and colleagues, 2017). Alveolar development likewise depends on the mechanical stretch of respiratory movements ([7.5.30](/7.5.30)).

## 18.4. The Pulmonary Circulation

### 18.4.1. Two Blood Circulations

The lung receives two blood supplies:

- The **pulmonary circulation** (Chapter 12): the right ventricle sends all of the mixed venous blood through the pulmonary artery into the lung, where gases are exchanged in the pulmonary capillaries and the blood returns through the pulmonary veins to the left atrium. It carries out the function of gas exchange.
- The bronchial circulation: bronchial arteries from the thoracic aorta, part of the **systemic circulation**, supply the trachea, bronchi and connective tissue of the lung, with a flow of only 1%–2% of **cardiac output**. Most of its venous blood drains into the pulmonary veins, so the left atrium receives 1%–2% more blood than the right ventricle ejects, and this portion of unexchanged venous blood slightly lowers the arterial $P_{\ce{O2}}$ (Chapter 19, Section 19.2.2).

The blood volume of the pulmonary circulation is about $450\,\mathrm{mL}$, about 9% of total blood volume, of which only about $70\,\mathrm{mL}$ is in the capillaries exchanging gases. Pulmonary vessels are highly distensible, and the lung can act as a blood reservoir: pulmonary blood volume increases in the supine position and in heart failure, and blood in the pulmonary vessels is transferred to the systemic circulation in hemorrhage; pulmonary blood volume can vary between about 50% and 200% of the normal value. The lung tissue also has abundant lymphatics that clear fluid and leaked plasma protein from the interstitium, mostly draining into the right lymphatic duct and the thoracic duct.

### 18.4.2. Low Pressure and Low Resistance

The pulmonary and systemic circulations are in series and carry the same cardiac output, but the pressure is much lower ($1\,\mathrm{mmHg} = 0.133\,\mathrm{kPa}$):

| Site | Systolic pressure / mmHg | Diastolic pressure / mmHg | Mean pressure / mmHg | Mean pressure at the corresponding systemic site |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Right ventricle | 25 | 0–1 | — | Left ventricle 120/0–10 |
| Pulmonary artery | 25 | 8 | 15 | Aorta about 95 |
| Pulmonary capillary | — | — | about 7 | about 17–30 |
| Pulmonary vein and left atrium | — | — | 2 (1–5) | Right atrium 0–2 |

By the Ohm's law of blood flow (Chapter 14), pulmonary vascular resistance $=(15 - 2)\,\mathrm{mmHg}/5\,\mathrm{L\,min^{-1}} \approx 2.6\,\mathrm{mmHg\,min\,L^{-1}}$, about 1/10 of systemic resistance. The pulmonary artery and its branches are wide, thin-walled and have little smooth muscle, so their **compliance** is high, and they can accommodate the blood ejected by the right ventricle without high pressure. The significance of low pressure is that the pulmonary capillary wall can be extremely thin (Section 18.1.3) without filtering too much fluid (Section 18.4.3); and the right ventricle needs only a thinner myocardium, doing only about 1/6 of the work of the left ventricle.

In exercise cardiac output can increase several-fold while pulmonary arterial pressure rises only slightly, because pulmonary vascular resistance falls as flow increases. There are two mechanisms: capillaries not previously open are opened (recruitment), and capillaries already open are widened (distension). This also increases exchange area and capillary blood volume, increasing the **diffusing capacity** of the lung (Chapter 19).

### 18.4.3. Fluid Balance of the Pulmonary Capillaries

The exchange of fluid across the pulmonary capillary wall likewise follows the balance of **Starling forces** (Chapter 15). By the classical estimate (Guyton):

| Force | Value / mmHg | Direction |
|---|---|---|
| Capillary hydrostatic pressure | 7 | Outward |
| Interstitial colloid osmotic pressure | 14 | Outward |
| Interstitial hydrostatic pressure (negative) | $-8$, i.e., 8 outward | Outward |
| Plasma **colloid osmotic pressure** | 28 | Inward |
| **Net filtration pressure** | $7 + 14 + 8 - 28 = 1$ | Outward |

A net pressure of about $1\,\mathrm{mmHg}$ causes a small continuous filtration of fluid, which is carried away by the lymphatics. The negative pressure of the pulmonary interstitium and lymphatic drainage keep the alveoli "dry": fluid in the interstitium does not enter the alveoli. The interstitial colloid osmotic pressure of the lung is high (pulmonary capillaries are more permeable to protein than those in muscle), so pulmonary capillary pressure is much lower than in the systemic circulation, which is necessary to maintain a net filtration near zero.

**Definition 18.4.1 (Pulmonary Edema)** The state in which fluid accumulates in excess in the pulmonary interstitium and then enters the alveolar lumen is called **pulmonary edema**.

There are two classes of cause of pulmonary edema: raised capillary hydrostatic pressure (left heart failure, mitral stenosis raising left atrial pressure; high-altitude pulmonary edema) and increased capillary permeability (infection, inhalation of toxic gas, acute lung injury). With normal plasma **colloid osmotic pressure**, obvious pulmonary edema appears only when pulmonary capillary pressure rises above about $25\,\mathrm{mmHg}$ (comparable to the plasma colloid osmotic pressure), i.e., the lung has a safety margin of about $20\,\mathrm{mmHg}$: when filtration increases, interstitial pressure rises and interstitial protein is diluted (colloid osmotic pressure falls), and lymph flow can increase to 10 times normal. In patients with chronic left atrial hypertension the lymphatics are dilated, and capillary pressures of $40$–$45\,\mathrm{mmHg}$ can be tolerated. After edema fluid fills the alveoli, the diffusion distance increases and fewer alveoli are ventilated, causing severe hypoxia; protein in the fluid also inactivates surfactant.

### 18.4.4. Hypoxic Pulmonary Vasoconstriction

Systemic arterioles dilate in hypoxia to increase oxygen delivery to tissue (Chapter 15); the arterioles of the lung do the opposite.

**Definition 18.4.2 (Hypoxic Pulmonary Vasoconstriction)** The phenomenon in which, when alveolar $P_{\ce{O2}}$ falls (below about $70\,\mathrm{mmHg}$), the smooth muscle of the adjacent small pulmonary arteries contracts and local blood flow decreases is called **hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction** (HPV).

HPV is a local mechanism peculiar to the lung and persists in the isolated lung, independent of nerves and humoral factors. The **mitochondria** of pulmonary arterial smooth muscle cells sense hypoxia and alter the production of reactive oxygen species, inhibiting voltage-gated $\ce{K+}$ channels ($\mathrm{K_v}1.5$, $\mathrm{K_v}2.1$); the resulting **depolarization** opens voltage-gated $\ce{Ca^2+}$ channels and the smooth muscle contracts; the endothelium also takes part in the regulation.

The physiological significance of HPV is to match ventilation and blood flow: when some alveoli are hypoventilated (for example a bronchus blocked by sputum), their blood flow is reduced and diverted to well-ventilated areas, reducing the entry of inadequately exchanged blood into the arteries (Section 18.6). In the fetus, the lung is filled with liquid, HPV makes pulmonary vascular resistance very high, and most of the right ventricular output bypasses the lung through the ductus arteriosus; at birth, the first breath makes alveolar $P_{\ce{O2}}$ rise abruptly, HPV is released, pulmonary vascular resistance falls by about 80% within minutes, and the pulmonary circulation is established (Chapter 38). But when hypoxia is global (altitude, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease), HPV raises vascular resistance throughout the lung, causing pulmonary hypertension and right ventricular hypertrophy; uneven constriction can expose some capillaries to excessive pressure and cause high-altitude pulmonary edema.

## 18.5. Gravity and the West Zones

In the upright position the apex of the lung is about $30\,\mathrm{cm}$ above the base, and the hydrostatic pressure of the blood column differs by about $\rho g h = 1060 \times 9.8 \times 0.3 \approx 3.1\,\mathrm{kPa}$ ($23\,\mathrm{mmHg}$). For a pulmonary circulation with a mean pressure of only 15 mmHg this is a large difference: arterial pressure at the apex may be only a few mmHg and at the base about 25 mmHg. Pulmonary capillaries are surrounded by alveolar gas, and whether a capillary is open depends on the relation between its internal pressure and alveolar pressure.

**Definition 18.5.1 (West Zones)** According to the relative magnitudes of pulmonary arterial pressure $P_{\mathrm{a}}$, pulmonary venous pressure $P_{\mathrm{v}}$ and alveolar pressure $P_{\mathrm{A}}$, the lung is divided from top to bottom into three zones (West and colleagues, 1964), called the **West zones**:

| Zone | Pressure relation | Blood flow | In normal people |
|---|---|---|---|
| Zone 1 | $P_{\mathrm{A}} > P_{\mathrm{a}} > P_{\mathrm{v}}$ | Capillaries are squeezed shut; no flow | Absent normally; appears at the apex in hemorrhage (lowered arterial pressure) or positive-pressure ventilation (raised alveolar pressure), becoming alveolar dead space |
| Zone 2 | $P_{\mathrm{a}} > P_{\mathrm{A}} > P_{\mathrm{v}}$ | Intermittent flow, with the flow determined by $P_{\mathrm{a}} - P_{\mathrm{A}}$ ("waterfall effect") | Apex to mid-lung: flow in systole, none in diastole |
| Zone 3 | $P_{\mathrm{a}} > P_{\mathrm{v}} > P_{\mathrm{A}}$ | Continuous flow, with the flow determined by $P_{\mathrm{a}} - P_{\mathrm{v}}$ | Lower lung |

The "waterfall" of zone 2: the downstream part of the vessel is squeezed by alveolar pressure into a collapsible resistance point, just as the flow of a waterfall depends on the upstream water level and the height of the lip of the fall and not on the water level downstream; blood flow is determined by $P_{\mathrm{a}} - P_{\mathrm{A}}$ and is independent of $P_{\mathrm{v}}$.

In exercise pulmonary arterial pressure rises, zone 2 at the apex changes to zone 3, and the whole lung has continuous flow. In the supine position the gravitational gradient is only about $20\,\mathrm{cm}$ in the anteroposterior direction, and the distribution of blood flow is more uniform. Ventilation is also affected by gravity, but to a smaller degree: alveoli at the base are compressed by the weight of the lung itself at the **functional residual capacity** and lie on a steeper part of the pressure–volume curve, so they expand more with each inspiration; ventilation at the base is therefore also greater than at the apex, but the difference is not as large as that of blood flow. Thus in the upright position the ventilation–perfusion ratio is high at the apex and low at the base (Section 18.6).

## 18.6. The Ventilation–Perfusion Ratio

**Definition 18.6.1 (Ventilation–Perfusion Ratio)** The ratio of alveolar ventilation per minute to pulmonary blood flow per minute, $\dot V_{\mathrm{A}}/\dot Q$, is called the **ventilation–perfusion ratio**.

For the whole lung, $\dot V_{\mathrm{A}}/\dot Q \approx 4.2/5.0 \approx 0.84$. The alveolar gas composition of a lung unit depends only on its ventilation–perfusion ratio (and on the compositions of inspired gas and mixed venous blood): by mass conservation (Chapter 17, Section 17.6.2), for $\ce{O2}$ and $\ce{CO2}$ respectively

$$
\frac{\dot V_{\mathrm{A}}}{\dot Q}\cdot\frac{P_{\mathrm{IO_2}} - P_{\mathrm{AO_2}}}{863} = C_{\mathrm{cO_2}} - C_{\bar{\mathrm{v}}\mathrm{O_2}},\qquad \frac{\dot V_{\mathrm{A}}}{\dot Q}\cdot\frac{P_{\mathrm{ACO_2}}}{863} = C_{\bar{\mathrm{v}}\mathrm{CO_2}} - C_{\mathrm{cCO_2}},
\tag{18.2}
$$

where $C$ is the gas content of blood ($\mathrm{mL}$ gas per $\mathrm{mL}$ blood), subscript $\mathrm{c}$ refers to end-capillary blood in equilibrium with alveolar gas, and $\bar{\mathrm{v}}$ to mixed venous blood. The two equations are coupled through the $\ce{O2}$ and $\ce{CO2}$ dissociation curves of hemoglobin (Chapter 19), and for a given $\dot V_{\mathrm{A}}/\dot Q$ one can solve for $P_{\mathrm{AO_2}}$ and $P_{\mathrm{ACO_2}}$.

In a normal upright person, the $\dot V_{\mathrm{A}}/\dot Q$ at the apex is about 3.3 ($P_{\mathrm{AO_2}}$ about 130 mmHg, $P_{\mathrm{ACO_2}}$ about 28 mmHg) and at the base about 0.6 ($P_{\mathrm{AO_2}}$ about 89 mmHg, $P_{\mathrm{ACO_2}}$ about 42 mmHg). The high $P_{\mathrm{AO_2}}$ at the apex is thought to be one reason why adult tuberculosis favors the apex of the lung (*Mycobacterium tuberculosis* prefers high oxygen).

The two extremes of the ventilation–perfusion ratio:

- A ratio of zero: the alveolus has blood flow but no ventilation (as in atelectasis, consolidated pneumonia, pulmonary edema), and the blood flowing through enters the arteries without exchange, called shunt (venous admixture). Even in normal people the return from the bronchial veins and the smallest cardiac veins forms an anatomical shunt of about 2%, which is one reason why arterial $P_{\ce{O2}}$ (about 95 mmHg) is lower than alveolar gas (about 100–104 mmHg).
- A ratio of infinity: the alveolus has ventilation but no blood flow (as in pulmonary embolism), and this ventilation is alveolar dead space (Chapter 17, Section 17.5.2).

Why does ventilation–perfusion mismatch cause mainly hypoxia, with little $\ce{CO2}$ retention? Imagine that half of the pulmonary blood flow passes through regions of very low ratio and the other half through regions of very high ratio. The $\ce{CO2}$ dissociation curve is nearly linear over the physiological range: the $\ce{CO2}$ not eliminated in the low-ratio region can be compensated by the extra $\ce{CO2}$ eliminated in the high-ratio region; moreover a slight rise in arterial $P_{\ce{CO2}}$ immediately stimulates the chemoreceptors (Chapter 20) to increase ventilation and return it to normal. The situation for $\ce{O2}$ differs: the **oxygen dissociation curve** is already flat above $P_{\ce{O2}} > 100\,\mathrm{mmHg}$, so blood from the high-ratio region can carry almost no more $\ce{O2}$ and cannot compensate for the low-saturation blood from the low-ratio region; increasing ventilation does not help either. Ventilation–perfusion mismatch is therefore the most common cause of clinical hypoxemia, and it increases the alveolar–arterial $P_{\ce{O2}}$ difference (Chapter 17, Formula 17.6.1), which breathing a high concentration of $\ce{O2}$ can correct (a shunt cannot, because shunted blood never contacts alveolar gas). Hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction (Definition 18.4.2) and the response of the airways to local $\ce{CO2}$ (low $P_{\ce{CO2}}$ constricts bronchioles) are two mechanisms that match ventilation and blood flow locally.

## 18.7. Evolution and Comparison

### 18.7.1. Gills and Lungs: Buoyancy and Surface Tension

The secondary lamellae of fish gills are extremely thin leaves projecting outward, held up in water by the buoyancy of water, with water filling the space between them; there is no gas–liquid interface and so no problem of surface tension. Out of water, surface tension makes the lamellae stick together and collapse, the exchange area falls abruptly, and this is why most fishes suffocate in air even though air holds far more $\ce{O2}$ than water. The exchange surface for air breathing must overcome two forces: gravity (without the support of buoyancy) and surface tension. The solution of the lung is to withdraw the exchange surface into the body, support it with a fiber network and the chest wall, and control surface tension with surfactant.

Many fishes have independently evolved air-breathing organs: the lungs of lungfishes and bichirs, the labyrinth organ above the gills of climbing perch and fighting fish, the use of the stomach or intestine in catfishes and loaches, and the use of the oral mucosa in the electric eel. Most of them live in oxygen-poor tropical waters, where the $\ce{O2}$ content of water is low and the temperature high.

### 18.7.2. The Ancient Origin of Surfactant

Surfactant is present in the lungs of all air-breathing vertebrates, as well as in the lungs of lungfishes and bichirs and the swim bladders of some bony fishes, and its phospholipid composition and the SP-B and SP-C proteins are similar among groups (Daniels and Orgeig, 2003). This shows that the surfactant system had already appeared before vertebrates came onto land, originating in the air sacs of early bony fishes and as ancient as the lung itself. Its original role may not have been to lower surface tension but "anti-adhesion": preventing the walls of the empty air sac from sticking together when it is emptied.

The composition of surfactant is adjusted with group and body temperature: in **poikilotherms** the proportions of cholesterol and unsaturated phospholipids in surfactant are higher, so it stays fluid and spreads rapidly at low temperature; mammals in **hibernation** also raise the proportion of cholesterol when body temperature falls. Mammals are rich in DPPC, which suits their smaller alveoli and higher respiratory frequency (which require $\gamma$ to be pressed close to zero).

### 18.7.3. Birds: A Thinner but Stronger Barrier

The **blood–gas barrier** of birds is thinner than that of mammals: the harmonic mean thickness is about $0.1$–$0.3\,\mathrm{\mu m}$ in most birds and about $0.3$–$0.6\,\mathrm{\mu m}$ in mammals, and the exchange area per body weight is also larger. It can be thinner without rupturing because the bird lung is rigid and its volume barely changes with breathing (ventilation is done by the air sacs, Chapter 17, Section 17.7.4), so the exchange tissue is not subjected to periodic stretch; and the extremely fine air capillaries (diameter $3$–$10\,\mathrm{\mu m}$) and blood capillaries interlace and support each other, the capillaries being "clamped" by the surrounding air capillaries so that the tension in their walls is very small. The radius of the air capillaries is much smaller than that of mammalian alveoli, so by Eq. (18.1) the effect of surface tension is greater, but they are supported by rigid tissue and need not expand and contract with breathing; the surfactant content of birds is lower and is used mainly to prevent liquid from entering the air capillaries. The alveoli of mammals must expand with each breath, and the barrier must be both thin and stretchable, which is the most fundamental design difference between the two kinds of lung.

### 18.7.4. Lungs of Reptiles and Mammals

The lungs of amphibians and many reptiles are large saccular structures with septa of varying degree on the inner wall that divide the exchange surface into small chambers (honeycomb-like small sacs, faveoli); their exchange area is far smaller than that of mammals, which suits their lower metabolic rate. In snakes often only one lung is well developed, and the posterior part is a thin-walled air sac without exchange function. The lungs of turtles and crocodilians have more complex internal partitions, and the lungs of monitor lizards have multiple chambers and unidirectional airflow (Chapter 17, Section 17.7.4). Mammals divide the exchange surface into hundreds of millions of alveoli, with an exchange area per body weight more than 10 times that of reptiles, which suits the high metabolic rate of **endothermy**; alveolar area per body weight scales with metabolic rate: the alveoli of small mammals (such as shrews) are smaller and the exchange area per body weight larger.

### 18.7.5. Lung Collapse in Diving Mammals

Deep-diving seals and whales adopt a strategy opposite to that of terrestrial mammals: letting the alveoli collapse. Seals exhale before diving and take only a little gas down; as depth increases, water pressure compresses the lung, and at about $30$–$100\,\mathrm{m}$ the alveoli collapse completely and the gas is squeezed into the cartilage-reinforced trachea and bronchi (where gas cannot be exchanged). Alveolar collapse prevents $\ce{N2}$ from dissolving into the blood at high pressure, thus avoiding decompression sickness on ascent, and also avoiding the toxicity of $\ce{O2}$ at high pressure; the $\ce{O2}$ needed for diving is stored mainly in hemoglobin and myoglobin (Chapter 19, Section 19.6). Their surfactant has a stronger "anti-adhesion" action, so that collapsed alveoli can reopen on ascent. This contrasts with terrestrial mammals, which do everything to prevent alveolar collapse, and shows that the requirement for stability depends on way of life.

## Questions

1. When the radius of an alveolus is halved, how does the pressure needed to keep it open change if surface tension is constant? What is the result if surfactant makes surface tension vary as the fourth power of the radius ($\gamma \propto r^4$)? Explain from this how surfactant stabilizes connected alveoli.

   **Answer** By Formula 18.3.1, with constant $\gamma$, $\Delta P \propto 1/r$; halving the radius doubles the required pressure, so the small alveolus has a high internal pressure, vents into the large alveolus and collapses. If $\gamma \propto r^4$, $\Delta P \propto r^3$ and halving the radius reduces the required pressure to 1/8: the internal pressure of the small alveolus is lower than that of the large one, gas flows from the large alveolus to the small one, and the two tend to equality. The condition for stability is $\mathrm{d}(\gamma/r)/\mathrm{d}r > 0$, i.e., $\gamma$ increases with $r$ faster than linearly; when a surfactant film is compressed $\gamma$ falls steeply, which satisfies this condition.

2. Why is alveolar fluid clearance nearly normal in AQP5 knockout mice, while $\alpha$ENaC knockout mice die soon after birth? What does this say about the rate-limiting step of the clearance process?

   **Answer** The driving force of alveolar fluid clearance is the osmotic gradient established by active $\ce{Na+}$ transport: entry apically through ENaC and extrusion basally by the Na⁺/K⁺ pump. Without $\alpha$ENaC, $\ce{Na+}$ cannot enter the epithelial cell, the osmotic gradient cannot be established, water cannot be absorbed, and the newborn mouse suffocates with the lung full of fetal lung liquid. Water can cross the epithelium by the paracellular route and through other channels, and AQP5 only raises the transcellular water permeability; where the rate of clearance is determined by $\ce{Na+}$ transport, water permeability is not rate limiting. The rate-limiting step is therefore the active transepithelial transport of $\ce{Na+}$.

3. A patient with mitral stenosis has a left atrial pressure of 30 mmHg. Estimate his pulmonary capillary pressure and explain whether pulmonary edema will occur; why do chronic patients tolerate it better than acute ones?

   **Answer** Pulmonary capillary pressure lies between pulmonary arterial pressure and left atrial pressure, slightly higher than left atrial pressure, so about 30 mmHg or more. By the **Starling forces** of Section 18.4.3 this already exceeds the plasma colloid osmotic pressure (about 28 mmHg), the net filtration pressure increases greatly, and in the acute situation pulmonary edema would develop rapidly. In chronic patients the lymphatics gradually dilate, and lymphatic drainage capacity can increase to more than 10 times normal; the pulmonary vessel walls also thicken and their permeability falls, so capillary pressures of 40–45 mmHg can be tolerated.

4. Why can breathing pure oxygen correct hypoxemia caused by ventilation–perfusion mismatch but hardly correct hypoxemia caused by a large shunt?

   **Answer** Lung units with a low ventilation–perfusion ratio are still ventilated, only inadequately; on breathing pure oxygen the $\ce{N2}$ in them is gradually washed out, alveolar $P_{\ce{O2}}$ rises greatly, and the blood flowing through can be fully oxygenated. Shunted blood never contacts alveolar gas (as when flowing through consolidated lung or in an intracardiac right-to-left shunt), and cannot be oxygenated no matter how high the inspired $P_{\ce{O2}}$; blood from well-ventilated areas can carry only a little more dissolved $\ce{O2}$ when pure oxygen is breathed (hemoglobin is already nearly saturated), not enough to compensate for the low oxygen content of the shunted blood. Oxygen therapy is therefore poorly effective for shunt, which is also the clinical way of distinguishing the two mechanisms.

5. The design of the alveolus faces a trade-off between "efficiency and stability". Give one example of trading efficiency for stability and one of trading stability for efficiency.

   **Answer** Trading efficiency for stability: AT2 takes no part in exchange yet occupies part of the alveolar surface and expends energy to synthesize surfactant, in return for the stability of alveoli at low volume; alveoli are roughly spherical or polyhedral, so the surface-to-volume ratio of a single unit is not the maximum, but they are mechanically stable, and the fractal branching tree compensates for total area at the level of the organ. Trading stability for efficiency: the liquid film is necessary for diffusion and for moistening the epithelium, but the surface tension it brings is the very source of alveolar instability; the blood–gas barrier is extremely thin, which gives high diffusing capacity at the price of susceptibility to stress failure at high pressure. Birds partly escape this trade-off by dividing labor between a rigid lung and air sacs.
