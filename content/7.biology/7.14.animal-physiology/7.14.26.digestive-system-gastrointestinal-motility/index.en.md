Animals obtain energy and the raw materials for building the body from food, but the macromolecules of food cannot enter cells directly: they must first be broken up mechanically in the digestive tract and hydrolyzed by enzymes to small molecules, which then cross the epithelium into blood and lymph. The digestive system is a tube that runs through the body and opens to the outside, and its contents are physiologically still "outside the body", so it carries out four tasks at once: transport, chemical processing, absorption and barrier function. This chapter is a general introduction to the digestive system. We first review how digestion came to be understood as a chemical process and then describe the structure of the gut wall (Section 26.1); next we turn to the forces that propel the contents, the electrical activity of gastrointestinal smooth muscle (slow waves, spikes and the interstitial cells of Cajal, Section 26.2), the enteric nervous system, extrinsic nerves and gastrointestinal hormones that control them (Section 26.3), and the splanchnic circulation (Section 26.4); and then follow the motility of each part from the mouth to the anus (Sections 26.5–26.9). The secretion of digestive juices is the subject of Chapter 27, the digestion and absorption of nutrients of Chapter 28, and the liver and the flow of substances among organs of Chapter 29. For the general properties of smooth muscle see [Chapter 10](/7.14.10), and for the definition of the enteric nervous system see [Chapter 7](/7.14.7#sec-7.4.5).

## 26.1. History and Basic Concepts of Digestive Physiology

### 26.1.1. From Trituration to Chemical Solution

The main controversy of the eighteenth century was whether digestion is mechanical trituration or chemical solution. Réaumur (1752) had a kite swallow perforated metal tubes containing meat; after a few hours it regurgitated them, and the meat inside was partly dissolved while the tubes were intact, showing that the stomach contains a fluid that can dissolve food. Spallanzani, in his work of 1780, carried out experiments of this kind systematically (including on himself, swallowing small linen bags and perforated wooden tubes containing food) and mixed the regurgitated gastric juice with meat in vitro, proving that digestion is mainly chemical solution by gastric juice and not just trituration; he also noticed that the solution was faster at body temperature than at room temperature.

In 1822 the American army surgeon Beaumont treated Alexis St. Martin, who had been shot in the abdomen; after the wound healed a fistula leading into the stomach remained. For about ten years Beaumont used the fistula to withdraw gastric juice, introduce food and observe the gastric mucosa directly, and in 1833 published his observations on gastric juice and the physiology of digestion: gastric juice is secreted in quantity only during eating, emotion affects gastric secretion and motility, and gastric juice outside the body can also digest food at body temperature. By the 1820s it was known that the gastric juice of a fasting animal is nearly neutral and becomes acid after feeding (Prout identified the acid of gastric juice as hydrochloric acid in 1824); Beaumont pointed out that acid alone is not enough to explain the breakdown of food. In 1836 Schwann obtained the active substance from gastric juice and named it pepsin. In the middle of the nineteenth century it was found that pancreatic juice, saliva and gastric juice differ in digestive power because they contain different chemical agents, which promoted the formation of the concept of the "enzyme" (Kühne coined the word *enzyme* in 1877). Later Pavlov studied the neural regulation of the secretion of digestive glands with sham feeding and isolated gastric pouches (Nobel Prize 1904), and Bayliss and Starling discovered secretin in 1902, which showed for the first time that a chemical messenger in the blood can regulate a distant organ and led to the concept of the "hormone" (Section 26.3.3).

### 26.1.2. Functions of the Digestive System

**Definition 26.1.1 (Digestion and Absorption)** The breakdown of the macromolecules of food in the digestive tract into small molecules that can cross the epithelium is called **digestion**. Of this, grinding food, mixing it with digestive juices and propelling it distally by the movements of the tract is called mechanical digestion, and the hydrolysis of macromolecules by the enzymes of digestive juices is called chemical digestion. The passage of the small molecules produced by digestion, together with vitamins, mineral salts and water, across the epithelium of the tract into blood and lymph is called **absorption**.

The digestive system comprises the digestive tract (mouth, pharynx, esophagus, stomach, small intestine, large intestine, anus) and the digestive glands (salivary glands, gastric glands, pancreas, liver and gallbladder, intestinal glands). Its functions can be summarized as five.

1. Transport: moving the contents from mouth to anus at a controlled rate (this chapter).
2. Digestion: mechanical and chemical digestion (Chapters 27 and 28).
3. Absorption: entry of nutrients, water and electrolytes into the **internal environment** (Chapter 28).
4. Elimination: excretion in the feces of undigestible and unabsorbable residues and of some metabolic wastes (products of bilirubin, cholesterol, metabolites of drugs).
5. Storage: the stomach holds a meal temporarily, the colon stores feces, and the liver stores glycogen, vitamins and iron (Chapter 29).

In addition, the digestive tract is the largest endocrine organ of the body (Section 26.3.3) and the largest immune organ (gut-associated lymphoid tissue, [7.15](/7.15)), and also the interface at which the body meets an enormous microbial community ([7.11.32](/7.11.32)). Digestive physiology in the broad sense also includes the sensory systems for finding and recognizing food (olfaction and taste, Chapter 43) and the regulation of feeding (Chapter 30).

### 26.1.3. Energy Budget and Diet-Induced Heat Production

Not all of the energy of ingested food can be used. It can be partitioned according to where it goes:

$$
E_{\text{intake}} = E_{\text{feces}} + E_{\text{urine}} + E_{\text{HI}} + E_{\text{maintenance}} + E_{\text{production}}.
$$

$E_{\text{intake}} - E_{\text{feces}}$ is the energy digested and absorbed (digestible energy), and subtracting the energy of the energy-containing substances in urine (mainly urea) gives the metabolizable energy; part of this is released as heat during the digestion, absorption and processing of food (the heat increment, that is, the rise in metabolic rate after eating, called the **specific dynamic action** of food in Chapter 30), and the rest is used for maintenance (basal metabolism, activity) and production (growth, storage, reproduction). Humans eating a mixed diet digest about 95%; herbivores living mainly on fiber digest only 40%–70%, and the undigested part is excreted in the feces (Section 26.10).

The digestive process itself (motility, secretion, absorption and post-absorptive synthesis) is an important source of heat. The gut wall has a rich blood flow, and the heat produced is quickly distributed through the body by the blood. In **endotherms** this heat reduces the need for specialized heat production (shivering, brown fat); in **ectotherms** the postprandial heat production and behavioral basking together raise body temperature and speed up digestion itself, and pythons even actively choose a warmer environment after a meal (Chapter 28).

**Example 26.1.1 (Diet-Induced Heat Production in a Day)** For an adult who takes in $10\,\mathrm{MJ}$ per day, if the heat increment is about 10% of the intake, that is $1\,\mathrm{MJ}$, the average power is $10^6\,\mathrm{J}/86400\,\mathrm{s} \approx 12\,\mathrm{W}$, about 15% of the resting metabolic rate (about $80\,\mathrm{W}$). The heat increment is concentrated in the few hours after a meal, when the extra heat production can reach $20$–$30\,\mathrm{W}$. The heat increment of protein (about 20%–30% of its energy) is far larger than that of carbohydrate (5%–10%) and fat (0%–3%), because absorbed amino acids must undergo energy-consuming processing such as deamination, urea synthesis and protein synthesis.

### 26.1.4. Layers of the Gut Wall

From the esophagus to the anus the gut wall is composed of four layers (from the lumen outward).

| Layer | Composition | Function |
|---|---|---|
| Mucosa | Epithelium (in direct contact with the luminal contents); lamina propria (loose connective tissue containing capillaries, lymphatics, immune cells and small glands); muscularis mucosae (thin layer of smooth muscle) | Secretion, absorption, barrier and immunity; the muscularis mucosae moves the mucosa locally, forming folds and squeezing out glandular secretions |
| Submucosa | Dense connective tissue, larger blood vessels and lymphatics, the submucosal plexus (Meissner plexus), and in some segments submucosal glands (such as the Brunner glands of the duodenum) | Support and blood supply; the plexus mainly controls secretion, absorption and local blood flow |
| Muscularis externa | Inner circular and outer longitudinal muscle (the stomach also has oblique muscle), with the myenteric plexus (Auerbach plexus) between the two layers | Motility; the plexus mainly controls motility |
| Serosa (or adventitia) | Simple squamous epithelium (mesothelium) and a thin layer of connective tissue beneath it; the esophagus (except its short abdominal part) and the lower rectum have no serosa but only an adventitia | Secretes serous fluid for lubrication so that organs slide without friction during heartbeat, breathing and intestinal peristalsis; the mesentery carries the blood vessels, lymphatics and nerves |

The pharynx, the upper third of the esophagus and the external anal sphincter are skeletal muscle, innervated by somatic motor nerves and under voluntary control; the lower two thirds of the esophagus down to the internal anal sphincter are smooth muscle.

## 26.2. Electrical Activity of Gastrointestinal Smooth Muscle

### 26.2.1. A Functional Syncytium

Gastrointestinal smooth muscle is a typical **single-unit smooth muscle** ([Chapter 10](/7.14.10)). A single smooth muscle cell is about $200$–$500\,\mathrm{\mu m}$ long and $2$–$10\,\mathrm{\mu m}$ in diameter, and about 1000 cells are arranged in parallel to form a muscle bundle. The cells within a bundle are connected by numerous low-resistance **gap junctions**, and electrical signals spread rapidly from cell to cell; different bundles fuse with one another at many points, and there are also low-resistance connections between longitudinal and circular muscle. The whole muscle layer is therefore electrically a functional syncytium: excitation that begins at one place spreads in all directions along the layer and makes a large area of muscle contract together.

Compared with skeletal and cardiac muscle, gastrointestinal smooth muscle has a low **excitability** and contracts slowly, and its rhythm is far less regular than that of cardiac muscle; it maintains a continuous weak contraction (**tone**) and its tension changes little when it is stretched (high extensibility, which suits the changes in volume of a hollow organ); it is insensitive to electrical stimulation but sensitive to stretch, temperature and chemical stimuli, which fits its **adequate stimulus**, the luminal contents.

### 26.2.2. Resting Potential, Slow Waves and Spikes

The **resting potential** of gastrointestinal smooth muscle is about $-50$ to $-60\,\mathrm{mV}$ (typically $-56\,\mathrm{mV}$), shallower than that of skeletal muscle, and is determined mainly by the **equilibrium potential** of $\ce{K+}$ but with a large background conductance for $\ce{Na+}$ and $\ce{Cl-}$; stretch, **neurotransmitters** and hormones can all change it and with it the excitability of the muscle.

**Definition 26.2.1 (Slow Wave)** The spontaneous periodic slow depolarization and repolarization of gastrointestinal smooth muscle on top of the resting potential is called a **slow wave**. The amplitude of slow waves is about $5$–$15\,\mathrm{mV}$ and their frequency about 3–12 per minute, depending on location; they determine the rhythm of contraction of gastrointestinal smooth muscle and are also called the basic electrical rhythm (BER).

The slow wave itself usually causes no contraction or only a weak one. If, during the plateau of the slow wave, the membrane potential exceeds a critical value (about $-40\,\mathrm{mV}$, called the electrical threshold), one or a train of spike potentials is superimposed. Spikes are the **action potentials** of smooth muscle; each lasts about $10$–$20\,\mathrm{ms}$ (more than 10 times as long as the action potential of a nerve) and occurs in clusters at 1–10 per second on the peak of the slow wave; their depolarization is caused by the opening of L-type voltage-gated $\ce{Ca^2+}$ channels and a large influx of $\ce{Ca^2+}$ (and a little $\ce{Na+}$), so the channels open slowly and the duration is long. The more spikes are superimposed on each slow wave, the more $\ce{Ca^2+}$ enters the cell and the stronger the contraction. Two thresholds can be distinguished: when the membrane potential exceeds the mechanical threshold (slightly below the electrical threshold), the small $\ce{Ca^2+}$ influx through L-type channels is already enough to cause a mild contraction; on reaching the electrical threshold spikes are triggered and the contraction is greatly strengthened.

| Location | Slow-wave frequency (per min) |
|---|---|
| Stomach (corpus and antrum) | about 3 |
| Duodenum | 11–12 |
| Terminal ileum | 8–9 |
| Colon | Irregular, 2–10 |

The frequency of the slow wave is the maximal frequency of contraction possible in that segment: the peristaltic waves of the gastric antrum occur about 3 times per minute, and the segmenting contractions of the duodenum at most about 12 times per minute. Nerves and hormones do not change the frequency of slow waves (or change it only slightly) but change the height of the slow-wave plateau, thereby determining whether spikes appear and how many. Sustained spike discharge, or sustained $\ce{Ca^2+}$ influx caused by hormones, causes tetanic contraction, for example the sustained contraction of sphincters.

### 26.2.3. Interstitial Cells of Cajal and the SIP Syncytium

Smooth muscle cells themselves can neither generate nor regenerate slow waves. The pacemakers of the slow wave are another kind of cell located in the muscle layers.

**Definition 26.2.2 (Interstitial Cells of Cajal)** The interstitial cells distributed in the muscle layers of the gastrointestinal tract that express the receptor tyrosine kinase Kit, are connected to one another and to smooth muscle cells by gap junctions, and can spontaneously generate and actively propagate slow waves are called **interstitial cells of Cajal** (ICC).

Cajal described these stellate, multiprocessed cells with silver staining at the end of the nineteenth century, but their pacemaker function was not demonstrated until the 1990s: *W/W*<sup>v</sup> mice with a mutation of the Kit gene lack the ICC of the myenteric plexus layer (ICC-MY), the small intestine loses its slow waves, and the smooth muscle shows only uncoordinated activity. ICC are classified by location: the ICC-MY between the longitudinal and circular muscle, which form a network, are the main pacemakers; the ICC-IM scattered within the muscle bundles are in close contact with nerve terminals and mediate enteric neurotransmission and the sensing of stretch; in the colon there is also a class of ICC-SM at the submucosal border.

ICC, smooth muscle cells (SMC) and a third kind of interstitial cell, the PDGFRα<sup>+</sup> cells that express the platelet-derived growth factor receptor α, are joined by gap junctions into an electrical unit called the SIP syncytium (SMC–ICC–PDGFRα<sup>+</sup> cell). The three kinds of cell divide the work as follows.

- ICC: pacemaking and active propagation. The cytosolic $[\ce{Cl-}]$ of ICC is high (accumulated by the $\ce{Na+}$–$\ce{K+}$–$2\ce{Cl-}$ cotransporter), and the periodic release of intracellular $\ce{Ca^2+}$ stores opens the $\ce{Ca^2+}$-activated $\ce{Cl-}$ channel Ano1 (TMEM16A); $\ce{Cl-}$ efflux depolarizes the membrane and forms the upstroke and plateau of the slow wave; T-type voltage-gated $\ce{Ca^2+}$ channels take part in synchronizing the slow waves of neighboring ICC and in the regenerative propagation of the slow wave through the ICC network. Active regeneration keeps the amplitude and speed of the slow wave over long distances, and so coordinates the rhythm and direction of contraction on the scale of the organ (such as gastric peristalsis and the propulsion of the small intestine).
- SMC: passive recipients and effectors. SMC lack the ionic mechanism for regenerating slow waves and merely receive the depolarization of ICC passively through gap junctions, and then generate spikes and contraction with their own L-type $\ce{Ca^2+}$ channels. Because the excitation of SMC varies continuously with the plateau height of the ICC output, the contraction of smooth muscle is continuously adjustable and not "all or none" as in cardiac and skeletal muscle. The weak but effective electrical coupling between ICC and SMC is a compromise: if it were too strong, the current of the ICC would be shunted by the large number of SMC and could not regenerate the slow wave; if too weak, it would not suffice to trigger the SMC.
- PDGFRα<sup>+</sup> cells: recipients of inhibitory input. The purine transmitters (β-NAD<sup>+</sup>, ADP-ribose and others) released by inhibitory motor neurons act on the P2Y1 receptors of PDGFRα<sup>+</sup> cells and activate their small-conductance $\ce{Ca^2+}$-activated $\ce{K+}$ channels (SK3), producing a strong hyperpolarization that is transmitted through gap junctions to SMC, lowers the open probability of L-type $\ce{Ca^2+}$ channels and relaxes the muscle.

This organization, "pacemaking in one cell type, execution in another and inhibition in a third", gives gastrointestinal smooth muscle both a reliable rhythm with coordination over long distances and a fine neural regulation; the structure and frequency of the ICC network differ from place to place, so that each segment shows a different pattern of motility: the powerful phasic contractions of the antrum, the segmentation of the small intestine, the complex rhythms of the colon. A loss of ICC is associated with several motility disorders, such as diabetic gastroparesis and slow-transit constipation.

### 26.2.4. Frequency Gradients and Coupled Oscillators

The slow-wave frequency of the small intestine falls gradually from about 12 per minute in the duodenum to about 8 in the ileum. When the small intestine is cut into short segments and each is recorded separately, the intrinsic frequency of each segment falls approximately continuously along the gut; in the intact intestine, however, the frequency is stepwise: the duodenum and upper jejunum have a "plateau" of identical frequency, below which is another plateau of lower frequency, with jumps in frequency between plateaus. This phenomenon can be explained by a chain of coupled oscillators: the ICC network of each segment is a **limit cycle** oscillator, and neighboring segments are coupled by gap junctions; the upper segment, with the higher intrinsic frequency, pulls the lower segment along through the coupling, so that a run of neighboring oscillators shows **frequency locking** at the same frequency; when the difference in intrinsic frequency exceeds the range that the coupling can pull in, the chain breaks into several plateaus.

The simplest model is a chain of phase oscillators (the one-dimensional nearest-neighbor form of the **Kuramoto model**, [7.9.16](/7.9.16)):

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}\theta_i}{\mathrm{d}t} = \omega_i + K\left[\sin(\theta_{i+1} - \theta_i) + \sin(\theta_{i-1} - \theta_i)\right],
$$

where $\theta_i$ is the phase of the slow wave in segment $i$, $\omega_i$ is its intrinsic angular frequency and $K$ is the coupling strength. Two oscillators can lock only if $|\omega_1 - \omega_2| \le 2K$; in a chain with a frequency gradient, the stronger the coupling, the wider and fewer the plateaus, and with infinite coupling the whole chain oscillates at one frequency.

A: 40 phase oscillators represent the ICC segments from the pylorus (0 m) to the terminal ileum (5 m), with intrinsic frequencies (dashed line) falling linearly from 12/min to 8/min; with coupling $K = 20\,\mathrm{min^{-1}}$ the actual frequency breaks into about 7 plateaus, and with $K = 80\,\mathrm{min^{-1}}$ only an upper and a lower plateau remain. B: slow waves ($\cos\theta$) at six locations over 30 s for $K = 20\,\mathrm{min^{-1}}$: within a plateau the downstream slow wave lags the upstream one by a phase, forming a wave that propagates in the aboral direction; between plateaus the downstream period is longer and a slow wave is "dropped" every few cycles](./plots/slow-wave-plateaus.py)

Within a plateau the phase of the slow wave lags progressively from the oral to the aboral side, so the slow wave propagates aborally as a wave (about $1$–$10\,\mathrm{cm\,s^{-1}}$ in the small intestine); this is one of the reasons why contractions as a whole are directed toward the anus. The jumps in frequency between plateaus mean that some slow waves cannot propagate to the next plateau. After surgical transection and reanastomosis of the small intestine, the segment below the anastomosis loses the pull of the upper segment and oscillates at a lower intrinsic frequency, which is consistent with the model. The pacemaker region of the stomach lies on the greater curvature of the mid-to-upper corpus, where the intrinsic frequency is highest and pulls the whole stomach into synchrony at about 3 per minute, so that peristalsis passes from the corpus to the pylorus; this is the same principle as the dominance of the whole heart by the **sinoatrial node** with the highest intrinsic frequency ([Chapter 13](/7.14.13)).

### 26.2.5. Excitation–Contraction Coupling

The direct signal for contraction of gastrointestinal smooth muscle is the cytosolic $\ce{Ca^2+}$ concentration and not the membrane potential itself: $\ce{Ca^2+}$ binds **calmodulin**, which activates myosin light chain kinase, so that phosphorylated **myosin** cycles with **actin** ([Chapter 10](/7.14.10)). There are two sources of $\ce{Ca^2+}$ and two modes of coupling.

- Electromechanical coupling: upstream signals (neurotransmitters, stretch) open **ion channels** in the membrane and cause spikes or graded local depolarization, and $\ce{Ca^2+}$ rises through $\ce{Ca^2+}$ entry via L-type channels and the subsequent release of intracellular calcium.
- Pharmacomechanical coupling: activation of receptors that are not ion channels (**G protein-coupled receptors**) changes $[\ce{Ca^2+}]$ and the sensitivity of the contractile apparatus to $\ce{Ca^2+}$ through **second messengers** ($\mathrm{IP_3}$ releasing intracellular calcium, or cAMP and cGMP relaxing smooth muscle), and the membrane potential need not change.

Besides neurotransmitters, local chemical factors ($\ce{O2}$, $\ce{CO2}$, pH, lactate, $\ce{K+}$) and hormones (**epinephrine**, norepinephrine, endothelin and the gastrointestinal hormones of Section 26.3.3) also regulate the contraction of gastrointestinal smooth muscle.

## 26.3. Neural and Hormonal Control of the Digestive Tract

### 26.3.1. Circuits of the Enteric Nervous System

The **enteric nervous system** (Definition 7.4.5, [Chapter 7](/7.14.7#sec-7.4.5)) is distributed throughout the gut wall; humans have about $2\times10^8$–$6\times10^8$ neurons, comparable to the spinal cord. It consists of two plexuses: the myenteric plexus (outer plexus) between the longitudinal and circular muscle layers mainly controls the speed, amplitude and frequency of motility, and the submucosal plexus (inner plexus) in the submucosa mainly controls secretion, absorption, local blood flow and the movement of the muscularis mucosae. There are many connections between the two.

The enteric nervous system contains complete local reflex circuits: intrinsic primary afferent neurons (sensing mechanical, chemical and thermal stimuli of the mucosa and stretch of the gut wall) → ascending and descending interneurons → motor neurons (excitatory or inhibitory motor neurons innervating smooth muscle, secretomotor neurons innervating epithelium and glands, vasomotor neurons innervating vessels). The enterochromaffin cells of the mucosa are a key class of sensory cells: mechanical and chemical stimulation by the contents makes them release 5-hydroxytryptamine (5-HT; about 90% of the 5-HT of the body is in the digestive tract), which activates the endings of afferent nerves.

Control is exerted by circuits at three levels.

1. Local reflexes within the enteric nervous system (an isolated segment of gut still shows peristaltic and secretory reflexes).
2. Reflexes of the enteric nervous system through the prevertebral ganglia (celiac, superior and inferior mesenteric ganglia) and sympathetic nerves, which transmit signals between widely separated parts of the digestive tract (such as the gastroileal and enterogastric reflexes of Section 26.8.3).
3. Reflexes between the enteric nervous system and the spinal cord and brainstem, such as the vagovagal reflex (sensory signals travel via the vagus nerve to the nucleus of the solitary tract and return via the vagus nerve; the receptive relaxation of Section 26.7.1) and the defecation reflex (Section 26.9.2).

The main transmitters are as follows.

| Class | Transmitters | Actions |
|---|---|---|
| Excitatory | Acetylcholine (ACh), tachykinins (substance P) | Contraction of smooth muscle, secretion of glands |
| Inhibitory | Nitric oxide (NO), vasoactive intestinal peptide (VIP), ATP and β-NAD<sup>+</sup> (purines) | Relaxation of smooth muscle (opening of sphincters, receptive relaxation, relaxation ahead of the peristaltic wave) |
| Modulatory | 5-HT, somatostatin, dopamine, CCK, enkephalins, GRP and others | Varied actions depending on the target cell and receptor, which cannot simply be classed as excitatory or inhibitory |

### 26.3.2. Extrinsic Nerves

The preganglionic fibers of the **parasympathetic nervous system** (mainly the vagus nerve, which supplies the esophagus to the transverse colon; the pelvic nerves supply the descending colon and below) terminate on enteric neurons and, through the enteric nervous system, on the whole excite motility and secretion of the digestive tract and relax sphincters; some parasympathetic postganglionic neurons release VIP and NO and have an inhibitory action (for example the receptive relaxation of the stomach). About 80% of the fibers in the vagus nerve are afferent and carry the stretch and chemical information of the gut to the brainstem; this is the main "ascending" channel for regulation of the digestive tract and one of the routes by which satiety signals reach the brain (Chapter 30).

The postganglionic fibers of the **sympathetic nervous system** release norepinephrine, which acts mainly on enteric neurons (inhibiting the release of ACh) and on blood vessels (constricting them), inhibiting motility, secretion and blood flow and contracting sphincters. Strong sympathetic excitation (as in stress, peritonitis and after surgery) can bring intestinal motility to a stop (paralytic ileus). The salivary glands are an exception, where sympathetic nerves promote the secretion of a small amount of protein-rich saliva (Chapter 27).

### 26.3.3. Gastrointestinal Hormones

**Definition 26.3.1 (Gastrointestinal Hormones)** The peptides secreted by endocrine cells scattered in the gastrointestinal mucosa that regulate, by **endocrine**, **paracrine** or neurocrine action, the motility, secretion, absorption and growth of the digestive tract and the activity of other organs (such as the pancreatic islets and the brain) are collectively called **gastrointestinal hormones**.

The endocrine cells of the digestive tract outnumber the cells of all the other **endocrine glands** of the body together. Most are "open-type" cells: conical, with apical microvilli projecting into the lumen that directly sense nutrients and pH in the lumen; a few are "closed-type" cells that do not contact the lumen and are regulated by nerves and local factors. Many gastrointestinal hormones also occur in the brain as neuropeptides (such as CCK, gastrin, somatostatin and neurotensin) and are called brain–gut peptides.

In 1902 Bayliss and Starling cut all the nerves of a loop of dog jejunum, leaving only the blood vessels, and injected dilute hydrochloric acid into the lumen; pancreatic juice was still secreted in quantity, and an extract of jejunal mucosa ground up with hydrochloric acid injected into a vein also caused pancreatic secretion. From this they discovered secretin, and three years later Starling named such chemical messengers carried by the blood "hormones" (Greek for "to arouse"). Gastrin (Edkins, 1905) and cholecystokinin (Ivy and Oldberg, 1928) were discovered soon afterward.

| Hormone | Secreting cell and location | Stimuli for secretion | Main actions |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gastrin | G cells; gastric antrum, duodenum | Peptides and amino acids, gastric distension, vagus nerve (via GRP); antral luminal pH < 3 inhibits its secretion | Stimulates gastric acid secretion (mainly through histamine from ECL cells) and growth of the gastric mucosa; enhances antral motility |
| Cholecystokinin (CCK) | I cells; duodenum, jejunum | Fatty acids, peptides and amino acids | Gallbladder contraction and relaxation of the sphincter of Oddi, secretion of pancreatic enzymes; inhibits gastric emptying; produces satiety through vagal afferent fibers |
| Secretin | S cells; duodenum, jejunum | Duodenal luminal pH < 4.5, fatty acids | Secretion of $\ce{HCO3-}$ and water in pancreatic juice and bile; inhibits gastric acid secretion and gastric emptying; growth of the exocrine pancreas |
| Glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP, also called gastric inhibitory polypeptide) | K cells; duodenum, jejunum | Glucose, fatty acids, amino acids | Stimulates **insulin** secretion (incretin effect); inhibits gastric acid secretion at high doses |
| Motilin | M cells; duodenum, jejunum | Released periodically in the fasting state; acid, fat, neural stimulation | Initiates the migrating motor complex of the interdigestive period (Section 26.8.2) |
| **Ghrelin** | X/A-like cells; gastric fundus | Fasting, falls after eating | Increases appetite (Chapter 30), stimulates **growth hormone** secretion, promotes gastric motility |
| Glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) | L cells; ileum, colon | Luminal nutrients | Stimulates insulin secretion, inhibits glucagon; delays gastric emptying (the "ileal brake"); satiety |
| Peptide YY (PYY) | L cells; ileum, colon | Fat, nutrients | Inhibits gastric emptying and pancreatic secretion; satiety |
| Somatostatin | D cells; stomach, small intestine, pancreatic islets | Gastric acidification, other gastrointestinal hormones | Widespread paracrine inhibition: inhibits the secretion of gastrin, gastric acid and almost all gastrointestinal hormones |

GIP and GLP-1 are together called incretins: oral glucose causes much more insulin secretion than intravenous glucose that produces the same rise in blood glucose, and the excess (about 50%–70%) comes from them. GLP-1 receptor agonists (such as semaglutide) have become important drugs for **type 2 diabetes** and obesity ([7.1.29](/7.1.29)). The table shows a general logic of regulation in the digestive tract: the contents themselves are the signal. Protein entering the stomach stimulates gastrin and gastric acid begins to be secreted; acid, fat and hypertonic chyme entering the duodenum stimulate secretin and CCK, which on the one hand bring pancreatic juice and bile to neutralize and digest them and on the other inhibit gastric emptying and secretion, so that the next portion of chyme enters only after this one has been dealt with. This is a **negative feedback** with the contents as the regulated quantity, and we shall meet it again in the secretion of gastric and pancreatic juice in Chapter 27.

## 26.4. The Splanchnic Circulation

The venous blood of the digestive tract, spleen and pancreas does not return directly to the inferior vena cava but flows into the portal vein and passes through the liver, where in the hepatic sinusoids it mixes with the blood of the hepatic artery and then returns to the inferior vena cava through the hepatic veins (Chapter 29). Portal blood flow is about $1050\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}}$ and, with about $300\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}}$ from the hepatic artery, the splanchnic circulation receives about one quarter of the **cardiac output** at rest ([Chapter 15](/7.14.15)). This arrangement in series has two consequences: all absorbed water-soluble nutrients (monosaccharides, amino acids) first pass through processing and storage by the liver; and the macrophages of the hepatic sinusoids (Kupffer cells) remove bacteria and other potentially harmful substances that enter the portal blood from the gut, preventing them from reaching the systemic circulation. Lipid nutrients, by contrast, are absorbed as **chylomicrons** through the lymphatics of the intestine (lacteals), bypass the liver and enter the **systemic circulation** through the thoracic duct (Chapter 28).

Blood flow in the digestive tract is matched to its degree of activity: after eating, the blood flow of the intestinal mucosa increases within minutes, up to twice the resting value (postprandial hyperemia). The mechanisms of regulation include vasodilator substances released by the mucosa and glands (the gastrointestinal hormones CCK, VIP, gastrin and secretin, and bradykinin), the fall in tissue $\ce{O2}$ and accumulation of adenosine caused by metabolism, indirect vasodilation by the parasympathetic nerves through increased glandular activity and hormone secretion, and the direct vasoconstrictor action of the sympathetic nerves.

The vessels of the intestinal villus are arranged countercurrently: an arteriole rises along the axis of the villus to its tip, then branches into a capillary network that descends along the surface of the villus and drains into a venule. The ascending and descending vessels are only about $20$–$30\,\mathrm{\mu m}$ apart, and $\ce{O2}$ can diffuse directly from the arteriole to the neighboring venule without passing through the tip of the villus (**countercurrent exchange**, the same as in the vasa recta of the renal medulla in Chapter 24). This structure allows nutrients and water absorbed at the base of the villus to be carried away rapidly, but at the price of a lower $\ce{O2}$ partial pressure at the tip of the villus. Normally this hypoxia may favor epithelial turnover and barrier function; when blood flow is severely reduced (shock, mesenteric ischemia), the tips of the villi are the first to become hypoxic and necrotic.

The splanchnic circulation is also a mobilizable blood reserve. The splanchnic vascular bed (especially the liver and the mesenteric veins) contains a large volume of blood, and sympathetic excitation constricts splanchnic arterioles and veins, shifting several hundred milliliters of blood to the systemic circulation within tens of seconds while reducing splanchnic blood flow to a small fraction of the resting value, giving way to exercising muscle, heart and brain ([Chapter 14](/7.14.14), [Chapter 16](/7.14.16)).

## 26.5. Purposes and Basic Forms of Gastrointestinal Motility

The movements of the digestive tract serve at least six purposes.

1. Mixing: bringing food into full contact with digestive juices.
2. Propulsion: moving the contents distally at a controlled rate and holding them where necessary.
3. Spreading: bringing the contents into close contact with the mucosal surface to promote absorption.
4. Clearing: sweeping out residual food, shed cells and bacteria between meals (Section 26.8.2).
5. Grinding and fragmentation: the antrum grinds food particles to below $1$–$2\,\mathrm{mm}$.
6. Providing sensory signals: stretch of the gut wall and contact of the contents are the input of many reflexes.

There are two basic forms of movement: propulsive peristalsis, and nonpropulsive contractions that mainly serve mixing (segmentation and the to-and-fro movement of the antrum).

**Definition 26.5.1 (Peristalsis)** When a segment of the digestive tract is distended by its contents, the circular muscle on the oral side of the distension contracts and that on the aboral side relaxes, and this contraction ring together with the relaxed segment moves along the tract toward the anus and propels the contents forward; this movement is called **peristalsis**.

The directionality of peristalsis comes from the polarity of the enteric nervous system. Bayliss and Starling described the "law of the intestine" in 1899: stimulation of a point of the intestine causes contraction orally and relaxation aborally. Its neural basis is as follows: distension excites the intrinsic primary afferent neurons, which through ascending interneurons excite the excitatory motor neurons on the oral side (ACh, substance P) and through descending interneurons excite the inhibitory motor neurons on the aboral side (NO, VIP, ATP). The contraction ring pushes the contents into the relaxed segment ahead, the contents distend the next segment, the reflex occurs again and the contraction ring advances. Atropine cannot abolish peristalsis completely, because the excitatory neurons also release tachykinins; when the enteric nervous system is absent (as in congenital megacolon, Hirschsprung disease, in which the distal colon congenitally lacks ganglia), that segment cannot relax and the contents accumulate above it.

## 26.6. Mastication and Swallowing

### 26.6.1. Mastication

The front teeth cut food and the back teeth grind it; the masticatory muscles (temporalis, masseter, pterygoids) provide the force of chewing, and the maximal bite force of humans can reach about $900\,\mathrm{N}$ (roughly the weight of 90 kg). Mastication is controlled by the brainstem, and its rhythm is generated by a **central pattern generator** in the brainstem (Chapter 6); food in the mouth causes the chewing reflex: the bolus presses on the mouth and reflexly relaxes the jaw muscles, the lowering of the jaw causes a **stretch reflex** that makes the muscles contract again, and so on repeatedly. The functions of chewing are to break the cellulose walls of plant cells (unchewed vegetables are hardly digested at all); to cut food up and increase the contact area between digestive juices and food (digestive enzymes act only on the surface of particles); to mix food with saliva into a bolus that is easy to swallow; and to stimulate taste and reflexly initiate the cephalic phase of gastric and pancreatic secretion (Chapter 27).

### 26.6.2. Swallowing

Swallowing is a complex, precisely timed reflex act with three phases.

1. Oral phase (voluntary): the tongue pushes the bolus upward and backward into the pharynx.
2. Pharyngeal phase (involuntary, about $1\,\mathrm{s}$): the bolus stimulates the swallowing receptor area of the pharynx, and impulses travel through the trigeminal, glossopharyngeal and vagus nerves to the brainstem. The swallowing center (sensory input in the nucleus of the solitary tract, motor output in the nucleus ambiguus, located in the medulla and lower pons) initiates a fixed motor program: the soft palate rises and seals the nasopharynx, the larynx rises, the epiglottis folds down and the glottis closes, sealing the airway, the upper esophageal sphincter relaxes, and the pharyngeal constrictors contract in sequence from top to bottom to push the bolus into the esophagus. At the same time the swallowing center briefly inhibits the respiratory center of the medulla, so that breathing is interrupted for about $1$–$2\,\mathrm{s}$ in any phase.
3. Esophageal phase (involuntary): the peristalsis initiated by the swallowing center is called primary peristalsis and passes from the pharynx along the esophagus to the stomach, taking about $8$–$10\,\mathrm{s}$ (in the upright position gravity brings liquids there faster). If primary peristalsis fails to deliver the entire bolus to the stomach, the residual bolus distends the esophageal wall and causes secondary peristalsis through the enteric nervous system and the vagovagal reflex, until the esophagus is emptied.

The esophagus is about $25\,\mathrm{cm}$ long, so the speed of primary peristalsis is about $2.5$–$3\,\mathrm{cm\,s^{-1}}$. The upper third of the esophagus is skeletal muscle, activated directly in sequence according to a program by somatic motor fibers in the vagus nerve; the lower part is smooth muscle controlled by the vagus nerve through the enteric nervous system, and the middle part is a mixture of the two kinds of muscle. The circular muscle of the lower $3$–$5\,\mathrm{cm}$ of the esophagus normally maintains a tonic contraction of about $20\,\mathrm{mmHg}$ ($1\,\mathrm{mmHg} = 133.3\,\mathrm{Pa}$, about $2.7\,\mathrm{kPa}$; clinically, pressures in the digestive tract are still customarily given in mmHg), called the lower esophageal sphincter (gastroesophageal sphincter), which prevents reflux of gastric contents; before the peristaltic wave arrives, inhibitory neurons (NO, VIP) relax it and the bolus enters the stomach. Too low a tone of the lower esophageal sphincter or too many transient relaxations cause gastroesophageal reflux disease; loss of the inhibitory neurons of the myenteric plexus, so that the sphincter cannot relax, causes achalasia.

## 26.7. Motility of the Stomach

The stomach has three motor functions: to store a large amount of food until it can be processed in the duodenum; to mix the food with gastric juice into a semifluid chyme; and to empty chyme into the duodenum at a rate suited to digestion and absorption in the small intestine. Functionally the stomach is divided into a proximal orad region (the fundus and upper corpus, with mainly tonic contraction, responsible for storage) and a distal caudad region (the lower corpus and antrum, with mainly peristalsis, responsible for mixing, grinding and emptying).

### 26.7.1. Receptive Relaxation

**Definition 26.7.1 (Receptive Relaxation)** The reflex relaxation of the smooth muscle of the gastric fundus and upper corpus, caused during chewing and swallowing by food stimulating receptors in the pharynx and esophagus and after food enters the stomach by distension of the gastric wall, which enlarges the volume of the stomach with essentially no rise in intragastric pressure, is called **receptive relaxation**.

The first part (relaxation caused by stimulation of the pharynx and esophagus) occurs before the food arrives and is a **feedforward control**; the second part (relaxation caused by distension of the gastric wall, often called adaptive relaxation) is a vagovagal reflex: stretch receptors in the gastric wall send signals through vagal afferent fibers to the brainstem, and vagal efferent fibers excite inhibitory neurons in the gastric wall that release NO and VIP. Local reflexes of the enteric nervous system also take part. Receptive relaxation allows the stomach to accommodate $0.8$–$1.5\,\mathrm{L}$ of food with an increase of intragastric pressure of only a few mmHg; after vagotomy, the receptivity of the stomach is greatly reduced and patients feel full after eating only a small amount. This dysfunction is one of the causes of functional dyspepsia.

### 26.7.2. Mixing and Grinding

The pacemaker region on the greater curvature of the mid-to-upper corpus produces slow waves at about 3 per minute, which propagate toward the pylorus, becoming faster and stronger as they go (the circular muscle of the antrum is thicker). After eating, spikes are superimposed on the slow waves and peristaltic waves are produced. When a peristaltic wave reaches the antrum, the contraction ring speeds up while the pylorus closes, and most of the chyme is squeezed back into the corpus (retropulsion), only a few milliliters of liquid and particles smaller than $1$–$2\,\mathrm{mm}$ passing through the pylorus into the duodenum. This to-and-fro retropulsion grinds and mixes the chyme repeatedly in the antrum and acts as a "pyloric pump". Larger particles remain in the stomach until they are ground smaller or are cleared out in the interdigestive period by the migrating motor complex (Section 26.8.2).

### 26.7.3. Gastric Emptying

**Definition 26.7.2 (Gastric Emptying)** The passage of chyme from the stomach into the duodenum is called **gastric emptying**.

The driving force of gastric emptying is the difference between intragastric and intraduodenal pressure, produced mainly by contraction of the antrum (the pyloric pump), and the resistance is the pyloric sphincter. Complete emptying of a mixed meal takes $3$–$4\,\mathrm{h}$. The emptying of liquids is approximately exponential, with a half-emptying time of about $10$–$20\,\mathrm{min}$, and depends mainly on the gastroduodenal pressure difference created by the tone of the proximal stomach; solid food has first a lag phase (about $30$–$60\,\mathrm{min}$, the time needed to grind it small enough to pass the pylorus) and is then emptied approximately linearly. The emptying rates of the three nutrients are carbohydrate > protein > fat.

Gastric emptying is controlled by two groups of opposing factors.

- Promoting factors from the stomach: the volume of gastric contents (distension of the gastric wall strengthens the pyloric pump and inhibits the pyloric sphincter through local reflexes of the enteric nervous system and the vagovagal reflex); gastrin strengthens antral motility.
- Inhibitory factors from the duodenum (more important): duodenal distension, acid (pH < 3.5–4), hypertonicity, breakdown products of fat and protein, and mucosal irritation inhibit gastric emptying by two routes. One is the neural enterogastric reflex, through the enteric nervous system, the prevertebral sympathetic ganglia and vagal afferent fibers, which inhibits antral contraction and increases the tone of the pyloric sphincter; its response to duodenal acidification is the fastest and appears within seconds. The other is hormonal: CCK (the most important, caused by the breakdown products of fat and protein), secretin, GIP, GLP-1 and PYY.

This set of feedbacks makes the duodenum "collect" chyme at a rate it can process: the rate of gastric emptying is ultimately limited by the caloric content of the nutrients in the duodenum, about $2$–$3\,\mathrm{kcal}$ per minute ($8$–$13\,\mathrm{kJ}$), over a considerable range independent of the volume and composition of the food. This is a **negative feedback** with duodenal contents as the regulated quantity: each portion of chyme, after entering the duodenum, inhibits the emptying of the next until it has been neutralized and digested.

**Example 26.7.1 (Emptying Time of a Meal)** For a mixed meal of $600\,\mathrm{kcal}$ ($2.5\,\mathrm{MJ}$), if emptying is limited by calories to about $2.5\,\mathrm{kcal\,min^{-1}}$, emptying takes about $240\,\mathrm{min} = 4\,\mathrm{h}$, in agreement with measurement. The same volume of plain water contains no calories and is limited only by the pressure difference, so most of it is emptied in twenty or thirty minutes; and $300\,\mathrm{kcal}$ of concentrated sugar solution, although a liquid, has its emptying time prolonged to about $2\,\mathrm{h}$. This shows that gastric emptying is controlled mainly by calories and osmolality and not by the physical form of the food.

## 26.8. Motility of the Small Intestine

### 26.8.1. Segmentation and Peristalsis

**Definition 26.8.1 (Segmentation)** The rhythmic contraction and relaxation of mainly the circular muscle of the small intestine, in which on a stretch of gut containing chyme the circular muscle contracts simultaneously at many points and divides the chyme into many segments, after which the sites that contracted relax and the sites that were relaxed contract, so that each segment is divided in two and neighboring halves join to form new segments, and so on repeatedly, is called **segmentation**.

The frequency of segmentation equals that of the slow wave of the segment (at most about 12 per minute in the duodenum and 8–9 in the ileum) and is regulated by the activity of the enteric nervous system. Its functions are to mix chyme thoroughly with digestive juices to facilitate chemical digestion; to bring chyme into close contact with the gut wall, creating conditions for absorption; and to squeeze the gut wall, which aids the return of blood and lymph. Segmentation itself hardly propels the contents, but because the frequency in the upper segment is higher than that in the lower, there is a weak net downward push.

Peristaltic waves of the small intestine travel at about $0.5$–$2\,\mathrm{cm\,s^{-1}}$ and die out after traveling only about $3$–$5\,\mathrm{cm}$, faster proximally than distally. The net movement of chyme in the small intestine is therefore very slow, averaging only about $1\,\mathrm{cm\,min^{-1}}$, and it takes $3$–$5\,\mathrm{h}$ from the pylorus to the ileocecal valve, which gives ample time for digestion and absorption; as it moves, the chyme is spread over the surface of the gut wall. When the intestinal mucosa is strongly stimulated (as in infectious diarrhea), a peristaltic rush appears, traveling fast and far and sweeping the contents into the colon within minutes, which serves to clear them.

Motility of the small intestine is caused mainly by mechanical distension of the gut wall by chyme through local reflexes of the enteric nervous system, and is also regulated by extrinsic nerves and hormones.

- After eating, distension of the stomach causes a general increase in small intestinal motility through the enteric nervous system and extrinsic nerves (the gastroenteric reflex).
- Gastrin, CCK, motilin and 5-HT enhance small intestinal motility, whereas secretin, somatostatin, VIP, GLP-1 and PYY inhibit it.
- Parasympathetic nerves enhance and sympathetic nerves inhibit small intestinal motility.

### 26.8.2. The Migrating Motor Complex

Between meals (starting about $2$–$3\,\mathrm{h}$ after eating), the motility pattern of the stomach and small intestine is completely different from that of the digestive period.

**Definition 26.8.2 (Migrating Motor Complex)** The periodic pattern of motility that appears in the stomach and small intestine in the interdigestive period, with a period of about $90$–$120\,\mathrm{min}$ and migrating slowly from the stomach (or duodenum) along the small intestine to the ileum, is called the **migrating motor complex** (MMC). Each cycle is divided into three phases: phase I is a quiescent period (about 40%–60%); phase II consists of irregular contractions; and phase III is a period of strong contractions lasting about $5$–$10\,\mathrm{min}$, in which the segment contracts with every slow wave (the frequency reaching the maximum set by the slow-wave frequency).

Phase III acts as a "street sweeper": in the stomach, strong contractions with the pylorus open sweep large particles that could not pass the pylorus in the digestive period, shed cells and mucus into the small intestine, and then drive them along the small intestine toward the ileum at a few centimeters per minute (about $5$–$10\,\mathrm{cm\,min^{-1}}$ proximally, slowing distally), taking about $1.5\,\mathrm{h}$ to reach the terminal ileum, by which time the next cycle begins in the stomach. It prevents excessive proliferation of bacteria in the small intestine: patients lacking the MMC (as in scleroderma and diabetic autonomic neuropathy) are prone to small intestinal bacterial overgrowth. The "stomach growling" of fasting mostly occurs in phase III.

The MMC is generated by the enteric nervous system and regulated by hormones: the appearance of phase III in the stomach is synchronized with periodic peaks of plasma motilin, and intravenous motilin can induce phase III; erythromycin is an agonist of the motilin receptor and is used clinically as a prokinetic drug. Ghrelin also promotes gastric phase III. Eating interrupts the MMC immediately and switches to the motility pattern of the digestive period.

### 26.8.3. The Ileocecal Sphincter

The circular muscle at the junction of the terminal ileum and the cecum is thickened and forms the ileocecal sphincter, which together with the ileocecal valve separates the small and large intestines. The sphincter is normally in a state of mild contraction, with a pressure about $20\,\mathrm{mmHg}$ ($2.7\,\mathrm{kPa}$) higher than that of the colon, which keeps chyme in the ileum for a longer time and favors complete digestion and absorption; the structure of the ileocecal valve is such that the valve closes when colonic pressure rises, preventing the reflux of colonic contents (containing large numbers of bacteria) into the ileum.

Control of the ileocecal region is likewise feedback in nature: distension or irritation of the cecum causes, through the enteric nervous system and reflexes of the prevertebral ganglia, contraction of the sphincter and inhibition of ileal motility; at the next meal, distension of the stomach causes enhanced ileal peristalsis and relaxation of the sphincter (the gastroileal reflex), and gastrin also relaxes the sphincter, sending the chyme in the ileum into the cecum. The thinner the chyme and the higher the ileal pressure, the more easily it passes. About $1.5$–$2\,\mathrm{L}$ of chyme enters the cecum each day.

## 26.9. Motility of the Large Intestine, Defecation and Vomiting

### 26.9.1. Motility of the Colon

The functions of the colon are to absorb water and electrolytes (the proximal half) and to form and store feces (the distal half) (Chapter 28). Its motility is accordingly slow and mainly mixing, and the contents stay in the colon for $1$–$3\,\mathrm{d}$, much longer than in the small intestine.

- Haustration: contraction of the circular muscle and of the three longitudinal muscle bands along the colon (the teniae coli) divides the colon into pouches (haustra), and the contraction of the pouches slowly churns the contents and brings them into full contact with the mucosa, similar to segmentation of the small intestine.
- Mass movement: 1–3 times a day, in a stretch of the transverse or descending colon (about $20\,\mathrm{cm}$), the haustra disappear and the whole segment contracts, rapidly driving the contents into the next segment, lasting $10$–$30\,\mathrm{min}$. Mass movements most often occur within $15\,\mathrm{min}$ after breakfast and are caused by distension of the stomach and duodenum, called the gastrocolic reflex and the duodenocolic reflex, mediated by the enteric nervous system and the autonomic nerves. Mass movement pushes feces into the rectum and often causes the urge to defecate.

### 26.9.2. The Defecation Reflex

The rectum is normally almost empty, because there is a functional sphincteric action at the junction of the sigmoid colon and rectum. After mass movement has pushed feces into the rectum, distension of the rectal wall triggers the defecation reflex. The anus has two sphincters: the internal sphincter is a thickening of the circular smooth muscle, innervated by autonomic nerves and involuntary; the external sphincter is skeletal muscle, innervated by the pudendal nerve (a somatic nerve) and under voluntary control.

- Intrinsic defecation reflex: distension of the rectum causes, through the enteric nervous system (myenteric plexus), peristalsis of the descending colon, sigmoid colon and rectum and at the same time relaxes the internal sphincter. If the external sphincter also relaxes, defecation occurs. This reflex is weak and alone is not enough to accomplish defecation.
- Parasympathetic defecation reflex: stretch signals from the rectum travel via the pelvic nerve to the sacral cord, and parasympathetic efferent fibers in the pelvic nerve greatly strengthen the peristalsis of the colon, rectum and anus and relax the internal sphincter, amplifying the effect of the intrinsic reflex many times. At the same time, other movements initiated by the spinal cord assist defecation: after a deep inspiration the glottis is closed, the abdominal muscles contract to raise intra-abdominal pressure, and the pelvic floor muscles relax.

Defecation is under voluntary control by the cerebral cortex: the external sphincter can be kept contracted under conscious control, inhibiting defecation; the rectum then gradually accommodates and the reflex subsides within a few minutes, until more feces enter the rectum and cause it again. In general the urge to defecate appears when rectal pressure rises to about $18\,\mathrm{mmHg}$ ($2.4\,\mathrm{kPa}$), and at about $55\,\mathrm{mmHg}$ ($7.3\,\mathrm{kPa}$) both sphincters relax and defecation is involuntary. Spinal cord injury interrupts control from above the sacral cord, and reflex defecation persists but without voluntary control. The defecation reflex of infants is automatic before the development of cortical control.

### 26.9.3. Vomiting

Vomiting is a protective reflex that forcefully expels the contents of the stomach (sometimes together with those of the duodenum) through the mouth. Stimuli that cause vomiting include excessive distension or irritation of the digestive tract (especially the duodenum), toxins (bacterial toxins and chemotherapeutic drugs make enterochromaffin cells release 5-HT, which excites vagal afferent fibers through 5-HT<sub>3</sub> receptors), chemicals in the blood (acting on the area postrema at the floor of the fourth ventricle in the medulla, which has no **blood–brain barrier** and is called the chemoreceptor trigger zone), abnormal vestibular stimulation (motion sickness) and psychological factors. These signals converge on the vomiting center near the nucleus of the solitary tract in the medulla, which coordinates vomiting with a fixed motor program: first nausea and copious salivation, and reverse peristalsis in the small intestine that returns duodenal contents to the stomach; then a deep inspiration, closure of the glottis, elevation of the soft palate sealing the airway and nasopharynx, strong contraction of the diaphragm and abdominal muscles compressing the stomach, while the lower esophageal sphincter and esophagus relax and the gastric contents are expelled. Severe or repeated vomiting loses large amounts of $\ce{H+}$, $\ce{Cl-}$ and $\ce{K+}$ and causes **metabolic alkalosis** and hypokalemia ([Chapter 25](/7.14.25)). 5-HT<sub>3</sub> receptor antagonists (ondansetron) block this pathway peripherally and centrally and are the main antiemetic drugs in chemotherapy.

## 26.10. Evolution and Comparison

### 26.10.1. From Intracellular Digestion to the Digestive Tube

Protozoa (such as amoebae) engulf food by phagocytosis, enclose it in a food vacuole and digest it intracellularly with the hydrolytic enzymes of **lysosomes**; this is intracellular digestion. The choanocytes of sponges also feed in this way. Cnidarians and flatworms have a digestive cavity with only one opening (a gastrovascular cavity): gland cells secrete enzymes into the cavity for extracellular digestion first, and the cells of the cavity wall then engulf fragments to complete intracellular digestion; undigestible residues are still expelled through the mouth. Most bilaterally symmetrical animals from nematodes and annelids onward have a through-gut with separate mouth and anus, in which food moves in one direction and the tube can be divided along its long axis into regions of different function (storage, grinding, digestion, absorption, water absorption), each handling food of a different stage at the same time like an assembly line. The advent of extracellular digestion allowed animals to use large pieces of food, without being limited by the phagocytic capacity of a single cell. Traces of intracellular digestion remain in the human body: disaccharides and small peptides undergo their final hydrolysis at the brush border and inside the cells of the intestinal epithelium (Chapter 28).

### 26.10.2. Foregut, Midgut and Hindgut of Insects

The digestive tract of insects is divided into three parts: the foregut and hindgut are formed by invagination of the **ectoderm** and lined with chitinous cuticle, which is renewed with the exoskeleton at every **molt**; the midgut is derived from the **endoderm** and is the main site of enzyme secretion and absorption. The foregut includes the pharynx, esophagus, crop (storing food) and proventriculus (with chitinous teeth that grind food); the epithelium of the midgut secretes the peritrophic membrane, a semipermeable thin membrane of **chitin** and protein that separates the food from the epithelium and protects the epithelium from mechanical damage and attack by pathogens, and at the same time divides digestion into an endoperitrophic and an ectoperitrophic compartment in which enzymes are recycled between the compartments (similar to the bile acid cycle of vertebrates); the hindgut absorbs water and ions and, together with the Malpighian tubules, takes part in excretion and **osmoregulation** (Chapter 21). Termites and many beetles house symbiotic microbes in an enlarged hindgut and carry out the fermentation of cellulose (Chapter 28).

### 26.10.3. Crop and Gizzard of Birds

Birds have no teeth and do not chew. In many birds (such as pigeons and chickens) the lower esophagus is enlarged to form a crop, which temporarily stores swallowed food and lets the bird take in a large quantity in a short time and digest it slowly in a safe place afterward. In the pigeon during the rearing of the young, the epithelium of the crop proliferates and is shed under the action of **prolactin**, forming "crop milk" rich in fat and protein with which the young are fed. The avian stomach has two parts: the anterior glandular stomach (proventriculus) secretes gastric acid and pepsinogen and corresponds to the acid-secreting region of mammals; the posterior muscular stomach (gizzard) has well-developed smooth muscle and a hard keratinoid lining, often contains swallowed grit, and grinds food with strong rhythmic contractions, taking the place of teeth. To-and-fro movement between the gizzard and the proventriculus moves food back and forth between them, which is functionally similar to the retropulsion of the mammalian antrum. Seed-eating birds have a well-developed gizzard, whereas carnivorous and fruit-eating birds have a small one. The size of the gizzard can also change seasonally: the red knot eats soft insects on its Arctic breeding grounds but swallows hard-shelled bivalves whole at its stopover and wintering sites, and its gizzard shrinks or enlarges accordingly within a few weeks.

### 26.10.4. Rumination

The stomach of ruminants (cattle, sheep, deer, giraffes and others) has four chambers: **rumen**, reticulum, omasum and abomasum. The first three are the forestomachs, which, like the abomasum, develop from the embryonic stomach (an older view derived them from the esophagus); they have no digestive glands and are lined with stratified squamous epithelium; the rumen contains large numbers of bacteria, archaea, protozoa and fungi that ferment cellulose (Chapter 28). The abomasum is the "true stomach" that secretes gastric acid and pepsin. Rumination is the process in which a coarse bolus of food from the rumen is regurgitated into the mouth, chewed again and swallowed again: a complex sequence of contractions of the reticulum and rumen drives reticular contents toward the cardia, while an inspiratory movement with the glottis closed lowers intrathoracic pressure and sucks the bolus into the esophagus, from which reverse peristalsis returns it to the mouth. Cattle ruminate about $8\,\mathrm{h}$ a day, chewing tens of thousands of times. Rumination grinds plant fiber repeatedly and increases the surface area for microbial attachment; only particles ground fine enough and dense enough can pass the reticulo-omasal orifice into the following chambers, and coarse particles remain in the rumen for further fermentation. This "sorting by particle size" has the same principle as the passage of only small particles through the mammalian pylorus.

### 26.10.5. Conservation of Slow-Wave Pacemaking

The use of slow waves to drive the motility of the digestive tract is highly conserved in vertebrates: the gastrointestinal tracts of fishes, amphibians, reptiles, birds and mammals all have slow waves, and the pacemaker cells are all ICC-like cells expressing Kit; the gut of zebrafish embryos already contracts rhythmically before the arrival of enteric neurons of **neural crest** origin, and the gut motility of Kit-mutant zebrafish is disordered. This shows that the rhythm arises in the muscle layer itself (myogenic) and that nerves mainly serve to modulate, as in the vertebrate heart ([Chapter 13](/7.14.13)). In invertebrates the rhythm of the digestive tract often comes from nerves: the stomatogastric ganglion of crustaceans (about 30 neurons) produces the rhythmic chewing of the gastric mill and the filtering movements of the pylorus, and is a classic model for the study of **central pattern generators** and neuromodulators ([Chapter 6](/7.14.6)); the digestive tracts of mollusks and insects are also controlled by a stomatogastric nervous system. The rhythm of the digestive tract thus provides an example parallel to that of the heart: the same functional need (a sustained, adjustable rhythm) is met by the muscle itself or by neural circuits in different groups.

## Questions

1. The slow-wave frequency of the stomach is about 3 per minute and that of the duodenum about 12 per minute. Why is the rhythm of gastric emptying not the rhythm of the duodenum? What would happen if there were no electrical isolation between the antrum and the duodenum?

   **Answer** At the pylorus between stomach and duodenum there is little electrical connection in the muscle layer (a layer of connective tissue separates them), and the two pacemaker systems are essentially independent, each oscillating at its own intrinsic frequency. The rhythm of gastric emptying is determined by antral peristalsis (3 per minute), whereas segmentation of the duodenum proceeds at 12 per minute, and the two are coordinated by the opening and closing of the pylorus and the enterogastric reflex. If the two were strongly coupled electrically, then by the principle of coupled oscillators the duodenal pacemaker, of higher frequency, would try to pull the antrum along (or the two would lock at an intermediate frequency); the slow waves of the antrum would be too fast, peristalsis would not have time to grind adequately, and the contractions of the duodenum might also propagate retrogradely into the stomach, destroying the coordination of emptying and the prevention of reflux. Electrical isolation at the pylorus is therefore, like that at the ileocecal region, necessary for segmental control.

2. Use the phase-oscillator model of Section 26.2.4 to explain why the slow-wave frequency of the segment below the anastomosis falls after surgical transection and reanastomosis of the small intestine.

   **Answer** In the intact small intestine, the oscillators of the lower segments, with lower intrinsic frequencies, are pulled by coupling to the higher-frequency oscillators above and locked at a higher plateau frequency. After transection and reanastomosis, the tissue at the anastomosis (scar) makes the coupling $K$ almost zero, the first segment below loses the pull from above and can be influenced only by the oscillators below it with still lower frequencies, and the whole chain below re-forms plateaus at a lower frequency. As the muscle layer and the ICC network at the anastomosis gradually regenerate, coupling is restored and the frequency partially recovers.

3. A patient has had a vagotomy for a gastric ulcer. Predict the changes in his gastric motility and explain the reasons.

   **Answer** (1) Receptive relaxation is impaired: receptive relaxation is mainly a vagovagal reflex, and after vagotomy intragastric pressure rises after eating even a small amount and there is early satiety, while the emptying of liquids is actually faster (the tone of the proximal stomach rises and the pressure difference increases). (2) Antral motility and the emptying of solids are weakened: the vagus nerve excites antral contraction, and after its section the emptying of solid food is delayed and gastric retention can occur, which is why a pyloroplasty is often done at the same time. (3) The cephalic phase of gastric acid secretion is abolished (this is the purpose of the operation, Chapter 27). The slow wave itself is produced by ICC and persists with essentially unchanged frequency, but the amplitude of spikes and contractions is reduced.

4. After a meal containing a large amount of fat, gastric emptying is markedly slowed. Describe the receptors, signals and effectors involved and explain the physiological significance of this regulation.

   **Answer** The products of fat digestion (long-chain fatty acids, monoacylglycerols) enter the duodenum and jejunum and are sensed by I cells (through receptors such as GPR40/GPR120), which release CCK, and by L cells, which release GLP-1 and PYY; at the same time fatty acids act on the mucosa and, through the enteric nervous system and vagal afferent fibers, cause the enterogastric reflex. CCK acts on the stomach through the blood and also excites vagal afferent fibers, through the vagovagal reflex relaxing the proximal stomach, weakening antral contraction and increasing the tone of the pyloric sphincter. Significance: the digestion of fat requires emulsification by bile and pancreatic lipase and is slow; slowing gastric emptying keeps the amount of fat in the duodenum within what bile and pancreatic juice can handle, ensures that fat is fully digested and absorbed and avoids steatorrhea. CCK at the same time contracts the gallbladder and stimulates the secretion of pancreatic enzymes, matching "supply" to "demand".

5. In children with Hirschsprung disease the distal colon lacks ganglia, and that segment is in a state of sustained contraction and narrowing, with marked dilatation of the colon above it. Explain this seemingly paradoxical phenomenon in terms of the action of the enteric nervous system.

   **Answer** Gastrointestinal smooth muscle itself has slow waves and tone and does not relax when it loses its nerves. The inhibitory motor neurons of the enteric nervous system (NO, VIP, ATP) relax the circular muscle continuously and, ahead of the peristaltic wave, phasically; peristalsis needs "contraction orally and relaxation aborally". A segment without ganglia loses its inhibitory input, the circular muscle contracts continuously and cannot relax, the contents cannot enter when the peristaltic wave arrives, and a functional obstruction results; in addition, extrinsic parasympathetic and sympathetic fibers proliferate in that segment and further increase the contraction. The contents pile up in the normal bowel above and cause megacolon. This shows that one of the main actions of the enteric nervous system on gut motility is to provide the inhibition that "allows passage".

6. (Open question) The foregut and hindgut of insects must renew their cuticular lining at every molt, whereas the midgut does not. Discuss the advantages and disadvantages of this regionalization from the standpoint of function and development.

   **Answer** Key points: the foregut and hindgut are derived from the ectoderm and lined with chitinous cuticle, which is tough and suited to grinding (the teeth of the proventriculus), storage (the crop) and withstanding the hyperosmotic environment of water recovery, but the cuticle is almost impermeable to macromolecules and cannot secrete enzymes or absorb nutrients, and it has to be shed and rebuilt at every molt, so the insect cannot feed during molting. The midgut is derived from the endoderm, its epithelium can secrete enzymes and absorb nutrients, and it is protected by the peritrophic membrane and not by cuticle; the peritrophic membrane can be renewed continuously and need not be replaced with the molt; it also divides digestion into compartments, so that enzymes are recycled between the inner and outer compartments, raising the efficiency of digestion. The cost is that the midgut epithelium is exposed and vulnerable to pathogens and toxins (the Bt toxin of *Bacillus thuringiensis* acts on the midgut epithelium). This regionalization has parallels in vertebrates: the esophagus and anal canal have stratified epithelium (resistant to abrasion), whereas the stomach and intestine have simple columnar epithelium (secretion and absorption).
