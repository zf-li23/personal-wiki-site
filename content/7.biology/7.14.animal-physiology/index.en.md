**Physiology** studies how a living organism works. Every cell in an animal's body is bathed in extracellular fluid whose composition, temperature and pressure are almost constant; this is the **internal environment**. The world in which animals live, however, changes all the time, and the body itself constantly consumes and produces materials and heat. The central problem of the course is therefore **homeostasis**. How do the heart, blood vessels, lungs, kidneys, gut and endocrine glands divide the work of delivering $\ce{O2}$, nutrients, water, ions and heat to where they are needed and of removing waste? How do nerves and hormones sense deviations and correct them, so that blood pressure, blood gases, osmolality, blood glucose and body temperature are held within narrow limits? And how does the nervous system sense the outside world and produce movement, linking the organism to its environment? The language for describing these processes is that of regulation and control: **negative feedback** corrects deviations, **feedforward control** anticipates them, **positive feedback** carries out all-or-none events, and fast neural regulation and slow humoral regulation are nested on different time scales. Another basic feature of the course is that it is quantitative. Diffusion time grows with the square of distance, membrane potentials are given by the Nernst and GHK equations, blood flow obeys the relation between pressure and resistance, renal function is computed from clearances, and metabolic rate scales with the $3/4$ power of body mass. Wherever possible, each chapter gives the equations, typical values of the measured parameters and numerical calculations, and its last section, on evolution and comparison, discusses the same function in animal groups other than humans.

The course is organized by systems and proceeds from the cell to the whole organism. The first four chapters lay the foundation for every function: the introduction sets up the framework of the internal environment, homeostasis and feedback, followed by cell motility, membrane transport and the movement of water, and the generation and conduction of membrane potentials and **action potentials**. Because the control of circulation, breathing and body temperature all depends on the nervous system, neurons and synapses, neural circuits and rhythm generation (including **central pattern generators**), and the organization of the nervous system and the **autonomic nervous system** are brought forward to Chapters 5–7. The next three chapters treat muscle, from the neuromuscular junction and **excitation–contraction coupling** and the mechanics and energetics of skeletal muscle to cardiac muscle, smooth muscle and the diversity of muscle. Chapters 11–16 cover blood and the circulation: blood, the pumping function of the heart and **cardiac output**, the electrical activity of the heart, hemodynamics, the microcirculation and interstitial fluid, and the regulation of the cardiovascular system, exemplified by the **baroreceptor reflex**. Chapters 17–20 treat respiration, from pulmonary ventilation, the alveolus and the pulmonary circulation to gas exchange, the **oxygen dissociation curve** and the control of breathing rhythm. Chapters 21–25 treat body fluids and the kidney, from **osmoregulation** and nitrogen excretion, the **glomerular filtration rate** and tubular transport, to **countercurrent multiplication** and the regulation of water and sodium balance, and finally acid–base balance. Chapters 26–31 deal with nutrition and energy: gastrointestinal motility, digestive secretions, the digestion and absorption of nutrients, the division of metabolic labor between the liver and other organs, energy metabolism and the regulation of food intake, and thermoregulation around a **set point**. Chapters 32–38 cover slow and widespread regulation: the principles of the **endocrine system** and the spatiotemporal dynamics of hormone signaling, the hypothalamus and pituitary, the adrenal glands and pancreatic islets, calcium and phosphate homeostasis, and the **hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal axis**, pregnancy and lactation. Chapters 39–46 link the organism to the outside world: the common principles of sensory physiology and the individual senses (mechanosensation, pain and thermal sensation, vision with **phototransduction** at its core, hearing and vestibular sensation based on **hair cells**, and the chemical senses), the control of movement by the spinal cord, brainstem, motor cortex, cerebellum and basal ganglia, and the higher functions of the cerebral cortex. The course summary introduces no new material; it reorganizes the whole course around five principles that run through all the systems (the distance limit of diffusion and bulk flow, gradients and pumps, the hierarchy of feedback, countercurrent arrangements and scaling laws) and tests them on situations that require all the systems to cooperate: exercise, diving and high altitude.

Animal physiology lies between the courses at the cellular level and those at the level of the organism and the population: it borrows mechanisms from the former and provides functional explanations for the latter. Metabolic pathways, the oxygen binding of hemoglobin and signal transduction come from [biochemistry](/7.1); membrane transport, the cytoskeleton, molecular motors and cell junctions come from [cell biology](/7.3); the development of the organs, gametogenesis and fertilization are treated in [developmental biology](/7.5). The Nernst–Planck and GHK equations, the cable equation, the Hodgkin–Huxley model, cross-bridges and the force–velocity curve, and the physical limits of sensation have all been derived in [biophysics](/7.6); this course cites the results and explains their physiological use. The general theory of feedback, bistability and oscillation is in [systems biology](/7.9). The convergence and homology discussed in the sections on evolution and comparison connect with [evolutionary biology](/7.10), and [plant physiology](/7.13), which likewise deals with transport, gas exchange, signaling and stress, can be read alongside this course to see how two forms of life solve the same physical problems with different structures. In turn, the physiology of leukocytes, inflammation and the lymphatic system is the starting point of [immunology](/7.15); the physiological facts about action potentials, synapses, circuits, sensory coding, the cerebellum and the basal ganglia are what [computational neuroscience](/7.16) models; and the circulation, respiration, excretion and nervous systems of the various animal groups, the mechanisms of sensation, feeding, reproduction and sleep, and metabolic scaling and thermal ecology provide the foundation for [zoology](/7.17), [animal behavior](/7.18) and [ecology](/7.19), respectively. The problems of feedback, hormone dynamics and rhythms in the course draw on [ordinary differential equations](/2.4), those of diffusion, cables and countercurrent flow on [partial differential equations](/2.5), quantal release on the Poisson distribution of [probability](/3.1), and the fluid mechanics, heat, electricity and optics on [physics](/5).

Among olympiad students there is a saying that of physiology and biochemistry, one is bound to trip you up. As I see it, physiology is hard because its knowledge is extremely dense and highly integrative: one has to remember the structure of every organ and the details of every regulatory pathway, yet a real problem usually calls on several systems at once. When I first encountered physiology I did not really understand it, because I treated it as a collection of isolated facts. Only after I had built a sense of systems at the level of the whole organism, seeing the circulation, respiration, the kidney and the endocrine system as control systems that serve the same internal environment and constrain one another, did physiology problems suddenly click. At university the course was taught in a highly quantitative style, and many ideas about systems that could otherwise only be described qualitatively were captured more clearly by equations and numbers: how much negative feedback reduces a deviation, why a slow loop is added on top of a fast one, how metabolic rate scales with body size. This is also why I have made this course as quantitative as possible. At the same time, both the olympiad problems and the university course paid close attention to the physiology of animals other than humans: how fish maintain their osmotic balance in seawater and in fresh water, how birds breathe at high altitude, how diving mammals protect the brain and heart during long breath-holds. Human physiology provides the most complete and precise main line, while other animals push the same principles to their extremes and make it clear which designs are universal and which are peculiar to humans. That is why I would rather call this course animal physiology.

As for the books, the first one I used was the fourth edition of *Medical Physiology* by Youfei Guan, which is very concise and consists almost entirely of densely packed key points, making it well suited to beginners. The book most widely used in the Chinese olympiad community is the *Physiology* edited by Danian Zhu, and physiology written for medical students is of course the best explained. The strength of *Human and Animal Physiology* by Mingxue Zuo is its comparisons with other animals, which is exactly what we care about, and the fourth edition of *Human Physiology* by Tai Yao is a very comprehensive reference in which every kind of claim can be looked up. I have not yet read the newly compiled *Principles of Physiology* of the 101 Program. Among English textbooks, the one I have used most is *Berne & Levy Physiology*, which is available in Chinese translation.
