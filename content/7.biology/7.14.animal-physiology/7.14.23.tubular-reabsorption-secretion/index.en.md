Each day the glomeruli filter about $180\,\mathrm{L}$ of fluid, more than $25\,000\,\mathrm{mmol}$ of $\ce{Na+}$ and $180\,\mathrm{g}$ of glucose, and almost all of it must be retrieved by the renal tubules, in amounts adjusted precisely to the needs of the body. This chapter explains how the tubules do so. We begin with the general principles of reabsorption and secretion, including the transport pathways, their driving forces and their limits (Section 23.1). We then follow $\ce{Na+}$ segment by segment, along with the reabsorption of $\ce{Cl-}$ and water (Section 23.2), and describe the glomerulotubular balance that makes reabsorption track filtration (Section 23.3). Next come the handling of $\ce{K+}$ (Section 23.4) and of urea, divalent ions, phosphate and organic compounds (Section 23.5), and finally the diuretics that act on the transporters of each segment (Section 23.6). Urine concentration and the hormonal regulation of $\ce{Na+}$ balance are the subject of Chapter 24, and the transport of $\ce{H+}$ and $\ce{HCO3-}$ of Chapter 25. For the basic mechanisms of membrane transport (channels, carriers, pumps and cotransport), see [Chapter 3](/7.14.3) and [7.3.5](/7.3.5).

## 23.1. General Principles of Reabsorption and Secretion

### 23.1.1. Peritubular Capillaries Favor Reabsorption

Reabsorbed substances first enter the interstitial fluid around the tubule and then the peritubular capillaries. This step is also governed by Starling forces, and the vascular architecture of the kidney favors reabsorption: after the blood has passed the efferent arteriole its hydrostatic pressure is low, and because about $20\%$ of the plasma has been filtered, the protein is concentrated and the colloid osmotic pressure is high. Typical values for the rat are a hydrostatic pressure of about $20\,\mathrm{mmHg}$ and a colloid osmotic pressure of about $35\,\mathrm{mmHg}$ at the start of the peritubular capillary, and about $15$ and $25\,\mathrm{mmHg}$ at its end; the interstitial hydrostatic pressure is about $8\,\mathrm{mmHg}$ and the interstitial colloid osmotic pressure about $6\,\mathrm{mmHg}$. The net reabsorptive pressure falls from $(35 + 8) - (20 + 6) = 17\,\mathrm{mmHg}$ at the start to $(25 + 8) - (15 + 6) = 12\,\mathrm{mmHg}$ at the end. It points into the capillary throughout, so the bed behaves like the "venous end" of a microcirculation. At the same time the peritubular capillaries supply the tubular cells with nutrients and oxygen.

### 23.1.2. Selectivity and the Two Pathways

Tubular reabsorption and secretion are highly selective. Glucose and amino acids are almost entirely reabsorbed; more than $99\%$ of $\ce{Na+}$, $\ce{Cl-}$, $\ce{HCO3-}$ and water is reabsorbed, in an adjustable proportion; about half of the urea is reabsorbed; creatinine is hardly reabsorbed at all; PAH and many drugs are secreted. These differences are determined by the transport proteins distributed along the tubule.

**Definition 23.1.1 (Transcellular and Paracellular Pathways)** Transepithelial transport in which a substance crosses first the apical and then the basolateral membrane of the epithelial cells follows the **transcellular pathway**; transport through the tight junctions and lateral intercellular spaces between adjacent cells follows the **paracellular pathway**.

The transcellular pathway can move solutes against their electrochemical gradient, and its selectivity is set by the different transport proteins in the apical and basolateral membranes. The paracellular pathway is purely passive, and its permeability and selectivity are determined by the **claudins** of the **tight junctions**. The tight junctions of the proximal tubule are "leaky", and water and small ions can pass through them in large quantities; the epithelium becomes progressively "tighter" toward the distal end, and in the collecting duct paracellular permeability is very low. This is why the proximal tubule can only reabsorb large volumes of isosmotic fluid, whereas the collecting duct can maintain large transepithelial concentration and potential differences. The two pathways correspond to the four features of transporting epithelia described in Chapter 21: polarity, a barrier function, multiple cell types and a high energy cost.

### 23.1.3. Primary and Secondary Active Transport

The reabsorption of $\ce{Na+}$ is the core of all tubular transport and proceeds in three steps.

1. $\ce{Na+}$ moves down its electrochemical gradient across the apical membrane into the cell (intracellular $[\ce{Na+}]$ is about $10$–$20\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$ and the inside of the membrane is about $-70\,\mathrm{mV}$); the carrier or channel involved differs from segment to segment.
2. The **Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase** (sodium–potassium pump) in the basolateral membrane pumps $\ce{Na+}$ into the interstitial fluid against its electrochemical gradient (**primary active transport**). This step maintains the low intracellular $[\ce{Na+}]$ and the negative potential.
3. $\ce{Na+}$ and water pass from the interstitial fluid into the peritubular capillary according to Starling forces.

The electrochemical gradient for $\ce{Na+}$ entry across the apical membrane in turn drives the secondary active transport of other substances. Glucose, amino acids, phosphate, lactate and others enter the cell together with $\ce{Na+}$ through **symporters** and leave across the basolateral membrane by **facilitated diffusion** through uniporters; $\ce{H+}$ is secreted into the tubular fluid through the $\ce{Na+}/\ce{H+}$ **antiporter**.

### 23.1.4. Limits of Transport: Transporter-Limited and Gradient-Time Systems

The capacity of the tubule to reabsorb a substance can be limited in two ways.

**Definition 23.1.2 (Transport Maximum)** For a substance carried by a limited number of carrier proteins, once the filtered load has risen to a certain level all carriers are saturated and the rate of reabsorption (or secretion) can no longer increase. The maximal rate at which the tubule can reabsorb (or secrete) the substance is called the **tubular transport maximum**, denoted $T_{\mathrm{m}}$.

Glucose is the classic transporter-limited system. In the proximal tubule it is taken up across the apical membrane by SGLT2 ($\ce{Na+}$:glucose $= 1:1$, low affinity and high capacity, reabsorbing about $90\%$ in the S1 and S2 segments) and by SGLT1 ($2:1$, high affinity and low capacity, recovering the remainder in the S3 segment), and leaves across the basolateral membrane through GLUT2 and GLUT1 (for the stoichiometry of the two SGLTs and the concentration gradients they can build, see [Chapter 3](/7.14.3)). The glucose $T_{\mathrm{m}}$ of the whole kidney is about $375\,\mathrm{mg\,min^{-1}}$ (about $2.1\,\mathrm{mmol\,min^{-1}}$). With a GFR of $125\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}}$ and a normal blood glucose (about $100\,\mathrm{mg\,dL^{-1}}$, $5.5\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$), the filtered load is about $125\,\mathrm{mg\,min^{-1}}$, only one third of $T_{\mathrm{m}}$, so all of it is reabsorbed.

**Definition 23.1.3 (Renal Threshold)** The plasma glucose concentration at which glucose begins to appear in the urine is called the **renal threshold for glucose**; normally it is about $180$–$200\,\mathrm{mg\,dL^{-1}}$ ($10$–$11\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$).

If every nephron had the same ratio of transport capacity to filtered load, glucosuria would appear abruptly when the whole kidney saturated (at a plasma concentration of $T_{\mathrm{m}}/\mathrm{GFR} = 300\,\mathrm{mg\,dL^{-1}}$). In reality the renal threshold is clearly lower than this value, and the reabsorption curve bends as it approaches saturation; this rounding of the curve is called splay. It arises mainly because the ratio of $T_{\mathrm{m}}$ to single-nephron GFR differs among nephrons, so the nephrons with relatively small transport capacity saturate first:

The filtered load (gray) increases linearly with blood glucose; the reabsorbed amount (green) approaches saturation with a curved profile near T_m; the excreted amount (red) begins to appear at the renal threshold of about 200 mg/dL (11 mmol/L), far below the idealized case in which all nephrons saturate at once (dashed line, with glucosuria appearing abruptly at 300 mg/dL). The blue band is the normal blood glucose range. The upper axis is in mmol/L (1 mmol/L glucose = 18 mg/dL)](./plots/glucose-titration.py)

In uncontrolled **diabetes mellitus** the blood glucose exceeds the renal threshold and glucose appears in the urine (hence the name of the disease, "passing through"). Glucose retained in the tubular fluid raises its osmolality and hinders the reabsorption of water, causing polyuria followed by thirst and polydipsia.

**Definition 23.1.4 (Osmotic Diuresis)** The increase in urine volume caused by an increased amount of unreabsorbable solute in the tubular fluid, which reduces water reabsorption, is called **osmotic diuresis**.

Mannitol, which is filtered but not reabsorbed, is given intravenously to induce diuresis and lower intracranial pressure, and this clinical use relies on osmotic diuresis. SGLT2 inhibitors (dapagliflozin, empagliflozin and others) deliberately cause glucose to be lost in the urine (about $60$–$80\,\mathrm{g}$ per day) and are used to treat type 2 diabetes. They are accompanied by a mild natriuresis and osmotic diuresis, and they reduce the pathologically elevated GFR of diabetes through **tubuloglomerular feedback**, because less NaCl is reabsorbed upstream and more reaches the macula densa; this is thought to be one of the mechanisms of their protection of the kidney and heart.

For another class of substances there is no clear upper limit to reabsorption: it depends on the electrochemical gradient that drives diffusion, the permeability of the epithelium and the time the tubular fluid spends in the tubule. These are called gradient-time systems. The reabsorption of $\ce{Na+}$ in the proximal tubule is of this kind: although the capacity of the pump is limited, the paracellular pathway of the proximal tubule is leaky, so the transepithelial gradient of $\ce{Na+}$ cannot be built up very high, and the amount reabsorbed is determined mainly by the speed of flow and the contact time of the tubular fluid.

## 23.2. Segmental Reabsorption of $\ce{Na+}$ and $\ce{Cl-}$

### 23.2.1. Fractions Reabsorbed in Each Segment

The $\ce{Na+}$ filtered per day is about $180\,\mathrm{L} \times 142\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}} \approx 25\,600\,\mathrm{mmol}$ (about $590\,\mathrm{g}$), of which only $100$–$200\,\mathrm{mmol}$, less than $1\%$, is normally excreted in the urine. The approximate fractions reabsorbed in each segment are as follows.

| Segment | Fraction reabsorbed | Features |
|---|---|---|
| Proximal tubule | about $67\%$ | Isosmotic reabsorption: $\ce{Na+}$ and water are reabsorbed proportionally and the osmolality of the tubular fluid does not change |
| Loop of Henle | about $25\%$ | The thick ascending limb transports NaCl actively without water; the thin limbs are mainly passive |
| Distal convoluted tubule | about $5\%$ | Impermeable to water; continues to dilute the tubular fluid |
| Collecting duct | about $3\%$ | Small amount, but the main site of regulation by aldosterone and other hormones |
| Excreted | less than $1\%$ | |

Because the filtered load is so large, a tiny change in the fraction reabsorbed means a large change in excretion. If reabsorption falls from $99.5\%$ to $99\%$, the $\ce{Na+}$ excreted per day increases by about $128\,\mathrm{mmol}$, the amount of $\ce{Na+}$ contained in about $0.9\,\mathrm{L}$ of extracellular fluid. Sustained over several days, this would markedly change total body $\ce{Na+}$, extracellular fluid volume and body weight.

### 23.2.2. Driving Forces for $\ce{Na+}$ Transport in Each Segment

The tubular fluid $[\ce{Na+}]$ and the transepithelial potential differ from segment to segment, and so does the direction of passive paracellular $\ce{Na+}$ movement. Converting the chemical gradient to an equivalent voltage (the **Nernst equation** for $\ce{Na+}$, $61.5\,\mathrm{mV}\cdot\lg$ of the concentration ratio at $37\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$) and taking the direction favoring reabsorption (lumen → blood) as positive gives the following table.

| Segment | Luminal $[\ce{Na+}]$ (mM) | Chemical driving force (mV) | Electrical driving force (mV) | Total driving force (mV) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Proximal tubule S1 | $142$ | $0$ | $-3$ | $-3$ |
| Proximal tubule S3 | $142$ | $0$ | $+3$ | $+3$ |
| Thick ascending limb | $100$ | $-9$ | $+15$ | $+6$ |
| Distal convoluted tubule | $70$ | $-19$ | $-5$ to $+5$ | $-24$ to $-14$ |
| Cortical collecting duct | $40$ | $-34$ | $-40$ | $-74$ |

A negative chemical driving force means that the luminal concentration is lower than that in blood and tends to make $\ce{Na+}$ leak back into the lumen; the sign of the electrical driving force is the potential of the lumen relative to the blood (a positive lumen pushes cations out of the lumen). The transepithelial potential is not zero because the transport of cations and anions is not completely coupled. The table shows that in the proximal tubule and the thick ascending limb the passive driving force for $\ce{Na+}$ is small and the paracellular pathway is permeable, so $\ce{Na+}$ can be reabsorbed passively through the paracellular route but can also leak back through it. Farther toward the distal end, $\ce{Na+}$ must be reabsorbed actively against ever larger electrochemical gradients, and the tight epithelium prevents back-leak. The cortical collecting duct reabsorbs $\ce{Na+}$ against a driving force of about $74\,\mathrm{mV}$, which is possible only in an epithelium of very low paracellular permeability.

### 23.2.3. Proximal Tubule

The early proximal tubule (S1) relies mainly on the apical $\ce{Na+}/\ce{H+}$ exchanger NHE3 and on various $\ce{Na+}$-dependent cotransporters (for glucose, amino acids, phosphate, lactate and citrate). Cotransport of $\ce{Na+}$ with glucose and the like is electrogenic and makes the lumen relatively negative (about $-3\,\mathrm{mV}$); this drives $\ce{Cl-}$ reabsorption through the paracellular pathway and also makes part of the reabsorbed $\ce{Na+}$ (about one third) leak back into the lumen paracellularly. The $\ce{H+}$ secreted by NHE3 combines with $\ce{HCO3-}$ in the tubular fluid to form $\ce{CO2}$ and water under the action of brush-border **carbonic anhydrase**; $\ce{CO2}$ diffuses into the cell, regenerates $\ce{HCO3-}$ and leaves through the basolateral $\ce{Na+}$–$\ce{HCO3-}$ cotransporter (NBCe1) into the interstitial fluid (Chapter 25). As a result the early proximal tubule preferentially reabsorbs $\ce{HCO3-}$, glucose and amino acids while $\ce{Cl-}$ is left behind, and the tubular fluid $[\ce{Cl-}]$ rises from $106$ to about $130\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$.

In the late proximal tubule (S2 and S3), few organic solutes and little $\ce{HCO3-}$ remain, and $\ce{Na+}$ is reabsorbed mainly as NaCl.

- Transcellular: apical NHE3 operates in parallel with a $\ce{Cl-}$/anion exchanger (exchanging formate, oxalate, $\ce{OH-}$ or $\ce{HCO3-}$), so that the net effect is NaCl entry into the cell; $\ce{Cl-}$ leaves through the basolateral $\ce{K+}$–$\ce{Cl-}$ cotransporter and through $\ce{Cl-}$ channels.
- Paracellular: the tubular fluid $[\ce{Cl-}]$ is higher than that of the interstitial fluid, so $\ce{Cl-}$ diffuses down its concentration gradient through the paracellular pathway, making the lumen relatively positive (about $+3\,\mathrm{mV}$), which in turn drives passive paracellular reabsorption of $\ce{Na+}$.

Water follows the solutes passively, by two routes: through the cells (both apical and basolateral membranes are rich in the **aquaporin** AQP1) and through the leaky tight junctions. The water permeability of the proximal tubule is so high that an osmolality difference of only a few $\mathrm{mOsm\,kg^{-1}}$, with the tubular fluid being lower, is enough to drive water reabsorption, so reabsorption is isosmotic on the macroscopic scale. Water flowing through the paracellular pathway also carries solutes with it:

**Definition 23.2.1 (Solvent Drag)** The carrying of solutes across an epithelium (or membrane) by the bulk flow of water is called **solvent drag**.

A considerable part of the $\ce{K+}$, $\ce{Ca^2+}$ and urea in the proximal tubule is reabsorbed paracellularly by solvent drag and diffusion.

### 23.2.4. Loop of Henle

The three parts of the loop of Henle have sharply different properties.

- Thin descending limb: contains AQP1 and is highly permeable to water, but has a very low permeability to NaCl. As the tubular fluid flows toward the deep medulla, water is drawn out by the hypertonic medullary interstitium and the osmolality of the tubular fluid rises.
- Thin ascending limb: has no water channels and is impermeable to water, but has a considerable passive permeability to NaCl, which diffuses out of the tubule down its concentration gradient.
- Thick ascending limb: reabsorbs NaCl actively while impermeable to water, making the tubular fluid hypotonic (down to about $100$–$150\,\mathrm{mOsm\,kg^{-1}}$); it is therefore also called the "diluting segment". The apical $\ce{Na+}$–$\ce{K+}$–$2\ce{Cl-}$ cotransporter NKCC2 carries one $\ce{Na+}$, one $\ce{K+}$ and two $\ce{Cl-}$ into the cell together; $\ce{Na+}$ is pumped out by the basolateral sodium–potassium pump, $\ce{Cl-}$ leaves through ClC-Kb channels (with the barttin subunit), and most of the $\ce{K+}$ returns to the lumen through apical ROMK channels and is recycled. The back-flux of $\ce{K+}$ makes the lumen positive (about $+15\,\mathrm{mV}$), which drives paracellular reabsorption of about half of the $\ce{Na+}$ and most of the $\ce{Ca^2+}$ and $\ce{Mg^2+}$ (the claudins claudin-16/19 form cation-selective paracellular pores). The thick ascending limb can therefore reabsorb about six $\ce{Na+}$ per ATP consumed (three $\ce{Na+}$ pumped out), which is more efficient than purely transcellular transport.

Loss-of-function mutations of NKCC2, ROMK, ClC-Kb or barttin all cause Bartter syndrome (salt wasting, hypokalemia, **metabolic alkalosis**), and loop diuretics such as furosemide act precisely by inhibiting NKCC2 (Section 23.6). The transepithelial osmotic difference generated by the thick ascending limb (about $200\,\mathrm{mOsm\,kg^{-1}}$) is the engine of the urine-concentrating mechanism (Chapter 24).

### 23.2.5. Distal Convoluted Tubule and Collecting Duct

The epithelium of the distal convoluted tubule is impermeable to water. In its early part the apical membrane carries the thiazide-sensitive $\ce{Na+}$–$\ce{Cl-}$ cotransporter NCC, which reabsorbs NaCl electroneutrally and dilutes the tubular fluid further; $\ce{Cl-}$ leaves through basolateral $\ce{Cl-}$ channels. The activity of NCC is regulated by the WNK kinase–SPAK pathway, which integrates the signals of plasma $\ce{K+}$ and angiotensin II (Section 23.4); loss of NCC function causes Gitelman syndrome. The distal convoluted tubule is also the main site of active $\ce{Ca^2+}$ reabsorption and of the action of **parathyroid hormone** (Section 23.5).

The late distal convoluted tubule, the connecting tubule and the collecting duct are composed of two types of cells.

**Definition 23.2.2 (Principal and Intercalated Cells)** The cells of the collecting duct (and connecting tubule) epithelium that reabsorb $\ce{Na+}$ through apical epithelial sodium channels, secrete $\ce{K+}$ and reabsorb water under the action of antidiuretic hormone are called **principal cells**; the cells rich in mitochondria and carbonic anhydrase that mainly transport $\ce{H+}$ and $\ce{HCO3-}$ are called **intercalated cells**, divided into type A cells that secrete $\ce{H+}$ and type B cells that secrete $\ce{HCO3-}$.

The apical epithelial sodium channel ENaC of the principal cell is sensitive to amiloride. $\ce{Na+}$ enters the cell through it down its electrochemical gradient and is pumped out by the basolateral sodium–potassium pump; because $\ce{Na+}$ enters alone, without an accompanying anion, the lumen becomes negative (about $-40\,\mathrm{mV}$ in the cortical collecting duct), and this potential drives the secretion of $\ce{K+}$ through ROMK and the paracellular reabsorption of $\ce{Cl-}$. The apical $\ce{Cl-}/\ce{HCO3-}$ exchanger pendrin of type B intercalated cells also reabsorbs $\ce{Cl-}$ and takes part in the regulation of acid–base balance (Chapter 25). Through the mineralocorticoid receptor, **aldosterone** increases the number and opening of ENaC and the number of sodium–potassium pumps, and is the main hormone regulating $\ce{Na+}$ reabsorption in this segment (Chapter 24). Gain-of-function mutations of ENaC (Liddle syndrome) make $\ce{Na+}$ reabsorption uncontrolled and cause hypertension and hypokalemia, showing that this last $3\%$ of $\ce{Na+}$ reabsorption is enough to determine total-body $\ce{Na+}$ balance and blood pressure.

### 23.2.6. Coupling of Water to $\ce{Na+}$

The reabsorption of water is always passive and follows that of solutes (mainly NaCl), while the transport of most other solutes is in turn coupled to that of $\ce{Na+}$. Thus $\ce{Na+}$ transport links the transport of water and of most solutes. The segments differ in whether water can keep up. The proximal tubule and thin descending limb are permeable to water, and water leaves in proportion to the solutes (proximal tubule) or in excess of the solutes (thin descending limb); the ascending limb and distal convoluted tubule are impermeable to water, so solutes are reabsorbed while water stays behind and the tubular fluid is diluted; whether the collecting duct is permeable to water depends on **antidiuretic hormone** (Chapter 24).

The ratio of the concentration of a solute in the tubular fluid to that in plasma (TF/P) summarizes whether it is reabsorbed more or less than water. Inulin is neither reabsorbed nor secreted, so its TF/P reflects only the reabsorption of water: it is about $3$ at the end of the proximal tubule, showing that $2/3$ of the water has been reabsorbed. The TF/P of various solutes along the proximal tubule is as follows:

![TF/P along the proximal tubule (schematic model). Water reabsorption increases along the tubule to 67%, and the TF/P of inulin rises to 3; Na⁺ is reabsorbed in proportion to water and its TF/P stays at 1 (proximal reabsorption is isosmotic); glucose and amino acids are almost completely recovered in the first quarter by cotransport with Na⁺; HCO₃⁻ falls to about 0.3 in the first half; the total anion content is conserved, so Cl⁻ rises to about 1.2 as HCO₃⁻ declines; urea and phosphate are partly reabsorbed, with TF/P rising to about 1.9 (reabsorbed less than water) and falling to about 0.7 respectively; PAH is secreted, and its TF/P exceeds that of inulin](./plots/tf-p-ratios.py)

From TF/P one can calculate the fraction of the filtered load reabsorbed up to a given point: the fraction of solute $X$ remaining is $(\mathrm{TF}/P)_X/(\mathrm{TF}/P)_{\mathrm{in}}$. For example, at the end of the proximal tubule $(\mathrm{TF}/P)_{\ce{Na+}} = 1$ and $(\mathrm{TF}/P)_{\mathrm{in}} = 3$, so $1/3$ of the $\ce{Na+}$ remains, that is, $67\%$ has been reabsorbed.

### 23.2.7. The High Oxygen Consumption of the Kidney Reflects $\ce{Na+}$ Transport

The oxygen consumption of the kidney is about $7\%$–$10\%$ of that of the whole body at rest, second only to the heart per unit mass, and about $80\%$ of it is used for the active reabsorption of $\ce{Na+}$. When GFR (and with it the filtered $\ce{Na+}$) is changed, renal oxygen consumption is linearly related to $\ce{Na+}$ reabsorption, and when $\ce{Na+}$ transport is inhibited oxygen consumption falls to a low basal value. Given that the sodium–potassium pump moves $3$ $\ce{Na+}$ per ATP hydrolyzed and that each $\ce{O2}$ molecule supports the synthesis of about $5$ ATP, transcellular active transport alone could reabsorb at most about $15$ $\ce{Na+}$ per $\ce{O2}$ consumed; the measured value for the whole kidney is about $25$–$30$. The surplus comes from passive paracellular reabsorption, which costs no energy (the $\ce{Cl-}$ gradient in the proximal tubule, the positive potential in the thick ascending limb). This also explains why the outer medulla, with its dense thick ascending limbs and little blood flow, is particularly sensitive to ischemia.

## 23.3. Glomerulotubular Balance

**Definition 23.3.1 (Glomerulotubular Balance)** The phenomenon in which, when GFR changes, proximal tubular reabsorption changes in the same direction so that the fraction reabsorbed remains roughly constant (about $65\%$–$70\%$) is called **glomerulotubular balance**.

Glomerulotubular balance is an intrarenal adaptive mechanism independent of nerves and hormones, which prevents changes in GFR from flushing large amounts of $\ce{Na+}$ and water to the distal nephron. For example, if GFR rises from $125$ to $150\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}}$ and proximal reabsorption stays unchanged ($81\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}}$), the fluid delivered to the loop of Henle would increase from $44$ to $69\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}}$, a rise of about $57\%$; with glomerulotubular balance the proximal tubule reabsorbs about $97\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}}$ and the outflow rises only to about $53\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}}$. Two kinds of mechanism are involved.

- Starling forces in the peritubular capillaries: when GFR increases at constant renal plasma flow, the filtration fraction rises, so the plasma entering the peritubular capillaries has a higher protein concentration and a lower hydrostatic pressure, which favors the movement of interstitial fluid into the capillaries and speeds reabsorption.
- Luminal factors: when GFR increases, the filtered load of glucose, amino acids and other solutes cotransported with $\ce{Na+}$ increases in proportion and more $\ce{Na+}$ is reabsorbed with them; increased luminal flow also bends the microvilli of the brush border of proximal tubule cells, which acts as a mechanical signal that increases NHE3 insertion.

Glomerulotubular balance and **tubuloglomerular feedback** (Chapter 22) act in opposite directions and complement each other: the former makes reabsorption follow filtration, the latter adapts filtration to the transport capacity of the distal nephron.

## 23.4. Handling of $\ce{K+}$

The extracellular fluid contains only about $60\,\mathrm{mmol}$ of $\ce{K+}$ (plasma $3.5$–$5.0\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$), and a single meal may supply as much. Acute $\ce{K+}$ balance depends on **insulin**, **epinephrine** and other agents that move $\ce{K+}$ into cells, whereas long-term balance rests entirely on the kidney: about $180 \times 4.2 \approx 760\,\mathrm{mmol}$ of $\ce{K+}$ is filtered per day, and excretion varies with intake between about $10$ and $400\,\mathrm{mmol\,d^{-1}}$ ("eat more, excrete more; eat less, excrete less; eat nothing, still excrete").

| Segment | Handling | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| Proximal tubule | about $65\%$ reabsorbed | Paracellular pathway: solvent drag and the positive potential of the late lumen |
| Thick ascending limb | about $25\%$ reabsorbed | Uptake by NKCC2, partly paracellular |
| Late distal convoluted tubule, connecting tubule and collecting duct | Secretion by principal cells (more or less), reabsorption by type A intercalated cells in $\ce{K+}$ depletion | Principal cells: the sodium–potassium pump pumps $\ce{K+}$ into the cell and it is secreted through apical ROMK and flow-activated BK channels; intercalated cells: reabsorption by the apical $\ce{H+}/\ce{K+}$-ATPase |

Because the fractions reabsorbed in the proximal tubule and loop of Henle are roughly constant, very little $\ce{K+}$ reaches the distal nephron, and virtually all urinary $\ce{K+}$ comes from distal secretion, so $\ce{K+}$ excretion is determined by secretion by the principal cells. Factors that affect secretion are as follows.

- Increased plasma $[\ce{K+}]$: directly stimulates the basolateral sodium–potassium pump of the principal cell and increases apical $\ce{K+}$ channels, and directly stimulates the zona glomerulosa of the adrenal cortex to secrete aldosterone.
- Aldosterone: increases ENaC (making the lumen more negative), sodium–potassium pumps and ROMK, promoting $\ce{Na+}$ retention and $\ce{K+}$ excretion.
- Distal flow and $\ce{Na+}$ delivery: high flow rapidly carries luminal $\ce{K+}$ away, keeping the concentration gradient steep, and activates BK channels; greater $\ce{Na+}$ delivery lets ENaC absorb more $\ce{Na+}$ and makes the lumen more negative. Diuretics acting upstream of the collecting duct (loop diuretics, thiazides) therefore all cause hypokalemia, whereas those acting on ENaC or the aldosterone receptor are potassium-sparing diuretics.
- Acid–base status: acute acidosis inhibits $\ce{K+}$ secretion and alkalosis promotes it.

There is an "aldosterone paradox" here: when blood volume is low, aldosterone rises to conserve $\ce{Na+}$ without causing massive $\ce{K+}$ excretion; in hyperkalemia, aldosterone rises to excrete $\ce{K+}$ without causing massive $\ce{Na+}$ retention. The explanation is that NCC in the distal convoluted tubule acts as a switch. Angiotensin II activates NCC, so $\ce{Na+}$ is reabsorbed in the distal convoluted tubule, little $\ce{Na+}$ and flow reach the principal cells, and $\ce{K+}$ secretion is limited; high $\ce{K+}$ inhibits NCC through the WNK pathway, so more $\ce{Na+}$ is delivered to the principal cells and exchanged for $\ce{K+}$. Recent work also shows that after a potassium-containing meal renal $\ce{K+}$ excretion increases even before plasma $[\ce{K+}]$ has risen, suggesting a **feedforward control** from the digestive tract.

## 23.5. Reabsorption and Secretion of Other Substances

### 23.5.1. Urea

Urea is freely filtered, and about half of the filtered urea is reabsorbed (Question 1 of Chapter 22). Water reabsorption in the proximal tubule raises the urea concentration of the tubular fluid, and about $40\%$–$50\%$ of the urea is reabsorbed passively through the paracellular route; the thin limbs of the loop take up urea from the medullary interstitium (urea transporter UT-A2); the distal convoluted tubule and the cortical and outer medullary collecting ducts are impermeable to urea, so urea is concentrated in the tubular fluid as water is reabsorbed; under the action of antidiuretic hormone the inner medullary collecting duct reabsorbs urea into the inner medullary interstitium through UT-A1 and UT-A3. This recycling makes urea one of the main solutes of the inner medullary hypertonicity, as detailed in Chapter 24.

### 23.5.2. $\ce{Ca^2+}$ and $\ce{Mg^2+}$

Total plasma calcium is about $2.4\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$, of which about $60\%$ (free and complexed with small anions) can be filtered; about $250\,\mathrm{mmol}$ (about $10\,\mathrm{g}$) is filtered per day and only $1\%$–$2\%$ is excreted.

| Segment | $\ce{Ca^2+}$ | $\ce{Mg^2+}$ |
|---|---|---|
| Proximal tubule | $60\%$–$70\%$, paracellular, with $\ce{Na+}$ and water | $15\%$–$25\%$ |
| Thick ascending limb | $20\%$–$25\%$, paracellular (positive potential, claudin-16/19), inhibited via the calcium-sensing receptor when plasma $\ce{Ca^2+}$ rises | $60\%$–$70\%$, paracellular, the main site of reabsorption |
| Distal convoluted tubule | $5\%$–$10\%$, transcellular: apical TRPV5 channel, transport by the intracellular calcium-binding protein calbindin, extrusion by the basolateral $\ce{Na+}/\ce{Ca^2+}$ exchanger and $\ce{Ca^2+}$-ATPase; promoted by parathyroid hormone and 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D | $5\%$–$10\%$, transcellular, TRPM6 channel |

Although the transcellular transport of the distal convoluted tubule is quantitatively small, it is the site of fine hormonal regulation. Loop diuretics abolish the positive potential of the thick ascending limb and increase the excretion of $\ce{Ca^2+}$ and $\ce{Mg^2+}$; thiazide diuretics, on the contrary, reduce $\ce{Ca^2+}$ excretion and are used to treat calcium-containing kidney stones.

### 23.5.3. Phosphate and the Threshold Model

About $80\%$ of the filtered inorganic phosphate ($\mathrm{P_i}$) is reabsorbed in the proximal tubule by the $\ce{Na+}$–phosphate cotransporters (NaPi-IIa and IIc, the SLC34 family), a little is reabsorbed distally, and the remainder (about $10\%$–$20\%$) is excreted. The reabsorption of phosphate has a $T_{\mathrm{m}}$, and this $T_{\mathrm{m}}$ is such that the normal plasma concentration lies near the saturation point, so that the kidney acts here as an "overflow valve". Let the maximal phosphate reabsorption be $T_{\mathrm{mP}}$; neglecting splay,

$$
\text{excretion} = \mathrm{GFR}\cdot\max\left(P_{\mathrm{P}} - C_0,\ 0\right), \qquad C_0 = \frac{T_{\mathrm{mP}}}{\mathrm{GFR}},
$$

where $C_0$ is the renal threshold for plasma phosphate, normally about $0.8$–$1.35\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$, almost coinciding with the normal plasma concentration of $1.0$–$1.4\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$. In the steady state excretion equals net intestinal absorption, so that plasma phosphate is $P_{\mathrm{P}} = C_0 + (\text{net absorption})/\mathrm{GFR}$.

**Example 23.5.1** Let GFR $= 120\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}}$ ($173\,\mathrm{L\,d^{-1}}$), $C_0 = 1.0\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$, and the net intestinal absorption of phosphate $30\,\mathrm{mmol}$ per day. The steady-state plasma phosphate is $1.0 + 30/173 = 1.17\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$. If dietary phosphate doubles (net absorption $60\,\mathrm{mmol}$), it rises only to $1.35\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$; whereas if $C_0$ falls to $0.7$ (increased parathyroid hormone or FGF23), it falls to $0.87\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$ even with unchanged intake.

:::

Plasma phosphate is therefore set mainly by $C_0$, and $C_0$ is regulated by hormones: parathyroid hormone and FGF23, secreted by osteocytes, both promote the endocytosis of NaPi-IIa, lowering $T_{\mathrm{mP}}$ and increasing phosphate excretion (Chapter 36). When GFR falls severely (chronic kidney disease), excretion cannot keep up with intake even when $C_0$ has been lowered to a very low value, plasma phosphate rises, and secondary hyperparathyroidism and vascular calcification follow.

### 23.5.4. Secretion of Organic Anions and Cations

The proximal tubule has two secretory systems that clear endogenous metabolites and foreign drugs and toxins from plasma.

| | Organic anions | Organic cations |
|---|---|---|
| Examples | PAH, uric acid, bile salts, prostaglandins, penicillin, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, loop and thiazide diuretics, glucuronide conjugates of many drugs | Creatinine, choline, dopamine, metformin, cimetidine |
| Basolateral uptake | OAT1, OAT3 (entry in exchange for intracellular $\alpha$-ketoglutarate) | OCT2 (entry driven by the membrane potential) |
| Apical efflux | MRP2, MRP4 (**ABC transporters**), OAT4 and others | MATE1, MATE2-K (exchange with $\ce{H+}$), P-glycoprotein |

Substrates of the same system compete with one another: probenecid inhibits OAT, and during the Second World War, when penicillin was scarce, probenecid was used to reduce penicillin secretion and prolong its action. Many drugs that are bound to plasma proteins and cannot be filtered reach the urine through this secretion; the target of loop and thiazide diuretics lies on the apical membrane, so they must first be secreted into the lumen to act, and in renal insufficiency secretion is reduced and the effect of the diuretics weakens. Uric acid is secreted via OAT and reabsorbed via the apical URAT1, with a net fractional excretion of only about $10\%$; uricosuric drugs such as benzbromarone inhibit URAT1 and are used to treat gout.

## 23.6. Diuretics

**Definition 23.6.1 (Diuretic)** A drug that increases urine volume is called a **diuretic**. Most clinical diuretics inhibit the reabsorption of $\ce{Na+}$ in a particular tubular segment and so increase the excretion of $\ce{Na+}$ and water (natriuretic diuresis).

The sites of action of diuretics are the main transporters of the segments in Section 23.2, and their maximal effect depends on the share of reabsorption of that segment and on how much the downstream segments can compensate.

| Class | Examples | Site and target | Maximal natriuresis (% of filtered load) | Effect on $\ce{K+}$ and other effects |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Carbonic anhydrase inhibitors | Acetazolamide | Proximal tubule, inhibiting $\ce{HCO3-}$ reabsorption | about $3$–$5$ | $\ce{K+}$ loss; **metabolic acidosis**; also used for glaucoma and altitude sickness |
| Osmotic diuretics | Mannitol | Proximal tubule and thin descending limb, hindering water reabsorption | Dose-dependent | Mainly water loss |
| Loop diuretics | Furosemide, bumetanide | Thick ascending limb, NKCC2 | $20$–$25$ | $\ce{K+}$ loss; increased $\ce{Ca^2+}$ and $\ce{Mg^2+}$ excretion; disrupts the medullary gradient and weakens concentrating ability |
| Thiazides | Hydrochlorothiazide, indapamide | Distal convoluted tubule, NCC | $5$–$8$ | $\ce{K+}$ loss; reduced $\ce{Ca^2+}$ excretion; may cause hyponatremia |
| Potassium-sparing diuretics: ENaC blockers | Amiloride, triamterene | Principal cells of the collecting duct, ENaC | $2$–$3$ | $\ce{K+}$ retention, may cause hyperkalemia |
| Potassium-sparing diuretics: mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists | Spironolactone, eplerenone | Principal cells of the collecting duct, aldosterone receptor | $2$–$3$ | $\ce{K+}$ retention; used in hyperaldosteronism and heart failure |
| SGLT2 inhibitors | Empagliflozin, dapagliflozin | Proximal tubule, SGLT2 | Mild | Glucosuria and osmotic diuresis |

Although the proximal tubule reabsorbs about two thirds of the $\ce{Na+}$, diuretics acting there are not very effective, because the loop of Henle downstream has a large reserve capacity and can reabsorb most of the extra NaCl delivered to it; loop diuretics are the most powerful because the thick ascending limb has a large share and the segments downstream of it have limited capacity. After prolonged use of a diuretic acting on one segment, the cells of the downstream segments hypertrophy and their transport capacity increases ("diuretic resistance"), and combining diuretics acting on different segments (such as a loop diuretic plus a thiazide) works better.

## 23.7. Evolution and Comparison

### 23.7.1. Birds and Reptiles: Secretion of Uric Acid and Postrenal Reabsorption

In uricotelic birds and squamates, uric acid is, besides being filtered, mainly secreted by the proximal tubule (about $90\%$ of the amount excreted) and forms colloidal spheres a few micrometers in diameter with protein in the tubular fluid, flowing supersaturated without precipitating; this "packaging" allows the uric acid concentration of the tubular fluid to greatly exceed its solubility without blocking the tubule. After the urine enters the cloaca it is, in many birds, driven by reverse peristalsis into the rectum and ceca, whose epithelium reabsorbs water and $\ce{Na+}$ (regulated by aldosterone), while the uric acid remains and is excreted as a white semisolid. The urine of birds thus undergoes "postrenal processing" by the gut after it leaves the kidney: the urine produced by the kidney is at most about 2–2.5 times as concentrated as plasma, yet the cloaca and gut can recover more water. Seabirds with salt glands do the opposite, reducing intestinal reabsorption of $\ce{Na+}$ and handing the $\ce{Na+}$ to the salt glands for excretion (Chapter 21).

### 23.7.2. Insects: Malpighian Tubules and Hindgut

The excretory system of insects is a two-stage system in which secretion and reabsorption are spatially separated (Chapter 22).

- Malpighian tubules: the apical V-type $\ce{H+}$-ATPase pumps $\ce{H+}$ into the lumen, and a $\ce{K+}/\ce{H+}$ (or $\ce{Na+}/\ce{H+}$) antiporter uses this gradient to secrete $\ce{K+}$; $\ce{Cl-}$ follows through stellate cells or the paracellular pathway, and water enters through **aquaporins**, forming a $\ce{K+}$-rich primary urine that is isosmotic with the hemolymph. Metabolic wastes and foreign substances are secreted into the lumen, and uric acid precipitates after acidification in the lumen. Fluid secretion is controlled by diuretic hormones: corticotropin-releasing factor-like diuretic hormone activates transport in the principal cells through cAMP, and kinin-type peptides increase the $\ce{Cl-}$ permeability of stellate cells through $\ce{Ca^2+}$. The blood-sucking kissing bug (*Rhodnius*) takes in a blood meal equal to 10 times its body weight, and under the action of serotonin and diuretic hormone the secretion rate of its Malpighian tubules rises about 1000-fold, so that most of the water and salt of the meal is excreted within a few hours.
- Hindgut and rectum: water, ions and useful substances are reabsorbed, and the final excreta can be much more hypertonic than the hemolymph. In the locust, the rectal pad cells transport ions actively and build a local hypertonic region in the narrow spaces between the cells, which draws water out of the rectal lumen, and the ions are then recovered downstream (the mechanism of the local osmotic gradient differs from the **countercurrent multiplication** of Chapter 24). In the tenebrionid beetle (mealworm), the ends of the Malpighian tubules lie against the rectum and are enclosed in an impermeable sheath (the cryptonephridial system); KCl accumulates in the tubules to a very high osmolality and draws water out of the feces, even allowing the animal to absorb water vapor from air at a relative humidity above about $88\%$.

The vertebrate nephron uses ultrafiltration plus reabsorption and insects use secretion plus reabsorption, and the two differ completely in detail, yet each consists of a "primary-fluid-forming segment" and a "composition-adjusting segment" in series, and the epithelia of the latter all depend on gradients built by ion pumps to move water. This is convergence under the same physical constraints.

## Questions

1. A patient with diabetes has a blood glucose of $400\,\mathrm{mg\,dL^{-1}}$, a GFR of $120\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}}$ and a glucose $T_{\mathrm{m}} = 375\,\mathrm{mg\,min^{-1}}$. Neglecting splay, how much glucose is lost in the urine per minute? What is this equivalent to in energy per day?

   **Answer** The filtered load is $120 \times 4 = 480\,\mathrm{mg\,min^{-1}}$ and the excretion is $480 - 375 = 105\,\mathrm{mg\,min^{-1}}$, about $151\,\mathrm{g}$ per day, or about $2.6\,\mathrm{MJ}$ (about $600\,\mathrm{kcal}$) at $17\,\mathrm{kJ\,g^{-1}}$; this is one of the causes of the weight loss in uncontrolled diabetes. Allowing for splay, the actual excretion is slightly larger.
2. At the end of some tubular segment the TF/P of inulin is $5$ and that of a substance $X$ is $0.5$. How much of the filtered $X$ has been reabsorbed up to this point?

   **Answer** The fraction remaining is $0.5/5 = 10\%$, so $90\%$ has been reabsorbed. It also follows that $1 - 1/5 = 80\%$ of the water has been reabsorbed.
3. Why can the proximal tubule reabsorb about $67\%$ of the $\ce{Na+}$ yet produce only an isosmotic reabsorbate, whereas the collecting duct can continue to reabsorb $\ce{Na+}$ from tubular fluid whose $\ce{Na+}$ concentration is only $40\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$?

   **Answer** The tight junctions of the proximal tubule are leaky and its water permeability is extremely high: any osmotic difference is immediately abolished by water flow and the concentration gradient of $\ce{Na+}$ is canceled by paracellular back-leak, so only large-volume isosmotic reabsorption is possible (a gradient-time system). The tight junctions of the collecting duct are very tight and the paracellular permeability to $\ce{Na+}$ is very low, so principal cells can reabsorb $\ce{Na+}$ against an electrochemical driving force of about $74\,\mathrm{mV}$ without back-leak; water permeability can also be switched off by antidiuretic hormone, so a large transepithelial concentration difference can be maintained.
4. Why are patients on loop diuretics prone to hypokalemia and hypomagnesemia, while patients with hypercalcemia are sometimes given them?

   **Answer** Loop diuretics inhibit NKCC2, so large amounts of NaCl and fluid reach the distal nephron. Distal flow and $\ce{Na+}$ delivery increase, ENaC of the principal cells absorbs more $\ce{Na+}$ and the lumen becomes more negative, BK channels are activated by flow, and $\ce{K+}$ secretion increases; at the same time the reduced blood volume activates aldosterone, which further promotes $\ce{K+}$ excretion. Inhibition of NKCC2 also abolishes the positive luminal potential generated by $\ce{K+}$ back-flux, paracellular reabsorption of $\ce{Mg^2+}$ and $\ce{Ca^2+}$ decreases, and hypomagnesemia results; the same effect increases $\ce{Ca^2+}$ excretion, and together with fluid replacement it can be used to lower the blood calcium of patients with hypercalcemia.
5. Use the threshold model of phosphate to explain why a rise in plasma phosphate appears relatively late in patients with chronic kidney disease.

   **Answer** In the steady state $P = C_0 + J/\mathrm{GFR}$. When GFR falls, the term $J/\mathrm{GFR}$ rises, but FGF23 and parathyroid hormone increase accordingly, lowering $T_{\mathrm{mP}}$ and hence $C_0$, which partly offsets the rise, so plasma phosphate can remain normal for a long time. When GFR falls below about $20\%$–$30\%$ of normal, $C_0$ has already fallen close to zero and cannot fall further, and $P \approx J/\mathrm{GFR}$ rises markedly as GFR declines. The price is that the long-term high levels of FGF23 and parathyroid hormone are themselves harmful to bone and the cardiovascular system.
6. Why is it said that "aldosterone acts on the same cell, yet can perform the two separate tasks of conserving sodium and excreting potassium"? What side effects would a high-potassium diet have without the NCC switch?

   **Answer** Aldosterone increases ENaC and ROMK in the principal cell, and $\ce{Na+}$ reabsorption and $\ce{K+}$ secretion are electrically coupled in the same cell. Which one predominates depends on the amounts of $\ce{Na+}$ and fluid reaching the principal cell, which is determined by NCC upstream: angiotensin II activates NCC, so $\ce{Na+}$ is absorbed already in the distal convoluted tubule (sodium retention with little potassium excretion); high $\ce{K+}$ inhibits NCC, so $\ce{Na+}$ is delivered to the principal cells and exchanged for $\ce{K+}$ (potassium excretion with little sodium retention). Without this switch, the rise of aldosterone caused by a high-potassium diet would also markedly increase $\ce{Na+}$ retention, causing volume excess and a rise in blood pressure; in fact a high-potassium diet lowers blood pressure, partly because it inhibits NCC.
