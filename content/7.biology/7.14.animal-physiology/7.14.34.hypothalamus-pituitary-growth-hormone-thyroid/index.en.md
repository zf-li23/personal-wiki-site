The hypothalamus and pituitary are the command center of the **endocrine system**: the hypothalamus translates information from the brain (stress, temperature, **circadian rhythm**, nutritional state) into hormonal signals, and the anterior pituitary amplifies these faint signals and sends them to the target glands and tissues of the whole body. This chapter first treats the structure of the pituitary and its control by the hypothalamus (Section 34.1), then the two hormones of the adenohypophysis that act directly on tissues, growth hormone (Section 34.2) and prolactin (Section 34.3), then the neurohypophysis (Section 34.4), and finally the first complete endocrine axis, the thyroid and the hypothalamic–pituitary–thyroid axis (Sections 34.5 and 34.6). The general principles of the endocrine system (receptors, clearance, feedback) are in [Chapter 32](/7.14.32); flow dilution in portal systems, pulsatile secretion, local activation by deiodinases and the kinetic models of thyroid hormone are in [Chapter 33](/7.14.33) and are cited directly here. The **hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis** and the gonadal axes are in Chapters 35 and 37, and the renal actions of antidiuretic hormone are in [Chapter 24](/7.14.24#sec-24.4).

## 34.1. The Pituitary

### 34.1.1. Anatomy and Developmental Origin

The pituitary lies in the hypophyseal fossa (sella turcica) of the sphenoid bone at the base of the skull and is connected to the hypothalamus through the pituitary stalk; it is about $1\,\mathrm{cm}$ in diameter and weighs about $0.5$–$1\,\mathrm{g}$ (it enlarges in pregnancy because of hyperplasia of lactotrophs). It consists of two parts of different origin:

**Definition 34.1.1 (Adenohypophysis and Neurohypophysis)** The part of the pituitary that develops from Rathke's pouch, an upward outgrowth of the ectoderm of the embryonic stomodeum, and consists of epithelial endocrine cells is called the **adenohypophysis**, that is, the anterior lobe of the pituitary (including the pars distalis, pars tuberalis and pars intermedia); the part that develops from the infundibulum, a downward outgrowth of the floor of the diencephalon, and consists of the axon terminals of hypothalamic neurons and glial cells is called the **neurohypophysis**, that is, the posterior lobe.

The two parts form from different cell lineages in development (the development of the adenohypophysis is treated in [7.5.22](/7.5.22)), and so they are also linked to the hypothalamus in different ways: the neurohypophysis is an extension of the hypothalamic neurons themselves, and the hypothalamic hormones are released directly into the blood there; the adenohypophysis has no neural connection with the hypothalamus, and the hypothalamus controls it through portal vessels (Section 34.1.3). The pars intermedia has regressed in humans, but is well developed in amphibians and fish, where it secretes melanocyte-stimulating hormone (MSH) and controls changes of body color.

### 34.1.2. Cells and Hormones of the Adenohypophysis

The adenohypophysis has five kinds of endocrine cells, which secrete six main hormones:

| Cell | Proportion | Hormone | Chemical nature | Main target and action | Main hypothalamic control |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Somatotrophs | $30\%$–$40\%$ | Growth hormone (GH) | Protein, 191 aa | Almost all tissues: growth and metabolism; liver: IGF-1 | GHRH (+), somatostatin (−) |
| Corticotrophs | About $15\%$–$20\%$ | Adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) | Peptide, 39 aa (from POMC) | Adrenal cortex: cortisol and androgens | CRH (+), ADH (+) |
| Thyrotrophs | About $5\%$ | Thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) | Glycoprotein, α + β | Thyroid | TRH (+), somatostatin (−) |
| Gonadotrophs | About $5\%$ | Follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), luteinizing hormone (LH) | Glycoproteins, α + β | Gonads: gametogenesis and sex hormones | GnRH (+) |
| Lactotrophs | About $5\%$ (more in pregnancy) | Prolactin (PRL) | Protein, 199 aa | Mammary gland: lactation | Dopamine (−), TRH (+) |

TSH, FSH and LH (and placental hCG) share the same α subunit, and each β subunit determines receptor specificity. GH and PRL belong to the same protein family (also homologous with the human placental lactogen hPL of the placenta), arose by gene duplication, and have partly overlapping biological activity. Among the adenohypophyseal hormones, TSH, ACTH, FSH and LH act on another **endocrine gland** and are called tropic hormones; GH and PRL act mainly directly on nonendocrine tissues.

### 34.1.3. The Hypothalamic–Pituitary Portal System and the Hypothalamic Regulatory Hormones

Parvocellular neuroendocrine neurons at the base of the hypothalamus release regulatory hormones into the primary capillary plexus of the median eminence, from which they reach the secondary capillary plexus of the adenohypophysis through the long portal veins in the pituitary stalk; this is the **hypothalamo-hypophyseal portal system** (Definition 33.5.2). By the flow dilution relation (Eq. (33.13)), the concentration seen by the adenohypophysis is about $10^4$ times that if these hormones entered the **systemic circulation** directly (Example 33.5.1), and the hypothalamus needs to secrete only nanogram amounts to control the adenohypophysis.

| Hormone | Structure | Main nucleus of origin | Action | Receptor and messenger |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Thyrotropin-releasing hormone (TRH) | Tripeptide (pGlu–His–Pro–$\ce{NH2}$) | Paraventricular nucleus (parvocellular part) | Increases secretion of TSH and PRL | $\mathrm{G_q}$ |
| Corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH) | 41 aa | Paraventricular nucleus (parvocellular part) | Increases secretion of ACTH | $\mathrm{G_s}$ |
| Gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) | Decapeptide | Preoptic area (and in primates the mediobasal hypothalamus) | Increases secretion of LH and FSH | $\mathrm{G_q}$ |
| Growth hormone–releasing hormone (GHRH) | 44 aa | **Arcuate nucleus** | Increases secretion of GH | $\mathrm{G_s}$ |
| Somatostatin (SS) | 14 aa (and 28 aa) | Periventricular nucleus (anterior part) | Decreases secretion of GH and TSH | $\mathrm{G_i}$ |
| Dopamine (prolactin release-inhibiting hormone) | Amine | Arcuate nucleus (tuberoinfundibular system) | Decreases secretion of PRL | D2, $\mathrm{G_i}$ |

Except for dopamine, these regulatory hormones are all peptides and are therefore also called hypothalamic regulatory peptides. One regulatory hormone can affect more than one pituitary hormone (TRH also stimulates PRL, and somatostatin also inhibits TSH), and one pituitary hormone can be controlled by several regulatory hormones. For most adenohypophyseal hormones the hypothalamic action is mainly stimulatory, and their secretion falls after section of the pituitary stalk; prolactin is the exception: the hypothalamus inhibits it continuously with dopamine, and after section of the stalk its secretion rises instead.

## 34.2. Growth Hormone

### 34.2.1. Structure and the Rhythm of Secretion

**Definition 34.2.1 (Growth Hormone)** The protein hormone secreted by the somatotrophs of the adenohypophysis, containing 191 amino acids (about $22\,\mathrm{kDa}$, two disulfide bonds), that promotes postnatal somatic growth and regulates protein, fat and carbohydrate metabolism is called **growth hormone** (GH, also somatotropin).

About $10\%$ of the GH in plasma is a $20\,\mathrm{kDa}$ splice variant; about $40\%$ of the 22 kDa GH is bound to the high-affinity GH-binding protein (the cleaved extracellular domain of the GH receptor), and the half-life of GH is about $20$–$25\,\mathrm{min}$ (the two-compartment model of Chapter 33, Eq. (33.7)).

GH is secreted in pulses: an adult has about $6$–$12$ pulses a day, the largest occurring in slow-wave sleep about an hour after sleep onset; between pulses plasma GH falls very low (in adults below about $1\,\mathrm{ng\,mL^{-1}}$) and the peak of a pulse can reach $10$–$30\,\mathrm{ng\,mL^{-1}}$ or more. Secretion is highest at puberty and then declines gradually with age. Since the concentration varies several tens of times during the day, a single measurement of GH has almost no diagnostic value, and clinically IGF-1 is measured (Section 34.2.4), or stimulation and suppression tests are used.

Factors affecting GH secretion:

| Promoting | Inhibiting |
|---|---|
| Hypoglycemia (one of the strongest stimuli), starvation and fasting, a fall in plasma free fatty acids | Hyperglycemia, a rise in plasma free fatty acids |
| Amino acids (arginine) and a high-protein diet | Obesity |
| Exercise, stress, trauma, fever | Aging |
| Slow-wave sleep | Somatostatin |
| **Ghrelin** | GH itself (short-loop feedback), IGF-1 (long-loop feedback) |
| Estrogen and androgen at puberty | Excess **glucocorticoids** |

The regulation of GH secretion matches its metabolic actions: during fasting, hypoglycemia and exercise, GH rises, mobilizing fat and sparing glucose while conserving protein.

### 34.2.2. The Antagonism of GHRH and Somatostatin

GH cells receive two opposing hypothalamic signals at once. GHRH secreted by the arcuate nucleus acts on a $\mathrm{G_s}$-coupled receptor and raises cAMP; PKA opens L-type $\ce{Ca^2+}$ channels, and the $\ce{Ca^2+}$ entry triggers exocytosis of GH; in the long term, through CREB and the pituitary-specific transcription factor Pit-1, it promotes transcription of the GH gene and proliferation of GH cells. Somatostatin secreted by the periventricular nucleus acts on a $\mathrm{G_i}$-coupled receptor, inhibits **adenylyl cyclase** and opens $\ce{K+}$ channels, causing **hyperpolarization** of the cell and reducing $\ce{Ca^2+}$ entry; when both are present, the inhibition by somatostatin predominates. The pulses of GH are thought to arise mainly from the alternating rhythm of the two: when the secretion of somatostatin weakens and at the same time a burst of GHRH appears, GH releases a pulse. **Ghrelin** acts on another receptor (the GH secretagogue receptor, $\mathrm{G_q}$) and promotes GH secretion at both the hypothalamic and pituitary levels.

There are three feedback loops: GH itself inhibits GHRH through **short-loop feedback** and promotes the release of somatostatin; the IGF-1 induced by GH directly inhibits GH cells and, through **long-loop feedback**, inhibits GHRH and promotes somatostatin.

### 34.2.3. Metabolic Actions

The metabolic actions of GH are of two kinds, acute and chronic, and they do not point in the same direction:

1. Increasing the accumulation of protein in tissues (anabolism): increased transmembrane transport of amino acids; increased translation of mRNA and transcription of DNA; reduced protein breakdown, making **nitrogen balance** positive.
2. Mobilizing fat: promoting the release of fatty acids from adipocytes (lipolysis) and the oxidation of fatty acids to acetyl-CoA for energy, so that body fat falls and the formation of **ketone bodies** may increase.
3. Reducing the utilization of glucose: lowering glucose uptake by skeletal muscle and adipose tissue (**insulin resistance**) and increasing hepatic glucose production, so that blood glucose rises; the hyperglycemia in turn stimulates insulin secretion.

Items 2 and 3 are opposite to the actions of **insulin** and are called the anti-insulin or diabetogenic actions of GH: about one-third of patients with long-standing GH excess (acromegaly) have abnormal glucose tolerance. The metabolic actions of GH can be summarized as "burn fat for energy, spare glucose and protein," which is just what is needed in fasting and exercise.

The discovery of GH came from animal experiments. Evans and Long (1921) repeatedly injected anterior pituitary extract into rats, and the rats grew into giant individuals; later, purified GH injected into young rats produced a greater gain in body weight than in littermate controls, the gain being mainly protein and water, while body fat actually decreased.

The GH receptor is a cytokine receptor: one GH molecule binds two receptor monomers at once, changing the conformation of the preformed receptor dimer and activating the associated JAK2 kinase, which regulates target genes through STAT5 (and the MAPK and PI3K pathways) (the **JAK–STAT pathway**, [7.1.15](/7.1.15#sec-15.2)).

### 34.2.4. Insulin-like Growth Factor 1

Most of the action of GH on the growth of bone and cartilage is not direct but is mediated by another hormone that it induces.

**Definition 34.2.2 (Insulin-like Growth Factor 1)** The polypeptide of 70 amino acids, homologous in structure with proinsulin, produced mainly by the liver under stimulation by growth hormone (and also locally by tissues such as cartilage, bone and muscle), that mediates the growth-promoting action of GH through the IGF-1 receptor (a **receptor tyrosine kinase**), is called **insulin-like growth factor 1** (IGF-1), formerly somatomedin C.

The properties of IGF-1 form a contrast with those of GH:

- More than $99\%$ of the IGF-1 in plasma is bound to IGF-binding proteins (IGFBPs, six in all, synthesized by the liver and other tissues), and about $75\%$–$80\%$ forms with IGFBP-3 and the acid-labile subunit (ALS) a ternary complex of about $150\,\mathrm{kDa}$ that cannot leave the vessels. The half-life of free IGF-1 is about $10\,\mathrm{min}$, and in the ternary complex about $12$–$15\,\mathrm{h}$.
- Therefore, although GH varies several tens of times during the day, plasma IGF-1 fluctuates by less than twofold, and is suitable as an integrated index of total GH secretion: IGF-1 rises reliably in GH excess and falls in GH deficiency.
- IGF-1 in plasma acts in an endocrine manner, but in the **growth plate**, locally produced IGF-1 acts in **paracrine** and **autocrine** ways. In mice with liver-specific knockout of the IGF-1 gene, plasma IGF-1 falls by about $75\%$ but growth is almost normal, showing that locally produced IGF-1 is crucial for growth; the original "somatomedin hypothesis" (GH acts only through IGF-1 produced by the liver) has therefore been revised.
- The relation of IGF-1 level to growth rate differs with the stage of development: IGF-1 peaks at puberty, in agreement with the growth spurt; growth is fastest in infancy while IGF-1 is low, and growth then depends more on nutrition, insulin and other factors; in adults the epiphyses have closed, IGF-1 stays at a moderate level, but the long bones no longer lengthen.

From the standpoint of signal processing, the GH–IGF-1 system is a series of first-order low-pass filters (Eq. (33.2) of Chapter 33): the clearance of GH (half-life 20 min) lets pulses on an hourly scale through, while the expression of the IGF-1 gene in the liver (time constant of several hours) and the clearance of IGF-1 (half-life about 15 h) smooth them out. The GH pulses convey information to hepatocytes, which are sensitive to temporal pattern (such as the sexually dimorphic pattern of GH described in Chapter 33), whereas the growth of bone sees a steady IGF-1 signal that reflects the total amount of GH.

### 34.2.5. Disorders of Growth Hormone Secretion

**Definition 34.2.3 (Gigantism and Acromegaly)** Excessive secretion of GH before the epiphyses close, with excessive growth of the long bones and abnormally tall stature, is called **gigantism**; excessive secretion of GH after the epiphyses have closed, when the long bones no longer lengthen but bones such as those of the hands, feet, mandible and skull and the soft tissues become enlarged, is called **acromegaly**.

The great majority of both are caused by an adenoma of the pituitary GH cells. The tallest person on record, Robert Wadlow (1918–1940), was $2.72\,\mathrm{m}$ tall; his gigantism was caused by excessive GH from the pituitary and he was still growing when he died; the professional wrestler André Roussimoff (1946–1993, billed at $2.24\,\mathrm{m}$ and $236\,\mathrm{kg}$) had both gigantism and acromegaly. Acromegaly begins slowly and is often diagnosed only many years after onset; it presents with coarse facial features, enlarged hands and feet, increased shoe and ring sizes, macroglossia, sweating and arthropathy, and is often accompanied by abnormal glucose tolerance, hypertension and myocardial hypertrophy; untreated patients have a shortened life span. The diagnosis rests on a raised IGF-1 and on the failure of GH to be suppressed after an oral glucose load (in normal people GH falls very low). Treatment includes surgical removal of the adenoma, somatostatin analogues (octreotide, which acts on the somatostatin receptors of GH cells) and GH receptor antagonists.

GH deficiency in children causes pituitary dwarfism: short stature with normal proportions and normal intelligence. Since 1985 treatment has used recombinant human GH; before that, GH extracted from cadaveric pituitaries was used, which infected some of the children with a prion disease (Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease). Patients with a defect of the GH receptor (Laron syndrome) have very high GH and very low IGF-1, are likewise short and do not respond to GH treatment, and need recombinant IGF-1: this is a typical example of normal (even high) hormone levels with absent action (Chapter 32).

## 34.3. Prolactin

**Definition 34.3.1 (Prolactin)** The protein hormone secreted by the lactotrophs of the adenohypophysis, containing 199 amino acids, that stimulates mammary gland development and milk production is called **prolactin** (PRL).

Prolactin is the only pituitary hormone mainly under inhibitory control by the hypothalamus: dopamine secreted by tuberoinfundibular neurons of the **arcuate nucleus** reaches the lactotrophs through the portal system and continuously inhibits their secretion by acting on D2 receptors. TRH, vasoactive intestinal peptide and oxytocin can stimulate it, but under normal conditions inhibition predominates. During pregnancy, estrogen causes hyperplasia of lactotrophs, and plasma PRL rises gradually to 10–20 times the nonpregnant level; after delivery, nerve impulses from the infant's suckling at the nipple ascend through the spinal cord to the hypothalamus and inhibit the secretion of dopamine, and each nursing causes a peak of PRL secretion.

The actions of PRL (through the JAK2–STAT5 pathway):

- together with estrogen, progesterone, cortisol, insulin and GH, it promotes the development of the mammary alveoli during pregnancy;
- after delivery it promotes the synthesis of milk (casein, whey proteins, lactose and lipids). The high concentrations of progesterone and estrogen during pregnancy inhibit the action of PRL on milk production, and lactation begins only after the placenta is delivered and progesterone falls abruptly;
- it inhibits the pulsatile secretion of GnRH (partly through kisspeptin neurons), so the high PRL of lactation inhibits ovulation (lactational amenorrhea, Chapter 38).

A prolactinoma is the most common functioning pituitary adenoma and causes galactorrhea, amenorrhea and infertility in women and hypogonadism in men. Dopamine D2 receptor agonists (bromocriptine, cabergoline) can inhibit PRL and shrink the tumor; conversely, antipsychotic drugs that block D2 receptors often cause hyperprolactinemia.

## 34.4. The Neurohypophysis

### 34.4.1. The Magnocellular Neurosecretory System

The hormones of the neurohypophysis are synthesized by the magnocellular neurons of the supraoptic and paraventricular nuclei of the hypothalamus. These neurons have large cell bodies and long axons, which form the hypothalamo-hypophyseal tract and end around the capillaries of the neurohypophysis through the pituitary stalk. The hormones are synthesized in the cell body as prohormones, packaged with carrier proteins (neurophysins) in **secretory granules**, transported to the terminal by fast **axonal transport** (Example 32.3.1), and stored in the swellings of the terminals and axons (Herring bodies). When the action potential of the neuron reaches the terminal, $\ce{Ca^2+}$ entry triggers exocytosis. The magnocellular neurons of the supraoptic nucleus mainly synthesize antidiuretic hormone and those of the paraventricular nucleus mainly oxytocin, but both nuclei contain both kinds of neurons.

The neurohypophysis also contains a large number of specialized glial cells called pituicytes. They do not secrete hormones but envelop the nerve terminals and regulate the contact of terminals with the capillary basement membrane: during dehydration or lactation, the processes of pituicytes retract, letting more terminals face the vessels directly, which favors hormone release.

### 34.4.2. Antidiuretic Hormone

The synthesis of **antidiuretic hormone** (arginine vasopressin), the regulation of its secretion (**osmoreceptors**, volume and pressure receptors) and its action in the collecting duct (V2 receptor, cAMP, AQP2) were covered in [Chapter 24](/7.14.24#sec-24.4). Two of its other actions are relevant here: constricting vessels through V1 receptors, which helps maintain blood pressure in severe hemorrhage; and, through V1b receptors of the adenohypophysis, strengthening the stimulation of ACTH secretion by CRH, acting synergistically with CRH in stress (Chapter 35).

### 34.4.3. Oxytocin

**Definition 34.4.1 (Oxytocin)** The nonapeptide hormone synthesized by hypothalamic magnocellular neurons and released in the neurohypophysis, differing from antidiuretic hormone in only two amino acids, whose main actions are to cause contraction of the myoepithelial cells of the mammary gland (milk ejection) and of uterine smooth muscle, is called **oxytocin**.

Oxytocin acts on the $\mathrm{G_q}$-coupled oxytocin receptor and raises intracellular $\ce{Ca^2+}$:

- Milk ejection: the infant suckles at the nipple, sensory impulses ascend through the spinal cord and brainstem to the supraoptic and paraventricular nuclei, and oxytocin is released in pulses; about $30$–$60\,\mathrm{s}$ later the myoepithelial cells around the mammary alveoli contract and squeeze milk into the ducts. This is a neuroendocrine reflex that is easily conditioned (a mother can eject milk on hearing her baby cry) and is also easily inhibited by anxiety and stress.
- Parturition: at the end of pregnancy, estrogen greatly increases the oxytocin receptors of the myometrium and the sensitivity of the uterus to oxytocin rises; during labor the fetal head distends the cervix and reflexively causes oxytocin secretion, and oxytocin strengthens uterine contraction, forming **positive feedback** (the **Ferguson reflex**, Chapter 32). After delivery oxytocin promotes uterine contraction and reduces bleeding. Clinically oxytocin is used to induce labor and to prevent or treat postpartum hemorrhage.
- Central actions: oxytocin is also projected by parvocellular neurons of the paraventricular nucleus to many regions of the brain and takes part in maternal behavior, pair bonding and social recognition (studied mostly in animals such as prairie voles).

The endocrine regulation of pregnancy, parturition and lactation is treated in detail in Chapter 38.

## 34.5. The Thyroid

### 34.5.1. Structure

The thyroid lies in the front of the neck, at the sides of and in front of the upper trachea, and consists of two lobes and an isthmus; in adults it weighs about $15$–$25\,\mathrm{g}$ and is one of the largest dedicated **endocrine glands**. Its **blood flow** is very large, about 4–5 times the weight of the gland per minute. The gland consists of millions of follicles: each follicle is a sac (diameter about $100$–$300\,\mu\mathrm{m}$) enclosed by a single layer of cuboidal follicular epithelial cells, and the lumen is filled with colloid, whose main component is thyroglobulin. The follicular epithelium becomes tall (columnar) and the colloid decreases when the gland is functionally active; the epithelium becomes flat and the colloid increases when function is low. Scattered between the follicles are parafollicular cells (C cells), which secrete **calcitonin** (Chapter 36).

**Definition 34.5.1 (Thyroid Hormone)** The iodinated thyronines synthesized and secreted by the thyroid follicular cells, mainly thyroxine (3,5,3′,5′-tetraiodothyronine, $\mathrm{T_4}$) and triiodothyronine (3,5,3′-triiodothyronine, $\mathrm{T_3}$), are collectively called **thyroid hormone**.

About $93\%$ of the hormone secreted by the thyroid is $\mathrm{T_4}$ and about $7\%$ is $\mathrm{T_3}$ (about $90$–$100\,\mu\mathrm{g}$ and $5$–$10\,\mu\mathrm{g}$ per day), with a very small amount of inactive reverse triiodothyronine ($\mathrm{rT_3}$). But the form that acts in the tissues is mainly $\mathrm{T_3}$: its affinity for the receptor is about 10 times that of $\mathrm{T_4}$, and about $80\%$ of plasma $\mathrm{T_3}$ comes from the deiodination of $\mathrm{T_4}$ in peripheral tissues (Section 33.4.4).

### 34.5.2. Iodide Uptake

The synthesis of thyroid hormone requires iodine. An adult needs about $150\,\mu\mathrm{g}$ of iodine a day (about $250\,\mu\mathrm{g}$ in pregnancy and lactation), that is, about $1\,\mathrm{mg}$ a week. Iodine absorbed from the digestive tract enters the blood as iodide ($\ce{I-}$), about one-fifth is taken up by the thyroid, and the rest is excreted mainly by the kidney. In iodine-deficient regions it is supplied by iodized salt: the classic ratio is about 1 part of NaI per $10^5$ parts of NaCl.

**Definition 34.5.2 (Sodium–Iodide Symporter)** The secondary active transport protein in the basolateral membrane of thyroid follicular cells that transports iodide into the cell against its concentration gradient with a stoichiometry of 2 $\ce{Na+}$ to 1 $\ce{I-}$ is called the **sodium–iodide symporter** (NIS).

The energy of NIS comes from the electrochemical gradient of $\ce{Na+}$, which is maintained by the **Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase** of the basolateral membrane ([Chapter 3](/7.14.3#sec-3.4.3)); the iodide that enters the cell then flows into the follicular lumen through channels such as pendrin (a $\ce{Cl-}$/$\ce{I-}$ exchanger) in the apical membrane. Follicular cells can therefore concentrate iodide to $20$–$40$ times the plasma level, a process called iodide trapping, which is promoted by TSH.

**Example 34.5.1 (The Concentration Limit of NIS)** Each cycle of NIS brings 2 $\ce{Na+}$ and 1 $\ce{I-}$ into the cell, a net movement of one positive charge. By a derivation like that of Eq. (3.6), when the transporter is at equilibrium
$$
\frac{[\ce{I-}]_{\mathrm{i}}}{[\ce{I-}]_{\mathrm{o}}} = \left(\frac{[\ce{Na+}]_{\mathrm{o}}}{[\ce{Na+}]_{\mathrm{i}}}\right)^2\mathrm{e}^{-FV_{\mathrm{m}}/RT}.
$$
Take $[\ce{Na+}]_{\mathrm{o}}/[\ce{Na+}]_{\mathrm{i}} = 145/12$, a membrane potential of the follicular cell $V_{\mathrm{m}} = -45\,\mathrm{mV}$ and $37\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$ ($RT/F = 26.7\,\mathrm{mV}$): $12.1^2 \times \mathrm{e}^{45/26.7} \approx 146 \times 5.4 \approx 800$. Thermodynamically NIS could concentrate iodide nearly a thousandfold; the measured $20$–$40$ is much smaller, because iodide continually flows out into the follicular lumen through the apical membrane and is organified, so that intracellular iodide is always far from equilibrium.

The substrate selectivity of NIS is not strict. Perchlorate ($\ce{ClO4-}$) and thiocyanate ($\ce{SCN-}$) competitively inhibit iodide uptake; radioactive pertechnetate ($\ce{^{99m}TcO4-}$) and $\ce{^{123}I-}$ are taken up by NIS and used for thyroid imaging; $\ce{^{131}I-}$ is taken up and used to treat hyperthyroidism and differentiated thyroid cancer. Mutations of the NIS gene cause congenital hypothyroidism, and mutations of the pendrin gene cause Pendred syndrome (goiter and deafness).

### 34.5.3. Synthesis, Storage and Secretion of Thyroid Hormone

**Definition 34.5.3 (Thyroglobulin)** The large glycoprotein (a dimer of about $660\,\mathrm{kDa}$) synthesized by follicular cells and secreted into the follicular lumen, whose tyrosine residues are iodinated and coupled to form thyroid hormone, and which is stored in the lumen as colloid, is called **thyroglobulin** (Tg).

Thyroid hormone is not a small free molecule synthesized in the cytoplasm, but is synthesized on the "template" of thyroglobulin at the border between the follicular lumen and the apical membrane:

1. Follicular cells synthesize thyroglobulin in the rough endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi apparatus and secrete it by exocytosis into the follicular lumen;
2. Dual oxidase (DUOX2) in the apical membrane produces hydrogen peroxide; thyroid peroxidase (TPO) uses the hydrogen peroxide to oxidize $\ce{I-}$ and adds iodine to the tyrosine residues of thyroglobulin (organification of iodine), producing monoiodotyrosine (MIT) and diiodotyrosine (DIT) residues;
3. TPO then catalyzes the coupling of two adjacent iodotyrosine residues: DIT + DIT → $\mathrm{T_4}$, MIT + DIT → $\mathrm{T_3}$. The newly formed hormone remains attached to thyroglobulin by peptide bonds; only some of the hundred or so tyrosine residues of thyroglobulin are iodinated, and fewer still form hormone;
4. Storage: the hormone stored in the follicular lumen is enough for 2–3 months, the largest reserve of any endocrine gland, which lets the body keep going for a time if the iodine supply is interrupted;
5. Secretion: TSH stimulates the follicular cells to take colloid droplets back in from the apical membrane by pinocytosis; the colloid droplets fuse with lysosomes, proteases hydrolyze thyroglobulin and release $\mathrm{T_4}$ and $\mathrm{T_3}$, which enter the blood through a transporter (MCT8) of the basolateral membrane; the MIT and DIT released at the same time are deiodinated by iodotyrosine deiodinase and the iodine is reused inside the cell.

When the iodine supply increases, the ratio of DIT and $\mathrm{T_4}$ rises; in iodine deficiency MIT increases and the proportion of $\mathrm{T_3}$ rises accordingly, producing more active hormone with less iodine. Antithyroid drugs (propylthiouracil, methimazole) inhibit TPO and thereby the synthesis of hormone, and are used to treat hyperthyroidism; propylthiouracil also inhibits peripheral D1. A large dose of iodine paradoxically inhibits organification and hormone release temporarily (the Wolff–Chaikoff effect), and after several days the thyroid "escapes" and synthesis resumes: this is a protective **autoregulation**, and is also used clinically in preparing patients for hyperthyroid surgery.

### 34.5.4. Plasma Transport and Peripheral Metabolism

After $\mathrm{T_4}$ and $\mathrm{T_3}$ enter the blood, the great majority is bound to three plasma proteins: thyroxine-binding globulin (TBG, about $70\%$–$75\%$), transthyretin (TTR, also called thyroxine-binding prealbumin, about $10\%$–$20\%$) and albumin (about $10\%$–$20\%$); free $\mathrm{T_4}$ is only about $0.02\%$–$0.03\%$ and free $\mathrm{T_3}$ about $0.3\%$ (the distribution table given by the PBK model of Chapter 33).

| | Total concentration | Free concentration | Free fraction | Plasma half-life |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $\mathrm{T_4}$ | $60$–$150\,\mathrm{nmol\,L^{-1}}$ | $10$–$25\,\mathrm{pmol\,L^{-1}}$ | About $0.02\%$ | $6$–$7\,\mathrm{d}$ |
| $\mathrm{T_3}$ | $1.2$–$2.8\,\mathrm{nmol\,L^{-1}}$ | $3$–$7\,\mathrm{pmol\,L^{-1}}$ | About $0.3\%$ | About $1\,\mathrm{d}$ |

The high affinity of the binding proteins releases the hormone slowly: after injection of $\mathrm{T_4}$ the action sets in slowly and lasts long ("slow onset, long duration"). Hormone enters cells not by free diffusion but through dedicated transporters (MCT8, OATP1C1 and others); children with mutations of MCT8 have raised plasma $\mathrm{T_3}$ but a brain deficient in thyroid hormone, with severe neurodevelopmental impairment. Inside the cell $\mathrm{T_4}$ is converted to $\mathrm{T_3}$ by D2 or inactivated to $\mathrm{rT_3}$ by D3 (Section 33.4.4). The final degradation of thyroid hormone occurs mainly in the liver, kidney and skeletal muscle: deiodination, or conjugation with glucuronic acid or sulfate and excretion in bile, with the iodine released being taken up again by the thyroid or excreted by the kidney.

### 34.5.5. Mechanism of Action

The actions of thyroid hormone arise mainly from the binding of $\mathrm{T_3}$ to nuclear receptors. The thyroid hormone receptor (TR) has two genes, TRα and TRβ, and several splice isoforms with different tissue distributions: TRα1 predominates in heart and bone, TRβ1 in liver, and TRβ2 in the pituitary and hypothalamus, where it mediates negative feedback on TSH and TRH. TR is a class II **nuclear receptor** ([Chapter 32](/7.14.32#sec-32.4.2)): it forms a heterodimer with RXR and binds the thyroid hormone response element of target genes even without hormone, recruiting a **corepressor** that inhibits transcription; after $\mathrm{T_3}$ binds, the receptor exchanges it for a **coactivator** and activates transcription. Different genes are activated to different degrees, and the result is increased synthesis of a large number of proteins and intensified cellular activity. Mutations of the TRβ gene cause the thyroid hormone resistance syndrome: hormone levels are high and TSH is not suppressed, yet the response of tissues is reduced.

Thyroid hormone also has rapid nontranscriptional actions: through a binding site on the plasma membrane integrin $\alpha_{\mathrm{v}}\beta_3$ it activates MAPK, affects ion channels, $\ce{Ca^2+}$-ATPases and the generation of **second messengers**, and acts directly on **mitochondria**.

### 34.5.6. Physiological Actions

Thyroid hormone acts on almost all cells of the body, mainly raising the metabolic rate, promoting growth and development, and exerting a **permissive action** on many organ systems.

1. Energy metabolism. Thyroid hormone raises oxygen consumption and heat production in the great majority of tissues (the brain, spleen and gonads are a few exceptions): it increases the number of mitochondria and their membrane area, increases the expression of the **Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase** and the sarcoplasmic reticulum $\ce{Ca^2+}$-ATPase and the "leak–pump" cycling of ions, increases the expression of uncoupling proteins, and strengthens anabolism and catabolism together, so that more of the energy is released as heat. With complete deficiency the **basal metabolic rate** falls to about $50\%$–$60\%$ of normal, and in extreme excess it can rise to about $160\%$–$200\%$ (Chapter 30). The action of thyroid hormone on heat production in brown fat is described in Chapter 31.
2. Substance metabolism. Carbohydrate: it promotes intestinal absorption of glucose, hepatic glycogenolysis and **gluconeogenesis**, speeds up the utilization of glucose by peripheral tissues, and strengthens the glucose-raising actions of **epinephrine**, **glucagon**, **cortisol** and GH; in hyperthyroid patients blood glucose rises rapidly after a meal, even with glycosuria, but soon falls again. Lipid: it promotes lipolysis and fatty acid oxidation, increases the **LDL receptors** of hepatocytes and the secretion of cholesterol into bile, and plasma cholesterol, phospholipids and triglycerides fall (they rise in hypothyroidism, which is prone to atherosclerosis). Protein: at physiological levels it promotes protein synthesis; in excess breakdown accelerates and muscle wastes and weakens. With the faster metabolism the body's need for vitamins increases and body weight falls.
3. Growth and development. Thyroid hormone is indispensable for the development of brain and bone: it promotes neuronal differentiation, the formation of processes and synapses and myelination; it promotes bone growth and the maturation of the epiphyses, acting synergistically with GH (promoting the secretion of GH and the action of IGF-1). The thyroid hormone needed for brain development in early fetal life comes from the mother, and the fetal thyroid begins to work only in mid-pregnancy. Congenital hypothyroidism, if not treated promptly, causes irreversible intellectual disability and short stature, that is, cretinism; screening of TSH in a heel-prick blood sample from newborns can detect it within a few days of birth, and it is treated with $\mathrm{T_4}$. Iodine deficiency is the leading preventable cause of intellectual disability worldwide.
4. Cardiovascular. It increases the number of β-adrenergic receptors of cardiac muscle (permissive action, Example 32.5.1) and the expression of the sarcoplasmic reticulum $\ce{Ca^2+}$-ATPase (SERCA2) and of α-myosin heavy chain, and acts directly on the **sinoatrial node**, increasing heart rate and contractility; the increased heat production dilates skin vessels and lowers the **total peripheral resistance**. The result is an increase in **cardiac output** and a wider **pulse pressure**. Hyperthyroid patients often have tachycardia and atrial fibrillation, and β-blockers relieve the symptoms rapidly.
5. Nerve and muscle. It raises the **excitability** of the central nervous system: in hyperthyroidism, nervousness, irritability, insomnia and fine tremor; in hypothyroidism, sluggishness, somnolence and impaired memory; the relaxation phase of the tendon reflex is shortened in hyperthyroidism and prolonged in hypothyroidism.
6. Other. It increases the rate and depth of breathing (matching the increased oxygen consumption); increases gastrointestinal motility and appetite; increases the **glomerular filtration rate**; maintains normal sexual function; and increases erythropoiesis and the 2,3-BPG of red cells.

### 34.5.7. Disorders of Thyroid Function

**Definition 34.5.4 (Hyperthyroidism and Hypothyroidism)** The state of excessive secretion of thyroid hormone with excessively increased tissue metabolism is called **hyperthyroidism**; the state of insufficient thyroid hormone with slowed metabolism is called **hypothyroidism**.

| | Hyperthyroidism | Hypothyroidism |
|---|---|---|
| Most common cause | Graves disease: autoantibodies stimulating the TSH receptor; toxic nodules | Chronic lymphocytic thyroiditis (Hashimoto disease, anti-TPO antibodies); iodine deficiency; after thyroidectomy or radioiodine therapy |
| Metabolism | Raised basal metabolic rate, heat intolerance and sweating, increased appetite with weight loss | Lowered basal metabolic rate, cold intolerance, weight gain |
| Cardiovascular | Tachycardia, increased cardiac output, wide pulse pressure | Bradycardia, decreased cardiac output |
| Nerve and muscle | Nervousness, tremor, insomnia, muscle weakness | Sluggishness, somnolence, prolonged relaxation phase of the tendon reflex |
| Other | Diarrhea; the exophthalmos of Graves disease | Constipation; myxedema (accumulation of glycosaminoglycans and water in the interstitial spaces); raised blood cholesterol |

**Definition 34.5.5 (Goiter)** A diffuse or nodular enlargement of the thyroid is called **goiter**.

A goiter is not the same as hyperthyroidism. In iodine deficiency, thyroid hormone synthesis is insufficient, **negative feedback** weakens, TSH remains elevated and stimulates hyperplasia and hypertrophy of the thyroid, producing endemic goiter, and the patient may have normal thyroid function or hypothyroidism; the stimulating antibodies of Graves disease likewise enlarge the thyroid, but with hyperthyroidism.

With the logic of feedback, measurement of TSH and free $\mathrm{T_4}$ can locate the site of the lesion:

| | TSH | Free $\mathrm{T_4}$ |
|---|---|---|
| Primary hypothyroidism (disease of the thyroid itself) | Raised | Lowered |
| Secondary hypothyroidism (pituitary or hypothalamic disease) | Lowered or inappropriately normal | Lowered |
| Graves disease, toxic nodules | Lowered (suppressed) | Raised |
| TSH-secreting pituitary adenoma (rare) | Raised or inappropriately normal | Raised |

## 34.6. The Hypothalamic–Pituitary–Thyroid Axis

### 34.6.1. TRH and TSH

**Definition 34.6.1 (Hypothalamic–Pituitary–Thyroid Axis)** The three-stage regulatory system in which TRH secreted by the paraventricular nucleus of the hypothalamus stimulates the adenohypophysis through the portal system to secrete TSH, TSH stimulates the thyroid to synthesize and secrete thyroid hormone, and thyroid hormone in turn inhibits the secretion of TSH and TRH by negative feedback is called the **hypothalamic–pituitary–thyroid axis** (HPT axis).

TRH is produced by parvocellular neurons of the paraventricular nucleus, enters the portal system through the median eminence, and acts on the $\mathrm{G_q}$-coupled receptors of the thyrotrophs, promoting the release of stored TSH, the synthesis of TSH and the glycosylation of TSH (which gives it full biological activity). TSH is a **glycoprotein** of about $28\,\mathrm{kDa}$, secreted in pulses with a **circadian rhythm**, peaking around sleep onset. It acts on the TSH receptor of the basolateral membrane of follicular cells (coupled mainly to $\mathrm{G_s}$–cAMP, and at high concentrations also to $\mathrm{G_q}$), and its actions include:

1. promoting pinocytosis of colloid and hydrolysis of thyroglobulin, increasing the release of hormone (acting within minutes);
2. enhancing the activity and expression of the iodide pump (NIS), increasing iodide uptake;
3. increasing the iodination and coupling of tyrosine, promoting the synthesis of hormone (expression of TPO and thyroglobulin);
4. enlarging the follicular cells and raising their secretory activity;
5. increasing the number of follicular cells, with folding of the epithelium to form more follicles (thyroid hyperplasia).

The sum of these actions is that TSH promotes the secretion of hormone in the short term and the growth of the gland in the long term.

### 34.6.2. Negative Feedback

Free thyroid hormone inhibits the secretion of TSH and TRH by **negative feedback**, mainly through its action on the pituitary: it lowers the transcription of the TSH β subunit gene and lowers the sensitivity of thyrotrophs to TRH (by reducing TRH receptors). What actually acts on TR is $\mathrm{T_3}$, but it is made on the spot, mainly in thyrotrophs and in the tanycytes of the hypothalamus, by D2 from plasma $\mathrm{T_4}$ (Section 33.4.4), so TSH is most sensitive to changes in plasma $\mathrm{T_4}$.

This feedback is very steep: a 2-fold change in free $\mathrm{T_4}$ changes TSH by about 100-fold (approximately linear on a logarithmic scale). TSH is therefore the most sensitive index for screening thyroid function, and a slight reduction of thyroid function raises TSH while free $\mathrm{T_4}$ is still within the normal range (subclinical hypothyroidism). The three-stage model below shows this feature: when the synthetic capacity of the thyroid falls to $40\%$, TSH rises about twofold and holds $\mathrm{T_4}$ at about $86\%$ of normal; after total thyroidectomy, $\mathrm{T_4}$ falls with a half-life of about a week and TSH rises several tens of times, and after daily replacement with $\mathrm{T_4}$ equal to the normal secretion, all quantities recover within weeks. Because the half-life of $\mathrm{T_4}$ is long, after adjusting the dose of levothyroxine one must wait 6–8 weeks (about 6 half-lives) before rechecking TSH.

### 34.6.3. Other Regulating Factors

- Cold: in rodents and human newborns, cold rapidly increases the secretion of TRH through the hypothalamus and raises thyroid hormone and heat production; this response is weak in adults, and people living long in cold regions have a slightly higher **basal metabolic rate**.
- Emotion: excitement and anxiety strongly activate the **sympathetic nervous system** and acutely reduce the secretion of TSH, possibly because these states themselves have already raised the metabolic rate and heat production.
- Nutrition: in fasting, **leptin** falls, the secretion of TRH decreases, and the peripheral conversion of $\mathrm{T_4}$ to $\mathrm{T_3}$ falls while the conversion to $\mathrm{rT_3}$ rises, so that the metabolic rate falls, an energy-saving adaptation (Chapter 30); a similar "low $\mathrm{T_3}$ syndrome" also occurs in severe illness.
- Stress and **glucocorticoids**: they inhibit the secretion of TSH; somatostatin and dopamine also inhibit TSH.
- **Autoregulation**: the thyroid can regulate its own iodide uptake and hormone synthesis according to the level of blood iodide, independently of TSH (the Wolff–Chaikoff effect and its escape, Section 34.5.3).
- Autonomic nerves: the thyroid is innervated by the sympathetic and the **parasympathetic nervous system**, and sympathetic excitation promotes hormone secretion, acting mainly in acute stress.
- Autoimmunity: the antibodies of Graves disease mimic TSH and escape feedback control entirely, so that free $\mathrm{T_4}$ rises and TSH is suppressed to undetectable levels.

## 34.7. Evolution and Comparison

### 34.7.1. Amphibian Metamorphosis

The action of thyroid hormone on amphibian metamorphosis was one of the earliest discoveries of endocrinology ([7.5.33](/7.5.33#sec-33.2)). Gudernatsch (1912) fed horse thyroid to tadpoles, and they metamorphosed early into miniature frogs; Allen (1916) removed the thyroid primordium of tadpoles, and they could not metamorphose and grew into giant tadpoles. At the peak of metamorphosis plasma thyroid hormone is highest, and the regression of the tail, the growth of the limbs, the remodeling of the gut, the loss of the gills and the development of the lungs are all driven by it; different tissues respond to the same signal in opposite ways (growth or apoptosis), which depends on the expression of TR and deiodinases in each tissue (tissues with more D2 respond first, whereas D3 protects tissues that are not yet due to change). Neotenic urodeles such as the axolotl keep the larval form throughout life and can reproduce, and giving $\mathrm{T_4}$ can induce them to metamorphose.

An important difference of amphibians and fish from mammals is that the main hypothalamic hormone stimulating the secretion of pituitary TSH is not TRH but CRH. CRH is also the initiator of the stress axis, which allows environmental stress (such as a drying pond or crowding) to accelerate metamorphosis and lets tadpoles leave an unfavorable body of water sooner.

### 34.7.2. The Downstream Migration of Fish

Salmon hatch in fresh water, and before migrating downstream to the sea the young fish undergo smoltification: the body color changes from striped brown to silvery white (deposition of guanine crystals in the skin), the body becomes slender, behavior changes from holding a territory to schooling and moving downstream, and at the same time the fish acquire the ability to excrete salt in seawater. This transformation is accompanied by a rise of thyroid hormone, GH, IGF-1 and cortisol: GH–IGF-1 and cortisol increase the number of gill chloride cells and the activity of $\ce{Na+}$/$\ce{K+}$-ATPase and are the main hormones in the acquisition of seawater tolerance; thyroid hormone is mainly related to silvering, to the change in behavior and to olfactory imprinting (by which adults return upstream to the river of their birth). It can be seen that in fish the same group of hormones also takes part in **osmoregulation**, linked to the **euryhaline** habit treated in Chapter 21 of this course.

### 34.7.3. The Origin of the Thyroid: The Endostyle

The evolution of the thyroid can be traced to the invertebrate chordates. Amphioxus and tunicates have a longitudinal groove on the floor of the pharynx called the endostyle, which secretes mucus for filter feeding; some cells of the endostyle can bind iodine and produce iodinated tyrosines. The larva of the lamprey (the ammocoete) still has an endostyle, and at metamorphosis to the adult part of the endostyle is transformed into thyroid follicles. This suggests that the vertebrate thyroid evolved from a pharyngeal gland of filter feeders, and that the chemistry of iodinated tyrosine may originally have been related to taking up iodine from food, becoming a hormone only later.

### 34.7.4. The Many Functions of Prolactin

Prolactin is known in mammals for lactation, but it is a very ancient hormone and has quite different roles in other vertebrates:

- Freshwater adaptation of fish: hypophysectomized killifish (*Fundulus heteroclitus*) survive in seawater but die soon after transfer to fresh water, and injection of prolactin lets them survive in fresh water (Pickford and Phillips, 1959). Prolactin reduces the permeability of the gills and skin to water and ions and promotes ion uptake, preventing loss of salt in hypotonic fresh water. In **euryhaline** fish, prolactin is the "freshwater hormone," and GH and cortisol are the "seawater hormones."
- Crop milk in birds: when rearing young, both the female and the male pigeon have their crop epithelium proliferate and slough under the action of prolactin, forming "crop milk" rich in fat and protein to feed the young. Prolactin was identified and named by Riddle and colleagues (1933) with the bioassay of pigeon crop proliferation. Prolactin also promotes incubation and brooding behavior in many birds.
- Amphibians: it promotes the return of adult newts to water for breeding (the "water drive").

That one hormone has been "recruited" in different groups to regulate osmotic balance, the rearing of young and lactation shows that the action of a hormone is determined by the receptors and downstream programs expressed in the target tissues during evolution, and not by the molecule itself (Chapter 32). For the comparison in insects, see Chapter 32: the brain–prothoracic gland–**ecdysone** and corpora allata–**juvenile hormone** systems oppose each other and together determine the type of molt, similar in logic to thyroid hormone driving amphibian metamorphosis, with the molecules being independent in each case.

## Summary

- Pituitary: the adenohypophysis (epithelial origin) and the neurohypophysis (neural origin) (Definition 34.1.1). Five cell types of the adenohypophysis secrete GH, ACTH, TSH, FSH/LH and PRL; the hypothalamus controls them through the portal system with releasing and inhibiting hormones (TRH and CRH from the paraventricular nucleus, GHRH and dopamine from the arcuate nucleus, somatostatin from the periventricular nucleus, GnRH from the preoptic area); the neurohypophysis releases ADH and oxytocin synthesized by magnocellular neurons of the supraoptic and paraventricular nuclei.
- Growth hormone (Definition 34.2.1): 191 aa, secreted in pulses (largest in slow-wave sleep), GHRH ($\mathrm{G_s}$–cAMP–$\ce{Ca^2+}$) and somatostatin ($\mathrm{G_i}$) antagonize, GH and IGF-1 give negative feedback. Acute actions: lipolysis, anti-insulin effect, protein synthesis; chronic actions: promotion of growth through IGF-1 (Definition 34.2.2, 70 aa, >99% bound, plasma fluctuation <2-fold). GH → IGF-1 is a series of low-pass filters. Excess: gigantism and acromegaly (Definition 34.2.3); deficiency: dwarfism; receptor defect: Laron syndrome.
- Prolactin (Definition 34.3.1): continuously inhibited by dopamine; suckling causes secretion; lactation, inhibition of ovulation. Oxytocin (Definition 34.4.1): milk ejection and uterine contraction, positive feedback.
- Thyroid: secretes $\mathrm{T_4}$ (93%) and $\mathrm{T_3}$ (7%) (Definition 34.5.1); NIS (2 $\ce{Na+}$ : 1 $\ce{I-}$, Definition 34.5.2) concentrates iodide 20–40-fold (thermodynamic limit about $10^3$, Example 34.5.1); iodine requirement about $150\,\mu\mathrm{g\,d^{-1}}$ (about 1 mg/week); iodination and coupling by TPO on thyroglobulin (Definition 34.5.3), storage for 2–3 months; bound in plasma to TBG, TTR and albumin, half-life of $\mathrm{T_4}$ about 6–7 d and of $\mathrm{T_3}$ about 1 d; peripheral D2 converts $\mathrm{T_4}$ to $\mathrm{T_3}$. Actions: changes transcription through TR–RXR, raises the metabolic rate, promotes the development of brain and bone, permits sympathetic actions. Hyperthyroidism, hypothyroidism (Definition 34.5.4) and goiter (Definition 34.5.5).
- HPT axis (Definition 34.6.1): TRH → TSH (five actions) → $\mathrm{T_4}$/$\mathrm{T_3}$ → negative feedback (mainly at the pituitary, mediated by $\mathrm{T_3}$ produced locally by D2); the feedback is steep, and TSH is the most sensitive index.
- Evolution: thyroid hormone drives amphibian metamorphosis (CRH stimulates TSH) and takes part in the smoltification of salmon; the thyroid originated from the endostyle of chordates; prolactin is a freshwater adaptation hormone in fish and promotes crop milk in pigeons.

## Questions

1. Why is IGF-1 measured clinically to assess GH secretion, whereas TSH is the first choice to assess thyroid function? Explain with the kinetics of the two systems.

   **Answer** GH is secreted in pulses with a half-life of about 20 min, and plasma concentration varies several tens of times during the day, so a single measurement depends on the moment of sampling; IGF-1 is driven by GH, forms a ternary complex with binding proteins and has a half-life of about 15 h, which is equivalent to a time integral (low-pass filter) of GH secretion, so one measurement reflects the total amount of GH over a period. Thyroid hormone itself has a long half-life and a stable concentration, but the negative feedback between free $\mathrm{T_4}$ and TSH is very steep (a 2-fold change in free $\mathrm{T_4}$ changes TSH about 100-fold), and TSH amplifies small deviations of free $\mathrm{T_4}$, so TSH is more sensitive than free $\mathrm{T_4}$ itself (provided that the pituitary and hypothalamus are normal).

2. A person who has lived long in a region of severe iodine deficiency develops a goiter. Explain the feedback mechanism of the disease; why does the ratio of $\mathrm{T_3}$ to $\mathrm{T_4}$ in plasma rise in this case?

   **Answer** Iodine deficiency reduces the synthesis of thyroid hormone, free $\mathrm{T_4}$ falls, negative feedback on the pituitary weakens, and TSH stays elevated; the long-term action of TSH enlarges and multiplies the follicular cells and increases the number of follicles, so that the gland swells (items 4 and 5 of Section 34.6.1), increasing iodide trapping. In iodine deficiency, the degree of iodination of the tyrosines of thyroglobulin is lower, MIT increases relative to DIT, and the $\mathrm{T_3}$ produced by coupling (MIT + DIT) increases relative to $\mathrm{T_4}$ (DIT + DIT); at the same time the raised TSH and the up-regulation of tissue D2 promote the peripheral conversion of $\mathrm{T_4}$ to $\mathrm{T_3}$. Synthesizing each $\mathrm{T_3}$ requires only 3 iodine atoms and gives a more active hormone, which is an adaptation to maintain hormone action with limited iodine.

3. After section of the pituitary stalk (destroying the hypothalamic–pituitary portal system), how do plasma GH, TSH, ACTH, LH and prolactin each change? What happens to the hormones of the neurohypophysis?

   **Answer** GH, TSH, ACTH, LH and FSH are stimulated mainly by hypothalamic releasing hormones, and without the input of the portal system their secretion falls, and the target glands (thyroid, adrenal cortex, gonads) atrophy and their function declines. Prolactin is continuously inhibited by dopamine, and without the inhibition its secretion rises (galactorrhea may occur). The hormones of the neurohypophysis are released by the axons of hypothalamic magnocellular neurons; after section of the stalk the axon terminals degenerate and the release of ADH and oxytocin falls temporarily, and diabetes insipidus can occur; but the cut ends of the axons can form new release sites near the median eminence and restore the secretion of ADH in part.

4. In Example 34.5.1, if a drug depolarizes the follicular cell to $-20\,\mathrm{mV}$, what does the thermodynamic concentration limit of NIS become? Explain, from the stoichiometry of NIS, why the membrane potential is important for iodide trapping.

   **Answer** $\mathrm{e}^{20/26.7} \approx 2.1$, so the limit is about $146 \times 2.1 \approx 310$, a reduction from about 800 at $-45\,\mathrm{mV}$ to about $40\%$. NIS moves a net positive charge per cycle, so the membrane potential enters the driving force directly (the factor $\mathrm{e}^{-FV_{\mathrm{m}}/RT}$); the chemical gradient of $\ce{Na+}$ contributes the squared term. Any factor that lowers the $\ce{Na+}$ gradient (inhibition of the **Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase**, as by ouabain) or causes **depolarization** of the cell reduces iodide trapping.

5. (Open question) In mammals prolactin mainly regulates lactation, in teleost fish mainly osmotic balance in fresh water, and in pigeons the secretion of crop milk. Discuss, from the standpoint of receptors, target tissues and downstream programs, how a hormone acquires new functions in evolution. Which of these functions of prolactin do you think is more likely the ancestral one?

   **Answer** Key points: hormone molecules are relatively conserved over long periods of evolution (the GH/PRL family arose by gene duplication), and new functions arise mainly in the target tissue: a tissue begins to express the receptor of the hormone and connects the downstream signal of the receptor (JAK2–STAT5) to a program that the tissue already has (ion transport of the gill, proliferation of the crop epithelium, secretion of the mammary gland), and the hormone has been "recruited" (compare molecular exploitation in Chapter 32). Such a change needs only a change in the tissue-specific expression of the receptor gene, at little cost, so one hormone often accumulates many functions. As to the ancestral function, it can be inferred by phylogeny: prolactin exists in all jawed vertebrates, and its osmoregulatory action is seen in fish, amphibians and even mammals (affecting water and salt transport of kidney and intestine), whereas lactation and crop milk are restricted to mammals and to a few birds such as the Columbiformes, respectively; osmoregulation (and, more generally, regulation of ion and water transport across epithelia) is therefore more likely close to the ancestral function, and lactation was acquired later in the mammary gland of mammals. It should be pointed out that this is an inference from distribution and can be tested further by methods such as ancestral receptor reconstruction.
