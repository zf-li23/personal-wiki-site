The previous chapter dealt with the movements that propel the contents of the gut; this chapter deals with the fluids secreted along it. An adult secretes about $7\,\mathrm{L}$ of digestive juices per day, more than twice the plasma volume, and almost all of the water and electrolytes are reabsorbed in the small intestine and colon (Chapter 28). The composition of each juice is matched to its task: saliva moistens food and begins starch digestion; gastric juice kills microbes with strong acid and starts protein digestion; pancreatic juice neutralizes the acid and supplies nearly all the enzymes that digest the three major nutrient classes; and bile emulsifies fat. Their secretion is timed precisely to the arrival of food, through the **enteric nervous system**, the extrinsic nerves and the **gastrointestinal hormones** of Chapter 26, and most of it is feedback signaled by the contents themselves. This chapter first treats the general mechanisms of secretion (Section 27.1), then saliva (Section 27.2), gastric juice (Section 27.3), pancreatic juice (Section 27.4), bile (Section 27.5), and intestinal juice (Section 27.6). The basic components of epithelial transport are described in [Chapter 3](/7.14.3#sec-3.7); the formation of bile in the liver is covered in Chapter 29.

## 27.1. General Mechanisms of Digestive Secretion

### 27.1.1. Types of Secretion and Their Stimuli

The glands of the digestive tract produce two basic kinds of secretion: digestive enzymes and mucus. Structurally, they include single-cell mucous glands (goblet cells) scattered over the surface of the tract, tubular glands in the mucosa (gastric glands, intestinal crypts), compound tubuloalveolar glands in the submucosa (the Brunner glands of the duodenum), and large glands outside the tract (salivary glands, pancreas, liver).

| Digestive juice | Daily volume (L) | pH | Main components |
|---|---|---|---|
| Saliva | 1.0 | 6.0–7.0 | Salivary amylase, mucin, lysozyme, IgA; hypotonic |
| Gastric juice | 1.5 | 1.0–3.5 | HCl, pepsinogen, intrinsic factor, mucus |
| Pancreatic juice | 1.0 | 8.0–8.3 | $\ce{HCO3-}$, more than ten enzymes including trypsinogen |
| Bile | 1.0 | 7.8 | Bile salts, phospholipids, cholesterol, bilirubin; no digestive enzymes |
| Intestinal juice | 1.8 | 7.5–8.0 | Water and electrolytes, mucus |
| Brunner glands | 0.2 | 8.0–8.9 | Alkaline mucus |
| Large-intestinal juice | 0.2 | 7.5–8.0 | Mucus, $\ce{HCO3-}$, $\ce{K+}$ |
| Total | about 6.7 | | |

Food evokes secretion by two basic mechanisms. First, direct contact of food with gland cells (a local, nonspecific stimulus). Second, food stimulates the mucosal epithelium and activates local reflexes of the enteric nervous system: tactile stimulation and distension of the wall are nonspecific and mainly determine whether a response occurs, whereas chemical stimuli (acid, fatty acids, amino acids) are selective and determine the magnitude and kind of the response.

Superimposed on these are extrinsic neural and hormonal controls:

- Parasympathetic stimulation almost always increases the secretory rate of digestive glands, most markedly in the upper tract (salivary, esophageal, gastric and pancreatic glands, Brunner glands) and in the distal large intestine;
- Sympathetic action is biphasic: acting directly on gland cells it usually increases secretion slightly, but it also constricts the blood vessels of the gland and reduces the blood flow that secretion requires, so when parasympathetic stimulation or hormones have already evoked copious secretion, sympathetic stimulation reduces it;
- Gastrointestinal hormones (Chapter 26), mostly peptides or peptide derivatives, regulate the amount and kind of secretion.

### 27.1.2. The Six Steps of Gland-Cell Secretion

Taking an enzyme-secreting acinar cell as the example, the formation and release of an organic secretion can be divided into six steps:

1. The raw materials for synthesis (amino acids, nucleotides, sugars) enter the basal part of the gland cell from the capillaries;
2. The dense mitochondria at the base supply ATP by **oxidative phosphorylation**;
3. The materials are synthesized into proteins in the **rough endoplasmic reticulum** and processed and concentrated in the Golgi apparatus;
4. The secretory product is packaged into secretory vesicles (zymogen granules) and carried to the apex of the cell;
5. The vesicles are stored at the apex until a neural or hormonal signal raises intracellular $\ce{Ca^2+}$ and triggers **exocytosis**, releasing the contents into the gland lumen;
6. Water and electrolytes are secreted along with the organic material: the $\ce{Na+}$–$\ce{K+}$–$2\ce{Cl-}$ cotransporter in the basolateral membrane accumulates $\ce{Cl-}$ inside the cell, $\ce{Cl-}$ flows into the lumen when apical $\ce{Cl-}$ channels open, $\ce{Na+}$ follows through the **paracellular pathway**, and water follows the solute osmotically, flushing out the dense secretion.

This mechanism, in which $\ce{Cl-}$ secretion drives water secretion, is the same in salivary glands, pancreatic acini, intestinal crypts and airway epithelium; only the type of apical $\ce{Cl-}$ channel differs ($\ce{Ca^2+}$-activated TMEM16A, or cAMP-regulated CFTR).

## 27.2. Saliva

### 27.2.1. Composition and Functions of Saliva

A person secretes $0.8$–$1.5\,\mathrm{L}$ of saliva per day, about $1\,\mathrm{L}$ on average. Per unit of gland mass, the secretory rate of the salivary glands (up to about $1\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}\,g^{-1}}$) is the highest of any gland in the body, and their blood flow is correspondingly high. The three pairs of major salivary glands are the parotid, submandibular and sublingual glands; in addition there are many minor salivary glands scattered in the oral mucosa (buccal, labial glands, and others). Saliva has two components: the serous secretion contains salivary amylase (α-amylase), which breaks down starch, and the mucous secretion contains mucin, which lubricates and protects. The parotid gland secretes almost purely serous fluid, the submandibular and sublingual glands secrete a mixture of serous and mucous fluid, and the buccal glands secrete only mucus.

The functions of saliva are:

1. Moistening and dissolving food so that it can be swallowed, and dissolving taste substances so that they can be sensed by the taste receptors;
2. Digestion: salivary amylase in the mouth and stomach (until the bolus is penetrated by gastric acid and the pH falls below about 4) hydrolyzes about 30%–40% of the starch in food; glands under the tongue also secrete lingual lipase, which begins hydrolyzing **triglycerides** in the acidic environment of the stomach;
3. Protection: it continuously rinses the mouth; lysozyme, lactoferrin, histidine-rich proteins and secretory IgA are antibacterial; $\ce{HCO3-}$ and phosphate neutralize acids in the mouth and protect tooth enamel. When salivary secretion is reduced (xerostomia, after radiotherapy), dental caries and oral infections increase markedly;
4. Excretion: some substances (iodine, lead, mercury, some drugs and viruses) can be excreted in saliva.

### 27.2.2. The Two-Stage Secretion Model

The ionic composition of saliva differs greatly from that of plasma: at rest $[\ce{K+}]$ is about $30\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$ (7 times that of plasma), $[\ce{HCO3-}]$ can reach $50$–$70\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$ on stimulation (2–3 times that of plasma), while $[\ce{Na+}]$ and $[\ce{Cl-}]$ are only about $15\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$ (1/10–1/7 of plasma). Saliva is hypotonic, with a pH of about 6–7. This composition arises in two stages:

1. Secretion of a primary fluid by the acini: acinar cells produce the primary fluid by the $\ce{Cl-}$ secretion mechanism of Section 27.1.2; it contains salivary amylase and mucin, and its ionic composition and osmolality are close to those of **extracellular fluid** (isotonic); water enters the lumen through the **aquaporin** AQP5 of the acinar cells.
2. Modification in the ducts: as the primary fluid flows through the striated and excretory ducts, the ductal epithelium reabsorbs $\ce{Na+}$ through apical $\ce{Na+}$ channels (ENaC) and $\ce{Na+}$/$\ce{H+}$ exchangers, reabsorbs $\ce{Cl-}$ through CFTR, and at the same time secretes $\ce{K+}$ and $\ce{HCO3-}$. Because more $\ce{Na+}$ and $\ce{Cl-}$ are reabsorbed than $\ce{K+}$ and $\ce{HCO3-}$ are secreted, and because the **tight junctions** and cell membranes of the ductal epithelium are poorly permeable to water, water cannot follow, and the saliva therefore becomes hypotonic.

Ductal modification takes time, so the composition of saliva varies with flow: the greater the flow, the shorter the residence time of the fluid in the ducts, the less $\ce{Na+}$ and $\ce{Cl-}$ are reabsorbed, and the closer the saliva is to plasma. At maximal flow $[\ce{NaCl}]$ can rise to 1/2–2/3 of the plasma value, $[\ce{K+}]$ falls to about 4 times the plasma value, and the osmolality approaches that of plasma. $\ce{HCO3-}$ is an exception: it rises with the strength of stimulation, because both acini and ducts secrete $\ce{HCO3-}$ faster when stimulated. **Aldosterone**, as in the distal nephron of the kidney, increases $\ce{Na+}$ reabsorption and $\ce{K+}$ secretion in the salivary ducts.

### 27.2.3. Neural Control of Salivary Secretion

Salivary secretion is controlled almost entirely by nerves, and no known hormone plays a major role. The parasympathetic system is dominant: fibers from the superior salivatory nucleus travel in the facial nerve (chorda tympani) to the submandibular ganglion and innervate the submandibular and sublingual glands; fibers from the inferior salivatory nucleus travel in the glossopharyngeal nerve to the otic ganglion and innervate the parotid gland. The superior and inferior salivatory nuclei receive taste and oral tactile signals from the nucleus of the solitary tract, as well as input from higher brain regions (gustatory cortex, hypothalamus, olfaction). The taste of food and tactile sensation in the mouth are the strongest stimuli; smooth objects (such as pebbles) can also evoke copious secretion, whereas rough objects evoke less or even inhibit it. The smell or thought of a favorite food (a conditioned reflex) and stimulation of the stomach and upper small intestine (as before nausea) also increase secretion. ACh acts on M3 receptors of the acinar cells and, through a rise in $\ce{Ca^2+}$, evokes copious thin saliva rich in water and electrolytes; atropine therefore causes a dry mouth.

Sympathetic nerves (from the superior cervical ganglion) also promote salivary secretion but much more weakly: through $\beta$ receptors and cAMP they evoke a small volume of viscous, protein-rich secretion (the dry mouth of nervousness is precisely this scanty, sticky saliva). This is one of the few examples in which the two autonomic divisions do not oppose each other at an effector. In addition, during parasympathetic stimulation the gland cells release kallikrein, which generates bradykinin; together with VIP released by the nerves, it dilates the blood vessels of the gland and increases the blood flow that secretion requires.

## 27.3. Gastric Juice

### 27.3.1. Structure of the Gastric Glands

The surface of the gastric mucosa is covered with mucus-secreting surface epithelial cells, and the mucosa contains two important kinds of tubular glands:

- The oxyntic glands (fundic glands) are located in the fundus and body of the stomach (about 80% of the gastric mucosa) and consist of three main cell types: mucous neck cells in the neck secrete mucus; parietal cells (oxyntic cells) secrete hydrochloric acid and intrinsic factor; chief cells at the base of the gland secrete pepsinogen and gastric lipase. The glands also contain enterochromaffin-like cells (ECL cells, which secrete histamine) and D cells (which secrete somatostatin).
- The pyloric glands are located in the antrum (about 20%) and are similar in structure to the oxyntic glands but contain almost no parietal cells: they secrete a little pepsinogen and a large amount of mucus, which protects the pyloric mucosa from gastric acid, and they contain the G cells that secrete gastrin, as well as D cells.

Pepsinogen is secreted by exocytosis and is itself inactive; at pH < 5 a peptide segment is cleaved from it to give pepsin, and pepsin in turn autocatalytically activates more pepsinogen (**positive feedback**). The pH optimum of pepsin is $1.8$–$3.5$; it is almost inactive above about pH 5 and is irreversibly denatured at neutral pH. It hydrolyzes proteins to polypeptides, accomplishing about 10%–20% of total protein digestion, and is especially important for the digestion of collagen (the main component of the connective tissue in meat, which other enzymes digest poorly). Gastric lipase is stable in acid and can hydrolyze 10%–30% of dietary triglycerides.

### 27.3.2. Hydrochloric Acid Secretion by Parietal Cells

The hydrochloric acid secreted by parietal cells has a concentration of about $150$–$160\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$, roughly isotonic with extracellular fluid; its pH is about 0.8, and $[\ce{H+}]$ is about $4\times10^6$ times that of blood ($40\,\mathrm{nmol\,L^{-1}}$). This is the steepest ion concentration gradient in the body. The mechanism is as follows:

1. Intracellular $\ce{H2O}$ dissociates into $\ce{H+}$ and $\ce{OH-}$ (more precisely, **carbonic anhydrase** catalyzes $\ce{CO2 + H2O -> H+ + HCO3-}$). The $\ce{H+}$/$\ce{K+}$-ATPase (the proton pump, a **P-type ATPase**) in the apical membrane (the membrane of the secretory canaliculus) pumps $\ce{H+}$ into the canalicular lumen against its concentration gradient for each ATP hydrolyzed, while exchanging $\ce{K+}$ into the cell; the $\ce{K+}$ that enters the cell returns to the lumen through apical $\ce{K+}$ channels (KCNQ1 and others) and is thus recycled.
2. $\ce{OH-}$ combines with $\ce{CO2}$ to form $\ce{HCO3-}$, which leaves into the interstitial fluid down its concentration gradient through the basolateral $\ce{Cl-}$/$\ce{HCO3-}$ exchanger (AE2), exchanging $\ce{Cl-}$ into the cell against its gradient; $\ce{Cl-}$ then enters the lumen through apical $\ce{Cl-}$ channels, maintaining electroneutrality.
3. Water enters the canaliculus driven by osmotic pressure. $\ce{H+}$, $\ce{Cl-}$ and water finally form hydrochloric acid at $150$–$160\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$.

**Definition 27.3.1 (Alkaline Tide)** For each $\ce{H+}$ secreted into the gastric lumen, the parietal cell releases one $\ce{HCO3-}$ into the gastric venous blood, so when acid is secreted copiously after a meal, the pH and $[\ce{HCO3-}]$ of gastric venous blood and of systemic arterial blood rise transiently, and the urine becomes alkaline. This phenomenon is called the **alkaline tide**.

The alkaline tide is usually inconspicuous, because after the acid enters the duodenum it is neutralized by the $\ce{HCO3-}$ of pancreatic juice, and pancreatic duct cells release an equal amount of $\ce{H+}$ into the blood when they secrete $\ce{HCO3-}$ (Section 27.4.3), so the two cancel. When gastric acid is lost by persistent vomiting or nasogastric drainage, this balance is broken and **metabolic alkalosis** results ([Chapter 25](/7.14.25)).

Parietal cells switch between a resting and an active state. At rest, most proton pumps are in the membrane of cytoplasmic tubulovesicles; on stimulation, the tubulovesicles fuse with the plasma membrane of the secretory canaliculus within a few minutes, the canalicular membrane extends many microvilli, its area increases about 5–10-fold, and the density of proton pumps in the membrane also rises greatly. When the stimulus ceases, the pumps return to the tubulovesicles by endocytosis. Parietal cells contain many mitochondria (about 30%–40% of the cell volume) to meet the energy demand of the pump.

**Example 27.3.1 (Free Energy Required to Concentrate Gastric Acid)** The free energy required to pump 1 mol of $\ce{H+}$ from the cytosol (or blood) at pH 7.4 into a lumen at pH 0.8 is at least

$$
\Delta G = RT\ln\frac{[\ce{H+}]_{\text{lumen}}}{[\ce{H+}]_{\text{cell}}} = 2.303\,RT\,\Delta\mathrm{pH} = 2.303 \times 8.314 \times 310 \times 6.6\,\mathrm{J\,mol^{-1}} \approx 39\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}.
$$

$\ce{H+}$/$\ce{K+}$ exchange is electroneutral, so there is no electrical term. Each liter of gastric juice contains $0.16\,\mathrm{mol}$ of $\ce{H+}$, requiring at least $0.16 \times 39 \approx 6.3\,\mathrm{kJ}$, about $1.5\,\mathrm{kcal}$ (that is, 1500 cal). The free energy of ATP hydrolysis in cells is about $-57\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ ([7.1.16](/7.1.16#sec-16.3), the **phosphorylation potential**), which is enough to transport only one $\ce{H+}$ per ATP against this gradient (transporting two would require $78\,\mathrm{kJ}$, plus about $5\,\mathrm{kJ}$ for exchanging $\ce{K+}$ into the cell); at smaller gradients (higher luminal pH) the pump can transport two $\ce{H+}$ per ATP. Against the gastric gradient the pump can therefore work only with a stoichiometry of one $\ce{H+}$ per ATP. Energetically, ATP could still build a steeper gradient (after the $\ce{K+}$ term about $52\,\mathrm{kJ}$, equivalent to about 8.8 pH units); gastric acid stops at $150$–$160\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$ mainly because the secreted fluid is roughly isotonic with extracellular fluid ($\ce{H+}$ and $\ce{Cl-}$ being its main ions) and because $\ce{H+}$ diffuses back across the mucosa. A person secretes about $1.5\,\mathrm{L}$ of gastric juice per day, and pumping the $\ce{H+}$ consumes at least about $0.2\,\mathrm{mol}$ of ATP.

### 27.3.3. Regulation of Acid Secretion: ACh, Gastrin and Histamine

Three substances directly stimulate acid secretion by parietal cells:

| Substance | Source | Receptor and messenger on the parietal cell | Indirect actions |
|---|---|---|---|
| Acetylcholine (ACh) | Released by the vagus nerve via enteric neurons | M3 receptor, $\ce{Ca^2+}$ | Stimulates histamine release from ECL cells; stimulates gastrin release from G cells via GRP; inhibits D cells |
| Gastrin | Antral G cells, via the blood | CCK2 receptor, $\ce{Ca^2+}$ (weak direct action) | Mainly stimulates histamine release from ECL cells; promotes proliferation of parietal and ECL cells |
| Histamine | ECL cells immediately adjacent to parietal cells, **paracrine** | H2 receptor, cAMP | — |

The three potentiate one another: the response when all are present is much greater than the sum of the individual responses, because the $\ce{Ca^2+}$ and cAMP pathways cooperatively activate the fusion of tubulovesicles. Physiologically, histamine is one of the final common pathways, and a large part of the actions of ACh and gastrin is exerted by stimulating histamine release. H2 receptor antagonists (cimetidine, ranitidine) therefore block not only histamine but also greatly weaken acid secretion evoked by ACh and gastrin; proton pump inhibitors (omeprazole) are activated in the acidic secretory canaliculus and bind covalently to the proton pump, blocking the final common step of all stimuli, and are the most potent acid suppressants.

The main inhibitor of acid secretion is somatostatin. It is secreted by D cells in the mucosa of the gastric body and antrum (hypothalamic neurons and pancreatic islet D cells also secrete somatostatin) and acts in a paracrine manner: it acts directly on receptors at the base of parietal cells to inhibit acid secretion, and indirectly on ECL cells and G cells to inhibit the release of histamine and gastrin. D cells are inhibited by ACh and stimulated by gastrin and by acid in the gastric lumen. When the pH in the antral lumen falls below 3, D cells release somatostatin, which inhibits G cells, and gastrin falls: this is a **negative feedback** in which luminal pH is the regulated variable of acid secretion. Dietary protein buffers gastric acid, keeping the pH high for some time after a meal so that gastrin continues to be secreted; as the food empties and the buffering capacity is exhausted, the pH falls and acid secretion automatically weakens. **Prostaglandin** $\mathrm{E_2}$ also inhibits parietal cells (through an inhibitory G protein that lowers cAMP) and promotes the secretion of mucus and $\ce{HCO3-}$.

### 27.3.4. The Three Phases of Gastric Secretion

In the interdigestive period the stomach secretes almost no acid; the secretion consists mainly of mucus and a little pepsinogen. Gastric secretion evoked by eating is divided into three phases according to the site of stimulation:

1. Cephalic phase (about 30% of the secretion of the digestive period): the sight, smell and taste of food, chewing and swallowing excite the vagus nerve through conditioned and unconditioned reflexes, and ACh directly stimulates parietal cells and stimulates G cells to release gastrin. Pavlov's sham-feeding experiment demonstrated this phase: in a dog whose esophagus has been cut and brought out to the exterior, the food it swallows leaks from the esophageal fistula and never enters the stomach, yet gastric juice is secreted copiously; after vagotomy this secretion disappears. Cephalic secretion is strongly influenced by emotion: anticipation before a meal and hunger enhance it, whereas anorexia and tension inhibit it.
2. Gastric phase (about 60%): after food enters the stomach, distension stimulates secretion through vagovagal reflexes and local reflexes of the enteric nervous system; the products of protein digestion (peptides and amino acids) directly stimulate G cells to release gastrin. Coffee (with or without caffeine), alcohol and calcium also stimulate the secretion of gastrin and gastric acid.
3. Intestinal phase (about 10%): protein digestion products in the chyme entering the duodenum stimulate the duodenal mucosa to release a little gastrin and other acid-promoting substances, and absorbed amino acids also have a direct effect.

At the same time, the entry of chyme into the duodenum also inhibits gastric secretion, and this inhibition dominates in the intestinal phase: acid, fat, hypertonic solutions and protein breakdown products in the duodenum inhibit acid secretion through the enterogastric reflex (the same reflex that inhibits gastric emptying, Chapter 26) and through a group of hormones collectively called enterogastrones (secretin, GIP, CCK, PYY, GLP-1). Gastric secretion and emptying are jointly inhibited by the same set of signals from the duodenum, which ensures that the acid entering the small intestine does not exceed the neutralizing capacity of pancreatic juice.

### 27.3.5. Protection of the Gastric Mucosa

The $[\ce{H+}]$ in the stomach is millions of times that of the submucosal interstitial fluid, and pepsin, which digests protein, is present, yet the gastric mucosa is not digested. Protection rests on several lines of defense:

**Definition 27.3.2 (Mucus–Bicarbonate Barrier)** The mucus secreted by the surface epithelial cells of the gastric mucosa forms an unstirred gel layer about $0.2$–$0.5\,\mathrm{mm}$ thick on the mucosal surface, and the surface epithelial cells also secrete $\ce{HCO3-}$ into the gel layer; the mucus slows the diffusion of $\ce{H+}$ and pepsin, and $\ce{HCO3-}$ in the gel layer neutralizes inwardly diffusing $\ce{H+}$, so that the pH at the epithelial surface is maintained at about 7 while the pH at the luminal face of the gel layer is about 2. This protective structure is called the **mucus–bicarbonate barrier**.

The main component of mucus is mucin (highly glycosylated macromolecular **glycoproteins**), whose secretion is stimulated by ACh and by low luminal pH; the secretion of $\ce{HCO3-}$ is likewise stimulated by the vagus nerve and by luminal acid. In addition, the apical membranes of the epithelial cells and the **tight junctions** between them are extremely impermeable to $\ce{H+}$ (called the gastric mucosal barrier by Davenport); after epithelial injury, neighboring cells migrate to cover the defect within tens of minutes (rapid repair), and **stem cells** in the neck of the gastric glands renew the surface epithelium about every 3–5 days; the abundant blood flow of the mucosa carries away $\ce{H+}$ that has diffused back and supplies the raw material for $\ce{HCO3-}$ secretion. **Prostaglandins** ($\mathrm{PGE_2}$, $\mathrm{PGI_2}$) strengthen all of these, so nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs that inhibit cyclooxygenase (aspirin, ibuprofen) are an important cause of gastric ulcer; the other main cause is infection with *Helicobacter pylori*, which lives in the mucus layer and neutralizes the surrounding acid with the $\ce{NH3}$ produced by the breakdown of urea by its urease (Marshall and Warren, Nobel Prize 2005).

Protection of the duodenum: after gastric acid enters the duodenum and the luminal pH falls below 4.5, S cells secrete secretin, which inhibits gastric acid secretion, stimulates the pancreas to secrete alkaline pancreatic juice, and stimulates the duodenal mucosa and Brunner glands to secrete $\ce{HCO3-}$ locally.

### 27.3.6. Intrinsic Factor

Parietal cells also secrete intrinsic factor, a glycoprotein that binds vitamin $\mathrm{B_{12}}$ and is essential for its absorption in the terminal ileum (Chapter 28). Intrinsic factor is the only indispensable component of gastric juice: after total gastrectomy, protein digestion can be compensated by pancreatic enzymes, but vitamin $\mathrm{B_{12}}$ must be supplemented by injection for life. Autoimmune gastritis destroys parietal cells and causes pernicious anemia ([Chapter 11](/7.14.11)).

## 27.4. Pancreatic Juice

### 27.4.1. Structure of the Pancreas and Pancreatic Enzymes

The pancreas lies behind and below the stomach. Its exocrine part accounts for about 98% of its mass and resembles the salivary glands in structure: it consists of acini and ducts, the acinar cells synthesizing and secreting enzymes and the ductal epithelial cells secreting water and electrolytes. Pancreatic juice drains through ducts of successive orders into the main pancreatic duct, joins the common bile duct at the major duodenal papilla (the ampulla of Vater), and enters the duodenum through the sphincter of Oddi. A person secretes about $1$–$1.5\,\mathrm{L}$ of pancreatic juice per day, isotonic, with a pH of about 8. Pancreatic juice is the most important digestive juice; it contains more than ten enzymes that digest the three major nutrient classes:

| Substrate | Enzyme (form secreted) | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Protein | Trypsinogen, chymotrypsinogen, proelastase, procarboxypeptidases A and B | The first three are endopeptidases that hydrolyze proteins to polypeptides; carboxypeptidases cleave amino acids one at a time from the carboxyl end |
| Carbohydrate | Pancreatic amylase (α-amylase, active) | Hydrolyzes starch to maltose, maltotriose and α-limit dextrins; cannot hydrolyze cellulose |
| Lipid | Pancreatic lipase and procolipase, prophospholipase $\mathrm{A_2}$, cholesterol esterase | Hydrolyze triglycerides to fatty acids and 2-monoacylglycerol; colipase anchors lipase to the surface of fat droplets coated with bile salts |
| Nucleic acid | Ribonuclease, deoxyribonuclease | Hydrolyze nucleic acids to nucleotides |

The proteolytic enzymes are all synthesized, stored and secreted as inactive **zymogens**, and the acinar cells simultaneously secrete a trypsin inhibitor (SPINK1), so that even if a little trypsinogen is accidentally activated in the pancreas it is inhibited immediately.

**Definition 27.4.1 (Enteropeptidase)** A protease on the brush border of the duodenal and jejunal epithelium that cleaves a hexapeptide from the amino terminus of trypsinogen to convert it to active trypsin is called **enteropeptidase** (formerly enterokinase).

Trypsin in turn activates more trypsinogen (autocatalysis) and all the other zymogens, forming a cascade. Placing the first step of activation on the brush border of the intestinal epithelium, in the intestinal lumen, keeps the pancreas itself from being exposed to active enzymes; it is a spatial safety design. When this design fails (obstruction of the pancreatic duct, a gallstone impacted in the ampulla, alcohol injury, or mutations of the trypsinogen gene *PRSS1* that make it more prone to autoactivation), trypsin is activated within the pancreas and activates other enzymes, and the pancreas digests itself: acute pancreatitis.

### 27.4.2. Regulation of Acinar Cells

Secretion by acinar cells is regulated by several humoral factors: ACh (M3 receptors), CCK (CCK1 receptors), gastrin-releasing peptide (GRP) and substance P raise intracellular $\ce{Ca^2+}$ through $\mathrm{IP_3}$ ($\ce{Ca^2+}$ is the principal intracellular messenger of acinar cells and often appears as $\ce{Ca^2+}$ waves beginning at the apex and as **calcium oscillations**) and activate **protein kinase C**; secretin and VIP activate **protein kinase A** through cAMP. The effects of the two groups of pathways are additive and synergistic: secretin alone hardly evokes enzyme secretion but markedly enhances the effect of CCK. How the various pathways finally regulate exocytosis has not been fully elucidated. Notably, human acinar cells have few CCK1 receptors, and CCK at physiological concentrations probably acts mainly by exciting vagal afferent fibers in the duodenum, which release ACh through a vagovagal reflex to stimulate pancreatic enzyme secretion; rodent acinar cells, in contrast, respond directly to CCK.

Acinar cells also secrete a small amount of isotonic NaCl solution (the basolateral $\ce{Na+}$–$\ce{K+}$–$2\ce{Cl-}$ cotransporter accumulates $\ce{Cl-}$, which is secreted through apical $\ce{Cl-}$ channels, $\ce{Na+}$ follows through the paracellular pathway, and water follows osmotically), about 25% of the total volume of pancreatic juice; its role is to hydrate and dissolve the dense proteins secreted by the acinar cells and flush them into the ducts.

### 27.4.3. Ductal Bicarbonate Secretion and CFTR

The water and $\ce{HCO3-}$ of pancreatic juice are secreted mainly by the epithelial cells of the small ducts. The $[\ce{HCO3-}]$ of human pancreatic juice can reach about $140$–$145\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$ when stimulated, 5–6 times that of plasma and roughly equal to the $[\ce{H+}]$ of gastric acid (about $160\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$); the daily secreted volumes of the two are also similar, so pancreatic juice can neutralize most of the gastric acid entering the duodenum (the $\ce{HCO3-}$ of bile and Brunner glands neutralizes the rest), returning the duodenal pH to 6–7, which is the optimal environment for pancreatic enzymes, inactivates pepsin and protects the duodenal mucosa.

The $\ce{HCO3-}$ of ductal cells has two sources: the basolateral $\ce{Na+}$–$\ce{HCO3-}$ **cotransporter** (NBCe1, which brings in 2 $\ce{HCO3-}$ per $\ce{Na+}$ transported) carries $\ce{HCO3-}$ from the interstitial fluid into the cell; and intracellular carbonic anhydrase catalyzes $\ce{CO2 + H2O -> H+ + HCO3-}$, the $\ce{H+}$ produced being extruded to the interstitial fluid through the basolateral $\ce{Na+}$/$\ce{H+}$ exchanger (this is the acid that "cancels the alkaline tide" mentioned in Section 27.3.2). $\ce{HCO3-}$ enters the ductal lumen through the apical $\ce{Cl-}$/$\ce{HCO3-}$ exchanger (SLC26A6, exchanging 1 $\ce{Cl-}$ for 2 $\ce{HCO3-}$); the $\ce{Cl-}$ that enters the cell returns to the lumen through apical CFTR channels, maintaining luminal $\ce{Cl-}$ for the exchanger to use. In the distal duct the luminal $[\ce{Cl-}]$ falls very low and the exchanger can hardly continue to work; CFTR itself then also conducts $\ce{HCO3-}$ directly, allowing the luminal $[\ce{HCO3-}]$ to rise above $140\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$. Water enters the lumen through aquaporins, driven by osmotic pressure.

The main target of regulation is CFTR: secretin phosphorylates CFTR through cAMP–PKA and opens it, increasing $\ce{Cl-}$ recycling and $\ce{HCO3-}$ secretion. The ionic composition of pancreatic juice varies with flow (panel B of the figure in Section 27.2.2): at low flow the $\ce{Cl-}$-rich fluid of the acini makes up a large fraction; at high flow the $\ce{HCO3-}$-rich fluid of the ducts dominates; and $[\ce{Cl-}] + [\ce{HCO3-}]$, $[\ce{Na+}]$ and $[\ce{K+}]$ remain essentially independent of flow.

*Cystic fibrosis* is a disease caused by recessive mutations of the CFTR gene and is the most common lethal inherited disease in Caucasians (about 1 in 3000 newborns). The most common mutation, F508del, makes CFTR fold incorrectly, and it is degraded in the endoplasmic reticulum. When CFTR function is lost, the pancreatic ducts secrete little fluid and little $\ce{HCO3-}$, pancreatic enzymes become concentrated and precipitate in the ducts, blocking them and becoming activated, and the pancreas is gradually destroyed; about 85% of patients have exocrine pancreatic insufficiency at birth or in early childhood, manifested as steatorrhea and malnutrition. Mucus in the lungs and airways is likewise viscous and poorly cleared (**mucociliary clearance**, [Chapter 2](/7.14.2)), and recurrent infection is the main cause of death; the sweat duct cannot reabsorb $\ce{Cl-}$, so the sweat $[\ce{Cl-}]$ is elevated (the sweat test is the classic diagnostic method). In recent years, CFTR modulators (combinations of correctors and potentiators) have partially restored CFTR function in most patients.

### 27.4.4. Regulation of Pancreatic Secretion

Pancreatic secretion is regulated jointly by nerves and hormones, mainly hormones:

- Secretin: released by S cells of the duodenal and upper jejunal mucosa when luminal pH < 4.5 (fatty acids have a weaker effect); it acts on ductal cells and evokes copious pancreatic juice rich in $\ce{HCO3-}$ and poor in enzymes. This is a **negative feedback** in which duodenal pH is the regulated variable: the more acid, the more secretin, and the more $\ce{HCO3-}$ to neutralize the acid; when the pH recovers, secretin falls.
- CCK: released by I cells in response to protein breakdown products and fatty acids (and, more weakly, acid); it mainly evokes secretion of pancreatic enzymes (and gallbladder contraction), with less fluid and $\ce{HCO3-}$. CCK also enhances the action of secretin.
- ACh: released by the vagus nerve and the enteric nervous system; it mainly stimulates acinar cells to secrete enzymes, like CCK.

According to the site of stimulation, pancreatic secretion is also divided into three phases: the cephalic phase (the sight, smell and taste of food release ACh through the vagus nerve, and also act indirectly through gastrin) and the gastric phase (gastric distension through the vagovagal reflex) together evoke about 30% of pancreatic enzyme secretion, with very little fluid and electrolyte, the enzymes being retained mainly in the ducts; the intestinal phase is the most important (about 70%) and has three mechanisms: S cells–secretin, I cells–CCK, and ACh released by the enteropancreatic reflex, which together evoke copious juice rich in both $\ce{HCO3-}$ and enzymes.

Pancreatic enzyme secretion also has a feedback: in animals (rats), trypsin in the small intestine continually degrades a peptide that stimulates CCK release (CCK-releasing peptide); after a meal, dietary protein competes with it for trypsin, the CCK-releasing peptide escapes degradation, and CCK and pancreatic enzyme secretion increase; when the dietary protein has been digested, free trypsin increases again, degrades the CCK-releasing peptide, and pancreatic enzyme secretion declines. This feedback matches the secretion of pancreatic enzymes to the amount of protein awaiting digestion in the lumen and prevents oversecretion. The importance of this mechanism in humans is still debated. In the interdigestive period pancreatic juice is also secreted in short periodic bursts synchronized with the **migrating motor complex**, helping to clear residue from the small intestine.

## 27.5. Bile

### 27.5.1. Composition of Bile

Bile is produced continuously by hepatocytes, about $1\,\mathrm{L}$ per day ($0.6$–$1\,\mathrm{L}$). Bile secreted by hepatocytes (hepatic bile) flows into the bile canaliculi and bile ducts of successive orders; between meals most of it is stored in the gallbladder via the cystic duct, and it is discharged into the duodenum only after eating. Bile contains no digestive enzymes; its main components are:

| Component | Description |
|---|---|
| Bile salts | Sodium and potassium salts of **bile acids** (synthesized from cholesterol in the liver) conjugated with glycine or taurine; the main components of bile that take part in fat digestion and absorption, about half of the solids of bile |
| Phospholipids | Mainly lecithin; together with bile salts they form mixed micelles that solubilize cholesterol |
| Cholesterol | The liver excretes part of its cholesterol directly into bile each day and converts another part to bile acids; bile is the main route by which cholesterol leaves the body |
| Bile pigments | Mainly conjugated bilirubin, a breakdown product of **heme** ([Chapter 11](/7.14.11), Chapter 29); gives bile its golden color |
| Water and electrolytes | Hepatic bile is isotonic with plasma and contains $\ce{HCO3-}$ (secreted by the bile duct epithelium under stimulation by secretin); pH about 7.4–7.8 |

The gallbladder has a capacity of about $30$–$60\,\mathrm{mL}$ and concentrates bile 5–10-fold (up to about 20-fold) by actively reabsorbing $\ce{Na+}$ (with $\ce{Cl-}$, $\ce{HCO3-}$ and water following), so that the roughly $450\,\mathrm{mL}$ of bile produced by the liver in 12 h can be stored in the gallbladder. During concentration the concentrations of bile salts, phospholipids, cholesterol and bilirubin rise while the osmolality is unchanged (they form micelles, which have low osmotic activity), $\ce{HCO3-}$ is absorbed, and gallbladder bile is weakly acidic (pH about 6.8).

Cholesterol itself is insoluble in water and is solubilized in mixed micelles formed by bile salts and lecithin. The ratio of the three determines whether cholesterol is supersaturated: if the proportion of cholesterol is too high (too much cholesterol secreted by the liver), bile salts are too few (impaired enterohepatic circulation), or the gallbladder concentrates excessively and empties less (prolonged fasting, pregnancy), cholesterol crystallizes out and gradually forms cholesterol gallstones.

### 27.5.2. Functions of Bile

1. Emulsification of fat: bile salts and lecithin are amphipathic molecules; they adsorb to the surface of fat droplets and lower the **interfacial tension** between oil and water, so that the movements of stomach and intestine can break large droplets into small droplets about $1\,\mathrm{\mu m}$ in diameter, greatly increasing the surface area on which pancreatic lipase acts (Chapter 28).
2. Promotion of the absorption of lipid digestion products: when the bile salt concentration exceeds the **critical micelle concentration** (about $1$–$2\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$), **micelles** about $3$–$10\,\mathrm{nm}$ in diameter form; fatty acids, 2-monoacylglycerol, cholesterol and fat-soluble vitamins enter the interior of the micelles to form mixed micelles, which are carried across the unstirred water layer at the surface of the intestinal epithelium to the site of absorption (Chapter 28). Without bile, up to 40% of ingested fat is lost in the feces, and the absorption of vitamins A, D, E and K is also affected.
3. Neutralization of gastric acid ($\ce{HCO3-}$); excretion of cholesterol, bilirubin, drugs and metabolites of heavy metals.
4. After bile salts are absorbed back into the liver they promote further bile secretion (choleretic action).

**Definition 27.5.1 (Enterohepatic Circulation)** Bile salts are discharged with bile into the small intestine, where more than about 95% are actively reabsorbed in the terminal ileum (a small amount is absorbed passively in the small intestine and colon), return to the liver through the portal vein, are taken up by hepatocytes, and are secreted into bile again. This cycle is called the **enterohepatic circulation** of bile salts.

The body contains only about $3\,\mathrm{g}$ of bile salts (the bile acid pool) yet secretes $12$–$36\,\mathrm{g}$ per day, because the pool circulates 4–12 times a day (2 or more times per meal). The quantitative treatment of the enterohepatic circulation and its mechanisms in the liver are given in Chapter 29; some drugs and endogenous substances (estrogens, urobilinogen, a metabolite of bilirubin) also take part in enterohepatic circulation.

### 27.5.3. Regulation of Bile Secretion and Release

The formation of bile (liver) and its release (gallbladder) are regulated separately. The formation of hepatic bile depends mainly on the amount of bile salts returning to the liver (bile salt–dependent bile flow); secretin increases the secretion of $\ce{HCO3-}$-rich fluid by the bile duct epithelium (without increasing bile salts), and the vagus nerve and gastrin also have weaker stimulatory effects.

Emptying of the gallbladder is caused mainly by CCK: after a meal, fatty acids and amino acids in the duodenum stimulate I cells to release CCK, which contracts gallbladder smooth muscle and at the same time relaxes the sphincter of Oddi; bile is discharged copiously into the duodenum within $1\,\mathrm{h}$, and the gallbladder empties 50%–70% within about $1$–$2\,\mathrm{h}$ after the meal. The vagus nerve (cephalic and gastric phases) causes weaker gallbladder contraction. The activity of the gallbladder and that of the sphincter of Oddi are coordinated; they do not contract at the same time. Meals rich in protein and fat evoke the greatest bile release, and carbohydrates the least. This too is a system driven by the contents and matched to demand: stored bile is released only when the duodenum contains fat that needs emulsifying. After cholecystectomy, bile flows into the small intestine continuously in small amounts, digestion of a normal diet is usually not noticeably affected, and indigestion may occur only after a single large fatty meal.

## 27.6. Intestinal Juice and Large-Intestinal Juice

The duodenal glands (Brunner glands) lie in the submucosa of the first few centimeters of the duodenum and secrete an alkaline fluid rich in mucus and $\ce{HCO3-}$, which protects the duodenal epithelium from gastric acid. They respond to three stimuli: tactile and irritating stimulation of the duodenal mucosa, the vagus nerve, and secretin. Sympathetic stimulation strongly inhibits secretion by the Brunner glands, so the duodenal bulb loses its protection in people under prolonged tension and high sympathetic activity, which may be one reason why duodenal ulcers occur mostly at this site.

The intestinal glands (crypts of Lieberkühn) are distributed throughout the small intestine, between the villi. Epithelial cells in the crypts secrete $\ce{Cl-}$ through CFTR, with $\ce{Na+}$ and water following, about $1.8\,\mathrm{L}$ per day; this fluid is almost immediately reabsorbed by the epithelium at the villus tips, forming a fluid circulation between crypts and villi that provides the solvent for the absorption of nutrients at the villus surface. Paneth cells at the base of the crypts secrete defensins and lysozyme to defend against invading microorganisms; **stem cells** in the crypts divide continuously, and the new cells migrate toward the villus tip and differentiate and are shed at the tip after 3–5 days, so the small-intestinal epithelium is one of the fastest-renewing tissues in the body ([7.3](/7.3)). The enzymes needed for the final steps of digestion of carbohydrate and protein in the small intestine (disaccharidases, peptidases) are not secreted into the intestinal fluid but are fixed in the brush-border membrane of the epithelial cells (Chapter 28), and enter the intestinal fluid with shed cells.

The regulation of crypt secretion is mainly by local reflexes of the enteric nervous system: mechanical distension by chyme (the most sensitive stimulus) and chemical stimuli release ACh and VIP from the secretomotor neurons of the submucosal plexus, and gastrin, secretin and others also stimulate secretion. Cholera toxin and the enterotoxins of some *Escherichia coli* strains raise cAMP persistently in epithelial cells and hold CFTR open, so that the crypts secrete large amounts of fluid, causing secretory diarrhea of up to ten-odd liters per day (for the principle of oral rehydration see [Chapter 3](/7.14.3#sec-3.7)).

The mucosa of the large intestine has no villi and contains many goblet cells; it secretes mainly mucus, which lubricates the feces and protects the mucosa. Large-intestinal juice also contains $\ce{HCO3-}$ and $\ce{K+}$, and its secretion is stimulated by the pelvic nerves (parasympathetic).

## 27.7. Evolution and Comparison

### 27.7.1. Comparison of Salivary Glands

Salivary glands have arisen repeatedly in the animal kingdom, with diverse functions. Annelids, most mollusks (except bivalves) and arthropods have salivary glands; fishes have none, only single-cell mucous glands in the mouth, since swallowing in water needs no lubrication; the salivary glands of amphibians are of the mucous type and serve to glue captured insects. The venom gland of a snake is a specialized serous salivary gland, and the phospholipases, proteases and hyaluronidase in the venom both kill prey and begin digestion; the proteins of venom glands (kallikrein, for example) are homologous with those of the salivary glands of other reptiles and of mammals. Birds do not chew and have poorly developed salivary glands, only small mucus-secreting glands; swiftlets in the breeding season build their nests with the mucous glycoprotein secreted by the salivary glands ("edible bird's nest"). Mammalian salivary glands are generally well developed, and their structure varies with diet: those of cetaceans are reduced or absent, whereas herbivorous ruminants have very well developed parotid glands.

The composition of saliva has also evolved with diet. The human salivary amylase gene *AMY1* shows **copy number variation** (from 2 to more than 15), and populations that traditionally eat high-starch diets have on average more copies and more salivary amylase; in meat-eating canids and felids, saliva contains almost no amylase.

### 27.7.2. Saliva of Ruminants

Ruminants secrete saliva in very large amounts: cattle about $100$–$190\,\mathrm{L}$ per day, sheep about $6$–$16\,\mathrm{L}$. It contains almost no amylase, but its $[\ce{HCO3-}]$ is about $100$–$140\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$ and it contains abundant phosphate; it is an isotonic, strongly alkaline buffer. Microbes in the **rumen** produce tens of moles of **short-chain fatty acids** per day (more than 100 mol in high-yielding dairy cows; Chapter 28); without the continuous buffering by saliva, the rumen pH would quickly fall to a level the microbes cannot tolerate, and the prolonged chewing during rumination is the main factor stimulating copious salivary secretion. When cattle are fed a high-grain, low-roughage diet, chewing and saliva are reduced, fermentation speeds up, the rumen pH falls, and ruminal acidosis results. Saliva also carries urea from the blood back into the rumen, where microbes use it as a nitrogen source to synthesize protein (urea recycling), which is one reason ruminants can survive on low-protein food.

### 27.7.3. Blood-Feeding and Specialized Diets

The saliva of blood-feeding animals is a pharmacological toolkit: the saliva of mosquitoes, ticks, leeches and vampire bats contains anticoagulants (such as hirudin of leeches, which directly inhibits thrombin, and the fibrinolytic plasminogen activator of vampire bats), apyrase (adenosine triphosphate diphosphohydrolase, which breaks down ADP) that inhibits platelet aggregation, vasodilators, and anti-inflammatory and anesthetic substances, so that the host does not notice and the blood does not clot. The digestive system of vampire bats is also adapted to blood as the sole food (high in protein, low in sugar and fat): they have lost the sweet taste receptor genes, intestinal sucrase activity is very low, and the stomach is a tubular structure that absorbs water rapidly; within minutes after feeding they begin to excrete dilute urine to reduce their weight and facilitate flight.

### 27.7.4. Animal Chitinases

Chitin is the main component of the exoskeletons of insects and crustaceans and of fungal cell walls. The mammalian acidic chitinase (AMCase, gene *CHIA*) is secreted in the stomach and can hydrolyze chitin in an acidic environment. Comparative genomic studies have found that the ancestor of placental mammals probably had 5 functional *CHIA* genes, consistent with the insectivorous diet of early mammals; in later lineages that no longer eat insects (such as many carnivores, herbivores and cetaceans), the *CHIA* genes became pseudogenes by independent inactivation many times, whereas many lineages that still eat insects retain several functional copies. Humans have only one functional *CHIA*, and its activity is also low. This is a molecular example of gene loss after relaxed selection: digestive enzyme genes that are no longer needed are no longer protected by purifying selection and lose function through the accumulation of **neutral mutations**, becoming **pseudogenes**. The copy number and function of digestive enzyme genes can therefore serve as clues for inferring ancestral diet.

## Questions

1. A person's salivary $[\ce{Na+}]$ is $10\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$ at rest and rises to $70\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$ while chewing gum. Explain this change with the two-stage model. If aldosterone is injected, how would salivary $\ce{Na+}$ and $\ce{K+}$ be expected to change?

   **Answer** The primary fluid secreted by the acini has a $[\ce{Na+}]$ close to that of plasma (about $145\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$), and the ductal epithelium reabsorbs $\ce{Na+}$ (and $\ce{Cl-}$) as the fluid flows through while hardly reabsorbing water. At rest the flow is small, the fluid stays in the ducts for a long time, and $\ce{Na+}$ is almost completely absorbed; while chewing gum the flow increases several-fold, the residence time shortens, the transport capacity of the duct is limited (saturable) and reabsorption is incomplete, so the salivary $[\ce{Na+}]$ rises. Aldosterone, as in the renal collecting duct, increases the number of ENaC channels and **sodium pumps** in the ductal epithelium and strengthens $\ce{Na+}$ reabsorption and $\ce{K+}$ secretion, so salivary $[\ce{Na+}]$ falls and $[\ce{K+}]$ rises (the salivary $\ce{Na+}/\ce{K+}$ ratio can serve as a rough index of aldosterone activity).

2. Using the method of Example 27.3.1, calculate the minimum free energy required to pump one $\ce{H+}$ if the gastric luminal pH can fall only to 2.0. What is the maximum number of $\ce{H+}$ that can be pumped per ATP hydrolyzed at this point?

   **Answer** $\Delta\mathrm{pH} = 7.4 - 2.0 = 5.4$, $\Delta G = 2.303 \times 8.314 \times 310 \times 5.4 \approx 32\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$. ATP hydrolysis provides about $57\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$; after subtracting the few kilojoules needed to exchange $\ce{K+}$ into the cell, about $52\,\mathrm{kJ}$ remain, enough to pump only 1 $\ce{H+}$ (2 would require $64\,\mathrm{kJ}$). Only when the luminal pH is above about 3 ($\Delta G < 26\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$) can each ATP pump 2 $\ce{H+}$. This agrees with the experimental observation that the stoichiometry of the proton pump changes from 2 H⁺/ATP to 1 H⁺/ATP as the luminal pH falls.

3. Why can H2 receptor antagonists inhibit acid secretion evoked by vagal stimulation or by injection of gastrin? Why are proton pump inhibitors more effective than H2 receptor antagonists?

   **Answer** In addition to acting directly on parietal cells, ACh and gastrin both stimulate ECL cells to release histamine (gastrin mainly by this route); moreover, the action of histamine through cAMP is synergistic with that of ACh and gastrin through $\ce{Ca^2+}$, so when the cAMP signal of histamine is removed, the direct actions of the other two are also greatly weakened. The proton pump is the final common step of all stimuli (whichever receptor and messenger they use), and inhibiting it blocks acid secretion evoked by any pathway; furthermore, proton pump inhibitors are activated in the acidic secretory canaliculus and bind covalently to the pump, so the effect is long-lasting, and recovery requires synthesis of new pumps.

4. A patient after pancreatectomy and a patient with cystic fibrosis both have steatorrhea. Analyze the similarities and differences from the composition of pancreatic juice.

   **Answer** Similarities: both lack pancreatic lipase entering the duodenum (after pancreatectomy there are no acini; in cystic fibrosis the ducts are blocked by concentrated secretions and the acini are gradually destroyed), so fat cannot be hydrolyzed and is excreted in the feces as triglyceride; both also lack $\ce{HCO3-}$, so the duodenal pH is low, residual lipase is inactivated and bile salts precipitate, further impairing fat digestion. Differences: pancreatectomy also removes the islets, so the patient has diabetes and needs insulin; the pancreatic lesion of cystic fibrosis is at first mainly a defect of ductal $\ce{HCO3-}$ and fluid secretion, and later the islets may also be damaged (cystic fibrosis–related diabetes), and in addition there are CFTR-related lesions in many organs: lungs, sweat glands, intestine (meconium ileus) and reproductive ducts. In both, steatorrhea can be treated with oral pancreatic enzyme preparations.

5. How does the enterohepatic circulation of bile acids change after cholecystectomy? What may happen to the patient after a high-fat meal?

   **Answer** After cholecystectomy, bile secreted by the liver cannot be stored and flows continuously in small amounts into the duodenum; the bile acid pool also circulates between small intestine and liver between meals, and the number of cycles per day increases, but a large amount of bile cannot be released all at once during a meal as it could with a gallbladder. For a normal diet, the bile acid concentration is usually still above the critical micellar concentration and digestion is essentially normal; when a large amount of fat is ingested at once, the supply of bile acids cannot keep up with demand, emulsification of fat and micelle formation are insufficient, and abdominal distension and steatorrhea may occur. On the other hand, the bile acids that enter the intestine continuously reach the colon in larger amounts and may stimulate colonic secretion, causing chronic diarrhea in some patients.

6. (Open question) The secretory system of the digestive tract repeatedly uses the design of "placing activation where the enzyme is used" (pepsinogen is activated by acid in the gastric lumen, trypsinogen by enteropeptidase in the intestinal lumen). Identify other systems in the body that use a similar design, and discuss the advantages and disadvantages of this design.

   **Answer** Key points: clotting factors circulate in plasma as zymogens and are activated only at the site of vascular injury ([Chapter 11](/7.14.11)); the complement system is activated on the surface of pathogens; the hydrolases of **lysosomes** are transported as zymogens and activated in the acidic lysosome; plasminogen is activated on the surface of fibrin. Advantages: the active enzyme does not appear at the site of synthesis and transport, avoiding self-injury; storage as zymogens allows activation to be rapid and amplified by a cascade. Disadvantages: extra activation mechanisms and inhibitors are required; if the activation mechanism is triggered in the wrong place, cascade amplification makes the damage spread rapidly (acute pancreatitis, disseminated intravascular coagulation); these systems are therefore all accompanied by multiple inhibitors (trypsin inhibitor, antithrombin) as safeguards.
