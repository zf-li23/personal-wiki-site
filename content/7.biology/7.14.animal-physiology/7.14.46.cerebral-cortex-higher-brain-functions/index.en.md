The preceding chapters discussed sensation (Chapters 39–43) and movement (Chapters 44–45). Sensory signals finally arrive at the cerebral cortex, and motor commands are also issued from it, and between the two, most of the cortex is neither sensory nor motor: it integrates the various senses, compares them with memory, forms language, plans, and emotions, and decides what to do. This chapter, the last of the part on neural, sensory, and motor physiology, discusses the physiological basis of these "higher functions": the organization of cortex and thalamus, functional areas and language, the EEG and the cycle of wakefulness and sleep, learning and memory, emotion and motivation, and the energy cost that supports it all. The last section compares sleep, brain size, and cognition across animals and doubles as a comparative summary of the chapters on the nervous system. ## 46.1. The Cerebral Cortex and Thalamus

### 46.1.1. Basic Numbers

The cerebral cortex is the gray matter covering the surface of the cerebral hemispheres; it is the largest part of the central nervous system by volume and also the one that is least understood, and most of what we know of its function comes from patients with lesions and from animal experiments. The human cortex is $2$–$4\,\mathrm{mm}$ thick (thickest in the precentral gyrus), and its area when the two hemispheres are unfolded is about $0.2$–$0.25\,\mathrm{m^2}$, about two thirds of it hidden in the sulci and fissures. It is divided by the central sulcus and lateral fissure into the frontal, parietal, temporal, and occipital lobes, and deep in the lateral fissure there is also the insula.

The number of cortical neurons needs to be distinguished by method: by the isotropic fractionator method (dissolving tissue into a suspension of nuclei and counting them), the human cerebral cortex (including white matter) has about $1.6\times10^{10}$ neurons and the whole brain about $8.6\times10^{10}$ (Azevedo et al., 2009), about 80% of them in the cerebellum ([7.14.7](/7.14.7#sec-7.2.6)); by stereological counting, the neocortex has about $2\times10^{10}$ neurons and about $1.5\times10^{14}$ synapses (Pakkenberg et al., 2003). The commonly quoted figure of "100 billion neurons in the cerebral cortex" takes the number for the whole brain (and an old estimate at that) as the number for the cortex; the difference between "20 billion" and "16 billion" for the cortex comes from the counting method and is not a contradiction. On average each cortical neuron has about $7000$ synapses.

### 46.1.2. The Six-Layer Structure

**Definition 46.1.1 (Neocortex)** The cerebral cortex that consists of six layers of neurons and is unique to mammals is called the **neocortex**; it makes up about 90% of the human cerebral cortex, and the remainder is the evolutionarily older archicortex (hippocampus) and paleocortex (olfactory cortex), which have fewer layers.

The three main kinds of neurons of the neocortex are pyramidal cells (about 80%; glutamatergic, with a long apical dendrite reaching toward the surface and an axon leaving the cortex, the only output neurons of the cortex), granule cells (stellate cells, with short axons, interneurons that pass signals locally within the cortex), and fusiform cells (polymorphic cells). Inhibitory interneurons (GABAergic) make up about 20% ([7.14.6](/7.14.6#sec-6.3.3)). The functions of the layers:

| Layer | Name | Main contents | Function |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | molecular layer | very few neurons, mainly dendrites and axons of neurons of other layers | connections within the cortex, receiving diffuse input from the nonspecific thalamic nuclei and modulatory systems |
| II | external granular layer | small granule cells and pyramidal cells | together with layer III, responsible for association within the cortex, especially lateral connections between neighboring cortical areas |
| III | external pyramidal layer | small and medium pyramidal cells | gives off association fibers to the opposite hemisphere (through the corpus callosum) and to other cortical areas of the same side |
| IV | internal granular layer | dense granule cells | receives sensory input from the specific thalamic nuclei (thickest in sensory cortex, very thin in motor cortex, [7.14.45](/7.14.45#sec-45.1.1)) |
| V | internal pyramidal layer | large pyramidal cells (Betz cells in motor cortex) | gives off long projection fibers to the brainstem, spinal cord, and striatum |
| VI | multiform layer | fusiform cells and pyramidal cells | gives off many feedback fibers to the thalamus |

Signals flow in the cortex roughly in the direction "enter at layer IV → spread laterally and integrate in layers II/III → output from layers V/VI." Another organizing principle of the cortex is the column: a group of neurons perpendicular to the surface with a diameter of about $0.3$–$0.5\,\mathrm{mm}$ has the same receptive field properties (such as the same class of receptors from the same skin region in somatosensory cortex, or lines of the same orientation in visual cortex, [7.14.41](/7.14.41)) and is the basic unit of information processing.

### 46.1.3. The Thalamocortical Unit

The thalamus and cortex should be regarded as one functional whole:

- The connections between them are bidirectional: each thalamic nucleus projects to a particular cortical area, and layer VI of that cortical area projects back to the same thalamic nucleus with about 10 times as many fibers as the forward projection.
- Except for olfaction, almost all sensory information must be relayed in the thalamus before reaching the cortex; when a part of the thalamus is damaged, the function of the corresponding cortical area is almost completely lost.
- The thalamic reticular nucleus is a shell of GABAergic neurons around the thalamus that receives collaterals of thalamocortical and corticothalamic fibers, inhibits the relay neurons of the thalamus, and takes part in the generation of spindles in sleep and in the selection of attention.

The main thalamic nuclei:

| Nucleus | Input | Projects to |
|---|---|---|
| ventral posterolateral and ventral posteromedial nuclei | somatic sensation of the body and face (medial lemniscus, trigeminal lemniscus, spinothalamic tract), taste | primary somatosensory cortex ([7.14.39](/7.14.39#sec-39.6.2)) |
| lateral geniculate nucleus | retina | primary visual cortex ([7.14.41](/7.14.41)) |
| medial geniculate nucleus | inferior colliculus (hearing) | primary auditory cortex ([7.14.42](/7.14.42)) |
| ventrolateral and ventral anterior nuclei | cerebellum and basal ganglia | motor and premotor cortex ([7.14.45](/7.14.45)) |
| mediodorsal nucleus | amygdala, olfactory cortex, basal ganglia | prefrontal cortex |
| anterior nucleus | mammillary bodies | cingulate gyrus (limbic system) |
| pulvinar | superior colliculus and several cortical areas | parieto-occipito-temporal association cortex |
| intralaminar and midline nuclei | reticular formation, spinothalamic tract | widespread cortical areas and striatum (arousal and attention) |

The first three groups are specific projection systems that convey one kind of sensation, project to specific areas of the cortex, terminate in layer IV, and produce specific sensations; the nonspecific projection system formed by the intralaminar and other nuclei receives input from the reticular formation, projects diffusely to broad areas of the cortex, and maintains the excitatory state of the cortex (Section 46.4.1).

## 46.2. Functional Areas of the Cortex

### 46.2.1. Electrical Stimulation and Lesions

Penfield and colleagues, operating on epileptic patients in the 1930s–1950s under local anesthesia with the patient awake, stimulated the cortical surface with weak currents and recorded the patient's responses: stimulating the postcentral gyrus caused numbness or tingling somewhere on the opposite side of the body, stimulating the precentral gyrus caused twitching of some group of muscles on the opposite side, stimulating the occipital lobe caused flashes of light, stimulating the superior temporal gyrus caused the sensation of sound, and stimulating some parts of the temporal lobe occasionally evoked fragments of past events. The cortical "homunculi" of somatic sensation and movement drawn from this ([7.14.39](/7.14.39#sec-39.6.2), [7.14.45](/7.14.45#sec-45.1.1)) show the two principles of topographic mapping and "allocation of area according to fineness of use." Today these functional maps are supplemented and refined by functional magnetic resonance imaging, positron emission tomography, transcranial magnetic stimulation, and intracranial recording, but electrical stimulation and lesions remain the most direct evidence for determining whether an area is necessary.

The primary sensory and primary motor areas occupy only a small part of the human cortical area (about 20%), and the rest is association cortex.

### 46.2.2. Association Cortex

**Definition 46.2.1 (Association Cortex)** The cortical areas that neither receive the specific input of a single sensory modality nor issue motor commands directly but receive and integrate signals from several sensory and motor areas are called **association cortex**.

Association cortex is divided into three large areas:

1. Parieto-occipito-temporal association area: between the somatosensory, visual, and auditory cortices, it integrates information from the various senses. Within it, posterior parietal cortex analyzes the spatial coordinates of the parts of the body and of the surrounding objects (patients with damage to the right parietal lobe often neglect the space on the left and their own left half-body, called hemispatial neglect); Wernicke's area (posterior superior temporal gyrus) is responsible for the comprehension of language; the angular gyrus integrates vision and hearing and is necessary for reading; and the anteroinferior temporal lobe and fusiform gyrus are responsible for the recognition and naming of objects and faces (after damage, familiar faces cannot be recognized, called prosopagnosia).
2. Prefrontal association area: working closely with motor cortex, it plans complex movement patterns and action sequences and carries out complex thinking. It is responsible for working memory (Section 46.5.3), for choosing among several possible behaviors, and for inhibiting inappropriate responses in accordance with goals. Patients with prefrontal damage (the classic example is Phineas Gage, in whom an iron rod passed through the prefrontal lobe in 1848) can retain intelligence and memory essentially intact but have difficulty sticking to a goal, behave inappropriately in social situations, and lack foresight about the consequences of their behavior.
3. Limbic association area: located at the temporal pole, the orbital surface of the frontal lobe, and the cingulate gyrus, it is concerned with emotion, behavior, and motivation (Section 46.6).

### 46.2.3. Language

Language is the most prominent function of the human cortex and is carried out mainly by several areas in the dominant hemisphere (mostly the left) and their connections:

- Broca's area (posterior part of the inferior frontal gyrus): organizes the motor output of language and plans the structure of articulation and grammar.
- Wernicke's area (posterior part of the superior temporal gyrus): understands spoken and written language, selects words, and converts thoughts into language.
- Arcuate fasciculus: the white matter tract connecting Wernicke's area with Broca's area.

**Definition 46.2.2 (Aphasia)** A disorder of language comprehension or expression that is caused by brain damage and is not due to a disorder of the articulatory organs or of hearing itself is called **aphasia**.

| Type | Lesion | Speech output | Comprehension | Repetition |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Broca aphasia (motor aphasia) | Broca's area and surroundings | nonfluent, effortful, telegraphic, function words missing | largely preserved | poor |
| Wernicke aphasia (sensory aphasia) | Wernicke's area | fluent but empty, with many paraphasias, unaware of it | poor | poor |
| Conduction aphasia | arcuate fasciculus | fluent, with paraphasias | preserved | especially poor |
| Global aphasia | large perisylvian region | almost unable to speak | poor | poor |

In addition, damage to the angular gyrus causes alexia (normal vision but inability to understand written words), and damage to the posterior part of the middle frontal gyrus causes agraphia. The Wernicke–Geschwind model describes language as a serial pathway "heard word → primary auditory cortex → Wernicke's area → arcuate fasciculus → Broca's area → motor cortex," which explains the classical types of aphasia well, but modern imaging and lesion studies show that language depends on more widely distributed networks: a ventral pathway (temporal lobe) is responsible for word meaning and a dorsal pathway (parieto-frontal) for the mapping between speech sounds and articulation, and the subcortical basal ganglia and thalamus also take part; signers of sign language develop aphasia similar to that for spoken language after left-hemisphere damage, showing that these areas process language itself and not just sound.

### 46.2.4. Lateralization of the Cerebral Hemispheres

The two hemispheres are roughly symmetrical in structure and have a division of labor in function:

- Language: about 95% of right-handed people and about 70% of left-handed people have the left hemisphere as the dominant hemisphere for language. Damage to Wernicke's area in the adult left hemisphere causes lasting aphasia; after the same damage in a young child, language function can often transfer to the right hemisphere, which shows that lateralization becomes fixed gradually in development.
- The right hemisphere is more dominant in spatial relations, face recognition, music, and the comprehension of the intonation and emotion of speech.

The *split brain*. To control intractable epilepsy, some patients underwent surgery that cut the corpus callosum. Sperry and Gazzaniga (1960s) devised experiments in which an image was presented to only one visual field: when the image was presented in the right visual field (projecting to the left hemisphere), the patient could name it; when it was presented in the left visual field (projecting to the right hemisphere), the patient could not say what had been seen but could pick it out from a pile of objects with the left hand (controlled by the right hemisphere). Each hemisphere can perceive, remember, and learn on its own but cannot share information; only the experience of the left hemisphere can be reported in words. The left hemisphere also "makes up" plausible explanations for behavior caused by the right hemisphere (called the interpreter by Gazzaniga). In daily life these patients behave almost normally, because scanning eye movements and bilateral sensation let both hemispheres obtain most of the information.

## 46.3. The Electroencephalogram

### 46.3.1. Brain Waves

**Definition 46.3.1 (Electroencephalogram)** The spontaneous, rhythmic potential changes of the cerebral cortex recorded with electrodes placed on the scalp are called the **electroencephalogram** (EEG); those recorded with electrodes placed directly on the cortical surface are called the electrocorticogram.

The amplitude of the scalp EEG is $10$–$200\,\mu\mathrm{V}$, and by frequency it is divided into several basic waveforms:

| Wave | Frequency | Amplitude | State in which it appears |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\delta$ wave | $0.5$–$4\,\mathrm{Hz}$ | $20$–$200\,\mu\mathrm{V}$ | deep sleep (N3) in adults, infants; in awake adults it suggests brain pathology |
| $\theta$ wave | $4$–$8\,\mathrm{Hz}$ | $20$–$150\,\mu\mathrm{V}$ | drowsiness, light sleep, children; hippocampal $\theta$ rhythm is related to spatial navigation and memory |
| $\alpha$ wave | $8$–$13\,\mathrm{Hz}$ | $20$–$100\,\mu\mathrm{V}$ | awake, quiet, eyes closed, most marked over the occiput; disappears on opening the eyes or thinking ($\alpha$ blocking) |
| $\beta$ wave | $13$–$30\,\mathrm{Hz}$ | $5$–$20\,\mu\mathrm{V}$ | eyes open, alert, thinking, most marked over the frontal region |
| $\gamma$ wave | $> 30\,\mathrm{Hz}$ | very small | attention and binding of sensations, mostly recorded with intracranial electrodes |

The general rule is that the more active the cortex and the more intensely it processes information, the higher the frequency and the lower the amplitude of the EEG, called desynchronization; the quieter it is (deep sleep, anesthesia), the lower the frequency and the higher the amplitude, called synchronization.

### 46.3.2. Generation of Brain Waves

The scalp EEG comes mainly not from action potentials but from the postsynaptic potentials on the apical dendrites of large numbers of pyramidal cells. Pyramidal cells are arranged in parallel with apical dendrites perpendicular to the cortical surface, synaptic currents flow in along the dendrite and out near the soma (or the reverse), and each cell constitutes a small current dipole; when many dipoles are aligned in the same direction and active at the same time, their fields add up on the scalp (the same as the dipole field of the electrocardiogram, [7.6.30](/7.6.30)). The action potential lasts only about $1\,\mathrm{ms}$ and is hard to sum synchronously; postsynaptic potentials last $10$–$100\,\mathrm{ms}$ and overlap easily in time, so the EEG reflects the sum of synaptic inputs.

**Example 46.3.1 (Amplification by Synchrony)** Suppose there are about $N = 10^7$ pyramidal cells beneath some place on the scalp, each contributing an oscillation of amplitude $a$ on the scalp. If their phases are completely random, the summed amplitude grows only as $\sqrt{N}\,a \approx 3\times10^3 a$ (the same as a random walk); if they are completely synchronous, the amplitude is $N a = 10^7 a$, about 3000 times larger. So even if only 1% of the cells are synchronous ($10^5 a$), their signal is 30 times that of the random activity of all the cells. The scalp EEG sees almost only the synchronous component, which is why the EEG amplitude is large in deep sleep: a few rhythm pacemaker mechanisms synchronize large numbers of neurons.

There are two sources of rhythm: one is the thalamocortical circuit, in which thalamic relay neurons produce rhythmic bursts through T-type $\ce{Ca^2+}$ channels when hyperpolarized and inhibition from the thalamic reticular nucleus synchronizes the thalamic neurons, producing spindles and the $\delta$ rhythm; the other is the interaction of excitatory and inhibitory neurons within the cortex, producing the $\gamma$ rhythm ([7.14.6](/7.14.6#sec-6.4.1)). The ascending arousal system of the brainstem depolarizes thalamic neurons, the burst mode changes to continuous single-spike firing, the rhythm disappears, and the EEG desynchronizes.

Epilepsy is abnormal, excessively synchronous discharge of cortical neurons, which appears on the EEG as sharp waves, spikes, or spike-and-slow-wave complexes. In generalized seizures the discharge involves both hemispheres (such as the $3\,\mathrm{Hz}$ spike-and-wave of absence seizures, which arises in the thalamocortical circuit); focal seizures start from a local lesion and can spread.

### 46.3.3. Evoked Potentials

**Definition 46.3.2 (Evoked Potential)** The potential change recorded locally in the cortex that has a fixed temporal relation to a stimulus, when the sensory afferent system or some part of the brain is stimulated, is called a cortical **evoked potential**.

The amplitude of an evoked potential ($1$–$10\,\mu\mathrm{V}$) is much smaller than that of the spontaneous EEG, and it is hard to distinguish when superimposed on it. Because the time relation between an evoked potential and the stimulus is fixed whereas the spontaneous EEG is unrelated to the stimulus, aligning the records after $n$ stimuli and averaging leaves the evoked potential unchanged while the amplitude of the spontaneous EEG decreases as $1/\sqrt{n}$, improving the signal-to-noise ratio by $\sqrt{n}$ fold: averaging 100 trials gives a tenfold improvement. Clinical visual, auditory (brainstem auditory evoked potentials, used to test the hearing and brainstem function of infants), and somatosensory evoked potentials, and cognitive event-related potentials (such as the P300 that appears at about $300\,\mathrm{ms}$ to a rare stimulus), are all recorded by this method.

## 46.4. Wakefulness and Sleep

### 46.4.1. The Arousal System

Moruzzi and Magoun (1949) found that stimulating the reticular formation of the cat brainstem desynchronized the EEG of sleeping animals and woke them, and that animals in which the brainstem was transected at the midbrain (cutting only the ascending pathway of the reticular formation while preserving the specific sensory pathways) remained in a sleep-like state. They therefore proposed the ascending reticular activating system of the brainstem: the reticular formation receives collaterals of the various sensory afferents and excites the cortex widely through the nonspecific projection system of the thalamus and through the hypothalamus, maintaining wakefulness.

It is now known that what maintains wakefulness in the "reticular formation" is several chemically distinct groups of modulatory neurons, all of which project widely to the forebrain:

| Nucleus | Transmitter | Wakefulness | Non-REM sleep | REM sleep |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| locus coeruleus | norepinephrine | high | low | almost stopped |
| raphe nuclei | serotonin | high | low | almost stopped |
| tuberomammillary nucleus of the hypothalamus | histamine | high | low | stopped |
| pedunculopontine and laterodorsal tegmental nuclei, basal forebrain | acetylcholine | high | low | high |
| lateral hypothalamus | orexin (also called hypocretin) | high | low | low |

Antihistamines cause drowsiness precisely because they block central H1 receptors.

### 46.4.2. Stages of Sleep

Sleep is a reversible state of reduced responsiveness to external stimuli, and by means of the EEG, electrooculogram, and electromyogram (together called polysomnography) it is divided into two broad classes of stages:

**Definition 46.4.1 (Non-REM Sleep and REM Sleep)** The stage of sleep in which the EEG is synchronized, spindles and slow waves appear, muscles retain some tone, and there are no rapid eye movements is called **non-rapid eye movement sleep** (NREM), the deepest part of which, dominated by $\delta$ waves, is called slow-wave sleep; the stage in which the EEG is desynchronized and resembles wakefulness, skeletal muscle tone is almost completely lost, and bursts of rapid eye movements occur is called **rapid eye movement sleep** (REM), also called paradoxical sleep.

NREM sleep is divided under the current standard (American Academy of Sleep Medicine) into three stages: N1, in which $\alpha$ waves disappear and low-amplitude $\theta$ waves appear, the transition from wakefulness to sleep; N2, in which $11$–$16\,\mathrm{Hz}$ spindles and high-amplitude K complexes appear, making up about half of adult sleep; and N3, in which $\delta$ waves occupy more than 20%, that is, slow-wave sleep, from which arousal is most difficult. Stages 3 and 4 of the old classification are now merged into N3.

Over a night, sleep goes from N1 to N2 and N3, back to N2, and then into the first REM sleep, making a cycle of about $90\,\mathrm{min}$; there are 4–5 cycles a night. In the cycles of the first half of the night there is more N3, and in those of the second half less N3 and a progressively longer REM sleep; REM sleep makes up about 20%–25% of total sleep time in adults and about 50% in newborns.

| Physiological change | NREM sleep | REM sleep |
|---|---|---|
| EEG | synchronized, spindles, K complexes, $\delta$ waves | desynchronized, low-amplitude mixed frequencies, sawtooth waves |
| Skeletal muscle | tone reduced, occasional changes of position | tone almost absent (active inhibition of motor neurons by the brainstem), with bursts of limb twitching and rapid eye movements |
| Heart rate, blood pressure, respiration | reduced, regular; sympathetic activity decreased, parasympathetic increased | irregular, rising in bursts; the risk of cardiac events increases during bursts of sympathetic activity |
| Thermoregulation | set point lowered, regulation persists | essentially stopped: almost no sweating and no shivering |
| Cerebral blood flow and metabolism | 20%–40% lower than in wakefulness | similar to or higher than in wakefulness |
| Endocrine | large secretion of growth hormone in slow-wave sleep ([7.14.34](/7.14.34)) | |
| Dreams | fewer, simple in content | frequent and vivid (about 80% report dreaming when awakened) |
| Other | night terrors and sleepwalking occur in N3 | penile and clitoral erection; patients with REM sleep behavior disorder lose the inhibition of muscle tone and act out their dreams |

### 46.4.3. The Sleep Switch

The onset of sleep is not the passive fatigue of the arousal system but an active process. The ventrolateral preoptic nucleus (VLPO) of the anterior hypothalamus contains a group of GABA- and galanin-containing neurons that are active in sleep and inhibit each of the arousal nuclei above; the arousal nuclei (especially the norepinephrine and serotonin neurons) in turn inhibit the VLPO. The two mutually inhibiting groups of neurons form a "flip-flop" (Saper et al., 2001): the system can rest stably in only one of two states, and transitions between states are rapid and complete rather than lingering between the two. This is the same as the general principle of the bistable switch ([7.9.16](/7.9.16)).

The orexin neurons of the lateral hypothalamus excite all the arousal nuclei and stabilize the flip-flop on the wake side. Loss of orexin neurons (mostly caused by autoimmunity) causes narcolepsy: sudden sleep during the day and intrusion of components of REM sleep into wakefulness, such as sudden loss of muscle tone on emotional excitement (cataplexy), and hallucinations and sleep paralysis on falling asleep or waking. Dogs with a mutation of the orexin receptor gene (Lin et al., 1999) and mice with an orexin knockout have the same symptoms. Between REM sleep and NREM sleep there is another similar switch formed by "REM-on" and "REM-off" neurons in the pons; the loss of muscle tone in REM comes from glycinergic and GABAergic inhibition of spinal motor neurons by descending pontine pathways through the medulla.

### 46.4.4. Homeostatic Regulation of Sleep and Adenosine

The longer one stays awake, the stronger the drowsiness, and the more and deeper the slow waves in the sleep that follows: slow-wave activity (the power of the $0.5$–$4.5\,\mathrm{Hz}$ component of the EEG) is highest after sleep onset and declines approximately exponentially as sleep proceeds; after sleep deprivation, slow-wave activity increases according to the length of wakefulness. This shows that there is a "sleep pressure" that accumulates with wakefulness and is dissipated in sleep.

Adenosine is one of the candidate molecules for this pressure: in wakefulness neurons and astrocytes are very active, ATP consumption increases, and extracellular adenosine accumulates in the basal forebrain and cortex; adenosine inhibits cholinergic arousal neurons through A1 receptors and excites neurons near the VLPO through A2A receptors, promoting sleep. Caffeine is an antagonist of A1 and A2A receptors and therefore keeps us alert. The molecular basis of sleep pressure may be more than adenosine: the phosphorylation level of many proteins in the mouse cortex increases with time awake and is restored in sleep (Wang et al., 2018), which may be a more direct molecular record.

### 46.4.5. The Suprachiasmatic Nucleus and Circadian Rhythms

The timing of sleep is also controlled by the **circadian rhythm**: even in an environment without any time cues (constant light and temperature), human sleep–wake, body temperature ([7.14.31](/7.14.31)), and the secretion of cortisol and melatonin still free-run with a period close to 24 hours (about $24.2\,\mathrm{h}$ in humans).

**Definition 46.4.2 (Suprachiasmatic Nucleus)** The pair of small nuclei in the anterior hypothalamus directly above the optic chiasm (about $10^4$ neurons on each side in humans), whose neurons generate rhythms of nearly 24 hours autonomously, receive light signals from the retina that synchronize the rhythm with the day, and transmit the rhythm to the tissues of the whole body, and which are the master clock of mammals, are called the **suprachiasmatic nucleus** (SCN).

- The molecular clock: in each SCN neuron (and in almost all cells of the body), the transcriptional activator CLOCK–BMAL1 promotes the transcription of the *Per* and *Cry* genes, and after the PER and CRY proteins accumulate and enter the nucleus they inhibit CLOCK–BMAL1 and thereby inhibit their own transcription; after PER and CRY are phosphorylated and degraded, the inhibition is released and the next cycle begins. The delays of this transcription–translation negative feedback loop (protein synthesis, modification, nuclear entry, and degradation) determine the period of about 24 hours and are an example of oscillation produced by delayed negative feedback ([7.9.16](/7.9.16)). Coupling among SCN neurons makes the rhythm more stable and more precise than that of single cells.
- Light entrainment: intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells containing melanopsin ([7.14.41](/7.14.41)) project directly to the SCN through the retinohypothalamic tract. Light in the evening delays the clock and light in the early morning advances it; this phase adjustment corrects the intrinsic period of 24.2 hours to 24 hours every day. After a flight across time zones, the clock can adjust only about 1 hour per day, which produces jet lag.
- Output: the SCN influences sleep–wake, feeding, and body temperature through the paraventricular and dorsomedial nuclei of the hypothalamus, and through the sympathetic pathway controls the synthesis and secretion of melatonin by the pineal gland at night, which light suppresses.

Animals with a destroyed SCN still sleep, and the total amount of sleep is essentially unchanged, but sleep and wakefulness are distributed randomly through the day; when the SCN of *tau*-mutant hamsters (period about 20 h) was transplanted into normal hamsters whose SCN had been destroyed, the recipients acquired the period of the donor (Ralph et al., 1990), proving that the SCN determines the period of the rhythm.

The *two-process model*. Borbély (1982) decomposed the regulation of sleep into two mutually independent processes: process S is the sleep pressure, which grows toward an upper limit in saturating fashion during wakefulness and decays exponentially during sleep; process C is the circadian rhythm generated by the SCN, which determines the fluctuation with time of the upper threshold of S (at which sleep begins) and the lower threshold (at which waking occurs):

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\text{wake:}\ S(t) = 1 - \bigl(1 - S_0\bigr)\mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau_{\mathrm{w}}}, \qquad \text{sleep:}\ S(t) = S_0\,\mathrm{e}^{-t/\tau_{\mathrm{s}}},\\
&H(t) = H_0 + a\,C(t), \qquad L(t) = L_0 + a\,C(t).
\end{aligned} \tag{46.1}
$$

Fitting measurements of slow-wave activity gives $\tau_{\mathrm{w}} \approx 18.2\,\mathrm{h}$ and $\tau_{\mathrm{s}} \approx 4.2\,\mathrm{h}$ (Daan, Beersma, and Borbély, 1984): pressure accumulates slowly in wakefulness and is dissipated quickly in sleep, so 16 hours of wakefulness need only 8 hours of sleep to be repaid. After a night of sleep deprivation, $S$ is far above its usual level (figure) and there are more slow waves in the recovery sleep; but because the decay is fast, the recovery sleep is only a few hours longer than usual and does not need to "make up" all the sleep lost. The model also explains why a person staying up all night is sleepiest in the early morning (about 4–6 o'clock) and a little more alert by the late morning: $S$ is still rising, but the circadian rhythm raises the upper threshold even faster.

### 46.4.6. Functions of Sleep

Rats deprived of sleep die within 2–3 weeks (Rechtschaffen et al.), with disturbed thermoregulation, a great increase of energy expenditure, and loss of weight before death; patients with fatal familial insomnia have degeneration of the thalamus, cannot sleep, and die within months to years. Why sleep is necessary still has no generally accepted single answer, and the main hypotheses are:

- Clearance of metabolites: in sleep the extracellular space expands by about 60%, the exchange between cerebrospinal fluid along perivascular spaces and interstitial fluid speeds up, and the clearance of metabolites such as β-amyloid increases (Xie et al., 2013; for the concept of the glymphatic system see [7.14.15](/7.14.15)).
- Consolidation of memory: experiences encoded in the hippocampus in wakefulness are "replayed" in the following NREM sleep (place cells fire in sequence in the order of wakefulness but at a faster speed), synchronized with cortical spindles and slow oscillations, promoting the transfer of memory from the hippocampus to the cortex; sleep deprivation impairs new learning.
- Synaptic homeostasis: learning in wakefulness strengthens cortical synapses overall and increases energy consumption and space requirements, and in sleep synapses are generally weakened proportionally, restoring the ability to learn (the hypothesis of Tononi and Cirelli).
- Energy saving and repair: in sleep the metabolism of the brain is lowered, and the expression of many genes related to synthesis and repair is increased.

These functions are not mutually exclusive. Sleep exists in all animals that have been studied carefully (Section 46.8.1), which shows that its function is very basic.

## 46.5. Learning and Memory

### 46.5.1. Forms of Learning

Learning is the process by which the nervous system changes behavior according to experience, and memory is the storage and retrieval of the information acquired. There are two large classes of learning:

- Nonassociative learning: involves only one stimulus. *Habituation* is the gradual weakening of the response to a repeated, harmless stimulus (such as no longer attending to continuous noise in the environment); *sensitization* is the general strengthening of responses to other stimuli after a strong or noxious stimulus.
- Associative learning: establishes a link between two events. Classical conditioning (Pavlov): a conditioned stimulus that is originally neutral (a bell) is repeatedly paired with an unconditioned stimulus that already elicits a response (salivation caused by food), and the conditioned stimulus alone then elicits the response; if thereafter only the conditioned stimulus is given and the unconditioned stimulus no longer, the conditioned response gradually weakens, called extinction. Operant conditioning (Thorndike, Skinner): when a spontaneous action of an animal is followed by a reward (or punishment), the frequency of that action increases (or decreases) afterward. Eyeblink conditioning depends on the cerebellum ([7.14.45](/7.14.45#sec-45.3.6)), fear conditioning depends on the amygdala, and operant learning depends on the basal ganglia and dopamine ([7.14.45](/7.14.45#sec-45.4.4)).

Kandel studied the cellular mechanisms of these simple forms of learning with the gill-withdrawal reflex of the sea hare (*Aplysia*): habituation is a decrease of transmitter release at the synapse from sensory neuron to motor neuron (less $\ce{Ca^2+}$ enters the terminal with each action potential and fewer vesicles are releasable); sensitization is excitation of facilitating interneurons by a strong stimulus to the tail, which release serotonin and, through the cAMP–PKA pathway, close $\mathrm{K^+}$ channels in the sensory neuron terminal, prolong the action potential, and increase transmitter release. Short-term memory (minutes) requires only modification of existing proteins; long-term memory (days) requires PKA to enter the nucleus and, through the transcription factor CREB, to start new gene expression and the growth of new synapses.

### 46.5.2. Classification of Memory

By content, long-term memory is divided into two large classes (Squire):

| Type | Content | Examples | Brain regions depended on |
|---|---|---|---|
| Declarative memory (explicit memory) | facts and events that can be stated in words | episodic memory (yesterday's dinner), semantic memory (Paris is the capital of France) | hippocampus and medial temporal lobe (consolidation), neocortex (storage) |
| Nondeclarative memory (implicit memory) | skills and habits that need no conscious retrieval | procedural memory (riding a bicycle), priming, simple conditioned reflexes, nonassociative learning | striatum (habits and skills), cerebellum (motor conditioning), amygdala (emotional conditioning), reflex pathways |

### 46.5.3. Stages of Memory and the Hippocampus

By the length of retention, the human memory process is roughly divided into: sensory memory (less than 1 s, which disappears if not attended to); short-term memory (seconds to minutes, limited capacity of about $4$–$7$ items or chunks), the part of which used for temporary storage and manipulation in the current task is called working memory and depends on sustained neural activity in prefrontal cortex; and long-term memory (hours to a lifetime, with almost no limit of capacity). The transition from short-term memory to stable long-term memory is called memory consolidation, which requires new protein synthesis: giving a protein synthesis inhibitor, electroconvulsive shock, or cooling the brain soon after training can prevent the formation of long-term memory, while earlier memories are unaffected.

The hippocampus plays a key role in the consolidation of declarative memory, and the most famous evidence comes from the patient H.M. (Henry Molaison). In 1953 he had the medial temporal lobes of both sides (including most of the hippocampus) removed to treat epilepsy; afterward his intelligence, short-term memory, old memories from long before the operation, and the learning of motor skills were normal, but he could hardly form any new declarative memory (anterograde amnesia) and also forgot events of the years before the operation (retrograde amnesia). He could learn mirror drawing by practice but each time did not remember having practiced (Scoville and Milner, 1957). This shows that declarative and nondeclarative memory depend on different systems; that the hippocampus is not the final store of memory but is necessary to consolidate new experience into the neocortex; and that consolidation takes years.

The hippocampus also takes part in spatial memory and navigation: pyramidal cells in the rat hippocampus fire only when the animal is at a particular place in the environment, called place cells (O'Keefe and Dostrovsky, 1971); in the entorhinal cortex there are grid cells whose firing fields form a hexagonal lattice over the environment (the Mosers, 2005). Together they constitute a kind of "cognitive map" in the brain. The posterior hippocampus of London taxi drivers is larger in volume than that of controls and correlates with years of driving (Maguire et al., 2000).

The cellular mechanism of memory that is most favored is **long-term potentiation** ([7.14.5](/7.14.5#sec-5.5.2)): it was first discovered in the hippocampus (Bliss and Lømo, 1973) and has the properties required for memory, input specificity, associativity (coincidence detection by NMDA receptors), and persistence; blocking NMDA receptors or knocking out NMDA receptors in hippocampal area CA1 impairs spatial learning in rats and mice. But the relation between LTP and memory is still correlational and not completely proven: the storage of memory may be distributed over the combination of many synapses and groups of neurons ("engram cells"), and reactivating with optogenetics the dentate gyrus neurons of the hippocampus that were active during learning can make mice show the corresponding fear memory (Liu et al., 2012).

Forgetting is normal: memories that are not consolidated or no longer used gradually fade, and old and new memories also interfere with each other. Ebbinghaus (1885) found that the retention of nonsense syllables falls fastest in the first few hours after learning and then more slowly.

## 46.6. The Limbic System, Emotion, and Motivation

**Definition 46.6.1 (Limbic System)** The group of structures around the diencephalon and corpus callosum, including the cingulate gyrus, parahippocampal gyrus and hippocampus, amygdala, septal area, hypothalamus and mammillary bodies, and anterior thalamic nuclei, that are connected with emotion, motivation, memory, and visceral activity is called the **limbic system**.

The limbic system lies between the cerebral cortex and the hypothalamus: the hypothalamus produces the autonomic and endocrine manifestations of emotion ([7.14.7](/7.14.7#sec-7.4.6)), and the cortex produces the subjective experience of emotion and the evaluation of the situation.

- Hypothalamus: Hess (1920s–1940s) stimulated the hypothalamus of awake cats and elicited complete behavioral responses: stimulating the lateral area caused feeding and attack, stimulating the ventromedial nucleus caused satiety and calm ([7.14.30](/7.14.30)), and stimulating certain sites caused "sham rage": piloerection, pupil dilation, arching of the back, hissing, and a rise of blood pressure, but the response disappeared at once when the stimulation stopped and was not directed at any particular object. Animals in which the cerebral cortex had been removed while the hypothalamus was preserved showed this sham rage to slight stimuli, which shows that the cortex normally inhibits and guides the emotional responses of the hypothalamus.
- Amygdala: it receives sensory and visceral input of all kinds and evaluates the emotional significance of stimuli, especially threat; its central nucleus elicits the autonomic responses of fear (faster heart rate, higher blood pressure, freezing) and the secretion of stress hormones through the hypothalamus and brainstem ([7.14.35](/7.14.35)). Monkeys with the amygdala destroyed lose their fear of dangerous objects such as snakes, explore objects with the mouth, and show inappropriate sexual behavior (Klüver–Bucy syndrome); patient S.M., with calcification of both amygdalae, can hardly feel fear and cannot recognize fearful expressions on other people's faces. Acquisition of fear conditioning depends on synaptic plasticity in the lateral nucleus of the amygdala.
- Reward: Olds and Milner (1954) found that rats with electrodes implanted in the lateral hypothalamus and medial forebrain bundle pressed a lever ceaselessly to stimulate their own brains, thousands of times an hour, preferring not to eat or drink. The core of this "reward system" is the dopamine pathway from the ventral tegmental area of the midbrain to the nucleus accumbens and prefrontal cortex ([7.14.45](/7.14.45#sec-45.4.4)). Addictive drugs (cocaine and amphetamine block or reverse the reuptake of dopamine, and opioids and nicotine increase the firing of dopamine neurons) all increase dopamine in the nucleus accumbens; long-term use produces lasting changes in the plasticity of this pathway and biases motivation toward the drug.

## 46.7. Energy Consumption of the Brain

The adult brain is only about 2% of body weight but consumes about 20% of the oxygen and about 25% of the glucose of the whole body at rest ([7.14.30](/7.14.30)).

**Example 46.7.1** The average blood flow of the human brain is about $50\,\mathrm{mL}\,(100\,\mathrm{g})^{-1}\,\mathrm{min^{-1}}$ and its oxygen consumption about $3.5\,\mathrm{mL}\,(100\,\mathrm{g})^{-1}\,\mathrm{min^{-1}}$. Estimate the blood flow, oxygen consumption, and power of a brain of mass $1.4\,\mathrm{kg}$, and the average power per neuron.

The blood flow is $50\times14 = 700\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}}$, about 14% of the resting cardiac output ($5\,\mathrm{L\,min^{-1}}$); the oxygen consumption is $3.5\times14 \approx 49\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}}$, about 20% of the resting oxygen consumption of the whole body ($250\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}}$). Taking the **energy equivalent of oxygen** as about $20\,\mathrm{kJ\,L^{-1}}$ (oxidation of glucose), the power is about $0.049\,\mathrm{L\,min^{-1}} \times 20\,\mathrm{kJ\,L^{-1}} \approx 0.98\,\mathrm{kJ\,min^{-1}} \approx 16\,\mathrm{W}$, of the same order as the commonly quoted "about 20 W for the brain." Divided among $8.6\times10^{10}$ neurons, the average is about $2\times10^{-10}\,\mathrm{W}$ each; the bulk of this energy goes to the sodium–potassium pump, to restore the $\mathrm{Na^+}$ that enters in action potentials and postsynaptic currents ([7.14.4](/7.14.4)).

The energy budget of the brain has several features:

- It is spent mainly on signaling: in gray matter, more than half of the energy goes to restoring ion gradients after postsynaptic currents and action potentials, and the rest to maintaining the resting potential, the recycling of transmitters, and "housekeeping" functions (Attwell and Laughlin, 2001); when neural activity increases, local blood flow increases (neurovascular coupling), which is the source of the signal of functional magnetic resonance imaging.
- The brain has almost no energy reserve: glycogen is scarce, and it depends entirely on the continuous supply of glucose and oxygen by the blood; consciousness is lost when blood flow is interrupted for about $10\,\mathrm{s}$, and irreversible damage appears after $4$–$5\,\mathrm{min}$. In prolonged starvation the brain can shift more than half of its energy requirement to ketone bodies produced by the liver ([7.14.29](/7.14.29)).
- Energy limits coding: the brain could not afford it if all neurons fired frequently; by the power estimate above, the mean firing rate of cortical neurons can be only of the order of about 1 per second, which makes sparse coding (only a few neurons active at any moment) inevitable.
- Relation to development and evolution: the brain of an infant uses about half of the resting metabolism of the whole body, and in childhood (about age 5) the glucose consumption of the brain peaks, when growth of the body is slowest (Kuzawa et al., 2014). Compared with the energy estimates of replacing the hormone system by a neural network (Examples 33.1.1 and 33.1.2), a brain of about $1.3\,\mathrm{L}$ already takes up one fifth of the resting metabolism, which shows that neural tissue is one of the most expensive tissues of the organism and is one of the reasons why brain size is constrained in evolution (Section 46.8.2).

## 46.8. Evolution and Comparative Aspects

### 46.8.1. Ubiquity and Diversity of Sleep

The behavioral definition of sleep can be applied to animals without cortical EEG: a spontaneous, reversible state of quiescence with a characteristic posture and place, with an elevated threshold of response to stimuli, controlled by homeostatic regulation (compensatory increase after deprivation) and by the circadian rhythm. By this definition sleep is almost everywhere:

- *Drosophila* has a sleep-like state, with rebound after deprivation, and caffeine reduces and antihistamines increase its sleep (Shaw et al., 2000; Hendricks et al., 2000); many genes that affect sleep (such as *minisleep*, the mutant of the potassium channel *Shaker*) were first discovered in *Drosophila*. The nematode *Caenorhabditis elegans* has a sleep-like period called "lethargus" before each molt.
- Cnidarians, which have no central nervous system, also sleep: the upside-down jellyfish (*Cassiopea*) pulses its bell less at night and responds more slowly to stimuli, and after being disturbed at night pulses less the next day (Nath et al., 2017); *Hydra* has a similar state. This shows that sleep existed before the centralization of the nervous system.
- A stage similar to REM sleep is clearly found in mammals and birds, and alternating sleep states were also recorded in the bearded dragon (a reptile; Shein-Idelson et al., 2016); in the "active sleep" of the octopus, body color and the movements of the eyes and arms change rapidly (Pophale et al., 2023). Whether REM-like sleep arose only once in amniotes is still disputed.

**Definition 46.8.1 (Unihemispheric Sleep)** The mode of sleep in which one cerebral hemisphere is in slow-wave sleep while the other remains awake is called **unihemispheric sleep**; the eye corresponding to the awake side (the contralateral eye) remains open.

Cetaceans (bottlenose dolphins, belugas) have almost only unihemispheric slow-wave sleep, with the two hemispheres sleeping in turn and never in slow-wave sleep on both sides at once, so that they can still swim to the surface to breathe and remain mobile and vigilant while sleeping; REM sleep in dolphins is scanty or inconspicuous. Fur seals sleep mainly with both hemispheres together on land but change to unihemispheric sleep at sea, with REM sleep almost disappearing. Birds can also sleep unihemispherically: in a row of roosting mallards, the individuals at the two ends use more unihemispheric sleep, keeping the outward-facing eye open (Rattenborg et al., 1999); frigatebirds continue to sleep during flights at sea lasting weeks, sleeping only about $40\,\mathrm{min}$ a day, mostly unihemispherically and mainly with the eye open that faces the direction of flight (Rattenborg et al., 2016).

Sleep time differs greatly among mammals: about 20 h a day in the big brown bat, about 12 h in cats, about 8 h in humans, and only 2–4 h in giraffes and African elephants (wild African elephants sometimes go 46 h without sleep). In general, species that are large, herbivorous, and easily preyed upon in open country sleep little, and species that are small, have high metabolic rates, and have safe nests sleep more; the relation of this difference to ecology (foraging time and predation risk) may be greater than its relation to the physiological needs of the brain.

### 46.8.2. Brain Size and the Encephalization Quotient

In vertebrates brain mass $E$ grows with body mass $P$ as a power law $E = kP^{\alpha}$, a straight line on logarithmic axes (**allometry**; for the regression methods of allometric scaling see [3.3](/3.3)). The lines for different classes are nearly parallel and offset (figure): at the same body mass, the brains of mammals and birds are about 10 times those of reptiles and fishes, that is, two independent episodes of "encephalization." The exponents within classes lie between $0.5$ and $0.75$: fitted across species within classes, about 0.7 for mammals (close to the $3/4$ of the **Kleiber's law** for metabolic rate, [7.14.30](/7.14.30)) and about 0.5 for birds, reptiles, and fishes; between individuals of the same species or between very closely related species the exponent is lower (0.2–0.3), so large dogs do not have relatively larger brains than small dogs.

**Definition 46.8.2 (Encephalization Quotient)** The ratio of the actual brain mass of a species to the brain mass expected for an animal of the same body mass according to the brain–body allometric relation of its group (usually mammals) is called the **encephalization quotient** (EQ):

$$
\mathrm{EQ} = \frac{E}{0.12\,P^{2/3}} \tag{46.2}
$$
where $E$ and $P$ are both in grams and $0.12\,P^{2/3}$ is the expected brain mass of mammals given by Jerison (1973).

The EQ of humans is about 7 ($E = 1350\,\mathrm{g}$, $P = 65\,\mathrm{kg}$), that is, the brain is about 7 times that of an "average mammal" of the same size; it is about 4–5 for the bottlenose dolphin, about 2.5 for the chimpanzee, about 1 for cats and dogs, and about 0.4–0.5 for rats and mice. The specific value of the EQ depends on the exponent used (about 5 for humans if 0.75 is used) and on the reference group, and it is suitable only for rough comparisons.

Brain mass is only a rough index. Counting with the isotropic fractionator, Herculano-Houzel et al. found that the relation between neuron number and brain mass differs among groups: in rodents, neurons become larger and density falls as the cortex enlarges, and the number of cortical neurons grows roughly as the 0.6 power of cortical mass; in primates, neuron size hardly changes as the cortex enlarges, and the number of neurons is almost proportional to mass. By the rodent rule, a brain with $8.6\times10^{10}$ neurons would weigh about $35\,\mathrm{kg}$; the human brain is in terms of neuron number just a "scaled-up primate brain" and is not special, and what is special is the scaling rule of primates itself (Herculano-Houzel, 2009). The enlargement of the brain has a cost: the brain is the most expensive tissue (Section 46.7), and in primates the increase of relative brain size has been accompanied by shortening of the digestive tract, cooked food and food of higher quality, and a longer period of development.

### 46.8.3. Avian Homologs of the Cerebral Cortex

The avian telencephalon has no six-layered neocortex; most of it is cerebral cortex (pallium) arranged in nuclei, including the dorsal hyperpallium (the Wulst) and the ventral dorsal ventricular ridge (DVR, containing the nidopallium and mesopallium). These structures were formerly mistaken for striatum (hence old names such as "hyperstriatum"), and the 2005 avian brain nomenclature meeting renamed them as pallium on the basis of gene expression and connections (Jarvis et al., 2005). The homology of the DVR with the mammalian neocortex is still disputed: one view holds that the area of the DVR that receives thalamic sensory input is homologous in cell types with the layer IV neurons of the neocortex, only with the arrangement changed from layers to nuclei; another holds that the DVR is closer to the claustrum and amygdala of mammals. Comparison of single-cell transcriptomes shows that the neuron types of the avian and mammalian cortex partly correspond, but that the two do not entirely coincide in the developmental regions of the cerebral cortex.

Whether homologous or not, corvids and parrots perform in many cognitive tasks on a par with apes: the New Caledonian crow can make and use hooked tools and can complete multistep tool tasks in sequence; the western scrub jay remembers "what, where, and when" when caching food, retrieves perishable food first, and moves its caches when seen by other birds, showing an ability like episodic memory (Clayton and Dickinson, 1998); the result of the magpie in the mirror test was reported to be positive, but later replication studies failed to confirm it. The density of neurons in the telencephalon of corvids and parrots is very high ([7.14.7](/7.14.7#sec-7.5)), and a brain of the same weight contains even more neurons than that of a primate; the caudolateral nidopallium (NCL) of corvids is functionally equivalent to prefrontal cortex, and its neurons show sustained firing in working memory tasks similar to that of the primate prefrontal cortex. This shows that complex cognition can be realized by telencephala organized differently, and what evolved convergently is the computational ability, not the six-layered structure of the cortex.

### 46.8.4. Learning in Cephalopods

The octopus is one of the groups with the strongest learning ability among invertebrates: it can learn to run mazes, discriminate objects of different shapes and colors, and open screw-top jars to take out prey, and it has individual "personality" differences. The octopus brain has a structure specialized for learning and memory, the vertical lobe, whose organization resembles that of the mammalian hippocampus and the insect mushroom body: about $2.5\times10^7$ small amacrine cells receive parallel fibers from about $1.8\times10^6$ input neurons (fan-out followed by convergence), and the synapses of the vertical lobe have activity-dependent long-term potentiation (Hochner et al., 2003); unlike in mammals, this LTP is expressed mainly presynaptically and does not depend on NMDA receptors. Octopuses with the vertical lobe destroyed can still learn, but long-term memory is impaired. The octopus can also learn to choose objects of a certain color by observing the choices of other individuals (Fiorito and Scotto, 1992); the result of this observational learning was later disputed, but it suggests that similar learning circuits evolved independently in animals very distantly related to vertebrates.

The comparison across the chapters on the nervous system can be summarized as follows: the basic building blocks (ion channels, synapses, plasticity, circuit motifs) are highly conserved among animals, while the ways in which they are assembled (centralized or distributed, layered or in nuclei, by segments or by function) differ among groups; functional convergence (cognition in birds and mammals, learning in octopuses and vertebrates, unihemispheric sleep in cetaceans and birds) is more common than structural homology.

## Questions

1. After a head injury, a person speaks fluently at a normal rate, but the content is empty and the words are wrong; he cannot understand what others say but is unaware of this himself. Another person can understand what others say but speaks with effort and haltingly, saying only a few key nouns. Infer the site of the lesion in each, and explain why the first person is unaware of his own errors.

   **Answer** The first person has Wernicke aphasia, with the lesion in Wernicke's area in the posterior superior temporal gyrus of the dominant hemisphere; the second has Broca aphasia, with the lesion in Broca's area in the posterior inferior frontal gyrus of the dominant hemisphere. Wernicke's area is responsible for comprehension of language, and its damage makes the patient unable not only to understand what others say but also to monitor and understand what he says himself, so he cannot notice his own errors; patients with Broca aphasia, in contrast, comprehend essentially normally, know clearly that they cannot speak, and are often frustrated by it.

2. Use the reasoning of Example 46.3.1 to explain: why is the EEG amplitude in deep sleep larger than in wakefulness, although the total activity of cortical neurons is greater in wakefulness? Why is the amplitude of the EEG especially large during an epileptic seizure?

   **Answer** The scalp EEG reflects the summation of the current dipoles of the synaptic currents of large numbers of pyramidal cells. If the activity of $N$ cells is independent with random phases, the summed amplitude grows only as $\sqrt{N}$; if it is synchronous, it grows as $N$. In wakefulness, the cortical areas process different information, and the activity of neurons is decorrelated; although the total activity is greater, the summed signal is small (low-amplitude fast waves). In deep sleep, the thalamocortical circuit makes large numbers of neurons switch synchronously between up and down states at the same slow rhythm, and even though the average activity of each cell is less, the summed amplitude is much larger (high-amplitude slow waves). In an epileptic seizure, neurons over a large area of cortex discharge abnormally in synchrony, the summed amplitude is larger still, and high-amplitude spikes and spike-and-slow-wave complexes appear.

3. Use the two-process model (Eq. (46.1)) to explain: (a) why shift workers cannot sleep long when they sleep during the day; (b) why people flying from China to the eastern United States (the clock needs to be delayed by about 12 h) cannot sleep at night and are drowsy in the day during the first days after arrival.

   **Answer** (a) Sleep onset and waking depend on the sleep pressure $S$ and on thresholds that fluctuate with the circadian rhythm. When one goes to sleep in the early morning after a night shift, $S$ is high and sleep comes easily at first; but after a few hours, as morning and noon come, the circadian rhythm raises the wake threshold $L$ (together with $H$), and although $S$ has not yet fallen to its nighttime level it already meets the raised $L$, so one wakes and sleep time is shortened. (b) After arrival, the rhythm of the suprachiasmatic nucleus still runs on the time of the departure place. The local evening corresponds to the original morning, when the circadian rhythm makes the sleep-onset threshold very high, so it is hard to fall asleep even though $S$ has risen; the local daytime corresponds to the original deep night, when the thresholds are lowered and $S$ is again high, so one is drowsy. The suprachiasmatic nucleus can be adjusted by light by only about 1 h a day, so many days are needed for full adaptation; exposure to light in the local morning can speed the adjustment.

4. After removal of the medial temporal lobes on both sides, patient H.M. could learn mirror drawing but not remember having learned it, and could recall childhood events but could not remember the doctor he had just met. What properties of memory do these phenomena show?

   **Answer** (1) Memory is not a single system: declarative memory depends on the hippocampus and medial temporal lobe, whereas motor skills (procedural memory, a kind of nondeclarative memory) depend on the striatum, cerebellum, and motor cortex, which were intact in H.M., so skills could be learned while the fact of "having learned" (episodic memory) could not be formed. (2) The hippocampus is not necessary for short-term memory and working memory, and H.M. could briefly remember information as long as his attention was not interrupted. (3) The hippocampus is necessary for the consolidation of new declarative memories but is not the place of long-term storage: memories from long ago have been consolidated in the neocortex, so childhood events could be recalled; the partial loss of memories from the years before the operation shows that consolidation takes years, during which it still depends on the hippocampus.

5. (Open question) Dolphins never let both hemispheres enter slow-wave sleep at the same time, frigatebirds can sleep in flight, and yet almost all animals must sleep. What do these facts imply for the various hypotheses about the function of sleep?

   **Answer** Key points: sleep is universal among animals and has not been eliminated by evolution even though it brings the risk of predation or drowning, which shows that it has basic functions that cannot be replaced by resting while awake. Unihemispheric sleep shows that the function of slow-wave sleep can be accomplished locally in one hemisphere, which fits the view that "sleep is a local process regulated by the degree of use": for example, the synaptic homeostasis hypothesis and the metabolite clearance hypothesis both predict that it is the cortical areas that have been used that need sleep, and the whole brain need not go offline at once. On the other hand, frigatebirds sleep only about 40 min a day in flight and sleep much more after returning to land, which shows that the need for sleep can be compressed temporarily under ecological pressure but cannot be abolished and remains subject to homeostatic regulation. Animals without a centralized nervous system, such as jellyfish, also sleep, which suggests that the original function of sleep may have been at the cellular level (energy, repair, clearance), and functions such as memory consolidation were added later in animals with complex brains. A reasonable conclusion is that sleep has multiple functions, and the weight of each function differs in different groups.
