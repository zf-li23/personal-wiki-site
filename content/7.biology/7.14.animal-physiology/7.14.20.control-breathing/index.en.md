Breathing is among the earliest physiological activities to appear and the last to cease; we are scarcely aware of it normally, yet it becomes extremely distressing when it fails. The control of breathing has two tasks: to generate a continuous and robust rhythm that never stops from the first breath at birth to the last moment of life, and to make ventilation follow changes in metabolism so as to keep the $P_{\ce{O2}}$, $P_{\ce{CO2}}$ and pH of arterial blood within narrow limits (**homeostasis**). This chapter first gives the framework of the respiratory control system (Section 20.1), then describes the respiratory centers in the brainstem (Section 20.2) and how their rhythm is generated (Section 20.3), which draws on the **central pattern generator** and the **half-center oscillator** of Chapter 6. It then covers the chemoreceptors that tell the centers about metabolic demand (Section 20.4) and the mechanoreceptors in the lungs and airways (Section 20.5). Section 20.6 discusses several integrated situations: exercise, sleep and periodic breathing, and altitude. The last section compares respiratory control in different animals and gives a comparative summary of the respiratory system of Chapters 17–20 (Section 20.7). The mechanics of the respiratory muscles and ventilation are in Chapter 17, the gas transport sensed by the chemoreceptors in Chapter 19, and the respiratory regulation of acid–base balance in Chapter 25.

## 20.1. The Framework of Respiratory Control

Like other homeostatic systems, the control of breathing consists of four parts:

| Component | In respiratory control |
|---|---|
| Controlled variable | Arterial $P_{\ce{CO2}}$ (and the pH linked to it) mainly, $P_{\ce{O2}}$ secondarily |
| Sensors | Peripheral chemoreceptors (carotid and aortic bodies), central chemoreceptors (medulla); pulmonary stretch receptors, irritant receptors, J receptors; proprioceptors in muscles and joints |
| Controller | Rhythm generator and pattern-forming network of the medulla, modulated by the pons; hypothalamus, limbic system and cerebral cortex impose emotional and voluntary control on it |
| Effectors | Respiratory muscles such as the diaphragm, intercostals and abdominal muscles; muscles of the pharynx, larynx and tongue (keeping the upper airway open) |

The rhythm generator of the brainstem is an "automatic timer": with no driving input it can stop (apnea), and the strength of the drive determines the frequency and depth of breathing. The most important drive comes from the chemoreceptors, and the most important controlled variables are $\ce{O2}$, $\ce{CO2}$ and pH; the vagus nerve also brings information about lung inflation and foreign bodies in the airways; and higher brain regions temporarily take over the respiratory muscles during speaking, singing, breath-holding, swallowing and vomiting.

## 20.2. The Respiratory Centers of the Brainstem

### 20.2.1. Respiratory Neuron Groups of the Medulla

Neurons that fire most strongly during inspiration or expiration are called respiration-related neurons. They are concentrated in two bilaterally symmetric regions of the medulla:

- The dorsal respiratory group (DRG) lies in the ventrolateral part of the nucleus of the solitary tract (NTS) in the dorsomedial medulla. The NTS receives most of the respiration-related sensory signals carried by the glossopharyngeal and vagus nerves (from chemoreceptors, pulmonary stretch receptors and others). The DRG consists of interneurons of the NTS and premotor neurons projecting to the phrenic motoneurons of the spinal cord and to the VRG; it contains mainly inspiratory neurons and takes part in the integration of sensory information.
- The ventral respiratory group (VRG) is a columnar region arranged longitudinally along the nucleus ambiguus and retroambigual nucleus, divided from rostral to caudal into several parts: the Bötzinger complex at the most rostral end (mainly inhibitory expiratory neurons), the pre-Bötzinger complex caudal to it (the core of rhythm generation, Section 20.3), the rostral VRG in the middle (inspiratory premotor neurons projecting to inspiratory motoneurons in the spinal cord; the motoneurons in the nucleus ambiguus innervate the muscles of the pharynx and larynx and widen the upper airway during inspiration), and the caudal VRG (expiratory premotor neurons driving the internal intercostal and abdominal muscles).

Not all respiration-related neurons regulate breathing: afferents from pulmonary stretch receptors also modulate cardiovascular activity, and the respiratory rhythm modulates the discharge of sympathetic nerves (Chapter 16). Conversely, neurons that regulate breathing are not necessarily rhythmic: chemosensitive neurons change the excitability of the respiratory centers by a sustained, nonrhythmic discharge.

### 20.2.2. Modulation by the Pons

The medial parabrachial nucleus and the Kölliker–Fuse nucleus in the dorsolateral pons are together called the pontine respiratory group, formerly the pneumotaxic center: they limit the duration of inspiration, promote the transition from inspiration to expiration, and coordinate breathing with actions such as swallowing and vocalization. The lower pons is also thought to contain an apneustic center, without a clearly defined nucleus but diffusely distributed, which excites inspiration.

This division comes from the classical transection experiments of Lumsden (1923) in the cat:

| Procedure | Effect on breathing |
|---|---|
| Transection at the upper end of the pons (removing structures above the midbrain) | Pattern of quiet breathing essentially unchanged |
| Transection at the middle of the pons | Small effect: tidal volume increased, respiratory rate decreased |
| Bilateral vagotomy | Similar to midpontine transection but milder (afferents of the lung stretch reflex interrupted, Section 20.5.1) |
| Midpontine transection + bilateral vagotomy | Apneustic breathing: inspiration is prolonged, with only occasional brief expirations |
| Transection between medulla and pons | Apneustic breathing disappears, irregular gasping-like breathing appears |
| Transection between medulla and spinal cord | Breathing stops |

From this Lumsden proposed a three-level hypothesis: the pneumotaxic center of the rostral pons prevents apneusis and maintains the normal rhythm, the apneustic center of the caudal pons promotes prolonged inspiration, and the medulla generates the basic rhythm. The experiments show that the two signals that "switch off" inspiration (modulation from the pons and the pulmonary stretch afferents of the vagus) are redundant: with either removed, breathing remains roughly normal; with both removed, inspiration cannot be terminated. Modern work retains the conclusion that the medulla generates the rhythm and the pons modulates the pattern, but the status of the apneustic center as an independent structure is doubtful.

## 20.3. Generation of the Respiratory Rhythm

### 20.3.1. The Pre-Bötzinger Complex

**Definition 20.3.1 (Pre-Bötzinger Complex)** A small group of neurons in the ventrolateral medulla, in the VRG immediately caudal to the Bötzinger complex, is called the **pre-Bötzinger complex** (preBötC). It is the core generator of the inspiratory rhythm in mammals.

The decisive evidence came from the in vitro experiments of Smith et al. (1991): a transverse slice about $0.5\,\mathrm{mm}$ thick containing the preBötC, cut from the medulla of a neonatal rat, continues to generate rhythmic discharges in the perfusion fluid and drives rhythmic output in the hypoglossal nerve root retained in the slice, in effect "inspiration in a culture dish". Experiments removing the brainstem layer by layer showed that the rhythm exists only when this region is retained, and destroying it in an isolated brainstem abolishes respiratory output. In adult animals, selectively killing the NK1 receptor-expressing neurons in the preBötC with a toxin first causes disordered breathing in sleep and then severe ataxic breathing even when awake (Gray et al., 2001). The excitatory neurons of the preBötC are mostly glutamatergic and derive from progenitors expressing Dbx1.

### 20.3.2. The Pacemaker Mechanism and the Network Mechanism

There are two basic explanations of how the rhythm is generated, corresponding to the two kinds of central pattern generators described in Section 6.4.2.

The pacemaker mechanism: some preBötC neurons have intrinsic rhythmicity, like the pacemaker cells of the sinoatrial node (Chapter 13), and can fire periodic bursts even without synaptic input. A persistent $\ce{Na+}$ current and a $\ce{Ca^2+}$-activated nonselective cation current gradually depolarize the membrane and trigger a burst of action potentials; outward currents terminate the burst. Between bursts, the transient outward A-type $\ce{K+}$ current opposes depolarization: each time the cell tries to depolarize, the A-type current is activated and pulls the membrane potential back, delaying the next burst. The larger the A-type current, the longer the interburst interval; the smaller, the shorter. After the pacemaker current is eliminated with blockers, isolated slices can still generate a rhythm under certain conditions, so pacemaker neurons may not be essential but may make the rhythm more robust.

The network mechanism: neurons that fire in different phases of the respiratory cycle are connected to one another and inhibit one another. Early-inspiratory neurons, excited at the beginning of inspiration, inhibit another group of neurons; late-inspiratory (postinspiratory) neurons, excited as inspiration is about to end, in turn inhibit the former; the two alternate, forming the transition between inspiration and expiration. This is exactly the structure of the **half-center oscillator** (Definition 6.4.3): reciprocal inhibition, a sustained drive (input from chemoreceptors), and a slow process that gradually makes the active side lose its advantage (adaptation, synaptic fatigue, postinhibitory rebound). By Formula 6.4.1, without slow adaptation reciprocal inhibition produces only a "winner-take-all" steady state; with sufficient adaptation an alternating rhythm appears, whose period is determined by the time constant of adaptation and the strength of the drive, consistent with the change of respiratory rate with chemical drive. Besides two reciprocally inhibiting groups of neurons, the rhythm can also arise from a network with excitatory recurrent connections and slow negative feedback (a generalization of the afterdischarge model of Section 6.2.1): recurrent connections among excitatory neurons in the preBötC are gradually recruited and synchronized in each cycle to form a burst ("group pacemaker") and are then terminated by synaptic depression and adaptation.

Both mechanisms contribute to rhythm generation and are not mutually exclusive. The currently prevailing view is that the inspiratory rhythm is generated mainly by a recurrent network of excitatory neurons in the preBötC, that pacemaker currents and inhibitory connections regulate its frequency and phase, and that inhibitory connections in the Bötzinger complex and elsewhere shape the output of one "inspiratory oscillator" into three phases: inspiration, postinspiration and expiration.

### 20.3.3. Single-Site and Multiple-Site Models

Besides the preBötC, the ventral medulla has other rhythmic regions, the most important being the parafacial respiratory group (pFRG) near the facial nucleus (overlapping the retrotrapezoid nucleus of Section 20.4.2): it generates an expiratory rhythm when active expiration is needed, as in exercise and hypercapnia, and is coupled with the inspiratory rhythm of the preBötC. There are four classes of models for the site of initial rhythm generation:

1. Switching of the CPG: there are several potential respiratory CPGs, and the site that dominates the rhythm can switch under different physiological or behavioral conditions (for example from the preBötC to the pFRG).
2. Redundancy and backup: that several regions are rhythmic is a sign of redundancy in the system, ensuring that breathing is not interrupted by failure of a single region, just as many cells in the sinoatrial node can fire first and other sites can take over when the node fails. In models of this type, some neurons with intrinsic oscillatory properties dominate the rhythm only under abnormal conditions (hypoxia, anesthesia, early development), producing abnormal breathing patterns such as gasping.
3. A single CPG with reinforcement: quiet breathing has only one CPG (the preBötC), and other regions can only reinforce the robustness of the rhythm without generating it independently.
4. Emergence: no single brain region is sufficient to produce all the features of quiet breathing; the normal rhythm requires several brainstem regions connected in a specific way and acting together, each necessary but not sufficient.

These models are not entirely exclusive: the preBötC is necessary for the inspiratory rhythm (single CPG), the pFRG joins when needed (condition-dependent switching), and the full phase structure of normal breathing requires the joint action of a pontomedullary network (emergence). Gasping in hypoxia is a "backup" rhythm sustained by the pacemaker mechanism of the preBötC: the inhibitory network fails first in hypoxia, and the remaining pacemaker neurons produce short inspiratory bursts, which helps autoresuscitation. Neuromodulators (for example, thyrotropin-releasing hormone can induce rhythmic discharge in NTS neurons, and opioids depress the preBötC and cause respiratory depression) change breathing by altering the relative strength of these mechanisms.

## 20.4. Chemoreceptors

### 20.4.1. Peripheral Chemoreceptors

**Definition 20.4.1 (Peripheral Chemoreceptors)** The receptors in the carotid body at the bifurcation of the common carotid artery and in the aortic bodies near the aortic arch that sense the arterial $P_{\ce{O2}}$, $P_{\ce{CO2}}$ and $\ce{H+}$ concentration and reflexly alter ventilation are called **peripheral chemoreceptors**. Afferents of the carotid body travel in the sinus nerve (a branch of the glossopharyngeal nerve) and those of the aortic bodies in the vagus nerve to the NTS.

The carotid body is the most important peripheral chemoreceptor (the aortic bodies play only a small role in humans). It is only the size of a grain of rice (about $2\,\mathrm{mg}$), yet has several unique properties:

- Its blood flow is extremely high, about $2\,\mathrm{L\,min^{-1}}$ per 100 g by weight, about 40 times that of the brain; its metabolic rate is high, but the blood flow is higher still, so the arteriovenous difference in $P_{\ce{O2}}$ is very small, and the tissue $P_{\ce{O2}}$ reflects arterial blood promptly and accurately. The capillary endothelium is fenestrated (as in the glomerulus), which favors exchange of substances.
- The sensing cells are type I cells (glomus cells), which are chemosensitive and have the properties of neurons: they can generate action potentials and release transmitters (ATP, ACh, dopamine and others). Type II cells (sustentacular cells) resemble glia and enwrap the glomus cells. Sensory endings of the sinus nerve form synapses with the glomus cells. Sympathetic nerves innervate the vessels and glomus cells of the carotid body, and parasympathetic fibers also innervate its vessels.
- It senses the $P_{\ce{O2}}$ of arterial blood, not the oxygen content. Therefore anemia and CO poisoning (oxygen content reduced but $P_{\ce{O2}}$ normal) hardly stimulate it (Section 19.3.4); but when carotid body blood flow falls in severe hypotension, the local $P_{\ce{O2}}$ falls and it is also excited.

The pathway by which glomus cells sense hypoxia is as follows: hypoxia (through signals from mitochondrial complexes I and IV, local AMP kinase and gaseous signaling molecules such as hydrogen sulfide and carbon monoxide; the details are still being studied) inhibits $\ce{K+}$ channels in the glomus cell membrane (TASK-type background $\ce{K+}$ channels and $\mathrm{BK}$ channels), the cell depolarizes, voltage-gated $\ce{Ca^2+}$ channels open, and $\ce{Ca^2+}$ entry triggers transmitter release; the main transmitter, ATP, acts on $\mathrm{P2X_{2/3}}$ receptors on the afferent ending and generates afferent action potentials. High $\ce{CO2}$ and acid also excite glomus cells through the same final common pathway ($\ce{H+}$ inhibits TASK channels).

The carotid body responds rapidly (within seconds), is the only chemoreceptor that senses hypoxia, and is the main site that responds to rapid changes in arterial $\ce{H+}$ (as in metabolic acidosis). Its responses to hypoxia and to $\ce{CO2}$ are multiplicative: increased $\ce{CO2}$ and decreased pH make the carotid body respond more strongly to hypoxia, and decreased pH also makes it respond more strongly to high $\ce{CO2}$. People whose carotid bodies have been removed bilaterally (for example as an old treatment for asthma) breathe essentially normally at rest but completely lose the hypoxic ventilatory response.

### 20.4.2. Central Chemoreceptors

**Definition 20.4.2 (Central Chemoreceptors)** The receptors in the medulla (mainly near the ventrolateral surface) that sense changes in the $\ce{H+}$ concentration of the local extracellular fluid and reflexly alter ventilation are called **central chemoreceptors**.

Central chemoreceptors do not sense the $\ce{H+}$ in arterial blood directly, because the **blood–brain barrier** (Definition 7.2.1) has a very low permeability to $\ce{H+}$ and $\ce{HCO3-}$ but lets $\ce{CO2}$ pass freely. When arterial $P_{\ce{CO2}}$ rises, $\ce{CO2}$ enters the cerebrospinal fluid and brain interstitial fluid and is hydrated to produce $\ce{H+}$; cerebrospinal fluid contains little protein and has a weak buffering capacity, so the same change in $P_{\ce{CO2}}$ changes its pH more than that of blood. Hence:

- The effective stimulus of central chemoreceptors is the rise in local $\ce{H+}$ caused by $\ce{CO2}$. They are sensitive to changes in the pH of arterial blood caused by $\ce{CO2}$ and hardly sensitive to metabolic changes of blood pH (not accompanied by a change in $\ce{CO2}$); but they are sensitive to any change in the pH of cerebrospinal fluid.
- The response is slow (minutes), because $\ce{CO2}$ has to diffuse into the brain and change the local pH; depth of breathing increases first, then frequency.
- They account for about 2/3 of the ventilatory response to $\ce{CO2}$, and peripheral chemoreceptors for about 1/3.

Central chemoreceptors are distributed over several regions of the ventrolateral medulla, the retrotrapezoid nucleus (RTN) being the most important: its glutamatergic neurons express the transcription factor Phox2b, sense acidification through the pH-sensitive TASK-2 channel and the proton receptor GPR4, and project directly to the respiratory centers (Guyenet et al.); mutations of *PHOX2B* cause congenital central hypoventilation syndrome, in which affected children breathe adequately when awake but have almost no response to $\ce{CO2}$ in sleep. Chemosensitive neurons also exist outside the ventrolateral medulla, for example serotonergic neurons of the medullary raphe nuclei that lie near blood vessels and are activated by acidification; together with GABAergic neurons they form a network of reciprocal control that innervates respiration-related motoneurons. Astrocytes release ATP on acidification and also take part in chemoreception.

### 20.4.3. Actions of $\ce{CO2}$, $\ce{H+}$ and $\ce{O2}$ and Their Interaction

$\ce{CO2}$ is the most important physiological stimulus of breathing. A certain level of $P_{\ce{CO2}}$ is necessary to maintain the basic activity of the respiratory centers: when hyperventilation lowers $P_{\mathrm{aCO_2}}$ below a threshold (the apneic threshold, about 3–5 mmHg below the resting value), voluntary breathing stops until $\ce{CO2}$ accumulates again. Over the physiological range, ventilation increases approximately linearly with $P_{\mathrm{aCO_2}}$, by about $1$–$3\,\mathrm{L\,min^{-1}}$ per mmHg at rest; when inspired $\ce{CO2}$ exceeds about 7%–10%, the excessive $\ce{CO2}$ instead depresses the central nervous system ($\ce{CO2}$ narcosis).

The direct action of hypoxia on breathing is depression: severe hypoxia depresses brainstem neurons. The increase in ventilation caused by hypoxia is achieved entirely through the peripheral chemoreceptors, which counteract the direct central depression. Normally $P_{\mathrm{aO_2}}$ must fall below about $60\,\mathrm{mmHg}$ before ventilation increases appreciably: this is exactly where the oxygen dissociation curve begins to steepen and the arterial oxygen content begins to fall markedly (Section 19.3.2), so the "alarm" for hypoxia is set just where an alarm is needed.

The three stimuli often change together, and their interaction can be summarized by an empirical model (Lloyd and Cunningham, 1963):

**Formula 20.4.1 (Ventilatory Model of the Chemoreflex)**

$$
\dot V_{\mathrm{E}} = S_0\left(1 + \frac{A}{P_{\mathrm{aO_2}} - 32}\right)\left(P_{\mathrm{aCO_2}} - B\right),\qquad \dot V_{\mathrm{E}} \ge 0,
\tag{20.1}
$$

where $B$ is the $P_{\ce{CO2}}$ at which the $\ce{CO2}$ response line extrapolates to zero ventilation (the apneic threshold, determined mainly by the central chemoreceptors), $S_0(1 + A/(P_{\mathrm{aO_2}} - 32))$ is the slope of the $\ce{CO2}$ response (the lower $P_{\mathrm{aO_2}}$, the greater the slope), $A$ measures the sensitivity of the carotid body to hypoxia, and 32 mmHg is the asymptote of the hyperbola.

## 20.5. Receptors of the Lungs and Airways

### 20.5.1. The Lung Stretch Reflex

**Definition 20.5.1 (Hering–Breuer Reflex)** The reflex that inhibits inspiration and promotes the switch from inspiration to expiration when the lung inflates (the inflation reflex), together with the reflex that evokes inspiration when the lung collapses (the deflation reflex), is called the lung stretch reflex or **Hering–Breuer reflex** (Hering and Breuer, 1868). The receptors of the inflation reflex are slowly adapting stretch receptors in airway smooth muscle, and the afferent fibers run in the vagus nerve.

Slowly adapting stretch receptors have a low threshold and adapt slowly; they are sensors that measure lung volume and provide negative feedback on tidal volume: when the lung is inflated to a certain extent they terminate inspiration and prevent overinflation, and they hasten the alternation of inspiration and expiration, increasing the respiratory rate and reducing tidal volume. Animals with bilateral vagotomy breathe more deeply and slowly. The strength of this reflex varies among species: it is obvious in rabbits and cats; in adult humans it operates only when tidal volume exceeds about 1–1.5 L (about three times the quiet tidal volume), so it plays little role in quiet breathing and takes part in regulation only in exercise; it is stronger in newborns. The deflation reflex appears only when the lung is excessively reduced in size (as in pneumothorax and atelectasis) and evokes inspiration.

### 20.5.2. Irritant Receptors and J Receptors

- Rapidly adapting receptors (irritant receptors) lie between airway epithelial cells, adapt rapidly, and are sensitive to both mechanical and chemical stimuli (smoke, ammonia, histamine, bradykinin and other inflammatory mediators); they detect an "abnormal or harmful environment" and evoke coughing, bronchoconstriction, mucus secretion and deep inspiration (the sigh). Sighs occur spontaneously every few minutes and reopen collapsed alveoli; they are generated by neurons in the preBötC that express a particular neuropeptide receptor (Li et al., 2016).
- C-fiber receptors (juxtacapillary receptors, J receptors) lie in the alveolar wall immediately next to pulmonary capillaries and send their afferents in unmyelinated C fibers; they are stimulated by mechanical changes in the pulmonary interstitium (interstitial swelling in pulmonary congestion and edema) and by chemical substances, are excited mainly in pathological states, and evoke apnea or rapid shallow breathing, bronchoconstriction and mucus secretion. The tachypnea of patients with left heart failure and pulmonary edema is partly caused by them.

### 20.5.3. Defensive Reflexes and Other Afferents

Coughing is the most important defensive respiratory reflex: irritant receptors in the mucosa of the larynx, trachea and bronchi send afferents through the vagus nerve and evoke a short deep inspiration, after which the glottis closes tightly and the expiratory muscles contract forcefully, so that alveolar and intrapleural pressure rise abruptly (to more than $100\,\mathrm{mmHg}$); the glottis then opens suddenly and gas rushes out at very high speed, carrying away foreign bodies and secretions. During violent coughing the raised intrapleural pressure impedes venous return. Sneezing is evoked by stimulation of the nasal mucosa with afferents in the trigeminal nerve; the uvula descends, the base of the tongue rises, and the airflow is expelled mainly through the nose.

Muscle spindles and tendon organs in the respiratory muscles constitute a proprioceptive reflex of the respiratory muscles: when airway resistance increases, shortening of the respiratory muscles is impeded, spindle afferent input increases, and contraction is reflexly strengthened, which helps overcome the resistance. Proprioceptors in the joints and muscles of the limbs provide a feedforward ventilatory drive during exercise (Section 20.6.1).

## 20.6. Integrated Situations

### 20.6.1. Ventilation in Exercise

In moderate exercise, oxygen consumption and $\ce{CO2}$ production can increase severalfold, ventilation increases proportionally, and the arterial $P_{\ce{O2}}$, $P_{\ce{CO2}}$ and pH hardly change (Section 17.6.2). This shows that the increase in ventilation is not driven by a change in chemical stimuli, since the chemical stimuli themselves have not changed. The change in ventilation has three phases:

1. Within the first few seconds of exercise ventilation rises abruptly, ahead of any change in blood composition. It arises from a feedforward neural drive: the motor cortex and hypothalamus drive the respiratory centers at the same time as they drive the limb muscles ("central command"), and afferent input from mechanoreceptors of limb muscles and joints also excites the respiratory centers.
2. Ventilation then rises slowly over about 1–3 min toward a level that matches the metabolic rate.
3. Steady state: ventilation matches $\ce{CO2}$ production precisely, and the chemoreceptors fine-tune the residual error by feedback.

When exercise stops, ventilation first falls abruptly and then recovers slowly, repaying the oxygen debt. Above an intensity of about 60% of maximal oxygen consumption (the ventilatory threshold), lactate produced by muscle is buffered by $\ce{HCO3-}$ and releases additional $\ce{CO2}$; the fall in pH stimulates the carotid body, ventilation increases more than oxygen consumption, and $P_{\mathrm{aCO_2}}$ in fact falls (respiratory compensation for metabolic acidosis).

### 20.6.2. Behavior, Sleep and Periodic Breathing

The cerebral cortex can control breathing voluntarily within limits: speaking, singing, playing wind instruments and breath-holding all require temporary override of the automatic rhythm. The cortical drive reaches the motoneurons of the respiratory muscles directly through the corticospinal tract, bypassing the brainstem; this voluntary control is limited, since during breath-holding $\ce{CO2}$ accumulates and the chemical drive eventually forces breathing to resume (the "breaking point"). Emotional and arousal systems also change breathing: fear, anger and pain suddenly alter the breathing pattern, and wakefulness itself provides a "wakefulness drive" that increases ventilation.

In sleep the wakefulness drive disappears, the sensitivity of the central chemoreceptors to $\ce{CO2}$ falls, and $P_{\mathrm{aCO_2}}$ rises by about $3$–$7\,\mathrm{mmHg}$; in rapid-eye-movement sleep breathing is irregular, and the tone of skeletal muscles other than the diaphragm almost disappears, including the pharyngeal muscles that keep the upper airway open.

**Definition 20.6.1 (Sleep Apnea)** The periodic cessation of airflow lasting more than 10 s during sleep is called **sleep apnea**. If the airway muscles relax and the upper airway collapses and is obstructed, with respiratory effort but no airflow, it is called obstructive sleep apnea; if the drive of the respiratory centers disappears and there is no respiratory effort, it is called central sleep apnea.

Obstructive sleep apnea is common in obese adults and in those with a narrow upper airway; each pause ends with a brief arousal caused by hypoxia and $\ce{CO2}$ accumulation, sleep is repeatedly interrupted, and sympathetic excitation and hypertension accompany it.

Central periodic breathing, represented by Cheyne–Stokes respiration, is characterized by breathing that gradually becomes deeper and faster, then gradually weaker, and then pauses, repeating with a period of about 40–90 s. It occurs in heart failure, brain injury and sleep at altitude. It is an example of instability of a feedback control system (the same principle as oscillation produced by delayed negative feedback in [7.9.16](/7.9.16)): the chemoreceptors sense the alveolar $P_{\ce{CO2}}$ after a circulatory delay; the correction of $\ce{CO2}$ by ventilation is "seen" by the receptors only after a delay, and if both the gain of the controller (the sensitivity to $\ce{CO2}$) and the delay are large enough, the correction overshoots, the oscillation does not decay but persists, and at the trough of ventilation $P_{\mathrm{aCO_2}}$ falls below the apneic threshold and apnea occurs.

The model explains several clinical facts: in patients with heart failure the circulation time is prolonged and the sensitivity of the carotid body is often raised, both of which push the system across the stability boundary; oxygen breathing lowers the gain of the peripheral chemoreceptors and can relieve periodic breathing at altitude and in heart failure; and the respiratory control system of premature infants is immature, so they often show periodic breathing and apnea.

### 20.6.3. Altitude

On arrival at altitude, the $P_{\ce{O2}}$ of inspired air is lowered (Section 17.2.1); when $P_{\mathrm{aO_2}}$ falls below about 60 mmHg (above about 3000 m), the carotid body is excited and ventilation increases. This acute hypoxic ventilatory response is self-limiting: the increased ventilation blows off $\ce{CO2}$, $P_{\mathrm{aCO_2}}$ and $\ce{H+}$ fall (respiratory alkalosis), the drive of the central chemoreceptors weakens and, like a brake, offsets the peripheral drive; alkalosis also shifts the oxygen dissociation curve to the left. Hence on first arriving at altitude ventilation increases only slightly (blue dashed line in panel B).

Over the next several days to a week or two, ventilation continues to increase gradually, which is called ventilatory acclimatization:

- The kidney excretes $\ce{HCO3-}$ and partly corrects the alkalosis of the blood; the $\ce{HCO3-}$ of the cerebrospinal fluid also falls, so that the pH around the central chemoreceptors returns to normal. In Formula 20.4.1 this corresponds to a downward shift of the apneic threshold $B$: the "brake" is released.
- The sensitivity of the carotid body itself to hypoxia increases within several days ($A$ increases).

After acclimatization, ventilation can be more than 50% higher than at sea level, $P_{\mathrm{aCO_2}}$ falls to 25–30 mmHg (at 4500 m) and $P_{\mathrm{aO_2}}$ rises correspondingly (panel C). Acetazolamide inhibits carbonic anhydrase, causing the kidney to excrete $\ce{HCO3-}$ and producing a mild metabolic acidosis, which amounts to completing this correction in advance, and can be used to prevent acute mountain sickness.

Acclimatization also includes changes in other systems: erythropoietin increases and hemoglobin rises over several weeks (Chapter 11); 2,3-BPG increases (Section 19.3.3); tissue capillary density and myoglobin increase; and pulmonary vascular resistance rises through hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction (Section 18.4.4). When acclimatization fails or ascent is too rapid, acute mountain sickness, high-altitude pulmonary edema and high-altitude cerebral edema occur. Populations resident at altitude have different genetic adaptations: Andeans have a high hemoglobin concentration, whereas Tibetans have hemoglobin close to that of lowlanders, a stronger hypoxic ventilatory response and greater nitric oxide production (Section 19.6.2).

## 20.7. Evolution and Comparison

### 20.7.1. Water Breathers Are Driven by $\ce{O2}$, Air Breathers by $\ce{CO2}$

Ventilation in mammals is driven mainly by $\ce{CO2}$/pH, whereas in fish it is driven mainly by $\ce{O2}$. This difference arises from the physical properties of the two media (Section 17.2.3). The solubility of $\ce{CO2}$ in water is about 28 times that of $\ce{O2}$, while the $\ce{O2}$ content of water is low: to obtain enough $\ce{O2}$, fish must pump large volumes of water over the gills, and this water carries $\ce{CO2}$ away with ease. The arterial $P_{\ce{CO2}}$ of fish is only $2$–$5\,\mathrm{mmHg}$, and even halving ventilation raises it by only a few millimeters of mercury, so it cannot serve as a sensitive signal; the $\ce{O2}$ content of water, by contrast, fluctuates widely with temperature, photosynthesis and pollution and is the variable that really needs monitoring.

| Comparison | Water breathing (fish) | Air breathing (mammals) |
|---|---|---|
| Main drive | $\ce{O2}$ (hypoxia increases ventilation severalfold) | $\ce{CO2}$/pH |
| Arterial $P_{\ce{CO2}}$ | 2–5 mmHg | 40 mmHg |
| Location of $\ce{O2}$ sensors | Neuroepithelial cells on the gill arches (sensing water and blood $P_{\ce{O2}}$), on the first and second arches, innervated by the glossopharyngeal and vagus nerves | Carotid and aortic bodies, derived from vessels of the pharyngeal arches |
| Acid–base regulation | Mainly by ion exchange in the gill epithelium ($\ce{Na+}$/$\ce{H+}$, $\ce{Cl-}$/$\ce{HCO3-}$) | Mainly by changing $P_{\ce{CO2}}$ through ventilation, supplemented by the kidney (Chapter 25) |

The $\ce{O2}$-sensing neuroepithelial cells of fish gills resemble the glomus cells of the mammalian carotid body: both are derived from the neural crest and both lie near vessels of the pharyngeal arches, and the carotid body is thought to have evolved from chemoreceptive cells of the fish gill. In the transition from water to air, $\ce{CO2}$ accumulated to a level that could be sensed, and central chemoreception of $\ce{CO2}$/pH gradually became the main drive; lungfish and amphibians, which use both gills and lungs, respond clearly to both hypoxia and $\ce{CO2}$ and occupy an intermediate state.

### 20.7.2. Intermittent Breathing in Amphibians and Reptiles

Many amphibians and reptiles do not breathe continuously as mammals do but breathe intermittently: a bout of several breaths is followed by a long breath-hold (apnea). Turtles can hold their breath for tens of minutes, and in hibernation can stay underwater for several months (relying partly on gas exchange through the skin and cloaca and on a very low metabolism). Intermittent breathing suits the low metabolic rate of ectotherms and their intracardiac shunts: during the breath-hold the $\ce{O2}$ store in the lung is gradually consumed and a right-to-left intracardiac shunt bypasses the lung (Chapter 16); when breathing resumes, the shunt turns left-to-right and pulmonary blood flow increases. The bullfrog brainstem contains two separable oscillators: one produces continuous buccal oscillation (small movements of the floor of the mouth) and the other produces bursts of lung ventilation; tadpoles first have only a gill ventilation rhythm, and at metamorphosis the lung ventilation rhythm gradually comes to dominate, a transition accompanied by an increase in central $\ce{CO2}$ sensitivity.

### 20.7.3. Evolution of the Respiratory Rhythm Generator

The respiratory rhythm generator has a common origin in vertebrates: the gill ventilation rhythm of the lamprey is generated by a region of the brainstem next to the trigeminal nerve (the paratrigeminal respiratory group), which, like the preBötC, depends on a glutamatergic excitatory network and is depressed by opioids, and which has been suggested to be a possible homolog of the preBötC; the lung rhythm generator of the frog is also similar to the mammalian preBötC in location and pharmacology. On this basis mammals differentiated a dual-oscillator structure of preBötC (inspiration) and pFRG (active expiration), matched to the appearance of the muscular diaphragm and the need for active expiration. The lungs of birds contain intrapulmonary chemoreceptors that are extremely sensitive to $\ce{CO2}$; they sense the $P_{\ce{CO2}}$ of gas in the parabronchi and regulate the frequency and depth of breathing in each breath, which no other vertebrate has; this fits the unidirectional, rigid crosscurrent design of the avian lung.

### 20.7.4. Respiratory Suppression in Diving Mammals

Diving mammals suppress breathing while diving, which is itself part of the diving reflex: when the face and nostrils are immersed, trigeminal afferents inhibit the respiratory centers and at the same time cause bradycardia and peripheral vasoconstriction (Chapter 16). Their chemoreflexes are less sensitive to $\ce{CO2}$ than those of terrestrial mammals, and they can tolerate a higher $P_{\ce{CO2}}$ without being forced to surface to breathe; after surfacing they rapidly clear $\ce{CO2}$ and replenish their $\ce{O2}$ stores with deep, rapid breaths. The tidal volume of a seal can approach its vital capacity, so that each breath almost completely renews the gas in the lung, whereas a human renews only about 1/7 (Section 17.6.1).

### 20.7.5. A Comparative Summary of the Respiratory System

Chapters 17–20 treated breathing at four levels: the physics of the medium and the exchanger, the mechanics of the lung, the transport by blood, and neural control. Comparing these levels across animals shows that each design responds to the same set of constraints (diffusion can bridge only very short distances; water holds little $\ce{O2}$ and is a heavy medium, whereas air is rich in $\ce{O2}$ but dries and collapses the exchange surface):

| Level | Fish (water breathing) | Amphibians, reptiles | Birds | Mammals | Insects |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Exchanger | Everted gills, supported by the buoyancy of water | Saccular lungs plus skin (amphibians) | Rigid lung, air capillaries | Hundreds of millions of alveoli | Tracheae and tracheoles, reaching the cells directly |
| Mode of ventilation | Unidirectional; buccal–opercular double pump, or ram ventilation | Tidal; buccal pump (positive pressure) or trunk-muscle inspiration | Unidirectional; air sacs as bellows | Tidal; diaphragm and intercostals (negative pressure) | Mainly diffusion, convection and discontinuous gas exchange in large species |
| Exchange pattern (Formula 17.3.1) | Countercurrent | Ventilated pool | Crosscurrent | Ventilated pool | No blood needed |
| Blood–gas barrier | Gill lamella epithelium about 1–10 μm | Thicker | About 0.1–0.3 μm | About 0.6 μm | — |
| Surface-tension problem | None | Surfactant (ancient origin) | Rigid small tubes, smaller problem | Surfactant + fibre network | Cuticular spiral thickenings of the tracheal wall |
| Respiratory pigment | Hemoglobin; Root effect | Hemoglobin | Hemoglobin, regulated by IPP | Hemoglobin, regulated by 2,3-BPG | Hardly used (hemolymph does not carry oxygen) |
| Main chemical drive | $\ce{O2}$ (gill neuroepithelial cells) | Both $\ce{O2}$ and $\ce{CO2}$ | $\ce{CO2}$ (including intrapulmonary chemoreceptors) | $\ce{CO2}$/pH (mainly central) | $\ce{CO2}$ and $\ce{O2}$ control opening and closing of spiracles |
| Rhythm | Gill rhythm of the brainstem | Two oscillators, buccal and pulmonary; intermittent breathing | Continuous | preBötC + pFRG | Rhythmic abdominal pumping (ganglionic CPG) |

Several conclusions run through the comparison. First, unidirectional flow designs, countercurrent and crosscurrent, are the most efficient and appear where the medium is most "expensive" (water) or the demand is most extreme (flight); mammals use the less efficient ventilated pool and compensate with a huge area and a very thin barrier. Second, the medium determines the signal of regulation: in water $\ce{CO2}$ is easily removed and $\ce{O2}$ is scarce, so $\ce{O2}$ is the drive; in air the opposite holds. Third, respiratory pigments and $\ce{O2}$ affinity have been adjusted repeatedly and independently to environment (altitude, diving, burrows) and body size, often converging at the molecular level. Fourth, the respiratory rhythm is built from a few kinds of dynamical building blocks (reciprocally inhibiting half-centers, pacemaker currents, excitatory recurrent networks), which retain a common origin in lampreys, frogs and mammals.

## Questions

1. A person hyperventilates for one minute before diving and then suddenly loses consciousness underwater (shallow-water blackout). Explain why, using the properties of the chemoreceptors.

   **Answer** Hyperventilation mainly lowers $P_{\mathrm{aCO_2}}$ (for example from 40 to 20 mmHg), whereas $P_{\mathrm{aO_2}}$ rises only slightly and the oxygen store of the blood hardly increases (the upper part of the oxygen dissociation curve is flat, Chapter 19). During a breath-hold, the sensation of "wanting to breathe" comes mainly from stimulation of central and peripheral chemoreceptors by accumulating $\ce{CO2}$; starting from a very low $P_{\ce{CO2}}$, it takes a long time to rise to the breaking point that forces breathing. In the meantime $\ce{O2}$ is continuously consumed, exercise accelerates its consumption, and $P_{\mathrm{aO_2}}$ may fall to a level at which the brain ceases to function before the $\ce{CO2}$ drive has appeared. The ventilatory drive of hypoxia itself acts through the carotid body, but it cannot restore consciousness, and breathing is impossible underwater anyway. One should therefore not hyperventilate before diving.

2. A patient with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease has long-standing raised $P_{\mathrm{aCO_2}}$ and lowered $P_{\mathrm{aO_2}}$. Why does $P_{\mathrm{aCO_2}}$ sometimes rise further after he is given a high concentration of oxygen?

   **Answer** In chronic hypercapnia the kidney retains $\ce{HCO3-}$, the $\ce{HCO3-}$ of the cerebrospinal fluid also rises, and the pH around the central chemoreceptors is compensated to near normal, so the central drive from $\ce{CO2}$ weakens and the drive from hypoxia through the carotid body accounts for a larger proportion of the maintenance of ventilation. Breathing a high concentration of oxygen removes the hypoxic drive and ventilation falls. In addition, oxygen relieves hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction in poorly ventilated regions and lets blood flow return to them, worsening the ventilation–perfusion mismatch (more dead-space-like ventilation); the increase in oxyhemoglobin in turn releases $\ce{CO2}$ through the Haldane effect. The three together raise $P_{\mathrm{aCO_2}}$. Oxygen for such patients should therefore be given at a controlled concentration, aiming to maintain $S_{\ce{O2}}$ at about 88%–92%.

3. Use Formula 20.4.1 and the metabolic hyperbola to explain: (a) why breathing pure oxygen at sea level hardly changes resting ventilation; (b) why ventilation increases only slightly on first arriving at 4500 m and increases appreciably only after several days.

   **Answer** (a) At sea level $P_{\mathrm{aO_2}}$ is about 95 mmHg, and $A/(P_{\mathrm{aO_2}} - 32)$ is already small; breathing pure oxygen makes it approach zero, and the slope of the $\ce{CO2}$ response line falls only slightly. The operating point is determined by the intersection of the response line with the metabolic hyperbola, and a small change in slope raises $P_{\mathrm{aCO_2}}$ only slightly, leaving ventilation almost unchanged. (b) On first arriving at altitude $P_{\mathrm{aO_2}}$ falls to about 35–40 mmHg and the slope increases greatly; but the operating point moves along the hyperbola toward lower $P_{\mathrm{aCO_2}}$, and $P_{\mathrm{aCO_2}}$ can fall only to a value slightly above the apneic threshold $B$, so the increment in ventilation is small. After several days the kidney excretes $\ce{HCO3-}$ and the pH of the cerebrospinal fluid is corrected, so $B$ shifts downward; the carotid body is sensitized and $A$ increases; both move the intersection toward high ventilation and low $P_{\mathrm{aCO_2}}$ (panel C).

4. Explain from the standpoint of delayed negative feedback why patients with heart failure are prone to Cheyne–Stokes respiration in sleep. Through which parameter does oxygen breathing relieve it, and through which does improving cardiac function?

   **Answer** The chemoreceptors sense blood gases after the circulatory delay from the lung to the carotid body and brain. Heart failure lowers cardiac output and prolongs the circulation time (larger delay); pulmonary congestion excites J receptors, and sympathetic excitation raises the sensitivity of the carotid body (larger gain); in sleep the wakefulness drive disappears and the apneic threshold lies closer to the resting $P_{\mathrm{aCO_2}}$. When the product of gain and delay exceeds the stability boundary, the correction of any perturbation overshoots and a sustained oscillation develops, with $P_{\mathrm{aCO_2}}$ below the apneic threshold at the troughs and apnea (red line in panel A). Oxygen breathing lowers the slope of the carotid body's response to $\ce{CO2}$, that is, lowers the gain; improving cardiac function shortens the circulation time, that is, reduces the delay. Both push the system back into the stable region (panel B).

5. Why does the carotid body need such a high blood flow? If its blood flow were only comparable to that of ordinary tissue, how would the sensing of $\ce{O2}$ be affected?

   **Answer** The carotid body has to measure the $P_{\ce{O2}}$ of arterial blood. The $P_{\ce{O2}}$ of the tissue around the sensing cells is determined by the balance between supply by blood flow and the tissue's own consumption (Section 19.2.3). With a very high blood flow, blood loses almost no $\ce{O2}$ as it passes through the carotid body, tissue $P_{\ce{O2}}$ is close to the arterial value, and the measurement is both accurate and fast; the carotid body itself has a high metabolic rate, and if its blood flow were only comparable to that of ordinary tissue, tissue $P_{\ce{O2}}$ would be far below the arterial value and would fluctuate with its own metabolism and blood flow, so the receptor would no longer report arterial blood but the local balance of supply and demand. This also explains why, when severe hypotension lowers carotid body blood flow, it is excited even though arterial $P_{\ce{O2}}$ is normal.

6. From the physical properties of the media, explain why ventilation in fish is driven mainly by $\ce{O2}$ and in humans mainly by $\ce{CO2}$. If a fish lives in water with a very high $\ce{CO2}$ (such as a densely stocked pond), how would you expect its breathing and acid–base state to change?

   **Answer** Water holds little $\ce{O2}$ and $\ce{CO2}$ is very soluble in it; the large volume of water that a fish pumps to obtain $\ce{O2}$ incidentally carries $\ce{CO2}$ away, the arterial $P_{\ce{CO2}}$ is only a few mmHg and insensitive to changes in ventilation, so it cannot serve as a sensitive feedback signal; $\ce{O2}$ is the limiting variable and fluctuates widely with the environment. In air $\ce{O2}$ is abundant, and tidal ventilation lets $\ce{CO2}$ accumulate in the alveoli to 40 mmHg; $P_{\ce{CO2}}$ is inversely proportional to ventilation (Eq. (17.7)) and is a sensitive signal that also directly determines pH. When environmental $\ce{CO2}$ is very high, the blood $P_{\ce{CO2}}$ of the fish rises with that of the water and respiratory acidosis develops; increasing ventilation hardly helps to expel $\ce{CO2}$ (the water itself has a high $P_{\ce{CO2}}$), so the fish compensates mainly by increasing $\ce{HCO3-}$ uptake and $\ce{H+}$ excretion across the gill epithelium; many fish also have $\ce{CO2}$-sensing neuroepithelial cells on the gills, and high $\ce{CO2}$ raises ventilation somewhat. Through the Root effect, acidosis also lowers the oxygen capacity of hemoglobin and further threatens oxygen supply.
