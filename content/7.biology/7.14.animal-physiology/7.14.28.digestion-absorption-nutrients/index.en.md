The previous two chapters described how the digestive tract moves its contents (Chapter 26) and which digestive juices it secretes (Chapter 27); this chapter deals with the ultimate purpose of digestion: breaking food down into small molecules that can be absorbed and carrying them, together with water and electrolytes, into the internal environment. The small intestine is the main site of digestion and absorption, and the task it faces is quantitative: every day it must recover 99% of the water from about $8.5\,\mathrm{L}$ of fluid, absorb several hundred grams of sugar, about $100\,\mathrm{g}$ each of protein and fat, and vitamins and trace elements in milligram or even microgram quantities, and it has only $3$–$5\,\mathrm{h}$ to do so. This chapter first sets out the accounts of fluid and area (Section 28.1), then the general mechanisms of absorption, especially of $\ce{Na+}$ and water (Section 28.2), then the digestion and absorption of sugars, proteins, lipids, vitamins and minerals in turn (Sections 28.3–28.6), and finally the colon and the gut microbiota (Section 28.7). The basic components of epithelial transport (the Na–K pump, SGLT, tight junctions) are described in [Chapter 3](/7.14.3#sec-3.7); how absorbed nutrients are distributed among organs is covered in Chapter 29, and the metabolic pathways of sugars, amino acids and lipids in [7.1](/7.1).

## 28.1. The Quantitative Background of Absorption

### 28.1.1. The Fluid Budget

About $8.5\,\mathrm{L}$ of fluid enter the small intestine each day: about $2\,\mathrm{L}$ from the diet and about $6.5\,\mathrm{L}$ from digestive juices (saliva 1.0, gastric juice 1.5, bile 0.7, pancreatic juice 1.5, intestinal juice 1.8; Chapter 27). The small intestine absorbs about $7\,\mathrm{L}$ of this (about 5.5 in the jejunum and 1.5 in the ileum), so only about $1.5\,\mathrm{L}$ per day pass through the ileocecal valve into the colon; the colon reabsorbs about $1.4\,\mathrm{L}$ more, and the water lost in the feces is only about $0.1\,\mathrm{L}$, less than 2% of the fluid that enters the digestive tract.

![Daily fluid budget of the gastrointestinal tract (typical adult, L/d). Left: the diet and the six digestive juices together deliver about 8.5 L to the small intestine; middle: the jejunum and ileum absorb about 7 L, and about 1.5 L enter the colon; right: the colon absorbs about 1.4 L, and only about 0.1 L of water remains in the feces](./plots/fluid-balance-sankey.py)

This accounting makes two points. First, the water and electrolytes of the digestive juices are almost entirely recovered; the digestive tract is a high-flow circulation, and the volume secreted each day is more than twice the plasma volume. If recovery is interrupted (severe diarrhea, vomiting, intestinal fistula), dehydration, loss of $\ce{Na+}$ and $\ce{K+}$, and acid–base disturbances result within hours to days. A patient with cholera can excrete $10$–$20\,\mathrm{L}$ of fluid per day, equal to the entire extracellular fluid. Second, the colon has a large reserve: it can absorb up to $5$–$8\,\mathrm{L}$ per day, and diarrhea occurs only when the fluid entering the colon exceeds this capacity or when the colon itself secretes more (infection, toxins).

### 28.1.2. Amplification of the Absorptive Area

The absorptive area of the small intestine is amplified by three levels of structure:

1. Circular folds (folds of Kerckring): circular folds of mucosa and submucosa that project into the lumen, best developed in the duodenum and jejunum, about $8\,\mathrm{mm}$ high;
2. Villi: finger-like projections of the mucosal surface about $0.5$–$1\,\mathrm{mm}$ high, 10–40 per square millimeter;
3. Microvilli: on the apical membrane of each absorptive cell (enterocyte) there are about $1000$–$3000$ projections about $1\,\mathrm{\mu m}$ long and about $0.1\,\mathrm{\mu m}$ in diameter, which appear under the light microscope as the brush border.

The classical textbook estimate is that the three levels amplify the area by about 3, 10 and 20 times respectively, about 600-fold in all (often summarized as "about 1000-fold"), giving a small-intestinal absorptive area of about $200$–$250\,\mathrm{m^2}$, "equal to a tennis court". A recent morphometric study (2014) gives a much smaller figure: the total mucosal area of the entire digestive tract is about $32\,\mathrm{m^2}$ (of which the large intestine is about $2\,\mathrm{m^2}$), about half a badminton court; the difference comes mainly from overestimates of the length of the living intestine and of the villous amplification factor (the intestine of a cadaver is longer because the muscle is relaxed). Whichever figure is taken, the absorptive area of the small intestine is more than two orders of magnitude larger than that of a smooth tube of the same length, which is the structural basis of its ability to complete absorption within a few hours.

**Example 28.1.1 (Why the Absorptive Area Must Be Large)** Suppose a person absorbs $300\,\mathrm{g}$ of glucose per day (about $1.7\,\mathrm{mol}$) and the maximal transport rate of brush-border membrane per unit area is of the order of $1\,\mathrm{\mu mol\,cm^{-2}\,h^{-1}}$ (per unit area of villous surface). If absorption is concentrated in about $3\,\mathrm{h}$ after a meal and takes place in about $1\,\mathrm{m}$ of upper small intestine, the area required is about $1.7\,\mathrm{mol}/(3\,\mathrm{h} \times 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{mol\,cm^{-2}\,h^{-1}}) \approx 6\times10^5\,\mathrm{cm^2} = 60\,\mathrm{m^2}$. A smooth tube $3\,\mathrm{cm}$ in diameter and $1\,\mathrm{m}$ long has only about $0.1\,\mathrm{m^2}$. This order-of-magnitude estimate shows that without the multilevel amplification of area, the transport rate of the carrier proteins alone could not accomplish absorption; in reality the density of transport proteins and the villous amplification together reach this capacity.

### 28.1.3. The Crypt–Villus Axis

The small-intestinal epithelium is divided in function along the crypt–villus axis: the crypts (crypts of Lieberkühn) lie at the base between the villi and contain stem cells, immature cells that secrete $\ce{Cl-}$ and water, and Paneth cells (Chapter 27); cells differentiate and mature as they migrate up the villus, and the mature enterocytes in the upper villus express digestive enzymes and nutrient transporters and are mainly responsible for absorption; after 3–5 days the cells are shed at the villus tip. Thus "the crypts secrete and the villi absorb", and a local fluid circulation is established between the two. At the center of each villus is a blind-ended lymphatic vessel (the central lacteal), into which absorbed lipids enter as chylomicrons; the arterioles, capillary network and venules of the villus are arranged in a countercurrent fashion ([Chapter 26](/7.14.26)) and carry absorbed water-soluble substances into the portal vein. The villus also contains smooth muscle, whose rhythmic contraction shortens the villus, squeezes the central lacteal and helps lymph flow.

### 28.1.4. Digestion Is Hydrolysis

The chemical digestion of every kind of nutrient in the digestive tract is hydrolysis:

$$
\ce{R''-R' + H2O -> R''OH + R'H}.
$$

Polysaccharides are hydrolyzed to monosaccharides, proteins to amino acids and small peptides, and triglycerides to fatty acids and monoacylglycerols. Hydrolysis is completed at three sites: in the lumen (enzymes in the digestive juices, such as amylase, trypsin and lipase), at the brush-border membrane (disaccharidases, oligosaccharidases and peptidases fixed in the microvillous membrane) and inside the cell (peptidases in the cytosol of enterocytes). The advantage of brush-border hydrolysis is that the products are generated at the membrane surface next to the transporters, so they can be absorbed without diffusing across the unstirred water layer and are also less accessible to intestinal bacteria.

## 28.2. Absorption of Water and Electrolytes

### 28.2.1. Division of Labor Between the Two Sides

Absorption by the small-intestinal epithelium follows the same principle ([Chapter 3](/7.14.3#sec-3.7)): the Na–K pump of the basolateral membrane maintains a low intracellular $[\ce{Na+}]$ and a negative membrane potential, and this is the only step that directly consumes ATP; transporters in the apical membrane use the electrochemical gradient of $\ce{Na+}$ to carry $\ce{Na+}$ into the cell together with nutrients (absorption of nutrients is **secondary active transport**); the nutrients then enter the interstitial fluid by **facilitated diffusion** through transporters in the basolateral membrane; and water follows passively, driven by osmotic pressure.

### 28.2.2. Four Modes of Na⁺ Absorption

There are four apical mechanisms by which intestinal epithelium absorbs $\ce{Na+}$, distributed differently along the intestine:

| Mode | Apical mechanism | Main site | Timing | Features |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Nutrient cotransport | $\ce{Na+}$–glucose (SGLT1), $\ce{Na+}$–amino acid and other symporters | Jejunum, ileum | The main mode after a meal | Electrogenic; operates only when nutrients are present |
| 2. $\ce{Na+}$/$\ce{H+}$ exchange | NHE3 | Duodenum, jejunum | After a meal, triggered by the alkaline pancreatic juice and bile | Luminal $\ce{HCO3-}$ neutralizes the extruded $\ce{H+}$, forming $\ce{CO2}$ and water and maintaining the driving force of the exchange |
| 3. Electroneutral NaCl absorption | NHE3 in parallel with the $\ce{Cl-}$/$\ce{HCO3-}$ exchanger (DRA) | Ileum, proximal colon | Interdigestive period | Net effect is absorption of NaCl, acid–base neutral |
| 4. Electrogenic $\ce{Na+}$ absorption | Epithelial $\ce{Na+}$ channel (ENaC) | Distal colon, rectum | Interdigestive period | Regulated by aldosterone; can absorb against a very large concentration gradient |

In all four modes the basolateral membrane carries the Na–K pump. Why do different segments of the intestine use different modes? Three considerations help:

- Available "fuel": nutrients appear mainly in the upper small intestine, where cotransport of $\ce{Na+}$ with nutrients accomplishes two things at once; the duodenum contains large amounts of $\ce{HCO3-}$ from pancreatic juice and bile, exactly what is needed to consume the $\ce{H+}$ extruded by NHE3.
- Tightness of the epithelium: the tight junctions of the small-intestinal epithelium are highly permeable (a "leaky" epithelium with low transepithelial resistance), suited to absorbing large amounts of water and solute isotonically under small osmotic gradients but unable to maintain large ionic concentration differences; the epithelium of the distal colon is tight (high resistance) and can maintain very large concentration differences. By the distal colon the fecal $[\ce{Na+}]$ has already fallen below about $30\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$, and only a highly selective channel such as ENaC, combined with a tight epithelium, can "squeeze" out more $\ce{Na+}$; this segment is therefore regulated by aldosterone, like the collecting duct of the kidney ([Chapter 24](/7.14.24)).
- Need for regulation: the proximal modes operate automatically according to "supply", whereas the distal mode is under hormonal control and raises the recovery rate when the body is short of sodium; this is exactly the same division of labor as in the renal tubule, "bulk proximally, fine adjustment distally" ([Chapter 23](/7.14.23)).

In addition, $\ce{K+}$ is absorbed passively in the small intestine, mainly through the paracellular pathway, and secreted in the colon (aldosterone also increases $\ce{K+}$ secretion), so prolonged diarrhea causes hypokalemia. $\ce{Cl-}$ is absorbed in the small intestine with $\ce{Na+}$ through the paracellular pathway or DRA; $\ce{HCO3-}$ is reabsorbed in the upper small intestine (indirectly through NHE3) and secreted in the ileum and colon (in exchange for $\ce{Cl-}$), which is why diarrhea is often accompanied by metabolic acidosis.

### 28.2.3. Absorption of Water

Water absorption is entirely passive, driven by the osmotic gradient created by solute absorption, and proceeds by two routes, transcellular (aquaporins) and paracellular. The epithelium of the upper small intestine is highly water-permeable, and the luminal fluid becomes isotonic with plasma within minutes: hypertonic chyme entering the duodenum draws water from the blood, and hypotonic fluid is absorbed rapidly. After solute is absorbed into the intercellular spaces, the spaces become slightly hypertonic locally, water follows, and the two flow together to the capillaries, so the absorbed fluid is isotonic overall. The flow of water can also carry solutes along the paracellular pathway (solvent drag), an additional route for absorption of glucose and ions at high concentration.

Oral rehydration salts exploit this mechanism: cholera toxin makes the crypts secrete $\ce{Cl-}$ and water continuously but does not damage SGLT1 of the villi; $\ce{Na+}$ and glucose prepared in a 1:1 ratio (the hypo-osmolar formula of the World Health Organization contains $\ce{Na+}$ $75\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$ and glucose $75\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$, with a total osmolarity of $245\,\mathrm{mOsm\,L^{-1}}$) are absorbed through coupled SGLT1 transport and draw $\ce{Na+}$ and water back into the body.

## 28.3. Digestion and Absorption of Sugars

An adult ingests about $250$–$400\,\mathrm{g}$ of carbohydrate per day, about half of it starch and the rest mainly sucrose, lactose, and dietary fiber that humans cannot digest (cellulose, hemicellulose, pectin).

Digestion: salivary amylase begins hydrolyzing starch in the mouth and stomach; pancreatic amylase in the duodenum hydrolyzes starch (linear α-1,4 bonds and branching α-1,6 bonds) to maltose, maltotriose and α-limit dextrins containing α-1,6 bonds; α-amylase cannot hydrolyze α-1,6 bonds, nor the β-1,4 bonds of **cellulose**. The final hydrolysis is completed at the brush border: sucrase–isomaltase (which hydrolyzes sucrose and α-1,6 bonds), maltase–glucoamylase and lactase hydrolyze oligosaccharides and disaccharides to glucose, fructose and galactose.

Absorption:

- Glucose and galactose are absorbed through SGLT1 in the apical membrane, each molecule being transported together with 2 $\ce{Na+}$, which allows them to be concentrated in the cell against their concentration gradient (Example 3.4.2);
- Fructose is absorbed by facilitated diffusion through GLUT5 in the apical membrane, independent of $\ce{Na+}$, so absorption is slower, and a large intake (especially without glucose) partly enters the colon, is fermented by bacteria, and causes bloating;
- All three monosaccharides leave the cell by facilitated diffusion through GLUT2 in the basolateral membrane and enter the portal vein. When the luminal glucose concentration is very high after a meal, GLUT2 may also be inserted into the apical membrane, providing an additional high-capacity route (the significance of this mechanism in humans is still debated).

Carrier-mediated absorption is saturable. As a bolus of chyme advances along the small intestine, the luminal glucose concentration falls progressively; with a small load, glucose is absorbed completely in the upper jejunum; with a large load, saturation of transport pushes absorption farther down the intestine, drawing on the reserve length of the small intestine. The passive paracellular route contributes more at high concentration.

![Saturation kinetics of glucose absorption and the absorption profile along the intestine (plug-flow model). A: the absorption rate of the carrier (SGLT1) saturates above about $10\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$, the passive route is proportional to concentration, and the total absorption rate grows nearly linearly at high concentration. B: a bolus of chyme advances at $1\,\mathrm{cm\,min^{-1}}$; for initial luminal glucose concentrations of 50, 150 and 300 mmol/L, the positions at which 95% is absorbed (dots) are at about 60, 130 and 210 cm; larger loads recruit longer segments of intestine](./plots/glucose-absorption-profile.py)

In most mammals lactase expression declines after weaning, and the ability to digest lactose falls in adulthood (lactose intolerance); undigested lactose enters the colon and is fermented, producing gas and osmotic diarrhea. In some pastoralist populations, mutations in the regulatory region of the lactase gene cause it to be expressed throughout life (lactase persistence), one of the strongest signals of positive selection in humans over the past ten thousand years ([7.10.8](/7.10.8)).

## 28.4. Digestion and Absorption of Proteins

About $150$–$170\,\mathrm{g}$ of protein enter the small intestine each day: $70$–$100\,\mathrm{g}$ from the diet, and about half as much again of endogenous origin (digestive enzymes, shed epithelial cells and plasma proteins). Almost all of the protein has been digested and absorbed by the time the chyme reaches the mid-jejunum, and the protein in feces comes mainly from bacteria and shed cells.

Digestion: pepsin accomplishes 10%–20% in the stomach; the endopeptidases of pancreatic juice (trypsin cleaves peptide bonds after Lys and Arg, chymotrypsin after aromatic amino acids, elastase after small neutral amino acids) and the exopeptidases (carboxypeptidases A and B) hydrolyze protein to amino acids (about 30%–40%) and oligopeptides of 2–6 residues (60%–70%); aminopeptidases and others at the brush border hydrolyze the oligopeptides further to dipeptides, tripeptides and amino acids.

The products absorbed are of two kinds, with different transporters:

- Dipeptides and tripeptides are absorbed through PepT1 in the apical membrane, which cotransports them with $\ce{H+}$ rather than $\ce{Na+}$ (the acidic microenvironment at the apical surface is maintained by NHE3) and has very low affinity for peptides of four or more residues. Dipeptides and tripeptides are absorbed even faster than the corresponding free amino acids; the absorbed peptides are hydrolyzed to amino acids in the cytosol by peptidases, so almost only free amino acids enter the portal vein. PepT1 also absorbs some peptide-like drugs (β-lactam antibiotics, ACE inhibitors).
- Free amino acids are absorbed through a variety of apical transporters, grouped into several systems by substrate: neutral amino acids (B<sup>0</sup>AT1, $\ce{Na+}$-dependent), basic amino acids and cystine (b<sup>0,+</sup>, exchanging with neutral amino acids), acidic amino acids (coupled to $\ce{Na+}$, $\ce{H+}$ and $\ce{K+}$), imino acids (proline), and so on. The same transporters also reabsorb amino acids in the renal proximal tubule, so a defect of B<sup>0</sup>AT1 (Hartnup disease) or of b<sup>0,+</sup> (cystinuria) affects both intestinal absorption and renal reabsorption; because of compensation by the dipeptide route, these patients are usually not deficient in amino acids.

The amount of intact protein absorbed is small but of physiological and pathological significance: small quantities of antigen are taken up by M cells of the intestine, which is the starting point of mucosal immunity and may also be the cause of food allergy. Neonatal rodents and ruminants can absorb large amounts of immunoglobulin from colostrum through the FcRn receptor on enterocytes and thereby acquire passive immunity; the IgG of the human fetus is acquired mainly before birth across the placenta, and the neonatal human intestine absorbs almost no intact antibody.

## 28.5. Digestion and Absorption of Lipids

Dietary lipids amount to about $60$–$100\,\mathrm{g}$ per day, more than 90% being triglycerides, the rest phospholipids, cholesterol and its esters, and fat-soluble vitamins. Lipids are insoluble in water, and their digestion and absorption must solve a physical problem: the enzymes are in the aqueous phase, the substrates in the oil phase, and the products must cross the water layer at the epithelial surface.

1. Emulsification: grinding in the stomach and partial hydrolysis by lingual and gastric lipases (the fatty acids produced are emulsifiers) first break the lipid into droplets; in the duodenum **bile acids** and lecithin adsorb to the surface of the droplets, and under the shear of intestinal motility the droplets shrink to about $1\,\mathrm{\mu m}$ in diameter, increasing the total surface area more than a thousandfold.
2. Hydrolysis: pancreatic lipase hydrolyzes triglycerides at the oil–water interface to 2-monoacylglycerol and two fatty acids. An interface coated with bile salts would normally displace the lipase; colipase (activated by trypsin from procolipase in pancreatic juice) binds both the bile salt–coated interface and the lipase and anchors the lipase to the droplet. Phospholipase $\mathrm{A_2}$ hydrolyzes phospholipids to lysophospholipids and fatty acids, and cholesterol esterase hydrolyzes cholesterol esters. Pancreatic lipase is secreted in great excess of need, and steatorrhea appears only when more than 90% of exocrine pancreatic function is lost.
3. Micellization and transport: the hydrolysis products (fatty acids, 2-monoacylglycerol, lysophospholipids, cholesterol, fat-soluble vitamins) form mixed **micelles** with bile salts, about $3$–$10\,\mathrm{nm}$ in diameter. On the surface of the intestinal epithelium there is an almost motionless water layer a few tens to a few hundreds of micrometers thick (the unstirred water layer); lipid products have extremely low solubility in water by themselves and can hardly cross it, and micelles raise their concentration in the aqueous phase 100–1000-fold and carry them to the brush border. The acidic microenvironment at the surface of the microvilli protonates the fatty acids, increasing their lipid solubility so that they are released from the micelles and enter the cell by simple diffusion and by membrane protein–mediated routes (CD36, FATP4). The bile salts of the micelles remain in the lumen to carry further products and are finally reabsorbed in the terminal ileum (enterohepatic circulation, Chapters 27 and 29).
4. Resynthesis and packaging: in the smooth endoplasmic reticulum of the enterocyte, fatty acids are activated to acyl-CoA and re-esterified with 2-monoacylglycerol to triglyceride (the monoacylglycerol pathway); cholesterol is esterified, and lysophospholipids are resynthesized to phospholipids. Triglyceride, cholesterol esters and fat-soluble vitamins are assembled with apolipoprotein B-48 under the action of microsomal triglyceride transfer protein (MTP), and with surface phospholipid and other apolipoproteins mature in the Golgi apparatus into **chylomicrons** ($80$–$600\,\mathrm{nm}$ in diameter, about 90% triglyceride).
5. Entry into lymph: chylomicrons are released by exocytosis into the intercellular space; they are too large to cross the capillary endothelium and enter the central lacteal, which has larger gaps, and pass through intestinal lymphatics and the thoracic duct into the left subclavian vein, bypassing the liver and entering the systemic circulation directly. After a fatty meal, lymph and plasma are milky because of the large number of chylomicrons. The fate of chylomicrons in the blood is covered in Chapter 29.

Medium-chain fatty acids (6–12 carbons) are more water-soluble and are absorbed without micelles; they are not re-esterified in the enterocyte and enter the portal vein directly, so medium-chain triglycerides are used as nutritional supplements for patients with insufficient bile or pancreatic secretion. Genetic defects of MTP or apolipoprotein B (abetalipoproteinemia) make chylomicrons impossible to assemble, lipids accumulate in the enterocytes, and absorption of fat and fat-soluble vitamins is impaired.

## 28.6. Absorption of Vitamins and Minerals

### 28.6.1. Vitamins

Fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K) enter micelles and chylomicrons with the lipids, so any disorder of lipid absorption (bile deficiency, pancreatic insufficiency, steatorrhea) causes their deficiency; deficiency of vitamin K appears first (fewer clotting factors, [Chapter 11](/7.14.11)). Most water-soluble vitamins are absorbed by specific transporters in the upper small intestine (for example vitamin C through the $\ce{Na+}$-dependent SVCT1 and folate through the $\ce{H+}$-coupled PCFT), and some vitamin K and B vitamins come from synthesis by the colonic microbiota.

The absorption of vitamin $\mathrm{B_{12}}$ (**cobalamin**) is the most complex and requires the successive participation of the stomach, pancreas and ileum:

1. Dietary $\mathrm{B_{12}}$ is bound to protein; in the stomach, acid and pepsin release it, and it is immediately bound by R protein (haptocorrin) from saliva and gastric juice, which at acid pH has a higher affinity for $\mathrm{B_{12}}$ than intrinsic factor does;
2. In the duodenum, trypsin degrades the R protein, and the $\mathrm{B_{12}}$ released binds intrinsic factor secreted by parietal cells (Chapter 27); the complex resists digestion by proteases;
3. In the terminal ileum, the intrinsic factor–$\mathrm{B_{12}}$ complex binds its receptor on enterocytes (the cubilin–amnionless complex, cubam) and is absorbed by receptor-mediated endocytosis; in the cell, $\mathrm{B_{12}}$ is transferred to transcobalamin II and enters the blood.

A person needs only about $1$–$3\,\mathrm{\mu g}$ of $\mathrm{B_{12}}$ per day, while the liver stores about $2$–$5\,\mathrm{mg}$, enough for 3–5 years. Hence after gastrectomy, autoimmune gastritis (intrinsic factor deficiency), pancreatic insufficiency or ileal resection, the megaloblastic anemia and neuropathy caused by $\mathrm{B_{12}}$ deficiency appear only after several years.

### 28.6.2. Iron

The diet contains about $10$–$20\,\mathrm{mg}$ of iron per day, of which only about $1$–$2\,\mathrm{mg}$ are absorbed, exactly compensating the amount lost daily through shed cells and bleeding ([Chapter 11](/7.14.11)). The body has no active route for iron excretion, so iron balance depends entirely on regulation of absorption. Iron is absorbed in the duodenum and upper jejunum:

- Nonheme iron: dietary $\ce{Fe^3+}$ has low solubility and is reduced to $\ce{Fe^2+}$ at the apical membrane by a ferric reductase (duodenal cytochrome b, Dcytb) and enters the cell through the divalent metal transporter DMT1, cotransported with $\ce{H+}$ (gastric acid and vitamin C promote this process; phytate and the polyphenols of tea and coffee inhibit it);
- Heme iron: the heme of meat is absorbed as an intact molecule (with high absorption, about 20%–30%), and the iron is released in the cell by heme oxygenase;
- Intracellular iron is either stored as ferritin (and lost when the cell is shed) or exported through the basolateral iron transporter ferroportin, then oxidized to $\ce{Fe^3+}$ by the membrane ferroxidase hephaestin and bound to plasma transferrin.

The core of regulation is hepcidin, secreted by the liver: when body iron is adequate or inflammation is present, the liver secretes more hepcidin, which binds ferroportin and causes its endocytosis and degradation, so iron in the enterocytes cannot enter the blood and is lost with the shed cells; in iron deficiency or accelerated erythropoiesis hepcidin falls and absorption rises. This is a negative feedback in which body iron stores are the regulated variable; insufficient hepcidin (hereditary hemochromatosis) leads to excessive iron absorption and deposition in organs.

### 28.6.3. Calcium

The diet contains about $1\,\mathrm{g}$ of calcium per day, of which about 25%–35% is absorbed. There are two routes of absorption. In the duodenum there is a transcellular active route: the apical $\ce{Ca^2+}$ channel TRPV6 lets $\ce{Ca^2+}$ enter the cell down its gradient, the intracellular calcium-binding protein (calbindin-D<sub>9k</sub>) carries it to the basolateral membrane while buffering cytosolic $\ce{Ca^2+}$, and it is extruded by the plasma membrane $\ce{Ca^2+}$-ATPase and the $\ce{Na+}$/$\ce{Ca^2+}$ exchanger; all components of this route are induced by the active form of vitamin D (1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D), so it plays the main role when calcium intake is low. In the jejunum and ileum, $\ce{Ca^2+}$ is absorbed passively down its concentration gradient through the paracellular pathway, which contributes most when calcium intake is high. The hormonal regulation of calcium homeostasis is covered in Chapter 36.

## 28.7. The Colon: Absorption, Feces and the Gut Microbiota

### 28.7.1. Colonic Absorption and Feces

Of the roughly $1.5\,\mathrm{L}$ of chyme entering the colon each day, most of the water and electrolytes are absorbed in the proximal half (by mechanisms similar to those of the ileum), and the distal half mainly stores feces. The colon absorbs $\ce{Na+}$ and $\ce{Cl-}$ and secretes $\ce{K+}$ and $\ce{HCO3-}$; because the distal colonic epithelium is tight and regulated by aldosterone, the fecal $[\ce{Na+}]$ is very low and the $[\ce{K+}]$ is relatively high.

Normal feces are about 75% water and 25% solids. Of the solids, about 30% are bacteria (dead and alive), about 30% are indigestible food residue (mainly fiber), about 20% are lipids (derived mainly from bacteria and shed cells rather than from food), about 20% are inorganic matter (calcium salts and phosphates), and about 2%–3% are protein. The brown color of feces comes from stercobilin and urobilin, bacterial metabolites of bilirubin, and the odor from indole, skatole, mercaptans and hydrogen sulfide produced by bacteria.

Diarrhea can be classified by mechanism: osmotic diarrhea (unabsorbable solutes remain in the lumen, for example lactose intolerance or ingestion of magnesium salts or lactulose); secretory diarrhea (toxins increase crypt secretion through cAMP or cGMP, for example cholera and enterotoxigenic *Escherichia coli*); inflammatory diarrhea (mucosal damage, with decreased absorption and increased exudation); and motility-related diarrhea (transit is too fast and contact time insufficient). Secretory diarrhea persists during fasting and osmotic diarrhea disappears, a simple clinical way of distinguishing the two.

### 28.7.2. Physiological Roles of the Gut Microbiota

The bacterial density in the colon is about $10^{11}$–$10^{12}$ per gram of contents, mainly anaerobes, with a total number comparable to that of human cells ([7.11.32](/7.11.32)). For the physiology of the host, their most important role is the **fermentation** of sugars not digested in the small intestine (dietary fiber, resistant starch, oligosaccharides), producing **short-chain fatty acids** (acetate, propionate and butyrate, about 60:20:20), $\ce{H2}$, $\ce{CO2}$ and $\ce{CH4}$. Short-chain fatty acids are almost completely absorbed in the colon (and at the same time promote the absorption of $\ce{Na+}$ and water): butyrate is the main energy source of colonic epithelial cells, propionate goes to the liver through the portal vein as a substrate for **gluconeogenesis**, and acetate enters the systemic circulation and is oxidized by peripheral tissues; they also act on receptors of L cells and promote the secretion of GLP-1 and PYY. In humans, short-chain fatty acids supply about 5%–10% of daily energy needs, and in herbivores more than 70% (Section 28.8). In addition, the microbiota synthesizes vitamin K and some B vitamins, converts primary bile acids to secondary bile acids (Chapter 29), and occupies ecological niches that resist colonization by pathogens. The detailed mechanisms of these actions and the consequences of dysbiosis are covered in [7.11.32](/7.11.32).

## 28.8. Evolution and Comparison

### 28.8.1. Diet and the Digestive System

The structure of the digestive system is closely related to diet: the food of carnivores is easily digested and rich in nutrients, and their intestines are short and simple; the food of herbivores consists mainly of cell walls and is low in nutrient concentration, and because vertebrates cannot themselves synthesize enzymes that hydrolyze cellulose, they depend on fermentation by symbiotic microbes and therefore have long, complex intestines and specialized fermentation chambers. As the ratio of intestinal length to body length, cats have about 4, dogs about 6, humans about 5–6, pigs about 15, and sheep and goats about 25–30. The intestine of omnivorous humans is intermediate, with a small colon and a long small intestine, close to that of primates that eat mainly high-quality food; some have suggested that this is related to the use of fire by human ancestors to process food and increase its digestibility.

**Definition 28.8.1 (Foregut and Hindgut Fermentation)** In herbivores, when the fermentation chamber of the symbiotic microbes is in the stomach or before it (food is fermented first and then digested and absorbed in the stomach and small intestine), the arrangement is called **foregut fermentation**; when the fermentation chamber is in the cecum and colon after the small intestine (food is first digested and absorbed in the small intestine, and the remainder is then fermented), it is called **hindgut fermentation**.

The two designs each have advantages and disadvantages:

| | Foregut fermentation | Hindgut fermentation |
|---|---|---|
| Examples | Ruminants (cattle, sheep, deer), camels, kangaroos, langurs, sloths, hoatzin | Horses, rhinoceroses, rabbits, rodents, elephants, many primates |
| Products of the microbes | Short-chain fatty acids are absorbed in the fermentation chamber; the microbes themselves then enter the stomach and small intestine and are digested, becoming a source of protein and vitamins for the host | Short-chain fatty acids are absorbed in the large intestine; microbial protein and vitamins are excreted in the feces and cannot be digested (unless by coprophagy) |
| For easily digested components | Sugar, starch and protein are also fermented first by microbes, with energy loss (as $\ce{CH4}$ and heat) | Easily digested components are first absorbed directly by the host in the small intestine, with high efficiency |
| For plant toxins | Microbes can degrade some toxins before absorption | Toxins are first absorbed in the small intestine |
| Passage rate | Slow; low-quality forage can be fermented thoroughly, but intake is limited | Fast; large amounts of low-quality forage can be eaten, making up for low digestibility by quantity |

In the rumen of ruminants, microbes ferment cellulose to short-chain fatty acids, which supply 60%–80% of the host's energy; the microbes of the rumen enter the abomasum and small intestine with the chyme and are digested, supplying more than half of the host's protein. To digest bacteria, the abomasum of ruminants expresses lysozyme in large amounts, and the lysozyme gene has acquired properties of adaptation to acid and resistance to pepsin during evolution; interestingly, the gastric lysozymes of langurs and the hoatzin (a South American bird that eats leaves and ferments them in its crop), which also live by foregut fermentation, have independently evolved similar amino acid substitutions, a classic example of **convergent evolution** at the molecular level.

**Definition 28.8.2 (Coprophagy)** The behavior of an animal eating its own feces (or those of conspecifics) to recover the protein and vitamins synthesized by hindgut microbes and the nutrients that were not fully digested is called **coprophagy**.

The rabbit is an extreme example of hindgut fermentation: it has a very large cecum, and the colon separates the chyme by particle size; large fiber particles quickly form hard feces and are excreted, whereas small particles and microbes are carried back to the cecum by retrograde peristalsis for further fermentation and are then excreted as soft feces (cecotropes) wrapped in mucus; the rabbit eats the soft feces directly from the anus (cecotrophy) and obtains microbial protein and B vitamins, and the soft feces eaten each day can account for 15%–20% of its protein intake. Preventing rats and rabbits from coprophagy slows their growth and causes vitamin deficiency.

### 28.8.2. Phenotypic Plasticity of the Digestive System: Pythons

Maintaining the digestive tract is expensive: intestinal tissue has a high metabolic rate and the epithelium is renewed every few days. Animals that eat rarely therefore let the digestive tract atrophy between meals and rebuild it rapidly after feeding. The Burmese python can swallow prey equal to 25%–100% of its own body mass and then not eat for weeks to months. Studies by Secor and Diamond found that within 1–2 days after feeding, the mass of the python's small intestine roughly doubles, enterocytes enlarge, microvillus length increases several-fold, and the activity of nutrient transporters per unit area increases several-fold to tenfold; the liver, pancreas, kidney and heart also enlarge (ventricular mass increases by about 40%). After digestion is complete, these organs return to the fasted state within a few days. The metabolic rate after feeding can rise to 10–40 times the resting value (depending on the size of the meal) and persist for one to two weeks; this postprandial increase in metabolism (specific dynamic action, Chapter 30) consumes about 20%–30% of the energy of the food, a considerable part of it for rebuilding the digestive system. Frequently feeding snakes (such as garter snakes) show no such large changes. Pythons have thus become a model for studying rapid organ growth and atrophy.

Birds show similar plasticity: shorebirds before migration let the digestive organs atrophy and reduce body mass before a long flight, rebuild the digestive tract within a few days after arriving at a stopover site, and then "refuel" rapidly (Chapter 29). Many small birds also have a feature that differs from mammals: their small intestine is short, but the permeability of the paracellular pathway is very high, and more than half of water-soluble nutrients such as glucose are absorbed passively by the paracellular route, which is thought to be an adaptation to the constraints that flight places on body mass and intestinal length.

### 28.8.3. Termites and Symbiotic Microbes

Termites feed on wood, in which cellulose and hemicellulose make up about 70% and nitrogen is scarce. The hindgut of more primitive termites ("lower termites") contains large numbers of symbiotic flagellate protists (parabasalids and oxymonads), which engulf wood particles and ferment cellulose inside the cell to acetate, $\ce{H2}$ and $\ce{CO2}$; symbiotic bacteria reduce $\ce{H2}$ and $\ce{CO2}$ to more acetate (reductive acetogenesis), the acetate is absorbed by the termite and provides most of its energy, and nitrogen-fixing bacteria supplement nitrogen. "Higher termites" have lost the protists and rely on bacteria and their own cellulases, and some groups (such as the Macrotermitinae) cultivate fungi in the nest to predigest the lignocellulose. The microbes of the termite hindgut are transmitted by the exchange of hindgut contents between individuals (trophallaxis), and the hindgut lining is shed at each molt so that the microbes must be reacquired, which is thought to be one of the reasons for the origin of termite social life. Termites also have their own cellulase genes (expressed in the salivary glands and midgut), showing that "animals cannot synthesize cellulase" is not absolute; but most animals still depend on microbes for the use of cellulose.

## Questions

1. A person has had the terminal $100\,\mathrm{cm}$ of ileum resected. Predict which absorptive disorders will appear. Which are short-term, and which appear only years later?

   **Answer** The terminal ileum is the only site of absorption of bile salts (ASBT) and vitamin $\mathrm{B_{12}}$ (intrinsic factor receptor). (1) Bile salts: bile salts that cannot be reabsorbed enter the colon and stimulate colonic secretion of $\ce{Cl-}$ and water, causing bile acid diarrhea; with longer resections hepatic synthesis cannot compensate for the loss (at most 4–6 g/d), the bile acid pool shrinks, micelle formation is insufficient, and steatorrhea and fat-soluble vitamin deficiency appear, all of which occur soon after surgery. (2) Vitamin $\mathrm{B_{12}}$: the liver stores $2$–$5\,\mathrm{mg}$ and the daily requirement is $1$–$3\,\mathrm{\mu g}$, so the megaloblastic anemia and neurological symptoms of deficiency appear only after 3–5 years, and lifelong injections are needed. (3) Electroneutral NaCl absorption and water absorption in the ileum are reduced, and loss of the ileocecal valve (if resected as well) allows colonic bacteria into the small intestine, aggravating diarrhea. In addition, unabsorbed fatty acids bind calcium in the colon, leaving oxalate free to be absorbed in large amounts and predisposing to renal calcium oxalate stones.

2. Using the plug-flow model in the figure of Section 28.3, explain why a patient with jejunal resection is more likely than one with ileal resection to develop osmotic diarrhea after an oral load of glucose.

   **Answer** Normally most glucose is absorbed in the upper jejunum (for loads of 50–150 mmol/L, 95% of absorption takes place within 60–130 cm), and the ileum is the "reserve" length. After jejunal resection the chyme goes directly into the ileum, whose SGLT1 density and absorptive area (shorter villi, fewer folds) are both smaller, corresponding to a lower $V_{\max}$ in the model; saturated transport cannot absorb the entire load in the remaining intestine, and unabsorbed glucose enters the colon, where it causes osmotic diarrhea and is fermented by bacteria to produce gas. After ileal resection the jejunum is intact, glucose has been largely absorbed before the resected site, and this problem does not readily arise (the problems of ileal resection are mainly those of bile salts and $\mathrm{B_{12}}$). Over time the villi of the remaining intestine undergo hyperplasia (intestinal adaptation) and absorptive capacity is partly restored.

3. A lipid-lowering drug inhibits MTP in the intestinal epithelium. Predict its effects on lipid absorption and blood lipids, and its possible side effects.

   **Answer** MTP loads triglyceride onto apolipoprotein B-48 to assemble chylomicrons, and in the liver assembles apoB-100–containing VLDL. Inhibiting MTP prevents the intestine from secreting chylomicrons and the liver from secreting VLDL, and plasma triglyceride and LDL cholesterol fall sharply (this is the principle by which lomitapide treats homozygous familial hypercholesterolemia). Side effects: resynthesized triglyceride accumulates in the enterocytes, causing steatorrhea and bloating; absorption of fat-soluble vitamins decreases; triglyceride cannot be exported from the liver, causing fatty liver and elevated transaminases. This is the same picture as in hereditary abetalipoproteinemia.

4. Compare the efficiency with which a ruminant and a horse use the same grass. Why can a horse survive on very poor-quality forage on which a cow would starve?

   **Answer** Cattle are foregut fermenters: food stays in the rumen a long time, only particles ground fine by rumination can leave, fiber is fermented fairly completely, the digestibility per unit of food is high, and protein is obtained by digesting rumen microbes. But this also limits the rate of passage: coarse, hard-to-digest fiber occupies the rumen for a long time, and intake is limited. The horse is a hindgut fermenter: food passes quickly, easily digested components are absorbed directly in the small intestine, and fiber is only partly fermented in the cecum and colon before being excreted, so digestibility per unit of food is low, but large quantities can be eaten. When forage is very poor (much fiber, little protein), the cow is limited by rumen capacity and passage rate and cannot eat enough; the horse obtains enough easily digested components from large amounts of low-quality feed by increasing intake. Thus ruminants have the advantage on good feed, and large hindgut fermenters on very poor feed.

5. (Open question) Pythons let the small intestine atrophy between meals and rebuild it after feeding. Estimate from an energetic standpoint when this strategy is advantageous, and discuss why frequently feeding animals do not adopt it.

   **Answer** Outline: suppose maintaining a complete, ready-to-use digestive system costs energy $M$ per day (intestinal tissue has a high metabolic rate and can account for more than 10%–20% of resting metabolism), and the interval between meals is $T$ days; atrophy can save nearly $MT$ of energy, whereas rebuilding the digestive tract after each meal requires a one-time cost $C$ (protein synthesis, cell proliferation), and absorption is temporarily less efficient during rebuilding. When $MT > C$, the atrophy–rebuilding strategy is advantageous: the rarer the feeding and the longer the interval between meals, the more worthwhile it is. Pythons eat once every few weeks to months, so $T$ is very large and the strategy pays; for an animal that eats daily $T$ is very small, the cost of rebuilding cannot be offset by the maintenance cost saved, and repeated atrophy and rebuilding would delay digestion at each meal. In addition, the barrier function of an atrophied digestive tract declines (bacterial translocation), a risk worth taking only during long fasts.
