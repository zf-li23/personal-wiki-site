The menstrual cycle of the previous chapter prepares for pregnancy: ovulation, the endometrium of the luteal phase and progesterone are all waiting for an embryo. This chapter deals with the physiology between mother and fetus after the embryo arrives: fertilization and implantation (Section 38.1); the placenta, a temporary organ that serves as the fetus's lung, kidney, gut and endocrine gland (Section 38.2), and the hormones it secretes (Section 38.3); the adaptations of the maternal systems (Section 38.4); parturition, which ends pregnancy (Section 38.5); lactation, which takes over from the placenta in nourishing the newborn (Section 38.6); and finally the principles of contraception (Section 38.7) and the transition of circulation and respiration at birth (Section 38.8). As the last chapter of the endocrine and reproductive part, Section 38.9 makes a more systematic comparison of the reproductive modes of the various animal groups.

The cell biology of fertilization and the early embryo is in [7.5.4](/7.5.4), and the development of the extraembryonic membranes is in [7.5](/7.5); fetal hemoglobin is in Chapter 19 ([7.14.19](/7.14.19#sec-19.3.5)), the efficiency of exchangers is in Chapter 17 (Eq. 17.3.1), the secreting cells of oxytocin and prolactin are in Chapter 34, and the mobilization of calcium in pregnancy and lactation is in Chapter 36.

## 38.1. Fertilization and Implantation

### 38.1.1. Gamete Transport and Fertilization

One ejaculation delivers over a hundred million sperm to the vagina, and only a few hundred reach the ampulla of the oviduct. Sperm swim in the thin cervical mucus of the ovulatory period and are propelled by contractions of the uterus and oviducts, reaching the oviduct in as little as a few minutes; some sperm are temporarily stored in cervical crypts and the isthmus of the oviduct and are released over the following days. Sperm can survive in the female tract for about 3–5 days, and the ovulated egg can be fertilized only for about 12–24 h, so the "window" for conception runs from about 5 days before ovulation to 1 day after.

Freshly ejaculated sperm cannot yet fertilize; they undergo **capacitation** in the reproductive tract: cholesterol is removed from the sperm membrane by albumin and other acceptors, membrane fluidity increases, intracellular $\ce{HCO3-}$ and $\ce{Ca^2+}$ rise (through the sperm-specific CatSper channel), and the sperm show high-amplitude asymmetric beating of the flagellum (hyperactivated motility). A capacitated sperm passes through the corona radiata, binds the zona pellucida, undergoes the **acrosome reaction**, penetrates the zona pellucida and fuses with the egg membrane. The egg then undergoes the **cortical granule reaction**, the zona pellucida hardens, preventing **polyspermy**, and the second meiotic division is completed. The molecular details of these processes are in [7.5.4](/7.5.4).

### 38.1.2. Embryo Transport and Implantation

The fertilized egg cleaves as it is propelled along the oviduct by cilia and smooth muscle, and after about 3–4 days enters the uterus as a **morula**; in the uterine cavity it develops into a **blastocyst**, which "hatches" from the **zona pellucida** at about day 5–6 after fertilization. The speed of transport in the oviduct is controlled by the ratio of estrogen to progesterone; when transport is blocked, the embryo implants in the oviduct (ectopic pregnancy), one of the main dangers of early pregnancy.

**Implantation** occurs about day 6–7 after fertilization, that is, day 20–21 of the menstrual cycle. The endometrium receives the blastocyst only during a brief "implantation window" (about days 20–24 of the cycle): under progesterone (acting through progesterone receptors induced beforehand by estrogen), pinopodes appear on the epithelial surface, adhesion molecules and cytokines such as LIF are expressed, and stromal cells begin to decidualize (enlarge, accumulate glycogen and lipid, and secrete prolactin and IGF-binding protein). The trophoblast of the blastocyst attaches to the epithelium and differentiates into the invading syncytiotrophoblast (a multinucleate syncytium) and the cytotrophoblast beneath it; the syncytiotrophoblast erodes maternal vessels and maternal blood flows into its lacunae, the beginning of the hemochorial placenta (Section 38.2).

The embryo is half foreign to the mother: it expresses paternal **antigens** and yet is not rejected. The reasons include: the syncytiotrophoblast, which is in contact with maternal blood, does not express classical MHC class I and II molecules, and the extravillous trophoblast expresses the nonclassical HLA-G, which inhibits killing by uterine natural killer cells; regulatory T cells increase in the decidua; and progesterone and local tryptophan metabolism also suppress immune responses. These belong to immunology and will be discussed in [7.15](/7.15).

## 38.2. The Placenta

### 38.2.1. Structure and Blood Flow

The human placenta at term is disc-shaped, about $20\,\mathrm{cm}$ in diameter and weighing about $500\,\mathrm{g}$. On the fetal side, the two umbilical arteries in the umbilical cord carry the fetal venous blood to the placenta and branch into the tree-like villi, and the capillaries in the villi reunite into a single umbilical vein that returns oxygenated blood to the fetus. On the maternal side, about 100 spiral arteries open into the intervillous space, and maternal blood gushes from the openings like a fountain, flows between the villi and leaves through the uterine veins; the intervillous space holds about $150\,\mathrm{mL}$ of maternal blood, which is renewed several times a minute. Maternal and fetal blood are separated by the syncytiotrophoblast on the villous surface, a little connective tissue and the endothelium of the fetal capillaries, and do not mix directly; at term the total surface area of the villi is about $12$–$14\,\mathrm{m^2}$, and the barrier is as thin as $2$–$5\,\mathrm{\mu m}$ at many sites.

A key event of pregnancy is remodeling of the spiral arteries: in early to mid pregnancy the extravillous trophoblast invades the spiral arteries, replaces their endothelium and destroys the smooth muscle and elastic tissue of the vessel wall, converting them into low-resistance channels that are several times wider and can no longer contract. Uterine blood flow then increases from about $50\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}}$ in the nonpregnant state to about $500$–$750\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}}$ at term ($10\%$–$15\%$ of **cardiac output**) and is no longer regulated by maternal vasoconstriction. Inadequate remodeling leaves the placenta poorly perfused and is an important cause of preeclampsia and fetal growth restriction.

### 38.2.2. Exchange of Substances

The placenta exchanges substances in the same ways as other epithelia:

| Substance | Mode | Remarks |
|---|---|---|
| $\ce{O2}$, $\ce{CO2}$ | **Simple diffusion** | Limited mainly by blood flow on the two sides (**perfusion limitation**, Chapter 19); see Section 38.2.3 |
| Water and electrolytes | Diffusion, channels, transporters | Several liters of water are exchanged per day at term |
| Glucose | Facilitated diffusion (GLUT1, membranes on both sides) | Fetal blood glucose is about $70\%$–$80\%$ of the mother's; the fetus itself carries out almost no gluconeogenesis and obtains glucose through this concentration gradient |
| Amino acids | Secondary active transport | Fetal plasma amino acids are higher than maternal |
| Fatty acids | Placental lipases hydrolyze maternal lipoproteins and transporters take up the products | Long-chain polyunsaturated fatty acids (needed for brain development) are preferentially transported |
| $\ce{Ca^2+}$, iron | Active transport | Fetal plasma calcium is higher than maternal; iron is endocytosed through the transferrin receptor |
| IgG | Transcytosis mediated by the neonatal Fc receptor | Provides passive immunity to the newborn |
| Hormones | Most protein hormones cannot cross; steroids are metabolized by the placenta | Placental 11β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase 2 inactivates maternal **cortisol** (Chapter 35); thyroid hormone crosses in small amounts |

The placenta is also an imperfect barrier: alcohol, nicotine, many drugs, certain viruses (rubella, cytomegalovirus, Zika virus) and *Toxoplasma* can all cross, and the effects are greatest during embryonic organogenesis (weeks 3–8 of pregnancy).

### 38.2.3. Oxygen Exchange: Geometry of Flow and Fetal Hemoglobin

The $P_{\ce{O2}}$ of fetal umbilical vein blood is only about $30\,\mathrm{mmHg}$ ($4\,\mathrm{kPa}$; $1\,\mathrm{mmHg} = 0.133\,\mathrm{kPa}$), much lower than that of maternal arterial blood. The efficiency of the placenta as a gas exchanger depends on the geometric relation of the two blood streams, which is entirely analogous to the comparison of fish gills, avian lungs and mammalian alveoli (Eq. 17.3.1):

- In the human intervillous space the maternal blood is roughly a mixed "pool" through which the fetal capillaries pass (multivillous pool exchange), and fetal blood can at most equilibrate with maternal blood leaving the pool, that is, the uterine vein;
- in the labyrinthine placenta of rabbits and guinea pigs maternal and fetal blood flow roughly in opposite directions (**countercurrent exchange**), and the $P_{\ce{O2}}$ of fetal blood at the exit can exceed that of maternal blood at its exit; in experiments umbilical vein $P_{\ce{O2}}$ has been measured to be higher than uterine vein $P_{\ce{O2}}$;
- in the cotyledonary placenta of sheep the relation of the two streams is more complex, close to a mixture of **concurrent exchange** and **crosscurrent exchange**.

The linear model (Eq. (17.4)) is only a qualitative comparison, because hemoglobin makes the $\ce{O2}$ capacity of blood vary with partial pressure. The model gives two conclusions. First, the human placenta is not an efficient oxygen exchanger, and the fetus lives at low $P_{\ce{O2}}$; its countermeasure is oxygen-carrying capacity rather than partial pressure: fetal blood has a high hemoglobin concentration (about $170\,\mathrm{g\,L^{-1}}$) and fetal hemoglobin has a low **P50** (Chapter 19), so at a $P_{\ce{O2}}$ of only $30\,\mathrm{mmHg}$ umbilical vein blood is still about $80\%$ saturated; the combined output of the two fetal ventricles per unit body weight is about $450\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}\,kg^{-1}}$, several times the adult's (about $70\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}\,kg^{-1}}$), and the tissue **oxygen extraction ratio** is high. Second, because exchange is close to **perfusion limitation**, fetal oxygen supply depends mainly on the blood flow on the two sides: a reduction in uteroplacental blood flow (preeclampsia, maternal hypotension, uterine contractions) matters more than a mild fall in maternal arterial $P_{\ce{O2}}$. The placenta also has a "double **Bohr effect**" and a "double **Haldane effect**": maternal blood, after taking up $\ce{CO2}$, has lower affinity and releases $\ce{O2}$, while fetal blood, after giving up $\ce{CO2}$, has higher affinity and binds $\ce{O2}$, and both promote the transfer of $\ce{O2}$ to the fetus and $\ce{CO2}$ to the mother; maternal hyperventilation in pregnancy (Section 38.4) lowers maternal $P_{\ce{CO2}}$ and further favors fetal elimination of $\ce{CO2}$.

## 38.3. Endocrinology of Pregnancy

### 38.3.1. Human Chorionic Gonadotropin and Rescue of the Corpus Luteum

Without a signal from the embryo, the corpus luteum regresses about 14 days after ovulation, progesterone falls, the endometrium is shed, and the embryo is lost with it. The embryo prevents all this with a hormone:

**Definition 38.3.1 (Human Chorionic Gonadotropin)** The glycoprotein hormone secreted by the syncytiotrophoblast of the embryo, whose α subunit is the same as that of LH and others and whose β subunit is highly homologous to that of LH (but has 24 additional amino acids at the C terminus and is heavily glycosylated), is called **human chorionic gonadotropin** (hCG). It acts on the LH receptor of the corpus luteum and keeps the corpus luteum secreting progesterone and estrogen in early pregnancy.

hCG can be detected in maternal blood about 8–10 days after fertilization (soon after implantation), which is the basis of pregnancy tests; in early pregnancy it doubles about every two days, peaks at weeks 8–10, and then falls to a lower plateau. The C-terminal extension of the β subunit carries many sialylated glycan chains, which makes the **half-life** of hCG (about $24$–$36\,\mathrm{h}$) much longer than that of LH (about $20$–$60\,\mathrm{min}$). The actions of hCG:

- It rescues the corpus luteum, making it the corpus luteum of pregnancy, and maintains progesterone secretion.
- It stimulates the Leydig cells of the testes of a male fetus to secrete testosterone, promoting masculinization of the reproductive organs in early pregnancy (when the fetus's own LH is not yet secreted).
- It has a weak action on the TSH receptor, and when hCG is very high (the peak of early pregnancy, multiple pregnancy, hydatidiform mole) it can cause transient **hyperthyroidism**; high hCG is also associated with nausea and vomiting in early pregnancy.

By weeks 7–9 of pregnancy the progesterone synthesized by the placenta is sufficient and the role of the corpus luteum is taken over by the placenta (the "luteal–placental shift"); removal of the corpus luteum before this causes abortion, and after it does not.

### 38.3.2. Progesterone and Estrogen

The placenta is an "incomplete" steroid endocrine gland:

- Progesterone: the syncytiotrophoblast uses LDL cholesterol from maternal plasma as substrate (the placenta itself synthesizes little cholesterol) and forms progesterone through CYP11A1 and 3β-HSD, about $250\,\mathrm{mg}$ per day at term, with a plasma concentration about ten times that of the luteal phase. Progesterone maintains the decidua, inhibits contraction of the myometrium (reducing oxytocin receptors and **gap junctions** and hyperpolarizing muscle cells), suppresses the maternal immune response to the embryo, and promotes development of the mammary alveoli.
- Estrogen: the placenta lacks CYP17 and cannot convert progestogens to androgens, so it needs the help of the fetus to synthesize estrogen.

**Definition 38.3.2 (Fetoplacental Unit)** The synthesis of estrogen in pregnancy requires a division of labor between fetus and placenta: the fetal zone of the fetal adrenal synthesizes DHEAS, part of which is 16α-hydroxylated in the fetal liver; these precursors enter the placenta and are converted by sulfatase and aromatase to estriol, estradiol and estrone. The whole of this cooperation is called the **fetoplacental unit**.

At term the fetal adrenal is as large as an adult's, and most of it is the fetal zone, which exists only in fetal life and regresses rapidly after birth. Estrogen in maternal blood and urine in late pregnancy is mainly estriol, about $90\%$ of which depends on precursors from the fetal adrenal and liver, and it was therefore used as an index of fetal health; mothers of anencephalic fetuses (lacking fetal pituitary and ACTH, with atrophy of the fetal zone) have very low estriol. Estrogen promotes growth and blood flow of the uterus and development of the mammary ducts and prepares for parturition at the end of pregnancy (Section 38.5).

### 38.3.3. Human Placental Lactogen and Other Placental Hormones

Human placental lactogen (hPL, also called human chorionic somatomammotropin) is a protein homologous to **growth hormone** and **prolactin**, secreted by the syncytiotrophoblast; at term the daily secretion can reach $1$–$3\,\mathrm{g}$, the largest amount of any hormone in the human body. Its actions resemble those of growth hormone: it lowers maternal insulin sensitivity and promotes lipolysis, so that the mother uses more fat and leaves glucose and amino acids for the fetus; together with the placental growth hormone variant (which replaces maternal pituitary growth hormone), progesterone and **cortisol** it produces the **insulin resistance** of mid to late pregnancy (Section 38.4).

The placenta also secretes CRH (Section 38.5), GnRH, kisspeptin, **leptin**, relaxin (mainly from the corpus luteum, which loosens the pelvic ligaments and the cervix) and others, and can almost be regarded as a "temporary hypothalamus–pituitary."

## 38.4. Maternal Adaptations

Pregnancy changes almost every maternal system, most of the changes beginning before the demands of the fetus appear, which is a feedforward.

| System | Change (at term relative to nonpregnant) | Mechanism and significance |
|---|---|---|
| Blood volume | Increases by $40\%$–$50\%$ (about $1.5\,\mathrm{L}$), plasma more than red cells, and hemoglobin concentration falls (physiological anemia) | Estrogen and progesterone activate the **RAAS**, and the body retains about $900\,\mathrm{mmol}$ of sodium; a reserve for placental perfusion and blood loss at delivery |
| Cardiovascular | **Cardiac output** increases by $30\%$–$50\%$ (heart rate up $10$–$20$ beats/min, **stroke volume** increased), **total peripheral resistance** falls, blood pressure is lowest in mid pregnancy; in the supine position the enlarged uterus compresses the inferior vena cava and reduces venous return, which can cause hypotension | Progesterone, relaxin and nitric oxide dilate vessels; after remodeling of the spiral arteries the uterus becomes a low-resistance parallel branch |
| Kidney | **Renal plasma flow** and **GFR** increase by about $50\%$, plasma creatinine and urea fall; plasma osmolality falls by about $10\,\mathrm{mOsm\,kg^{-1}}$ | The threshold of **osmoreceptors** shifts downward (resetting of the **antidiuretic hormone** set point, Chapter 24); filtered glucose increases and glucosuria may occur |
| Respiration | Minute ventilation increases by $30\%$–$50\%$ (mainly **tidal volume**), arterial $P_{\ce{CO2}}$ falls to about $30\,\mathrm{mmHg}$ ($4\,\mathrm{kPa}$), a compensated **respiratory alkalosis**; oxygen consumption increases by about $20\%$; **functional residual capacity** falls because the diaphragm is elevated | Progesterone raises central sensitivity to $\ce{CO2}$ (Chapter 20); low maternal $P_{\ce{CO2}}$ favors fetal elimination of $\ce{CO2}$ |
| Metabolism | Early pregnancy is mainly anabolic (fat storage); in mid to late pregnancy insulin sensitivity falls by about $50\%$, β cells proliferate and insulin secretion more than doubles; during fasting there is a faster shift to fat and ketone bodies ("accelerated starvation") | hPL, placental growth hormone, progesterone and cortisol; when β cells cannot compensate fully, gestational diabetes occurs |
| Calcium | Intestinal calcium absorption approximately doubles; the term fetal skeleton contains about $30\,\mathrm{g}$ of calcium, most deposited in late pregnancy | 1,25-Dihydroxyvitamin D approximately doubles (1α-hydroxylation in placenta and maternal kidney, Chapter 36) |
| Coagulation | Clotting factors and fibrinogen increase and **fibrinolysis** decreases (hypercoagulable state) | Reduces blood loss at delivery, at the cost of an increased risk of venous thrombosis |
| Thyroid | Thyroxine-binding globulin increases (estrogen), total $\mathrm{T_4}$ rises while free $\mathrm{T_4}$ is essentially normal; iodine requirement increases | In early pregnancy the fetus depends on maternal **thyroid hormone** |

Maternal weight gain is about $11$–$16\,\mathrm{kg}$: fetus about $3.4\,\mathrm{kg}$, placenta about $0.6\,\mathrm{kg}$, amniotic fluid about $0.8\,\mathrm{kg}$, uterus and breasts about $1.4\,\mathrm{kg}$, increased blood and extracellular fluid about $2.5$–$3\,\mathrm{kg}$, and the rest fat reserves (in preparation for lactation). The additional energy expenditure of the whole pregnancy is about $300\,\mathrm{MJ}$ (about $75\,000\,\mathrm{kcal}$).

## 38.5. Parturition

### 38.5.1. Initiation of Parturition

Human gestation is about 40 weeks counted from the last menstrual period (about 38 weeks from fertilization). What determines the time of birth? The answer differs among mammals.

- Sheep: in the 1960s Liggins and colleagues found that destroying the pituitary of a fetal lamb or removing its adrenals could prolong gestation indefinitely, whereas infusing ACTH or **cortisol** into the fetal lamb caused premature labor. The fetal **hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis** matures at the end of gestation, fetal cortisol rises and induces placental CYP17, diverting progesterone toward estrogen: progesterone falls, estrogen rises, and the uterus is activated. That is, the fetus determines the time of its own birth, and this signal is at the same time the cortisol that matures the fetal lung (Chapter 35), so the time of birth and the time when the fetus is ready to breathe are linked by the same signal.
- Humans: the human placenta has no CYP17 and maternal plasma progesterone does not fall before labor, so the mechanism of sheep cannot be transferred directly. It is now thought that human labor is initiated by several processes together:
  1. The placental CRH clock: CRH secreted by the placenta increases exponentially in the second half of pregnancy, while the CRH-binding protein that binds it falls in the last weeks, so free CRH rises rapidly. McLean and colleagues (1995) found that the time of delivery can be predicted from the CRH level as early as mid pregnancy, and the CRH curve rises earlier in women who deliver preterm. Unlike hypothalamic CRH, placental CRH is promoted by cortisol in a positive feedback; it stimulates the fetal adrenal to secrete DHEAS (the substrate for estrogen) and cortisol, forming a feedforward loop.
  2. Functional progesterone withdrawal: plasma progesterone does not fall, but the response of the myometrium to progesterone declines, through changes in the ratio of progesterone receptor isoforms (PR-A relative to PR-B increases), coactivators and local metabolism, and signals of **inflammation** (NF-κB) oppose the action of progesterone. The progesterone receptor antagonist mifepristone can cause uterine contractions and cervical softening at any stage of pregnancy, which shows that progesterone is the "brake" that maintains pregnancy.
  3. Activation of the myometrium: once the action of estrogen predominates, the myometrium expresses a set of "contraction-associated proteins": the oxytocin receptor (about 100–200 times higher at term than in early pregnancy), connexin 43 (**gap junctions**, making the myometrium a syncytium that contracts synchronously), prostaglandin receptors and ion channels; mechanical stretch of the uterus also promotes their expression.
  4. Ripening of the cervix: prostaglandins ($\mathrm{PGE_2}$, $\mathrm{PGF_{2\alpha}}$), relaxin and inflammatory cells cause degradation and rearrangement of cervical collagen and an increase in **hyaluronic acid**, so the cervix softens, shortens and can dilate. Prostaglandin preparations are used clinically to ripen the cervix and induce labor.

### 38.5.2. Positive Feedback in Labor

Once labor begins, it is driven by **positive feedback** until completion:

**Definition 38.5.1 (Ferguson Reflex)** The presenting part of the fetus dilates the cervix and upper vagina, and sensory signals relayed through the spinal cord cause neuroendocrine cells of the paraventricular and supraoptic nuclei of the hypothalamus to release oxytocin; oxytocin strengthens uterine contraction and dilates the cervix further. This neuroendocrine reflex with positive feedback is called the **Ferguson reflex**.

Uterine contraction at the same time promotes release of **prostaglandins** by the decidua and fetal membranes, and prostaglandins in turn strengthen contraction, forming another positive feedback loop. The exit of the positive feedback is the delivery of the fetus and placenta: the stretch stimulus disappears and the reflex stops.

**Example 38.5.1 (A Bistable Model of Labor)** Let $O$ be plasma oxytocin, $U$ uterine contractile activity, $S$ cervical stretch, and $R$ the oxytocin receptor density of the myometrium,

$$
\tau_O\frac{\mathrm{d}O}{\mathrm{d}t} = O_{\mathrm{b}} + \alpha S - O, \qquad
\tau_U\frac{\mathrm{d}U}{\mathrm{d}t} = \frac{R\,O^{n}}{K^{n} + O^{n}} - U, \qquad
\tau_S\frac{\mathrm{d}S}{\mathrm{d}t} = \beta U - S,
$$
where $\tau_O$ is set by the half-life of oxytocin of about $4\,\mathrm{min}$, $O_{\mathrm{b}}$ is the basal secretion, and $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are the reflex and mechanical gains. Because the response of the uterus to oxytocin is sigmoidal, this loop has two stable states, a quiescent uterus and a uterus contracting continuously (labor), separated by an unstable threshold. When $R$ is small the quiescent state is stable; at the end of pregnancy $R$ keeps increasing, and at a critical value the quiescent state disappears (a **saddle-node bifurcation**) and the system spontaneously "jumps" to the labor state.

![Bistable model of the oxytocin positive feedback in labor (Example 38.5.1; parameters are illustrative). A: The oxytocin receptor density R of the myometrium (gray dotted) increases slowly over 40 h; when R crosses the critical value, uterine activity (red) and oxytocin (blue dashed) rise explosively to the labor state within 1–2 h, with almost no change in the system for a long time before; delivery (vertical line) removes the cervical stretch, the feedback loop is broken, and both fall rapidly. The model explains two features of labor: it begins suddenly after a very long preparatory period and is hard to stop once begun; and the principle of induction, that when receptor density is sufficient (the cervix is ripe) one sufficiently large perturbation can start the positive feedback, whereas when receptor density is insufficient the same perturbation causes only a brief response.

Oxytocin is not the only indispensable signal for labor: mice lacking oxytocin or its receptor deliver normally (but cannot eject milk); in humans uterine contractions weaken but do not stop completely when oxytocin receptor antagonists are used. The redundant design of labor keeps it from depending on a single signal.

After delivery the uterus contracts strongly under oxytocin and compresses the vessels at the placental bed, which is the main mechanism for preventing postpartum hemorrhage; injection of oxytocin immediately after delivery has become routine.

## 38.6. Lactation

### 38.6.1. Development of the Mammary Gland

The mammary gland consists of ducts and terminal alveoli, and the alveoli consist of milk-secreting epithelial cells and an outer layer of contractile myoepithelial cells. Development of the gland is controlled in stages by several hormones: at puberty estrogen (with **growth hormone**, **IGF-1** and cortisol) promotes branching of the ducts; in each luteal phase progesterone promotes development of a few alveoli; in pregnancy estrogen, progesterone, prolactin and hPL cause massive proliferation of ducts and alveoli, and the gland is ready to secrete.

After mid pregnancy the gland can already secrete a small amount of colostrum (the first stage of lactation), but massive milk secretion is inhibited by the high level of progesterone. At delivery the placenta is expelled, progesterone falls abruptly within a day or two, and under the combined action of prolactin, **cortisol** and **insulin** the gland begins to secrete milk in large amounts 2–3 days after delivery (the second stage of lactation, "milk coming in"). Colostrum is rich in secretory IgA, lactoferrin and leukocytes, and has much protein and little fat and lactose.

### 38.6.2. Prolactin and Milk Production

**Prolactin** is secreted by the lactotrophs of the **adenohypophysis**. Unlike other adenohypophyseal hormones it is controlled mainly by hypothalamic inhibition: dopaminergic neurons of the **arcuate nucleus** release dopamine into the **portal system**, which continuously inhibits prolactin secretion through D2 receptors (Chapter 34); after section of the pituitary stalk prolactin instead rises. In pregnancy estrogen causes lactotroph hyperplasia, and prolactin gradually rises more than tenfold. During nursing, suckling stimulates the sensory nerves of the nipple and inhibits dopamine release, and prolactin rises about 10–20-fold within $30\,\mathrm{min}$, promoting synthesis of the milk (lactose, casein, milk fat) needed for the next feed. Milk output is finally regulated locally by the degree of emptying: when milk is not removed, inhibitory factors in the milk and distension of the alveoli reduce secretion, so "supply follows demand."

Human milk output is about $600$–$800\,\mathrm{mL}$ per day and contains about $4\%$ fat, $7\%$ lactose and $1\%$ protein; full lactation requires about $2\,\mathrm{MJ}$ (about $500\,\mathrm{kcal}$) of extra energy per day, partly from the fat reserves of pregnancy. The calcium in milk, about $200$–$300\,\mathrm{mg}$ per day, comes mainly from resorption of the maternal skeleton: the mammary gland secretes parathyroid hormone-related peptide (PTHrP), low estrogen also increases bone resorption, and a lactating mother can lose $5\%$–$10\%$ of her bone mass, which recovers within a few months after weaning (Chapter 36).

### 38.6.3. The Milk Ejection Reflex

Milk is stored in the alveoli and ducts, and the infant cannot extract it by suckling alone; a neuroendocrine reflex is needed:

**Definition 38.6.1 (Milk Ejection Reflex)** Suckling stimulates the sensory nerves of the nipple, impulses are relayed through the spinal cord, and neuroendocrine cells of the paraventricular and supraoptic nuclei of the hypothalamus release oxytocin through the neurohypophysis; oxytocin makes the myoepithelial cells around the alveoli contract, squeezing milk into the ducts and lactiferous sinuses and ejecting it. This reflex is called the **milk ejection reflex**.

It takes only about $30\,\mathrm{s}$ to $1\,\mathrm{min}$ from the start of suckling to ejection of milk. The reflex can be conditioned: a mother may eject milk when she hears her baby cry or even when she thinks of nursing; pain, anxiety and stress (sympathetic nerves and **epinephrine**) inhibit it. Mice lacking oxytocin can secrete milk but cannot eject it, and the pups starve, which shows that this reflex is indispensable for lactation.

### 38.6.4. Suppression of Reproduction by Nursing

Frequent nursing suppresses GnRH pulses (the neural signal of suckling acting through kisspeptin neurons, and the raised prolactin); LH pulses weaken, follicles cannot develop fully, ovulation does not occur, and lactational amenorrhea results. When a mother fully breastfeeds in the first 6 months after delivery and menstruation has not returned, the probability of pregnancy is below about $2\%$ (the lactational amenorrhea method of contraception); in populations without modern contraception, nursing is the main factor spacing births.

## 38.7. Physiological Principles of Contraception

Different methods of contraception act on different links of the reproductive chain:

| Method | Link acted on | Physiological principle |
|---|---|---|
| Combined oral contraceptive (estrogen + progestogen) | Ovulation | **Negative feedback** suppresses FSH and LH, preventing follicle development and the LH surge; progestogen makes cervical mucus viscous (Chapter 37) |
| Progestogen only (oral, injection, subcutaneous implant, progestogen-releasing intrauterine device) | Cervical mucus, endometrium, ovulation | Cervical mucus viscous, endometrium atrophic; ovulation suppressed at higher doses |
| Copper intrauterine device | Fertilization and implantation | Copper ions are toxic to sperm and egg and cause a sterile inflammatory reaction in the endometrium |
| Barrier methods (condoms and others) | Sperm transport | Prevent sperm from entering the reproductive tract; condoms also prevent sexually transmitted diseases |
| Emergency contraception (levonorgestrel, ulipristal) | Ovulation | Taken before the LH surge it can delay or prevent ovulation; ineffective against an established implantation |
| Fertility awareness methods | Conception window | Use basal body temperature, cervical mucus and cycle length to avoid the roughly 6 days around ovulation |
| Sterilization (tubal or vasa deferentia ligation) | Gamete transport | Permanently blocks the passage of gametes; vasectomy does not affect testosterone secretion |
| Lactational amenorrhea | Ovulation | Suckling suppresses GnRH pulses (Section 38.6.4) |

The effectiveness of contraception is expressed as the number of pregnancies per 100 women using the method for one year (Pearl index): long-acting intrauterine devices and subcutaneous implants are below 1, and oral contraceptives are about 0.3 with perfect use and about 7 with typical use, the main difference coming from missed pills.

## 38.8. Physiological Transitions of the Newborn

Within minutes of birth the newborn must switch from oxygen supplied by the placenta to oxygen supplied by the lungs. The **fetal circulation** has three shunts that let most of the blood bypass the lungs, which do not exchange gas:

- Ductus venosus: about half of the oxygenated blood of the umbilical vein passes directly through the ductus venosus into the inferior vena cava, bypassing the liver;
- Foramen ovale: most of the inferior vena caval blood entering the right atrium flows through the foramen ovale into the left atrium and is pumped to the head and upper limbs, so the brain receives the blood with the highest oxygen content;
- Ductus arteriosus: most of the blood pumped by the right ventricle flows from the pulmonary artery through the ductus arteriosus into the aorta, because the resistance of the fetal pulmonary vessels is very high (the alveoli are fluid-filled, and low $P_{\ce{O2}}$ causes **hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction**, Chapter 18).

The transition at birth:

1. First breath: compression in the birth canal, cold, touch, hypoxia and high $\mathrm{CO_2}$ stimulate the respiratory center, and the newborn generates a large negative pressure (up to $-40$ to $-60\,\mathrm{cmH_2O}$) to draw air into the fluid-filled lungs; **pulmonary surfactant** keeps the alveoli from collapsing on expiration (Chapter 18). Alveolar fluid is actively absorbed by epithelial ENaC around the time of birth (**alveolar fluid clearance**, promoted by the catecholamines and cortisol that rise at delivery), so infants born by cesarean section are more prone to transient tachypnea.
2. Opening of the **pulmonary circulation**: lung expansion and the rise in $P_{\ce{O2}}$ dilate the pulmonary vessels (nitric oxide), pulmonary vascular resistance falls by about 80% and pulmonary blood flow increases 8–10-fold; at the same time clamping of the umbilical cord removes the low-resistance placental circulation and the resistance of the **systemic circulation** rises.
3. Closure of the foramen ovale: increased pulmonary venous return makes left atrial pressure exceed right atrial pressure, the flap of the foramen ovale is pressed against the atrial septum and closes functionally, and in most it fuses anatomically within several months (about a quarter of adults still have a probe-patent foramen ovale).
4. Closure of the ductus arteriosus: the rise in arterial $P_{\ce{O2}}$ directly causes contraction of the ductal smooth muscle, and the change in clearance of prostaglandins by placenta and lung lowers circulating $\mathrm{PGE_2}$; the duct closes functionally within about 12–24 h after birth (in almost all term infants within 72 h) and becomes fibrosed into the ligamentum arteriosum within weeks. The ductus arteriosus of premature infants often fails to close, and **cyclooxygenase** inhibitors (indomethacin, ibuprofen) can be used to promote closure; conversely, some congenital heart diseases need infusion of $\mathrm{PGE_1}$ to keep the duct open while awaiting surgery.
5. Others: the ductus venosus and umbilical vessels constrict and close; the newborn loses the supply of glucose from the mother, blood glucose falls within 1–2 h after birth, and hepatic glycogenolysis and **gluconeogenesis** then start under **glucagon** and catecholamines; the newborn maintains body temperature by **nonshivering thermogenesis** in brown fat (Chapter 31).

## 38.9. Evolution and Comparison

### 38.9.1. Oviparity, Ovoviviparity and Viviparity

The reproductive modes of vertebrates can be classified along two axes: whether the embryo develops outside or inside the body, and whether its nutrition comes from the yolk (lecithotrophy) or from continuing supply by the mother during gestation (matrotrophy).

| Mode | Development | Nutrition | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Oviparity | External (egg) | Yolk | Most fishes, amphibians and reptiles, all birds, monotremes |
| Ovoviviparity (yolk-nourished live-bearing) | Internal, young born live | Mainly from yolk | Many sharks and rays, some snakes and lizards |
| Viviparity (matrotrophic live-bearing) | Internal | Mainly from the mother: placenta, uterine secretions ("uterine milk"), even eating unfertilized eggs or siblings | Placental mammals, marsupials, some sharks (yolk-sac placenta of hammerhead sharks), a few poeciliid fishes (such as *Poeciliopsis*; most poeciliids are mainly lecithotrophic), some skinks |

Viviparity has evolved independently more than about 150 times in vertebrates, about 100 of them in squamates (lizards and snakes), and also several times in fishes and amphibians. Viviparity in squamates occurs mostly in cold high-latitude and high-altitude regions (the "cold climate hypothesis"): by basking, the mother can give the embryos inside her a warmer and more stable temperature than a burrow in the soil. The cost of viviparity is that the mother's locomotor ability falls during gestation and the number of young per reproduction is limited by maternal volume. In many reptiles with **temperature-dependent sex determination** ([7.5.26](/7.5.26)), viviparity changes the relation between incubation temperature and sex, and in some viviparous lizards the mode of sex determination has changed accordingly.

Birds have never evolved viviparity; one explanation is that flight limits the load the mother can carry, and another is that avian embryonic development needs a temperature higher than the "fever" the mother can maintain for long.

### 38.9.2. Diversity of Placentas and Syncytins

The **placenta** itself has also originated independently many times in different groups, and in mammals differs greatly in structure. By the number of tissue layers retained between maternal and fetal blood (Grosser classification), the placentas of placental mammals fall into three types:

| Type | Layers retained on the maternal side | Examples | Shape |
|---|---|---|---|
| Epitheliochorial placenta | Uterine epithelium, connective tissue and vascular endothelium are all retained, and the villi are only apposed | Pig, horse, whale, lemur; ruminants (synepitheliochorial, in which fetal binucleate cells fuse with the uterine epithelium) | Diffuse (pig, horse); cotyledonary (cow, sheep) |
| Endotheliochorial placenta | Uterine epithelium and connective tissue are eroded and the maternal vascular endothelium is retained | Carnivores such as dog and cat | Zonary |
| Hemochorial placenta | All tissue on the maternal side is eroded and the trophoblast is bathed directly in maternal blood | Humans and other apes, rodents, rabbit, bats | Discoid |

Placentas with few layers have a short exchange distance, but the epitheliochorial placenta with more layers compensates with a larger exchange area and a longer gestation; molecular phylogenetic analyses indicate that the ancestor of placental mammals probably already had a fairly invasive placenta and that the epitheliochorial type evolved secondarily in lineages such as the ungulates. The transport of antibodies also varies with placental type: humans and rabbits with hemochorial placentas obtain IgG from the placenta before birth; cows, horses and pigs with epitheliochorial placentas obtain almost none, and the newborn must absorb antibodies from colostrum within the first day after birth or it very easily dies of infection.

A striking discovery in placental evolution is the syncytin: formation of the syncytiotrophoblast requires cell fusion, and the proteins mediating fusion are "domesticated" versions of retroviral envelope proteins (env). Syncytin 1 and 2 of primates, syncytin A and B of rodents, and the syncytins of carnivores, rabbits and ruminants each come from different endogenous retroviruses that integrated into the genome at different times; that is, the same function was "captured" independently in several mammalian lineages from different viral genes.

### 38.9.3. Marsupials and Monotremes

Marsupials represent another way of allocating maternal investment: pregnancy is very short and lactation very long. Gestation of the red kangaroo is only about 33 days, the placenta is mainly a **yolk sac** placenta (a few species have a brief chorioallantoic placenta), and the newborn is less than $1\,\mathrm{g}$ (less than a ten-thousandth of the mother's weight), with well-developed forelimbs and only bud-like hindlimbs, and it climbs into the pouch by its own effort and attaches to a teat. Subsequent development takes place almost entirely during lactation and lasts 9–12 months; milk composition changes with the development of the young (mainly carbohydrate early, with large increases in fat and protein later), and a kangaroo can even secrete two milks of different composition from two teats at the same time, feeding the newborn in the pouch and a large young that has already left the pouch. Kangaroos also show embryonic diapause: the embryo fertilized by mating immediately after birth stays at the blastocyst stage (**delayed implantation**) and resumes development only when the young in the pouch is weaned or dies, under control of the **prolactin** raised by suckling.

Monotremes (platypus and echidnas) lay eggs, which stay in the mother for about three weeks and are incubated for about ten days; they secrete milk but have no teats, and milk seeps from a mammary area on the abdomen and is lapped from the fur by the young. The existence of monotremes shows that lactation arose before viviparity.

### 38.9.4. Evolution of Milk

Lactation is one of the defining features of mammals, and the mammary gland may have evolved from structures related to skin glands, its original function perhaps keeping parchment-like eggshells moist and providing antimicrobial substances. The composition of milk in each species is adapted to the growth of the young and the way of life of the mother:

| Species | Fat | Protein | Lactose | Features |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Human | $4\%$ | $1\%$ | $7\%$ | Dilute, frequent nursing; newborn weight doubles in about 180 days |
| Cow | $4\%$ | $3.4\%$ | $5\%$ | Weight doubles in about 50 days |
| Rabbit | $15\%$ | $13\%$ | $2\%$ | Nursed only once a day for a few minutes; weight doubles in about 6 days |
| Hooded seal | $60\%$ | $5\%$ | $<1\%$ | Lactation lasts only about 4 days, and the pup gains about $7\,\mathrm{kg}$ per day |

The rule is that the faster the growth and the less frequent the nursing, the more concentrated the protein and fat in the milk; marine mammals, which must build an insulating fat layer within a short time, have the most fat in their milk, and the mother lives on stored fat during lactation without eating.

"Milk" has also arisen many times outside mammals by **convergent evolution**: pigeons and doves, under the action of **prolactin**, shed fat- and protein-rich cells from the crop epithelium of both parents to form "crop milk" to feed the chicks; flamingos and emperor penguins also secrete similar digestive tract secretions; embryos of the viviparous Pacific beetle cockroach obtain a "milk" containing protein crystals from the mother; and the female tsetse fly nurses a single larva inside her body with a "milk gland." In these examples prolactin-like signals and nutrient secretion are often linked, which shows that the ability to nourish offspring is a physiological function selected again and again.

Finally, "pregnancy" is not always the work of the female: in seahorses and pipefishes the male incubates the eggs that the female deposits in the brood pouch, and the epithelium of the brood pouch supplies the embryos with oxygen, regulates osmolality and transports nutrients; the changes in its gene expression resemble those of the mammalian uterus in pregnancy, with prolactin acting as a regulator. These comparisons in the endocrine and reproductive part suggest that the molecular components of hormone systems are fairly conserved, while the reproductive strategies assembled from them are extremely diverse.

## Questions

1. A woman at 6 weeks of pregnancy needs to have the ovary containing the corpus luteum removed because of an ovarian cyst. Why would the doctors give her progesterone after the operation? Would it still be needed if the operation were done at week 12?

   **Answer** In early pregnancy progesterone comes mainly from the corpus luteum of pregnancy maintained by hCG, and the progesterone synthesized by the placenta is sufficient to maintain pregnancy independently only at weeks 7–9 (the luteal–placental shift). If the corpus luteum is removed at week 6, progesterone falls abruptly, the decidua loses support, the quiescence of the myometrium is released, and abortion may occur, so progesterone must be given until about week 10. At week 12 the placenta can already secrete enough progesterone, removal of the corpus luteum no longer affects pregnancy, and replacement is generally not needed.
2. Using the model of Section 38.2.3, explain why maternal smoking in pregnancy (carbon monoxide in the blood) or severe anemia damages the fetal oxygen supply, whereas the mother living at an altitude of 2000 m (arterial $P_{\ce{O2}}$ about 25% lower) has a relatively small effect.

   **Answer** The human placenta is close to pool exchange and is perfusion-limited, so the oxygen the fetus receives depends mainly on the amount of oxygen delivered by the mother to the placenta (uterine blood flow × arterial oxygen content) and on the binding curves of the two bloods, not on the maternal arterial $P_{\ce{O2}}$ itself. Anemia directly lowers the oxygen content of maternal blood; carbon monoxide occupies hemoglobin and shifts the oxygen dissociation curve to the left, so maternal blood releases oxygen less readily in the placenta, and CO also enters fetal blood and lowers its oxygen-carrying capacity; both markedly reduce the fetal oxygen supply. Moderate altitude lowers maternal arterial $P_{\ce{O2}}$, but hemoglobin remains near the flat part of the oxygen dissociation curve and the fall in oxygen content is limited; the mother also compensates by increasing ventilation and hemoglobin, so the effect is small (at higher altitudes, however, fetal growth does slow).
3. Why does gestation in sheep lengthen after the fetal pituitary is destroyed, whereas in human anencephalic fetuses (also lacking fetal ACTH) only the variability of the time of delivery increases, without necessarily lengthening? What does this show about the difference in the initiation mechanism of labor in the two species?

   **Answer** Labor in sheep is initiated by the fetal HPA axis: fetal cortisol induces placental CYP17, converting progesterone to estrogen, progesterone falls, estrogen rises, and the uterus is activated; without a fetal pituitary there is no such signal and gestation can be prolonged indefinitely. The human placenta has no CYP17, maternal progesterone is not lowered by fetal cortisol, and labor relies on several parallel mechanisms such as the placental CRH clock, functional progesterone withdrawal, mechanical stretch of the uterus and inflammatory signals; the fetal adrenal takes part only partly through the synthesis of DHEAS and estrogen. When fetal ACTH is lacking, one pathway is impaired and the others can still start labor, only with less accurate timing. The human mechanism is more redundant and determined more by placenta and uterus.
4. A mother has necrosis of the anterior pituitary after severe postpartum hemorrhage (Sheehan syndrome). Why is her earliest symptom often inability to nurse? What other endocrine abnormalities will appear?

   **Answer** In pregnancy estrogen causes massive hyperplasia of the lactotrophs, the pituitary enlarges without a corresponding increase in blood supply, and it is especially sensitive to the hypotension and ischemia of severe postpartum hemorrhage. Establishment and maintenance of lactation depend on prolactin; after necrosis of the anterior pituitary prolactin is lacking and milk cannot be produced, which is the earliest sign noticed after delivery (milk ejection depends on oxytocin from the neurohypophysis and is usually unaffected, but there is no milk to eject). Deficiencies of the other adenohypophyseal hormones follow: gonadotropin deficiency (amenorrhea, infertility, loss of pubic and axillary hair), TSH deficiency (secondary hypothyroidism), ACTH deficiency (secondary adrenocortical insufficiency, with crisis possible under stress, but with no hyperkalemia because aldosterone is maintained by the RAAS), and growth hormone deficiency.
5. Using the bistable model of Example 38.5.1, explain why induction of labor by oxytocin infusion often fails when the cervix is not yet ripe, whereas the same infusion can succeed after the cervix has ripened at term. Why does artificial rupture of the membranes promote labor?

   **Answer** When the cervix is unripe, the uterine oxytocin receptor density is low and gap junctions are few; the system is at a position where the quiescent state is the only stable state or where the threshold in the bistable region is very high; oxytocin infusion acts as an externally applied input and can cause only a brief contraction proportional to the input, and when the infusion stops the system returns to the quiescent state. After the cervix has ripened at term, receptor density is high and the system lies in the bistable region at a position with a low threshold or close to the saddle-node point; exogenous oxytocin pushes contraction across the unstable threshold, the positive feedback of the Ferguson reflex and prostaglandins takes over, and labor continues even if the infusion stops. Artificial rupture of the membranes lets the fetal head press directly on the cervix and increases stretch (raising $S$) and promotes the release of prostaglandins, equivalent to a perturbation that crosses the threshold.
6. Why do most marsupials complete the greater part of development during lactation rather than during pregnancy? Discuss the advantages and disadvantages of this strategy relative to placental mammals in terms of flexibility of maternal investment and the placenta.

   **Answer** The placenta of marsupials (mainly a yolk-sac placenta) lasts only a short time and has limited exchange capacity, and maternal immune tolerance of the embryo may also be sustainable for only a short time, so the mother puts most of her investment into lactation after birth. Advantages: when the environment deteriorates (drought, food shortage) the mother can give up a young of only a few grams early, or stop development of a diapausing embryo, at little loss, so the investment is highly "retractable"; the investment in lactation can increase gradually with the needs of the young, and milk composition can be matched finely to each stage of development; kangaroos can also raise young of different stages at the same time. Disadvantages: the very immature newborn depends on the mother for a long time, and total development time is usually longer; early development of the brain and other organs takes place outside the body and needs special adaptations (the forelimbs, sense of smell and mouth of the newborn develop first); it has been argued that this limits the evolution of certain forms (such as fins and wings) in marsupials. Placental mammals complete more development inside the body with long gestation and an invasive placenta, and the newborn is more mature, but once the investment of gestation has begun it is harder to recover.
