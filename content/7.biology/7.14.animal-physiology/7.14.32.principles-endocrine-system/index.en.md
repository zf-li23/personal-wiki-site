Most of the regulation described in the preceding chapters depends on nerves: the baroreceptor reflex changes heart rate within a second or two (Chapter 16), and the respiratory centers adjust every breath (Chapter 20). But the body has another, slow and lasting, regulatory system: glands release chemical messengers into the blood, the messengers travel with the blood to the whole body, and only cells that carry the corresponding receptors respond. Antidiuretic hormone, the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system and atrial natriuretic peptide have already appeared in the chapters on body fluids and the kidney (Chapters 21–24), and all are members of this system. The seven chapters starting here deal with the endocrine system. This chapter treats the common principles: the modes of signaling (Section 32.1), what the endocrine system consists of (Section 32.2), what kind of molecules hormones are and how they are synthesized (Section 32.3), how they act through receptors (Section 32.4), what features they share (Section 32.5), how they are cleared (Section 32.6), how secretion is regulated (Section 32.7) and how hormones are measured (Section 32.8). Chapter 33 makes these principles quantitative and discusses the design of hormone signals in time and space; Chapters 34–38 deal in turn with the hypothalamus–pituitary and the thyroid, the adrenal glands and the pancreatic islets, calcium and phosphate metabolism, and the gonads and reproduction. The molecular mechanisms downstream of the receptor (G proteins, second messengers, kinase cascades, nuclear receptors) were covered in biochemistry ([7.1.14](/7.1.14), [7.1.15](/7.1.15)), and the integration of fuel metabolism by hormones is treated in [7.1.29](/7.1.29); here we discuss only the questions at the level of physiology.

## 32.1. Chemical Signals and Modes of Signaling

### 32.1.1. The Origin of Chemical Signals

Cells exchange information through chemical molecules, which is far older than nerves and than multicellular animals. A classic example is the luminous marine bacterium *Vibrio fischeri* (now *Aliivibrio fischeri*). A single bacterium does not emit light in seawater; when bacteria accumulate to a very high density in the light organ of the Hawaiian bobtail squid (*Euprymna scolopes*), the whole population glows together, canceling the shadow cast by moonlight for the nocturnal squid. Each bacterium synthesizes a small, freely diffusible autoinducer (an acyl homoserine lactone, synthesized by LuxI); at low density it diffuses away and is diluted in the environment; at high density the extracellular concentration exceeds a threshold, binds the intracellular receptor LuxR, activates the luminescence genes and further promotes the synthesis of LuxI (positive feedback). This is *quorum sensing*: cells "count" their neighbors by the concentration of a secreted substance and respond collectively (the cellular mechanism is treated in the microbiology course, [7.11](/7.11)).

Quorum sensing already contains all the elements of hormone signaling: a signal molecule, a **receptor** that recognizes it, and a mode of transport that carries the signal from sender to receiver. The animal hormone system has developed each of these three: the chemical variety of signal molecules is greatly increased; the receptor determines who can "hear" the signal; and the transport has evolved from local diffusion to distribution through the circulatory system to the whole body.

### 32.1.2. Modes of Intercellular Signaling

According to the path a signal takes from the sending cell to the receiving cell, chemical communication between cells can be divided into the following kinds:

| Mode | Path | Range of action | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Direct contact | Small molecules and ions through gap junctions; contact of membrane-bound ligand with receptor (juxtacrine) | Adjacent cells | Electrical coupling of cardiac muscle; Notch–Delta; T cells and antigen-presenting cells |
| Autocrine | The released signal acts on the cell that released it | The same cell | IL-2 of T cells; many growth factors |
| Paracrine | Diffuses through the interstitial fluid to act on nearby cells | A few micrometers to a few hundred micrometers | Nitric oxide relaxes vascular smooth muscle; somatostatin of the pancreatic δ cells |
| Endocrine | Enters the blood and reaches distant target cells with the blood flow | The whole body | Insulin, thyroid hormone, cortisol |
| Neurosecretory | Neurons release hormones into the blood | The whole body | Antidiuretic hormone, oxytocin, hypothalamic releasing hormones |
| Neurotransmitter | Released by a neuron at a synapse and acting on the postsynaptic cell | A synaptic cleft of about $20\,\mathrm{nm}$ | Acetylcholine, glutamate |

The three words **autocrine**, **paracrine** and **endocrine** have been defined in biochemistry ([7.1.29](/7.1.29)); here we add neurosecretion.

**Definition 32.1.1 (Neurosecretion)** The synthesis of a hormone by a neuron, its transport along the axon to the terminal, and its release into the blood by exocytosis triggered by action potentials, is called **neurosecretion**. The neurons that carry out neurosecretion are called neuroendocrine cells; they have the properties both of neurons (generating and conducting action potentials) and of endocrine cells (synthesizing and secreting hormones).

The same molecule often acts in several modes. Norepinephrine is a transmitter at sympathetic nerve terminals and a hormone when released into the blood by the adrenal medulla; somatostatin is a neurohormone in the hypothalamus, a paracrine factor in the pancreatic islets, and in the stomach also acts on the cell that secretes it. The difference between "hormone," "transmitter" and "local mediator" therefore lies not in the molecule but in the path the signal takes. The nervous and endocrine systems are not sharply separated either: the hypothalamic neurosecretory cells are the interface between the two, and many endocrine cells (pancreatic β cells, chromaffin cells of the adrenal medulla) trigger secretion like neurons by action potentials and $\ce{Ca^2+}$ entry.

Chemical signals that pass between individuals are called pheromones; for example, bombykol released by the female silkworm moth can attract males several kilometers away. They travel through the environment and act on other individuals of the same species; unlike the hormones inside the body, they do not belong to the endocrine system.

## 32.2. The Endocrine System

### 32.2.1. Endocrine Glands and Exocrine Glands

**Definition 32.2.1 (Endocrine System)** The system made up of endocrine glands and of endocrine cells scattered in the various organs, which transmits regulatory information through body fluids with hormones as messengers, is called the **endocrine system**.

Glands fall into two classes according to the destination of their secretion.

**Definition 32.2.2 (Endocrine Glands and Exocrine Glands)** A gland that has no duct and releases its secretion directly into the interstitial fluid and from there into the blood is called an **endocrine gland**; a gland that delivers its secretion through a duct to the body surface or into a lumen inside the body (the digestive or respiratory tract) is called an **exocrine gland**.

The structural difference corresponds to the functional one. An exocrine gland consists of acini and ducts and secretes a large volume (about $1\,\mathrm{L}$ of saliva and about $1.5\,\mathrm{L}$ of pancreatic juice per day, Chapter 27), acting on the local region to which the duct leads; an endocrine gland usually consists of glandular cells arranged in cords, clumps or follicles, with the cells in close contact with fenestrated capillaries, and its secretion is minute (micrograms to milligrams per day) but can reach the whole body. The pancreas is both kinds of gland at once: the acini and ducts are the exocrine part, and the scattered islets are the endocrine part.

### 32.2.2. The Endocrine Glands and Hormones of the Body

Endocrine tissue is distributed throughout the body. Besides the glands whose main function is the secretion of hormones (pituitary, thyroid, parathyroids, adrenals, pancreatic islets, pineal gland, gonads), many organs also contain endocrine cells:

Two features deserve notice. First, the endocrine glands mostly have no direct structural connection with one another; they form a unified system through the blood. Second, hormones affect almost all physiological processes, which can be summarized in four classes: maintaining the **homeostasis** of the internal environment (water and salt, acid–base, blood glucose, blood calcium, body temperature), regulating metabolism and energy balance, regulating growth and development, and regulating reproduction.

### 32.2.3. Where Tissue Specificity Comes From

Hormones flow with the blood throughout the body, and the target cells are only a part of it. The specificity comes mainly from the receiving end: only cells that express the corresponding receptor can read the signal, and the same hormone can connect in different cells to different receptor subtypes and downstream pathways (epinephrine relaxes bronchial smooth muscle through $\beta_2$ receptors and constricts skin vessels through $\alpha_1$ receptors). In addition, a target tissue can activate a precursor locally ($\mathrm{T_4} \to \mathrm{T_3}$), alter the affinity of binding proteins locally, or use a portal system to deliver a signal preferentially to a particular organ. These mechanisms at the "receiving end" and in "spatial structure" are the subject of Chapter 33.

## 32.3. The Chemical Nature and Synthesis of Hormones

### 32.3.1. Chemical Classification

By chemical structure, hormones fall into four classes. The significance of the classification is solubility: it nearly determines how a hormone is stored, how it is transported, which receptors it acts on and how fast it acts (examples of each class are in [7.1.29](/7.1.29#sec-29.1.3)).

| Property | Peptide and protein hormones | Steroid hormones | Amines: catecholamines | Amines: thyroid hormones |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Source | Amino acid chains | Cholesterol | Tyrosine | Tyrosine (and iodine) |
| Solubility | Hydrophilic | Lipophilic | Hydrophilic | Lipophilic |
| Storage | Secretory granules, large amounts | Almost none; synthesized on demand | Chromaffin granules | Thyroglobulin, enough for 2–3 months |
| Secretion | Exocytosis | Diffusion out of the cell | Exocytosis | Released through transporters after hydrolysis of thyroglobulin |
| Transport in blood | Mostly free | Bound to binding proteins | Free (partly loosely bound to albumin) | More than 99% bound |
| Half-life | Minutes | Minutes to hours | 1–3 minutes | 1 day ($\mathrm{T_3}$) to 1 week ($\mathrm{T_4}$) |
| Receptor | Membrane receptor | Intracellular (nuclear) receptor | Membrane receptor | Nuclear receptor |
| Onset of action | Seconds to minutes | Hours to days | Seconds | Hours to days |

The fourth class is the **eicosanoids** derived from arachidonic acid (**prostaglandins**, thromboxanes, leukotrienes), which almost all cells can synthesize; they act as paracrine and autocrine signals on membrane receptors, have half-lives of seconds, and are generally not circulating hormones.

Peptide hormones range in length from 3 amino acids (TRH) to about 200 (prolactin 199, growth hormone 191); those with more than about 100 amino acids are conventionally called protein hormones. The glycoprotein hormones (TSH, FSH, LH, hCG) consist of a common α subunit and a β subunit that determines specificity. Peptides cannot cross the lipid bilayer and therefore act on membrane receptors; they are hydrolyzed in the digestive tract and so cannot be taken orally (insulin must be injected), whereas steroids and thyroid hormones can be given orally.

### 32.3.2. Peptide Hormones: Preprohormone, Prohormone and Hormone

Peptide hormones are synthesized by the pathway of secreted proteins ([7.1.29](/7.1.29#sec-29.1.3)):

1. The ribosome first translates a long chain with an N-terminal **signal peptide**, called the preprohormone; the signal peptide guides it into the rough endoplasmic reticulum and is then cleaved, and the remaining molecule is called the prohormone;
2. The prohormone is folded, disulfide-bonded and glycosylated in the endoplasmic reticulum and transported to the Golgi apparatus;
3. In the Golgi apparatus and the secretory granules, prohormone convertases cleave at pairs of basic amino acids to produce the active hormone, which is packaged in secretory granules together with the cleaved fragments;
4. The secretory granules are stored in the cytoplasm, often docked near the plasma membrane, awaiting a stimulus;
5. A stimulus (depolarization of the membrane causing $\ce{Ca^2+}$ entry, or a second messenger generated by a membrane receptor) triggers exocytosis, and the hormone and fragments are released in equimolar amounts into the intercellular fluid.

**Definition 32.3.1 (Prohormone)** A precursor protein that is itself inactive or only slightly active and gives rise to a hormone after proteolytic processing is called a **prohormone**.

**Example 32.3.1 (The Precursor of Antidiuretic Hormone)** Arginine vasopressin (AVP, that is, **antidiuretic hormone**) has only 9 amino acids but is synthesized as a preprohormone of 164 amino acids: after the N-terminal signal peptide come, in order, AVP, the carrier protein neurophysin II and a glycopeptide of 39 amino acids, copeptin. After removal of the signal peptide the prohormone is packaged in the cell bodies of the magnocellular neurons of the supraoptic and paraventricular nuclei of the hypothalamus and is transported along the axon to the neurohypophysis, being cleaved into three pieces on the way; the three are released together when triggered by action potentials (Chapter 24). Because copeptin is much more stable than AVP and easier to measure, plasma copeptin is used clinically as a surrogate marker of AVP secretion.

Making a hormone from a long precursor has several advantages: the signal peptide ensures entry into the secretory pathway; a long chain folds correctly more easily than a short peptide (the C-peptide of proinsulin allows the disulfide bonds of the A and B chains to pair correctly); and one precursor can give rise to several hormones (pro-opiomelanocortin, POMC, yields ACTH in the anterior pituitary and α-MSH and β-endorphin in the intermediate lobe and hypothalamus), different tissues expressing different convertases and so obtaining different products from the same gene.

### 32.3.3. Steroid Hormones: Synthesis on Demand

All steroid hormones are synthesized from **cholesterol** and share a cyclopentanoperhydrophenanthrene skeleton of three six-membered rings and one five-membered ring. The cholesterol needed for synthesis comes mainly from **low-density lipoprotein** in the blood (taken up by receptor-mediated endocytosis), and a little is synthesized by the cell itself from acetyl-CoA. Different glands express different enzymes and obtain different products from the same raw material: the zona fasciculata of the adrenal cortex produces cortisol (a glucocorticoid), the zona glomerulosa aldosterone (a mineralocorticoid), and the gonads testosterone, estradiol and progesterone (Chapters 35 and 37).

Steroid hormones are lipophilic and pass freely through cell membranes, so they cannot be stored in vesicles as peptides are: once synthesized they diffuse out of the cell. Glandular cells have almost no reserve, the rate of secretion equals the rate of synthesis, and secretion is changed by regulating the rate-limiting step of synthesis within minutes to hours. This rate-limiting step is the transport of cholesterol from the outer to the inner mitochondrial membrane (mediated by the StAR protein), where the side-chain cleavage enzyme CYP11A1 converts it to pregnenolone. After entering the blood, steroids are largely bound to specific binding globulins (corticosteroid-binding globulin, CBG; sex hormone–binding globulin, SHBG) or to albumin (Section 32.6).

### 32.3.4. Amine Hormones

The amine hormones are all derived from tyrosine (melatonin from tryptophan), but their properties divide into two branches:

- The catecholamines (epinephrine, norepinephrine, dopamine) are hydrophilic and resemble peptides: they are synthesized in the cytoplasm, pumped into chromaffin granules by the vesicular monoamine transporter for storage, secreted by exocytosis, act on membrane receptors, and have a half-life in the blood of only 1–3 minutes (Chapter 35).
- Thyroid hormones are lipophilic and resemble steroids: they act on nuclear receptors and are almost entirely protein-bound in the blood. Their mode of storage is unique: the hormone is synthesized on a huge protein (thyroglobulin) and stored as colloid in the extracellular follicular lumen, in an amount sufficient for 2–3 months; for secretion the follicular cells take the colloid back in and hydrolyze it in lysosomes to release the hormone (Chapter 34).

## 32.4. Receptors and Signal Transduction

### 32.4.1. Types of Receptors

A hormone can act only by binding to its receptor. The affinity of receptors for hormones is very high (dissociation constants between $10^{-11}$ and $10^{-9}\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$), well matched to the free concentration of hormone in plasma (picomolar to nanomolar). Receptors are divided by location and mode of signal transduction into the following classes, whose molecular mechanisms are described in [7.1.14](/7.1.14) and [7.1.15](/7.1.15):

| Receptor | Mechanism | Hormone examples |
|---|---|---|
| Ion channel receptors | Ligand-gated ion channels, changing the membrane potential | Mainly neurotransmitters (the nicotinic receptor of acetylcholine, GABA$_{\mathrm{A}}$ receptors) |
| **G protein–coupled receptors** | Activate $\mathrm{G_s}$ / $\mathrm{G_i}$ (raise or lower **cAMP**) or $\mathrm{G_q}$ (phospholipase C, producing $\mathrm{IP_3}$, DAG and $\ce{Ca^2+}$ signals) | cAMP: ACTH, TSH, LH, FSH, glucagon, PTH, calcitonin, ADH (V2), β-adrenergic; $\mathrm{G_q}$: TRH, GnRH, oxytocin, ADH (V1), angiotensin II, α1-adrenergic; $\mathrm{G_i}$: somatostatin, dopamine (D2) |
| **Receptor tyrosine kinases** | The intracellular part of the receptor is itself a tyrosine kinase | Insulin, IGF-1 |
| Tyrosine kinase–associated receptors | The receptor has no enzymatic activity itself; on dimerization it activates a bound JAK kinase (the **JAK–STAT pathway**) | Growth hormone, prolactin, leptin, erythropoietin |
| Guanylyl cyclase receptors | The intracellular part of the receptor is a **guanylyl cyclase**, producing cGMP | Atrial natriuretic peptide |
| Intracellular receptors (**nuclear receptors**) | After the hormone binds, the receptor acts as a transcription factor and binds the **hormone response element** on DNA | Steroids, thyroid hormone, calcitriol, retinoic acid |

Membrane receptors act quickly (seconds to minutes) and mostly change cellular activity by modifying existing proteins; nuclear receptors change gene transcription, and their effect appears only after tens of minutes to hours and lasts longer. This division is not absolute: peptide hormones can also change transcription through kinase cascades (GH induces the IGF-1 gene through STAT5), and steroids and thyroid hormone also have rapid "nongenomic" actions through membrane receptors or cytoplasmic signaling (Chapter 34).

### 32.4.2. Nuclear Receptors and Tissue Specificity

The nuclear receptor family has 48 members in humans, of identical structure: an N-terminal transcription activation domain, a central DNA-binding domain (two zinc fingers) and a C-terminal ligand-binding domain. By mode of action they fall into two classes:

- Class I (the steroid receptors: glucocorticoid, mineralocorticoid, androgen, estrogen and progesterone receptors): in the absence of hormone they are bound to heat shock proteins and others and mostly remain in the cytoplasm; after the hormone binds they dissociate from the heat shock proteins, expose a nuclear localization signal, enter the nucleus and bind the hormone response element as homodimers.
- Class II (the thyroid hormone, vitamin D and retinoic acid receptors): they are in the nucleus and bound to DNA whether or not hormone is present, usually forming heterodimers with the retinoid X receptor (RXR); without hormone they bind a **corepressor** and repress transcription, and after the hormone binds they exchange it for a **coactivator** and activate transcription.

What a hormone–receptor complex can do depends not only on the hormone and the receptor but also on which coregulators are present in the cell: they recruit histone acetyltransferases or deacetylases and open or close chromatin. Different tissues express different coregulators, so the same hormone–receptor complex regulates different genes in different tissues and may even act in opposite ways. Selective estrogen receptor modulators are an example: tamoxifen blocks the estrogen receptor in the breast (and is used to treat breast cancer) but partially activates it in the endometrium and bone, because the conformation of the receptor after ligand binding is "read out" differently in different coregulator environments.

### 32.4.3. The Number and Sensitivity of Receptors Are Also Regulated

The response of target cells to a hormone is not fixed. After prolonged exposure to a high concentration of hormone, receptors are phosphorylated and uncoupled from G proteins and are internalized and degraded, and the number of receptors falls; this is called **receptor down-regulation**. A weakening of the response over a short time without a change in receptor number is called **desensitization** ([7.1.14](/7.1.14#sec-14.2.4)). Conversely, some hormones increase (up-regulate) the receptors for themselves or for other hormones: estrogen increases the progesterone receptors of the endometrium in the follicular phase of the menstrual cycle, preparing for the action of progesterone after ovulation; prolactin increases the number of its own receptors in the mammary gland at the end of pregnancy. Down-regulation and desensitization explain why many hormones are secreted in pulses, and why continuous administration of a GnRH analogue paradoxically inhibits the pituitary (Chapter 33).

## 32.5. General Features of Hormone Action

1. Messenger action. A hormone provides neither energy nor structural material, but only conveys information: it triggers reactions already present in the target cell, speeding or slowing them, without creating new ones.
2. Specificity. A hormone acts only on target cells that carry the corresponding receptor. The distribution of receptors varies greatly: the receptor for TSH is almost only on the thyroid, whereas the receptors for thyroid hormone and cortisol are found on almost all cells.
3. High potency and amplification. Hormones act at concentrations of $10^{-12}$–$10^{-9}\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$. One receptor activates many G proteins, one adenylyl cyclase produces many cAMP, one kinase phosphorylates many substrates, and the signal is amplified step by step within the cell ([7.1.14](/7.1.14#sec-14.2.3)); each gland of an endocrine axis amplifies again: nanogram amounts of CRH cause the secretion of microgram amounts of ACTH, which causes the secretion of milligram amounts of cortisol ([7.1.29](/7.1.29#sec-29.1.4)).
4. Interaction. When several hormones act on the same process, the effects do not simply add.

   - Synergism: glucagon, epinephrine, cortisol and growth hormone all raise blood glucose, and when several are present together the glucose-raising effect is greater than the sum of their separate effects.
   - Antagonism: insulin lowers blood glucose and antagonizes the hormones above; PTH raises blood calcium and calcitonin lowers it.

**Definition 32.5.1 (Permissive Action)** The situation in which a hormone itself produces no effect on a tissue but its presence is a necessary condition for another hormone to exert its normal effect is called **permissive action**.

**Example 32.5.1** Glucocorticoids do not themselves constrict vascular smooth muscle, but without glucocorticoids the vasoconstrictor action of catecholamines is greatly weakened, and patients with adrenocortical insufficiency easily develop circulatory failure under stress. One mechanism is that glucocorticoids maintain the expression and signaling pathway of the $\alpha_1$-adrenergic receptors of vascular smooth muscle. Likewise, thyroid hormone increases the number of β-adrenergic receptors in cardiac muscle and adipocytes, allowing catecholamines to produce their full cardiac and thermogenic effects (the tachycardia of hyperthyroid patients can be relieved by β-blockers, Chapter 34); cortisol allows glucagon and epinephrine to mobilize liver glycogen and promote gluconeogenesis fully.

## 32.6. Clearance of Hormones

### 32.6.1. Routes of Clearance

The action of a hormone must be able to end, or the signal could not change as needed. Hormones in plasma are cleared by the following routes:

1. Degradation by enzymes in blood and tissue: peptidases in plasma and tissue hydrolyze peptide hormones; catecholamines are inactivated by catechol-O-methyltransferase and monoamine oxidase;
2. Internalization and degradation after binding to target cells: many peptide hormones enter the cell together with the receptor by **receptor-mediated endocytosis** and are hydrolyzed in lysosomes, with part of the receptor recycled to the membrane;
3. Metabolism by the liver and excretion in bile: steroids and thyroid hormones are reduced and hydroxylated in the liver and conjugated with glucuronic acid or sulfate, becoming water-soluble and being excreted in bile or urine;
4. Filtration by the kidney and excretion in urine: small peptides and water-soluble metabolites are filtered at the glomerulus (about one-third of insulin is cleared in the kidney).

### 32.6.2. Metabolic Clearance Rate and Half-Life

The level of a hormone in plasma depends both on how fast it is secreted and on how fast it is cleared. Borrowing the concept of renal **clearance** (Chapter 22), the latter can be described quantitatively.

**Definition 32.6.1 (Metabolic Clearance Rate)** The volume of plasma completely cleared of a hormone per unit time is called the **metabolic clearance rate** (MCR) of that hormone, that is, MCR = clearance rate / plasma concentration.

In the steady state the secretion rate equals the clearance rate, so
$$
\text{secretion rate} = \mathrm{MCR}\cdot C. \tag{32.1}
$$
As long as the clearance and the plasma concentration are measured, the secretion rate of a gland, which cannot be measured directly, can be calculated.

**Example 32.6.1 (The Secretion Rate of Testosterone)** The metabolic clearance rate of testosterone in an adult man is about $1000\,\mathrm{L\,d^{-1}}$ and the plasma concentration about $6.5\,\mu\mathrm{g\,L^{-1}}$ ($3$–$10\,\mu\mathrm{g\,L^{-1}}$). By Eq. (32.1) the secretion rate is about $1000 \times 6.5 = 6500\,\mu\mathrm{g\,d^{-1}}$, that is, about $6.5\,\mathrm{mg}$ per day. $1000\,\mathrm{L\,d^{-1}}$ is already close to the hepatic plasma flow (about $0.9\,\mathrm{L\,min^{-1}} \approx 1300\,\mathrm{L\,d^{-1}}$): most of the testosterone that flows through the liver is taken up and metabolized in a single pass, which is why its half-life is only 10–20 minutes.

Treat the hormone as a single pool distributed in a volume $V$ and cleared by first-order kinetics; the concentration decays as $\mathrm{e}^{-kt}$ with $k = \mathrm{MCR}/V$, and the half-life is
$$
t_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{k} = \frac{\ln 2\cdot V}{\mathrm{MCR}}. \tag{32.2}
$$
Most clearance processes (uptake by hepatocytes, glomerular filtration, receptor binding) can act only on free hormone. If only a fraction $f$ of the hormone in plasma is free, and the clearance of free hormone is $\mathrm{CL}_{\mathrm{free}}$, then the total $\mathrm{MCR} \approx f\cdot\mathrm{CL}_{\mathrm{free}}$, and substituting in Eq. (32.2):
$$
t_{1/2} \approx \frac{\ln 2\cdot V}{f\cdot \mathrm{CL}_{\mathrm{free}}} \propto \frac{1}{f}. \tag{32.3}
$$
Binding proteins hide the hormone from the clearing organs, and the half-life is prolonged by the same factor by which the free fraction is reduced. The data in the figure below lie roughly along this line: free peptide hormones have half-lives of minutes, cortisol, which is 95% bound, 1–1.5 hours, and $\mathrm{T_4}$, whose free fraction is only about 0.03%, as long as a week. Testosterone is a conspicuous exception: it is bound only loosely to albumin and dissociates rapidly, and on passing through the liver the albumin-bound portion is also taken up extensively (uptake-driven unloading, Chapter 33), so its actual clearance is far greater than the estimate based on clearing only the free part.

The values are typical figures from medical physiology textbooks and are schematic only. The dashed line $t_{1/2} = 5\,\mathrm{min}/f$ is the expectation if only free hormone were cleared (Eq. 32.3); most hormones fall near it, and testosterone, which is extracted efficiently on passing through the liver, lies far below the line](./plots/half-life-binding.py)

The length of the half-life determines how fast a hormone signal can change: about 4–5 half-lives are needed for the concentration to change to a new steady state. Epinephrine can rise and fall within 1–2 minutes, suited to emergencies; the concentration of $\mathrm{T_4}$ hardly changes within a week, suited to maintaining the long-term metabolic level. Binding proteins also keep a large reserve pool in the plasma, buffering short-term fluctuations in secretion (Chapter 33).

## 32.7. Regulation of Hormone Secretion

### 32.7.1. Negative Feedback and the Set Point

The most basic mode of regulating hormone secretion is **negative feedback** ([Chapter 1](/7.14.1#sec-1.6.1)): the action of the hormone (or the hormone itself) returns to inhibit its own secretion. There are two kinds of feedback signal:

- Feedback of the metabolic change caused directly by the hormone. A rise in blood glucose stimulates insulin secretion, insulin lowers blood glucose, and secretion falls accordingly; a fall in blood calcium stimulates PTH secretion, and PTH raises blood calcium. What is controlled here is a variable such as blood glucose or blood calcium, and the hormone is the means.
- Feedback of the hormone itself to its superior. Thyroid hormone inhibits the secretion of TSH and TRH, and cortisol inhibits that of ACTH and CRH. What is controlled here is the concentration of the hormone itself.

In the second case, what is the "set point"? The figure below answers with the simplest model: the secretion rate of the gland $s(F)$ falls as the free hormone concentration $F$ rises (feedback), the clearance rate is proportional to $F$, and the steady state lies at the intersection of the two curves. Changing the sensitivity of the feedback (such as the threshold of the hypothalamus or pituitary to the hormone) or changing the clearance rate moves the intersection, and the set point changes with it; changing the concentration of the plasma binding protein changes neither curve (secretion senses free hormone and clearance acts on free hormone), so the steady-state concentration of free hormone is unchanged, and only the amounts of bound and total hormone change.

**Example 32.7.1 (Thyroid Hormone in Pregnancy)** In pregnancy, estrogen approximately doubles the thyroxine-binding globulin (TBG) synthesized by the liver. The newly added TBG immediately binds part of the free $\mathrm{T_4}$, and the free concentration falls; the pituitary senses this fall, TSH secretion increases, and the thyroid secretes more $\mathrm{T_4}$ until the free $\mathrm{T_4}$ returns to its former level. The result is a higher total $\mathrm{T_4}$ with normal free $\mathrm{T_4}$ and TSH, and the pregnant woman does not become hyperthyroid. Clinically, therefore, free $\mathrm{T_4}$ rather than total $\mathrm{T_4}$ is measured. Conversely, androgens or the nephrotic syndrome reduce TBG, and total $\mathrm{T_4}$ is low with normal function.

### 32.7.2. Multistage Axes and Feedback Loops

The hypothalamus, the anterior pituitary and the peripheral target glands form three-stage endocrine axes (hypothalamus–pituitary–thyroid, –adrenal and –gonad, Chapters 34, 35 and 37): the releasing hormones of the hypothalamus stimulate the anterior pituitary to secrete tropic hormones, and the tropic hormones stimulate the target glands to secrete hormones. The general rule is that the hormones of a higher stage promote the secretion of the lower stage, and the hormones of a lower stage inhibit the secretion of the higher stage. According to the source of the feedback signal, the feedback loops fall into three kinds:

**Definition 32.7.1 (Long-Loop, Short-Loop and Ultrashort-Loop Feedback)** The feedback of target gland hormones to the anterior pituitary and hypothalamus is called **long-loop feedback**; the feedback of anterior pituitary hormones to the hypothalamus is called **short-loop feedback**; and the feedback of hypothalamic hormones to the very neurons that secrete them is called **ultrashort-loop feedback**.

The multistage structure serves three purposes: each stage amplifies the signal once (Section 32.5); at the hypothalamic stage, inputs from the brain (stress, circadian rhythm, temperature, emotion) converge, subordinating the endocrine system to the needs of the whole organism; and feedback can be received at every stage, making the correction of perturbations by the system more reliable. A quantitative model of the three-stage axis is given in Chapter 34.

### 32.7.3. Positive Feedback

Positive feedback makes secretion greater and greater until some external event ends it, and so it is used only for processes that require a "one-time" burst:

- Before ovulation, after the estradiol secreted by the follicle has remained above a threshold for about two days, its action on LH secretion changes from negative to positive feedback, causing the LH surge that triggers ovulation; after ovulation the corpus luteum secretes progesterone and the positive feedback ends (Chapter 37);
- In parturition, the fetal head distends the cervix and reflexively causes oxytocin secretion, oxytocin strengthens uterine contractions, and the contractions distend the cervix further, until the fetus is delivered (the Ferguson reflex, Chapter 38);
- In lactation, suckling causes oxytocin secretion and milk ejection, continued suckling maintains the secretion, and the secretion ends when suckling stops.

Positive feedback combined with negative feedback can produce rhythmic hormone fluctuations, as in the menstrual cycle (Chapter 37).

### 32.7.4. Neural Regulation and Rhythms

Many endocrine glands are directly innervated: sympathetic nerves stimulate secretion by the adrenal medulla and inhibit insulin secretion; the vagus nerve promotes the secretion of insulin and gastrin during eating. Although the anterior pituitary has no direct neural innervation, it receives through the hypothalamus all influences from the brain.

Hormone secretion also has rhythms on different scales: pulses with periods of minutes to hours (GnRH has a pulse about every 1–2 hours, Chapter 33); **circadian rhythms** driven by the suprachiasmatic nucleus (cortisol peaks in the early morning before waking, growth hormone is secreted in large amounts during slow-wave sleep after sleep onset, and melatonin is secreted at night); the menstrual cycle with its monthly period; and seasonal breeding rhythms with a yearly period. When hormones are measured, the time of sampling must be recorded.

## 32.8. Measurement of Hormones

The plasma concentration of hormones lies between picomolar and nanomolar, and only methods of very high specificity can measure them:

- Radioimmunoassay (RIA, Yalow and Berson, 1959, first used for insulin): the hormone in the sample competes with a fixed amount of radiolabeled hormone for a limited number of **antibody** binding sites; the more hormone in the sample, the less labeled hormone is bound to the antibody. Yalow received the 1977 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for this.
- The **enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay** and its chemiluminescent variants: enzyme or luminescent labels replace the radiolabel, and the "sandwich" format uses two antibodies that recognize different epitopes, for greater specificity; this is now the main clinical method (principle in [7.1.7](/7.1.7#sec-7.4.4)).
- Liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry: it distinguishes structurally similar steroids by molecular weight and fragments, is free of the cross-reactivity of antibodies, and has become the reference method for measuring testosterone, estradiol and cortisol.

Three points need attention when interpreting measurements: whether the total or the free concentration was measured (Example 32.7.1); the relation of the sampling time to pulses and circadian rhythms; and the interference of prohormones, fragments and structural analogues with immunoassays. A "normal concentration" of a hormone does not mean "normal action": a defect in the receptor or downstream of it produces a high hormone level with absent action (hormone resistance syndromes, such as the growth hormone receptor mutation of Laron syndrome, Chapter 34).

## 32.9. Evolution and Comparison

### 32.9.1. Ancient Chemical Signals and Neurosecretion

In animal evolution, chemical signals preceded nerves and endocrine glands. The placozoan *Trichoplax*, without neurons or muscle, coordinates the beating of its cilia with a variety of peptides secreted by glandular cells ([Chapter 2](/7.14.2#sec-2.8.2)); the nerve nets of cnidarians such as *Hydra* already contain many neuropeptides. Invertebrates do not have as many separate endocrine glands as vertebrates, and their main hormones are produced by neurosecretory cells and released into the hemolymph through special neurohemal organs (structures where axon terminals accumulate in close contact with a blood sinus). Neurosecretion may therefore be the most ancient form of hormone signaling, and the vertebrate hypothalamus–neurohypophysis is a continuation of this ancient system.

Many hormone families can be found throughout the bilaterians: peptides of the oxytocin/vasopressin family occur in mollusks (conopressin), insects and nematodes; insulin-like peptides are secreted by nerve cells in nematodes and *Drosophila* and regulate growth, metabolism and lifespan; and GnRH-like peptides can be traced to the common ancestor of the bilaterians. New hormones and receptors mostly arise by divergence after gene duplication.

### 32.9.2. The Evolution of Steroid Receptors

Steroid receptors are a paradigm of evolutionary study, because ancestral sequences can be inferred by phylogenetics, synthesized in the laboratory, and the function of the ancestral receptor measured directly (ancestral sequence reconstruction). The studies of Thornton and colleagues led to several conclusions with experimental support:

- The six steroid receptors of vertebrates (two estrogen receptors, and the glucocorticoid, mineralocorticoid, progesterone and androgen receptors) arose by gene duplication from one ancestral receptor. The reconstructed ancestral receptor responded to estrogen, whereas the homologue of the estrogen receptor in a mollusk (the sea hare *Aplysia*) is active without any hormone (2003): the earliest steroid receptor was probably estrogen receptor–like.
- Before the mineralocorticoid and glucocorticoid receptors separated, the reconstructed ancestral corticosteroid receptor could already be activated by aldosterone, although aldosterone appeared only in tetrapods (2006). This "preadaptation" of a receptor to a hormone that arose later shows that new hormone–receptor pairs can form by recruiting the latent binding capacity of an existing receptor (molecular exploitation).
- The change of the glucocorticoid receptor from the ancestral state to specificity for cortisol required substitutions at only a few key sites, and the order of the substitutions was constrained by epistasis: certain permissive substitutions had to occur first (a series of studies from 2007 on).

These studies turned the question of how receptors and hormones coevolve into one that can be tested by biochemical experiment.

### 32.9.3. The Insect Endocrine System

The insect endocrine system provides an independent design in contrast to that of vertebrates; the molting and metamorphosis that it controls were covered in developmental biology ([7.5.33](/7.5.33#sec-33.3)):

| Component | Hormone | Action | Vertebrate analogue |
|---|---|---|---|
| Neurosecretory cells of the brain → corpora cardiaca (neurohemal organ) | Neuropeptides such as prothoracicotropic hormone (PTTH) | Stimulate the prothoracic gland | Hypothalamus → neurohypophysis |
| Prothoracic gland | **Ecdysone** (converted to 20-hydroxyecdysone in the periphery) | Initiates **molting** | Steroid hormones acting through nuclear receptors (EcR/USP) |
| Corpora allata | **Juvenile hormone** | Maintains larval characters; regulates reproduction in the adult | No direct counterpart |

The experiments of Wigglesworth on the blood-sucking bug *Rhodnius* established this system: a larva whose head is removed before a certain time after a blood meal does not molt, whereas removal after that time allows normal molting, showing that the head releases some factor promoting molting during a "critical period" after the meal. The insect system has the same structural logic as that of vertebrates (neurosecretion in the brain controls peripheral glands, and steroids alter gene expression through nuclear receptors), but the hormone molecules and the glands evolved independently, which suggests that "a neurosecretory center + peripheral glands + steroid nuclear receptors" may be a general scheme by which animals control long-lasting developmental processes.

## Summary

- Modes of intercellular chemical signaling: direct contact, autocrine, paracrine, endocrine, neurosecretion (Definition 32.1.1); the difference lies in the path the signal takes, not in the molecule itself.
- The endocrine system consists of ductless endocrine glands and scattered endocrine cells (Definitions 32.2.1, 32.2.2); specificity is determined mainly by the receptors of the target cells.
- The chemical class of a hormone determines its storage, transport, receptor and speed of action:

  | | Peptides, catecholamines | Steroids, thyroid hormones |
  |---|---|---|
  | Solubility | Hydrophilic | Lipophilic |
  | Storage | Secretory granules (thyroid hormone: thyroglobulin) | Steroids not stored; synthesized on demand |
  | Transport | Free | Binding proteins |
  | Receptor | Membrane receptor | Nuclear receptor |
  | Onset | Seconds to minutes | Hours to days |

- Peptide hormones are processed as preprohormone → prohormone (Definition 32.3.1) → hormone (Example 32.3.1).
- Features of hormone action: messenger action, specificity, high potency and amplification, interaction (synergism, antagonism, permissive action, Definition 32.5.1). The number and sensitivity of receptors are regulated (down-regulation, desensitization, up-regulation).
- Clearance: degradation, receptor-mediated endocytosis, liver–bile, kidney–urine. Secretion rate = MCR × C (Eq. 32.1); $t_{1/2} = \ln 2\cdot V/\mathrm{MCR} \propto 1/f$ (Eqs. 32.2, 32.3): binding proteins prolong the half-life.
- Regulation of secretion: negative feedback regulates the set point of free hormone, and binding proteins change only the total amount (Example 32.7.1); long, short and ultrashort feedback loops (Definition 32.7.1); positive feedback is used for the LH surge, parturition and milk ejection; neural regulation and rhythms on many scales.
- Evolution: neurosecretion is the ancient form of hormone; steroid receptors arose by gene duplication, and an ancestral receptor could bind in advance a hormone that appeared later; the brain–prothoracic gland–ecdysone system of insects is a parallel design that evolved independently.

## Questions

1. A peptide hormone has a distribution volume of $10\,\mathrm{L}$ and a metabolic clearance rate of $1.4\,\mathrm{L\,min^{-1}}$. Find its half-life. If it binds to a binding protein so that the free fraction falls to $5\%$ while the clearance of free hormone is unchanged, what does the half-life become?

   **Answer** By Eq. (32.2), $t_{1/2} = 0.693 \times 10/1.4 \approx 5\,\mathrm{min}$. After binding, the MCR becomes about $0.05 \times 1.4 = 0.07\,\mathrm{L\,min^{-1}}$; if the distribution volume is unchanged, $t_{1/2} \approx 0.693 \times 10/0.07 \approx 100\,\mathrm{min}$, a 20-fold increase (Eq. 32.3). In reality the binding protein itself stays mainly within the vessels, so after binding the distribution volume of the hormone becomes smaller, and the prolongation of the half-life is somewhat less than $1/f$.

2. Why is the secretion rate of steroid hormones almost equal to their synthesis rate, whereas insulin can be secreted in large amounts within minutes? What does this imply for the modes of regulation of the two kinds of hormone?

   **Answer** Steroids are lipophilic and can cross membranes, so they cannot be stored in vesicles; once synthesized they diffuse out of the cell, and they can be regulated only by changing the rate-limiting step of synthesis (entry of cholesterol into the inner mitochondrial membrane), which takes minutes to hours. Peptides such as insulin are stored in secretory granules and are released at once by exocytosis when a stimulus arrives (the first phase of insulin release occurs within minutes), after which synthesis is accelerated to replenish the stores. Peptide hormones are therefore suited to rapid, brief regulation, and steroids to slower and more lasting regulation; correspondingly, the former act on membrane receptors and have short half-lives, whereas the latter act on nuclear receptors, bind to binding proteins and have long half-lives.

3. A patient has a markedly raised total $\mathrm{T_4}$ but normal free $\mathrm{T_4}$ and TSH and no symptoms of hyperthyroidism. What are the possible causes? Why does the feedback system reach such a state?

   **Answer** The most likely cause is an increase of TBG in plasma (pregnancy, oral estrogens, hereditary TBG excess). The feedback system senses and regulates free hormone: when TBG increases, free $\mathrm{T_4}$ falls temporarily, TSH secretion increases, and the thyroid secretes more $\mathrm{T_4}$ until free $\mathrm{T_4}$ returns to the set point; at that point the newly added TBG has been filled with $\mathrm{T_4}$, and total $\mathrm{T_4}$ is above normal (Example 32.7.1 and panel B). Because tissues can take up only free hormone, the metabolic state is normal and no treatment is needed.

4. Using the concepts of this chapter, explain why an animal whose adrenal glands have been removed easily goes into shock on blood loss, which is hard to correct even with ample norepinephrine.

   **Answer** Glucocorticoids have a permissive action on the vasoconstrictor action of catecholamines (Definition 32.5.1, Example 32.5.1): they maintain the expression and signal transduction of the adrenergic receptors of vascular smooth muscle. Without glucocorticoids, the response of the vessels to norepinephrine is greatly weakened, and after blood loss the resistance vessels cannot constrict effectively to maintain arterial pressure. At the same time, the lack of mineralocorticoids makes the body lose $\ce{Na+}$ and reduces blood volume, worsening the circulatory failure further. Only after replacement of cortisol can the action of catecholamines be restored.

5. (Open question) The ecdysone of insects and the steroid hormones of vertebrates evolved independently, but both act on nuclear receptors and both control long-lasting developmental processes. Starting from the nature of the signal (lipophilic, acting on transcription, half-life), discuss why signals of the nuclear receptor–steroid type are suited to controlling developmental transitions, while peptide hormones–membrane receptors are better suited to the rapid adjustment of metabolism.

   **Answer** Key points: developmental transitions (molting, metamorphosis, puberty) require the expression of many genes to be changed simultaneously and in coordination in many tissues and maintained for hours to days; lipophilic steroids can enter all cells and act on nuclear receptors to change the transcription of a set of target genes directly, and the response of a tissue is determined by the expression of receptors and coregulators, so that one systemic signal can elicit different developmental programs in different tissues. A transcriptional response is slow and lasting, which matches the time scale of development, and is not troubled by brief fluctuations in the signal. Metabolic adjustments (blood glucose, blood pressure) must be completed within seconds to minutes and be revocable at any time: peptide hormones stored in granules can be released at once, modify existing enzymes through membrane receptors and second messengers, and a short half-life allows the signal to disappear quickly. The two designs correspond to two kinds of task, "rewriting the program" and "adjusting the running." The overlap of the two can be discussed further: thyroid hormone acts on nuclear receptors but mainly regulates the metabolic rate, and its half-life of a week shows precisely that it regulates the long-term metabolic level rather than rapid changes.
