Every day about $1000\,\mathrm{L}$ of plasma flows through the human kidneys, of which about $180\,\mathrm{L}$ is filtered into the renal tubules, and in the end only $1$–$1.5\,\mathrm{L}$ becomes urine. The kidney first filters out almost all small molecules without selection and then takes back what is needed; this design of "filter everything first, reclaim selectively afterward" lets it clear any substance that is not specifically reclaimed, including foreign compounds it has never encountered. This chapter deals with the first step of this design: the functions of the kidney (Section 22.1) and the structure of the urinary system and micturition (Section 22.2); the structure of the kidney, especially the nephron, the functional unit in which vessels and tubule are coupled (Section 22.3); the four processes of urine formation and the clearance used to quantify them (Section 22.4); and the physics (Section 22.5), determinants (Section 22.6) and regulation (Section 22.7) of glomerular filtration. How the tubules reabsorb and secrete is in Chapter 23, and the concentration of urine in Chapter 24. The Starling forces at capillaries were treated in [Chapter 15](/7.14.15) and are applied here to a special kind of capillary; the development of the kidney is in [7.5.25](/7.5.25).

## 22.1. Functions of the Kidney

| Function | Content | Time scale and regulation |
|---|---|---|
| Excretion | Filters the blood and excretes end products of metabolism (urea, creatinine, uric acid, metabolites of bilirubin), foreign compounds (drugs, toxins) and excess substances | Continuous |
| Regulation | Regulates water, electrolytes ($\ce{Na+}$, $\ce{K+}$, $\ce{Ca^2+}$, phosphate and others) and acid–base balance, and thereby the volume and osmolality of body fluids and arterial blood pressure | Minutes to days, under hormonal and neural control |
| Endocrine | Secretes **erythropoietin**, renin and prostaglandins; activates 25-hydroxyvitamin D to 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D (Chapter 36) | Hours to days |
| Metabolic | **Gluconeogenesis**: in prolonged fasting the kidney can produce $20\%$–$40\%$ of the body's glucose; degrades small peptide hormones (insulin, parathyroid hormone and others) | |

The kidney is the final executor in regulating the extracellular fluid: other organs can buffer or shift water and ions temporarily, but only the kidney can change their excretion as needed. For example, the daily dietary intake of NaCl can vary from less than $1\,\mathrm{g}$ to more than $20\,\mathrm{g}$, and within a few days urinary $\ce{Na+}$ excretion catches up with intake, with only a small change in extracellular volume and body mass (Chapter 24).

## 22.2. Structure of the Urinary System and Micturition

### 22.2.1. Ureters, Bladder and Urethra

Urine is formed in the nephrons, collects in the collecting ducts, and flows through the papillary ducts into the minor calyces, major calyces and renal pelvis. The smooth muscle of the walls of the calyces and pelvis contains pacemaker cells, whose rhythmic contractions squeeze urine into the ureter; peristaltic waves of the ureter (several per minute) carry urine into the bladder. The ureter runs obliquely through the bladder wall, and when the bladder fills and its internal pressure rises, it compresses this segment of ureter and prevents reflux of urine.

The bladder is built of smooth muscle (the detrusor), whose cells are electrically coupled by **gap junctions** and contract cooperatively; the upper part is the storage lumen, and the lower part, the trigone and bladder neck, connects with the urethra. Smooth muscle in the bladder neck forms the internal urethral sphincter, and where the urethra passes through the pelvic floor there is an external urethral sphincter of skeletal muscle. Contraction of the detrusor can raise bladder pressure to $40$–$60\,\mathrm{mmHg}$ ($5.3$–$8.0\,\mathrm{kPa}$; $1\,\mathrm{mmHg} = 0.1333\,\mathrm{kPa}$). The bladder wall is highly plastic (stress relaxation, the smooth muscle properties of [Chapter 10](/7.14.10)): as the volume increases from a few tens of milliliters to $300$–$400\,\mathrm{mL}$, the pressure hardly rises, and only after that does it rise rapidly.

### 22.2.2. Innervation and the Micturition Reflex

The bladder and urethra are innervated by three groups of nerves:

| Nerve | Segment | Transmitter and receptor | Action |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pelvic nerve (**parasympathetic nervous system**) | Sacral cord $\mathrm{S}_2$–$\mathrm{S}_4$ | ACh, $\mathrm{M}_3$ | Contraction of the detrusor, relaxation of the internal urethral sphincter: the main motor output for micturition |
| Hypogastric nerve (**sympathetic nervous system**) | $\mathrm{T}_{11}$–$\mathrm{L}_2$ | NE, $\beta_3$ (detrusor) and $\alpha_1$ (bladder neck) | Relaxation of the detrusor, contraction of the bladder neck: promotes storage; also innervates the vessels of the bladder |
| Pudendal nerve (somatic) | Sacral cord $\mathrm{S}_2$–$\mathrm{S}_4$ | ACh, nicotinic receptor | Contraction of the external urethral sphincter, under voluntary control |

The pelvic and hypogastric nerves also contain sensory fibers that carry stretch signals from the bladder wall (degree of filling) and pain.

**Definition 22.2.1 (Micturition Reflex)** The reflex in which, when the bladder fills, stretch receptors in its wall send signals through afferent fibers of the pelvic nerve to the sacral cord and parasympathetic efferent fibers then cause the detrusor to contract is called the **micturition reflex**.

The micturition reflex is self-reinforcing: once the detrusor begins to contract, the rise in bladder pressure further stimulates the stretch receptors and the contraction becomes stronger and stronger until it peaks; this is **positive feedback**. If the micturition reflex does not lead to voiding, the contraction subsides after several seconds to a minute, the bladder continues to fill, and the next reflex is stronger and more frequent. When the reflex is strong enough, another reflex through the pudendal nerve relaxes the external urethral sphincter; but the external sphincter is also under voluntary control, and in the end the brain decides whether voiding occurs.

### 22.2.3. Control of Micturition by the Brain

The brainstem and cerebral cortex contain centers that inhibit and excite micturition: the pontine micturition center (Barrington's nucleus) coordinates contraction of the detrusor with relaxation of the urethral sphincters; the periaqueductal gray integrates sensory information from the bladder; and the prefrontal cortex inhibits micturition when it is socially inappropriate. Normally the brain keeps the micturition reflex inhibited; when there is a wish to void, the brain releases the inhibition and actively initiates the reflex, at the same time relaxing the external urethral sphincter, and contraction of the abdominal muscles raises bladder pressure to assist voiding.

In patients whose spinal cord is transected above the sacral segment, control by the brain is lost; early on the bladder does not contract at all (spinal shock), after which the micturition reflex returns but is not under voluntary control and is often accompanied by dyssynergia of the detrusor and sphincters; in infants, before the brain's control matures, micturition is also accomplished mainly by the spinal reflex.

## 22.3. Structure of the Kidney

### 22.3.1. Gross Structure and Blood Supply

The kidneys lie behind the peritoneum, each weighing about $150\,\mathrm{g}$ in an adult. On section, an outer cortex and an inner medulla can be seen; the medulla is divided into 8–18 renal pyramids, whose tips (the renal papillae) project into the minor calyces, and between the pyramids lie extensions of cortex into the medulla (the renal columns).

Renal blood flow is very large: about $1.1$–$1.2\,\mathrm{L\,min^{-1}}$ for the two kidneys, about $20\%$–$22\%$ of resting **cardiac output**, although the two kidneys make up only about $0.4\%$ of body mass; per $100\,\mathrm{g}$ of tissue it is about $400\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}}$, severalfold that of the myocardium. This large flow is not for the kidney's own metabolism (the arteriovenous oxygen content difference of the kidney is very small) but for the continuous processing of plasma. The distribution of flow is highly uneven: about $94\%$ passes through the cortex, $5\%$–$6\%$ through the outer medulla and less than $1\%$ through the inner medulla; the low flow in the medulla is critical for maintaining its high osmolality (Chapter 24).

The vessels in sequence are: renal artery → segmental arteries → interlobar arteries → arcuate arteries (running along the corticomedullary border) → interlobular arteries → afferent arterioles → glomerular capillaries → efferent arterioles → peritubular capillaries (cortex) or vasa recta (juxtamedullary) → interlobular veins → arcuate veins → interlobar veins → renal vein. A distinctive feature of the kidney is two capillary beds in series, separated by a resistance vessel (the efferent arteriole): the pressure in the glomerular capillaries is high (about $50$–$60\,\mathrm{mmHg}$), suited to filtration; the pressure in the peritubular capillaries is low (about $15$–$20\,\mathrm{mmHg}$), and the plasma protein is concentrated after filtration, so the colloid osmotic pressure is high, suited to reabsorption (Chapter 23).

### 22.3.2. The Nephron

**Definition 22.3.1 (Nephron)** The structure that consists of a renal corpuscle (the glomerular capillaries and the Bowman's capsule surrounding them) and the renal tubule connected to it and that can form urine independently is called the **nephron**; it is the basic unit of renal structure and function.

Each human kidney has about $10^6$ nephrons (with large individual variation, about $0.2$–$2.7 \times 10^6$), and none are formed after birth. The renal tubule is divided in sequence into the proximal tubule (proximal convoluted tubule and straight part), the thin segments of the loop of Henle (thin descending and thin ascending limbs), the thick ascending limb, the distal convoluted tubule and the connecting tubule, and then joins the collecting duct. The collecting duct derives developmentally from the ureteric bud and does not belong to the nephron, but together with the distal tubule it makes the final adjustment of the urine. Functionally, the nephron is not just a tube but a unit in which vessels and tubule are coupled: the afferent and efferent arterioles control filtration, the peritubular capillaries take back the reabsorbed substances, and the distal tubule returns to its own glomerulus to monitor the composition of the filtrate (Section 22.3.3).

By the position of the renal corpuscle and the length of the loop of Henle, nephrons fall into two classes:

| | Cortical nephron | Juxtamedullary nephron |
|---|---|---|
| Proportion (human) | $85\%$–$90\%$ | $10\%$–$15\%$ |
| Renal corpuscle | In the outer and middle cortex, smaller | Near the corticomedullary border, larger |
| Loop of Henle | Short, reaching only the outer medulla, almost no thin segment | Long, reaching deep into the inner medulla or even the renal papilla |
| Afferent and efferent arterioles | Afferent wider (caliber about $2:1$) | Similar calibers |
| After the efferent arteriole | A peritubular capillary network wrapped around the tubules in the cortex | In addition to peritubular capillaries, gives off U-shaped vasa recta that run into the medulla parallel to the loop of Henle |
| Main function | Formation and reabsorption of urine | Concentration and dilution of urine (Chapter 24) |

The form of renal tubular epithelial cells reflects their transport tasks: proximal tubule cells are tall, with dense microvilli (brush border) and abundant mitochondria, and the basolateral membrane is deeply infolded; cells of the thin segments are flat, with few mitochondria; cells of the thick ascending limb are rich in mitochondria and lack a brush border; the collecting duct consists of two cell types, **principal cells** and **intercalated cells** (Chapters 23 and 25).

### 22.3.3. The Juxtaglomerular Apparatus

**Definition 22.3.2 (Juxtaglomerular Apparatus and Macula Densa)** The end of the thick ascending limb (just before the distal convoluted tubule) returns to the vascular pole of its own nephron and lies against the afferent and efferent arterioles. Here the tubular epithelial cells become taller and their nuclei crowded, forming the **macula densa**, which senses the NaCl concentration of the tubular fluid; cells in the wall of the afferent arteriole that derive from smooth muscle cells and contain renin granules are called juxtaglomerular cells (granular cells); the macula densa, the juxtaglomerular cells and the extraglomerular mesangial cells lying between the two arterioles together constitute the **juxtaglomerular apparatus**.

The juxtaglomerular apparatus is the "quality-control and regulating device" of the nephron: the macula densa examines the tubular fluid after it has passed the thick ascending limb; the information is passed through the extraglomerular mesangial cells (which have gap junctions with smooth muscle cells) to the afferent arteriole, regulating the GFR of its own nephron (tubuloglomerular feedback, Section 22.7.3), and to the juxtaglomerular cells, regulating the secretion of renin (Chapter 24).

## 22.4. The Basic Processes of Urine Formation and Clearance

### 22.4.1. The Four Processes

Urine formation includes four processes: (1) glomerular filtration: water and small solutes of plasma are filtered into Bowman's space, forming the primary urine (ultrafiltrate); (2) reabsorption: substances return from the tubular fluid to the peritubular blood; (3) secretion: substances pass from the peritubular blood or the tubular epithelial cells into the tubular fluid; (4) excretion: the final urine is eliminated through the collecting duct. For any substance $X$, the amount excreted per unit time is

$$
\text{excretion} = \text{filtration} - \text{reabsorption} + \text{secretion}.
$$

Different substances are handled differently, in four classes:

| Handling | Examples | Excretion compared with filtration |
|---|---|---|
| Filtered only, neither reabsorbed nor secreted | Inulin, (approximately) creatinine | Equal |
| Filtered, partly reabsorbed | $\ce{Na+}$, $\ce{Cl-}$, urea, water | Less |
| Filtered, completely reabsorbed | Glucose, amino acids | Almost zero normally |
| Filtered, then secreted | Organic acids and bases, PAH, many drugs, $\ce{H+}$, $\ce{K+}$ (as needed) | Greater |

### 22.4.2. Clearance

To quantify the handling of a substance by the kidney we use a quantity with the dimension of volume:

**Definition 22.4.1 (Clearance)** Let the plasma concentration of substance $X$ be $P_X$, its urine concentration $U_X$ and the urine flow $\dot V$. The equivalent volume of plasma completely cleared of $X$ by the kidney per unit time,

$$
C_X = \frac{U_X\,\dot V}{P_X}
$$
is called the renal **clearance** of $X$, in units of $\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}}$.

Clearance does not mean that part of the plasma is actually cleared completely while the rest is unchanged; it converts the excretion rate $U_X\dot V$ into "the amount of $X$ contained in so much plasma". Its value is that the clearances of different substances can be compared directly, and that the clearance of certain special substances equals important physiological quantities.

**Example 22.4.1** A substance has a plasma concentration of $2\,\mathrm{mg\,mL^{-1}}$, a urine concentration of $50\,\mathrm{mg\,mL^{-1}}$ and a urine flow of $1.5\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}}$. Its clearance is $C = 50 \times 1.5/2 = 37.5\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}}$: the amount the kidney excretes per minute ($75\,\mathrm{mg}$) equals the amount contained in $37.5\,\mathrm{mL}$ of plasma.

### 22.4.3. Glomerular Filtration Rate, Renal Plasma Flow and Filtration Fraction

**Definition 22.4.2 (Glomerular Filtration Rate)** The volume of ultrafiltrate formed by the two kidneys per unit time is called the **glomerular filtration rate** (GFR).

If a substance is freely filtered at the glomerulus (its concentration in the ultrafiltrate equals the plasma concentration) and is neither reabsorbed nor secreted nor metabolized in the tubule, then the filtered load $\mathrm{GFR}\cdot P_X$ equals the excreted amount $U_X\dot V$, so

$$
\mathrm{GFR} = \frac{U_X\,\dot V}{P_X} = C_X .
$$

The plant polysaccharide inulin (about $5\,\mathrm{kDa}$) satisfies all the conditions and is the gold standard for measuring GFR, but requires continuous intravenous infusion. In the clinic the endogenous creatinine is often used: it is produced by muscle at a roughly constant rate and its plasma concentration is stable; but the proximal tubule secretes a little creatinine, so creatinine clearance is about $10\%$–$20\%$ higher than true GFR. More commonly, only plasma creatinine is measured, and GFR is estimated (eGFR) from an empirical formula (such as the CKD-EPI equation) by age and sex. At steady state production of creatinine equals its excretion, $P_{\mathrm{Cr}} \approx$ production rate $/\mathrm{GFR}$, so when GFR is halved, plasma creatinine approximately doubles. The GFR of a normal adult is about $125\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}}$, that is, $180\,\mathrm{L\,d^{-1}}$.

**Definition 22.4.3 (Renal Plasma Flow and Filtration Fraction)** The volume of plasma flowing through the two kidneys per unit time is called the **renal plasma flow** (RPF); the ratio of GFR to RPF,

$$
\mathrm{FF} = \frac{\mathrm{GFR}}{\mathrm{RPF}}
$$
is called the **filtration fraction**.

If a substance is completely removed from the plasma in one pass through the kidney (filtration plus secretion), its clearance equals the RPF. Para-aminohippuric acid (PAH) nearly meets this condition: it is filtered and also secreted efficiently by the organic anion transporter of the proximal tubule, but the PAH in plasma that flows through tissue not taking part in filtration and secretion (the renal capsule, pelvis and part of the medulla) cannot be cleared. The extraction ratio of PAH, $E_{\mathrm{PAH}} = (P_{\mathrm{a}} - P_{\mathrm{v}})/P_{\mathrm{a}}$ (the difference between renal arterial and renal venous concentrations divided by the arterial concentration), is about $0.9$, so

$$
\mathrm{RPF} = \frac{C_{\mathrm{PAH}}}{E_{\mathrm{PAH}}}, \qquad \mathrm{RBF} = \frac{\mathrm{RPF}}{1 - H},
$$

where $H$ is the **hematocrit**. Normal RPF is about $650\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}}$ and the filtration fraction about $20\%$.

**Example 22.4.2** GFR is measured with inulin and RPF with PAH: plasma inulin $1.0\,\mathrm{mg\,mL^{-1}}$, urine inulin $125\,\mathrm{mg\,mL^{-1}}$; plasma PAH $0.05\,\mathrm{mg\,mL^{-1}}$, urine PAH $30\,\mathrm{mg\,mL^{-1}}$; urine flow $1.2\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}}$; PAH extraction ratio $0.9$; hematocrit $0.45$.

1. $\mathrm{GFR} = C_{\mathrm{in}} = 125 \times 1.2/1.0 = 150\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}}$.
2. $C_{\mathrm{PAH}} = 30 \times 1.2/0.05 = 720\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}}$, $\mathrm{RPF} = 720/0.9 = 800\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}}$, $\mathrm{RBF} = 800/0.55 = 1455\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}}$.
3. $\mathrm{FF} = 150/800 = 18.8\%$.

In such calculations one should check that the result is reasonable: the filtration fraction cannot approach $100\%$, otherwise the blood in the efferent arteriole would lose almost all its plasma and could not flow; if urine PAH were only $6\,\mathrm{mg\,mL^{-1}}$, the calculation would give $\mathrm{RPF} = 160\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}}$ and $\mathrm{FF} = 94\%$, indicating that the data are wrong.

:::

With GFR known, the clearance of any substance can be compared: $C_X < \mathrm{GFR}$ shows that it is net reabsorbed (such as $\ce{Na+}$ and urea), and $C_X > \mathrm{GFR}$ that it is net secreted (such as PAH). $C_X/\mathrm{GFR}$ equals the fractional excretion of $X$, the fraction of filtered $X$ that finally appears in the urine.

## 22.5. Glomerular Filtration

### 22.5.1. Massive Filtration, Nearly Complete Reclamation

The kidney filters $180\,\mathrm{L}$ of fluid per day, about 60 times the plasma volume ($3\,\mathrm{L}$), that is, all the plasma is "processed" by the kidney about 60 times a day; yet urine is only $1$–$1.5\,\mathrm{L\,d^{-1}}$, and more than $99\%$ of the filtrate is reabsorbed. This apparently wasteful design (reabsorption consumes a great deal of energy, Chapter 23) has two advantages: metabolites and foreign substances, whether or not they have a dedicated transporter, are cleared rapidly as long as they are not reabsorbed; and the composition of body fluids can be regulated accurately and quickly by adjusting the fraction reabsorbed.

### 22.5.2. Net Filtration Pressure and the Starling Forces

The driving force of filtration is the same as in ordinary capillaries, the algebraic sum of the four **Starling forces** ([Chapter 15](/7.14.15)):

**Definition 22.5.1 (Net Filtration Pressure)** The sum of the glomerular capillary hydrostatic pressure $P_{\mathrm{GC}}$ and the colloid osmotic pressure of the fluid in Bowman's space $\pi_{\mathrm{BS}}$, which promote filtration, minus the sum of the Bowman's space pressure $P_{\mathrm{BS}}$ and the colloid osmotic pressure $\pi_{\mathrm{GC}}$ of the glomerular capillary plasma, which oppose it, is called the **net filtration pressure**:

$$
P_{\mathrm{UF}} = (P_{\mathrm{GC}} - P_{\mathrm{BS}}) - (\pi_{\mathrm{GC}} - \pi_{\mathrm{BS}}),
\qquad
\mathrm{GFR} = K_{\mathrm{f}}\,\bar P_{\mathrm{UF}},
$$

where $\bar P_{\mathrm{UF}}$ is the mean along the capillary and $K_{\mathrm{f}}$ is the filtration coefficient.

Normally there is very little protein in Bowman's space, and $\pi_{\mathrm{BS}} \approx 0$. These pressures cannot be measured directly in humans, and the usual estimates (Guyton) are: $P_{\mathrm{GC}} \approx 60\,\mathrm{mmHg}$, $P_{\mathrm{BS}} \approx 18\,\mathrm{mmHg}$, and $\pi_{\mathrm{GC}}$ rising from about $28\,\mathrm{mmHg}$ at the afferent end to about $36\,\mathrm{mmHg}$ at the efferent end (mean about $32$), giving a mean net filtration pressure of about $10\,\mathrm{mmHg}$ ($1.3\,\mathrm{kPa}$). Micropuncture experiments in the rat directly give $P_{\mathrm{GC}} \approx 45$–$50\,\mathrm{mmHg}$, falling by only $1$–$3\,\mathrm{mmHg}$ along the capillary; $P_{\mathrm{BS}} \approx 10\,\mathrm{mmHg}$; $\pi_{\mathrm{GC}}$ rising from about $25\,\mathrm{mmHg}$ to about $35$–$40\,\mathrm{mmHg}$. Compared with ordinary capillaries, the hydrostatic pressure in the glomerular capillaries is much higher and hardly falls along the vessel, because downstream of it there is the resistance vessel of the efferent arteriole.

### 22.5.3. Filtration Equilibrium

Along the glomerular capillary water is filtered out and protein is left behind, so the plasma protein concentration and with it $\pi_{\mathrm{GC}}$ rise, while the hydrostatic pressure hardly changes. The net filtration pressure therefore decreases from the afferent toward the efferent end.

**Definition 22.5.2 (Filtration Equilibrium)** When $\pi_{\mathrm{GC}}$ has risen to equal $P_{\mathrm{GC}} - P_{\mathrm{BS}}$ and the net filtration pressure has fallen to zero, the remaining segment of the capillary no longer filters; this state is called **filtration equilibrium**.

The plasma flow $Q(x)$ along the capillary satisfies

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}Q}{\mathrm{d}x} = -K_{\mathrm{f}}\left[P_{\mathrm{GC}}(x) - P_{\mathrm{BS}} - \pi(C)\right], \qquad C = C_{\mathrm{a}}\,\frac{Q(0)}{Q(x)},
$$

where $x \in [0, 1]$ is the normalized capillary length and $C$ is the plasma protein concentration (protein is not filtered, so $CQ$ is conserved); the relation between colloid osmotic pressure and protein concentration is not linear, and the empirical Landis–Pappenheimer formula $\pi = 2.1C + 0.16C^2 + 0.009C^3$ ($\pi$ in mmHg, $C$ in $\mathrm{g\,dL^{-1}}$) is commonly used. Numerical solution shows:

- Filtration occurs mainly in the first half of the capillary near the afferent end; when filtration equilibrium is reached, the later capillary is a "reserve" filtration area.
- Under filtration equilibrium GFR depends on plasma flow: when flow increases, the protein concentration rises more slowly, the equilibrium point shifts downstream, the area taking part in filtration increases, and GFR increases while the filtration fraction is essentially unchanged or slightly lower. For example, rapid infusion of a large volume of physiological saline dilutes the plasma protein and increases renal plasma flow, and GFR rises with it; in severe hypoxia or shock, sympathetic nerves sharply reduce renal blood flow, and GFR also falls markedly.
- There is as yet no direct evidence whether human glomeruli reach filtration equilibrium; the estimated $K_{\mathrm{f}}$ of the human kidney is low, and filtration disequilibrium may prevail, in which case GFR is more sensitive to $K_{\mathrm{f}}$ and less sensitive to plasma flow.

### 22.5.4. The Filtration Coefficient

$K_{\mathrm{f}}$ equals the product of the hydraulic conductivity of the capillary and the filtration area. In humans $K_{\mathrm{f}}$ of the two kidneys is about $12.5\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}\,mmHg^{-1}}$, about $4.2\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}\,mmHg^{-1}}$ per $100\,\mathrm{g}$ of renal tissue, whereas ordinary tissues such as skeletal muscle have only about $0.01$, a difference of about 400-fold. There are two reasons: the filtration area is large (about $2 \times 10^6$ glomeruli in the two kidneys, with a total capillary area of about $0.5$–$2\,\mathrm{m^2}$, morphometric estimates differing), and the permeability per unit area is also high (the endothelium is fenestrated). Precisely because $K_{\mathrm{f}}$ is so large, a net filtration pressure of only about $10\,\mathrm{mmHg}$ can produce a filtration of $125\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}}$.

$K_{\mathrm{f}}$ varies little physiologically (contraction of intraglomerular mesangial cells can reduce the filtration area) but is often lowered in disease: chronic hypertension and diabetes thicken the glomerular basement membrane and cause sclerosis of some glomeruli, and glomerulonephritis narrows or occludes the capillary lumen.

### 22.5.5. The Filtration Barrier

The glomerular filtration barrier consists of three layers:

1. Capillary endothelium: it has fenestrae about $70$–$100\,\mathrm{nm}$ in diameter, which block blood cells but not plasma proteins; its surface is covered by a negatively charged glycocalyx.
2. Glomerular basement membrane: a gel made of type IV collagen, laminin and negatively charged heparan sulfate proteoglycan.
3. Podocytes: the visceral epithelial cells of Bowman's capsule extend interdigitating foot processes; the filtration slits between foot processes are about $25$–$40\,\mathrm{nm}$ wide and are bridged by the slit diaphragm (containing proteins such as nephrin and podocin).

The filtration barrier selects by molecular size and charge: neutral molecules with an effective radius below about $2\,\mathrm{nm}$ (about $10\,\mathrm{kDa}$) are freely filtered (inulin has an effective radius of about $1.5\,\mathrm{nm}$); those larger than about $4.2\,\mathrm{nm}$ (about $70\,\mathrm{kDa}$) are almost not filtered; for those in between, filtration decreases as the radius increases. Among dextrans of the same size, positively charged ones are filtered more readily than neutral ones, and negatively charged ones less readily, which shows that the barrier is negatively charged. The sieving coefficient (the ratio of concentrations in ultrafiltrate and plasma) of plasma albumin (about $66\,\mathrm{kDa}$, effective radius about $3.6\,\mathrm{nm}$, negatively charged) is only about $10^{-4}$–$10^{-3}$; the small amount of albumin filtered is reclaimed by receptor-mediated endocytosis in the proximal tubule, and normal urinary albumin is below $30\,\mathrm{mg\,d^{-1}}$. Genetic defects of the podocyte or slit diaphragm (such as the congenital nephrotic syndrome of the Finnish type caused by mutations of the nephrin gene) cause massive proteinuria, showing that the podocyte is a key layer of the barrier. The extent of charge selectivity has been disputed in recent years, but the clinical observation that proteinuria increases when the negative charge is reduced is firm.

Glomerular filtration is, in engineering terms, cross-flow filtration: the liquid flows along the surface of the filter membrane and only part of it passes perpendicularly through, and the retained macromolecules are carried off with the main stream rather than piling up on the membrane. Laboratories use cross-flow filtration to concentrate proteins on the same principle; if all the liquid were made to pass perpendicularly through, as in ordinary filtration (dead-end filtration), the protein would soon clog the membrane.

## 22.6. Determinants of GFR

From the expression for the net filtration pressure, GFR is determined by $K_{\mathrm{f}}$ and three pressures:

- A rise in Bowman's space pressure $P_{\mathrm{BS}}$ lowers GFR. Physiologically it varies little; obstruction of the urinary tract (stones, prostatic hyperplasia, compression by tumor) raises it and can severely lower GFR, and prolonged obstruction causes hydronephrosis and renal damage.
- A rise in plasma colloid osmotic pressure $\pi_{\mathrm{GC}}$ (as when dehydration concentrates plasma protein) lowers GFR; a fall in plasma protein (liver disease, nephrotic syndrome) tends to raise GFR. As described in Section 22.5.3, renal plasma flow affects GFR by changing the rate at which $\pi_{\mathrm{GC}}$ rises along the capillary.
- The glomerular capillary hydrostatic pressure $P_{\mathrm{GC}}$ is the main physiological means of regulating GFR, and it is determined by arterial blood pressure, the afferent arteriolar resistance $R_{\mathrm{A}}$ and the efferent arteriolar resistance $R_{\mathrm{E}}$.

The effects of $R_{\mathrm{A}}$ and $R_{\mathrm{E}}$ are asymmetric. When the afferent arteriole constricts, $P_{\mathrm{GC}}$ and renal blood flow fall together and GFR decreases monotonically; when the efferent arteriole constricts, $P_{\mathrm{GC}}$ rises while renal blood flow falls, and the two act oppositely on GFR: with mild constriction the rise of $P_{\mathrm{GC}}$ predominates and GFR increases; with strong constriction the reduced flow makes $\pi_{\mathrm{GC}}$ rise rapidly along the capillary (equilibrium is reached earlier), and GFR instead falls, a biphasic change. This relation can be calculated by placing the glomerular filtration model in series with the afferent, efferent and downstream resistances:

This asymmetry is important clinically: angiotensin II constricts the efferent arteriole preferentially and helps maintain GFR when renal perfusion pressure is lowered (as in renal artery stenosis and heart failure); if an angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor or AT1 receptor blocker is then used, the efferent arteriole dilates and GFR can fall abruptly. Conversely, **prostaglandins** dilate the afferent arteriole, and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, by inhibiting prostaglandin synthesis, can cause acute kidney injury in patients with a depleted blood volume.

## 22.7. Regulation of Renal Blood Flow and GFR

### 22.7.1. Neural and Humoral Regulation

The renal vessels are innervated by the **sympathetic nervous system** (preganglionic neurons in spinal segments of about $\mathrm{T}_{10}$–$\mathrm{L}_2$, postganglionic fibers mainly from the celiac and aorticorenal ganglia), which is distributed to vessels at all levels, to the juxtaglomerular cells and to the renal tubules, and there is almost no parasympathetic innervation. Strong sympathetic excitation (through $\alpha_1$ receptors) constricts the afferent and efferent arterioles, lowering renal blood flow and GFR, and at the same time (through $\beta_1$ receptors) promotes renin secretion and directly promotes reabsorption of $\ce{Na+}$ in the proximal tubule and loop of Henle. In quiet healthy people sympathetic nerves have little effect on renal blood flow; their role appears in emergencies: in strenuous exercise, severe hemorrhage or cerebral ischemia, the renal vessels constrict and give blood up to the heart, brain and skeletal muscle.

Humoral factors act mainly by constricting (lowering flow) or dilating (increasing flow) the afferent and efferent arterioles:

| Factor | Source | Action on renal vessels | GFR |
|---|---|---|---|
| Norepinephrine, **epinephrine** | Sympathetic nerve endings, adrenal medulla | Constrict afferent and efferent arterioles | ↓ |
| Endothelin | Damaged or stretched vascular endothelium (**paracrine**) | Constricts | ↓ |
| Angiotensin II | Circulating and locally formed in the kidney | Constricts, the efferent arteriole more sensitive than the afferent | Usually maintained or slightly lowered |
| Nitric oxide | Endothelium (shear stress stimulates endothelial **nitric oxide synthase**) | Dilates, maintains basal flow | ↑ |
| **Prostaglandins** ($\mathrm{PGE_2}$, $\mathrm{PGI_2}$) | Formed in the kidney | Dilate, buffering the constriction by sympathetic nerves and angiotensin II | ↑ |
| Bradykinin, atrial natriuretic peptide | Renal kallikrein–kinin system, atrium | Dilate the afferent arteriole (natriuretic peptide also constricts the efferent arteriole) | ↑ |

### 22.7.2. Autoregulation

**Autoregulation** ([Chapter 1](/7.14.1)) is most prominent in the kidney: when renal arterial pressure varies between about $80$ and $180\,\mathrm{mmHg}$ ($10.7$–$24\,\mathrm{kPa}$; the limits differ somewhat between measurements, the lower one about $75$–$80$ and the upper one about $160$–$180\,\mathrm{mmHg}$), renal blood flow and GFR remain essentially unchanged even when innervation has been removed (isolated perfused kidney or transplanted kidney). Its significance is to isolate excretion from fluctuations in blood pressure.

**Example 22.7.1 (What If Autoregulation Is Lost?)** Normally arterial pressure is $100\,\mathrm{mmHg}$, $\mathrm{GFR} = 180\,\mathrm{L\,d^{-1}}$, tubular reabsorption is $178.5\,\mathrm{L\,d^{-1}}$ and urine volume $1.5\,\mathrm{L\,d^{-1}}$. Suppose there is no autoregulation and that a $25\%$ rise in blood pressure to $125\,\mathrm{mmHg}$ also raises GFR by $25\%$, to $225\,\mathrm{L\,d^{-1}}$; if reabsorption stays the same, urine volume would be $225 - 178.5 = 46.5\,\mathrm{L\,d^{-1}}$, 30 times normal. Since the plasma totals only about $3\,\mathrm{L}$, severe dehydration would develop within a few hours. In fact the change of GFR with blood pressure is limited to a small range by autoregulation, and the residual change in GFR is further partly offset by a corresponding change in tubular reabsorption (**glomerulotubular balance**, Chapter 23); the two layers of protection make urine volume very insensitive to short-term fluctuations in blood pressure. (In the model of Section 22.6 without autoregulation, the change in GFR is even larger than the change in blood pressure, because the net filtration pressure is only $P_{\mathrm{GC}}$ minus a nearly constant large number.)

:::

It should be pointed out that "insensitive" applies only to short-term fluctuations; long-term changes in blood pressure markedly alter the excretion of $\ce{Na+}$ and water through **pressure natriuresis**, which is the basis of the long-term regulation of blood pressure (Chapter 24).

### 22.7.3. The Myogenic Mechanism and Tubuloglomerular Feedback

Autoregulation is accomplished by two mechanisms working together:

- The myogenic mechanism: when the smooth muscle of the afferent arteriole is stretched it depolarizes, stretch-activated cation channels admit $\ce{Ca^2+}$, and the arteriole constricts; when blood pressure falls it dilates. It acts within seconds and not only stabilizes GFR but also protects the glomerulus from damage by high pressure.
- Tubuloglomerular feedback:

**Definition 22.7.1 (Tubuloglomerular Feedback)** When the macula densa detects an increase in the NaCl reaching the beginning of the distal tubule, it causes the afferent arteriole of its own nephron to constrict and GFR to fall; when NaCl decreases, the opposite occurs. This **negative feedback**, with the composition of the tubular fluid as input and the GFR of the nephron as output, is called **tubuloglomerular feedback** (TGF).

Mechanism: macula densa cells take up NaCl through the apical $\ce{Na+}$–$\ce{K+}$–$2\ce{Cl-}$ cotransporter (NKCC2), the amount entering increasing with the NaCl concentration of the tubular fluid; the cell releases ATP, which is degraded extracellularly to adenosine, and adenosine acts on $\mathrm{A}_1$ receptors of the afferent arteriolar smooth muscle to make it constrict. Neuronal nitric oxide synthase in the macula densa weakens this response and adjusts its gain. When NaCl decreases, the macula densa also promotes renin secretion by the juxtaglomerular cells through $\mathrm{PGE_2}$ formed by cyclooxygenase 2, and angiotensin II constricts the efferent arteriole and helps restore GFR. Tubuloglomerular feedback requires NaCl to flow through the loop of Henle and responds more slowly than the myogenic mechanism (about $10$–$60\,\mathrm{s}$); the feedback loops formed by the two make the blood flow of a single nephron oscillate spontaneously at a frequency of about $0.02$–$0.05\,\mathrm{Hz}$, a physiological example of oscillation produced by delayed negative feedback as discussed in [7.9.16](/7.9.16).

The physiological significance of tubuloglomerular feedback is to match filtration to the transport capacity of the tubule: if GFR rises, the NaCl delivered to the distal tubule exceeds what it can handle finely, and the feedback lowers GFR. What it stabilizes is the GFR of the single nephron, not just the mean of the whole kidney.

## 22.8. Evolution and Comparison

### 22.8.1. Excretory Organs of Invertebrates

Although the excretory organs of animals differ in form, most follow the same two-step principle as the nephron: first produce a primary fluid, then alter its composition along a duct. The primary fluid is produced in one of two ways: by pressure-driven ultrafiltration, or by active secretion of ions that draws water along.

| Organ | Group | Production of primary fluid | Alteration of composition |
|---|---|---|---|
| Contractile vacuole | Freshwater protists, sponges | Fluid from the cytoplasm is secreted into the vacuole (proton pumps and water channels) | Periodic expulsion of excess water |
| Protonephridium | Flatworms, rotifers, nemerteans, larvae of some annelids | Flame cells (or tube cells) at the blind end create negative pressure by ciliary beating, and tissue fluid is ultrafiltered through narrow slits between cells into the lumen | Tube cells reabsorb ions, freshwater species excrete dilute urine |
| Metanephridium | Annelids, mollusks | Coelomic fluid is ultrafiltered by the coelomic epithelium outside the vessels (structures resembling podocytes); the nephrostome opening into the coelom collects the coelomic fluid | The long duct reabsorbs ions and useful substances; earthworms excrete hypo-osmotic urine |
| Antennal gland (green gland) | Crustaceans | Podocytes in the end sac ultrafilter hemolymph | The duct reabsorbs; freshwater crayfish excrete hypo-osmotic urine |
| Malpighian tubule | Insects, myriapods, arachnids | The tubule epithelium actively secretes $\ce{K+}$ (apical V-type $\ce{H+}$-ATPase and $\ce{K+}/\ce{H+}$ antiporter), $\ce{Cl-}$ and water follow, forming a primary urine isosmotic with hemolymph | The hindgut and rectum reabsorb water and ions (Chapter 23) |

The mollusk plan is closest to that of vertebrates: the pressure of the heart ultrafilters hemolymph into the pericardial cavity, and pericardial fluid enters the kidney through the nephrostome, where its composition is altered. Insects have no high-pressure circulation, and the primary urine is produced by secretion; the cost of this "secretory urine formation" is that organic substances cannot be cleared freely but only through dedicated secretory transporters, but it also works with the low-pressure open circulation.

### 22.8.2. The Vertebrate Nephron

The vertebrate nephron begins with glomerular ultrafiltration. In the embryo the kidney appears in three successive generations, pronephros, mesonephros and metanephros ([7.5.25](/7.5.25)): the functional kidney of adult fish and amphibians is the mesonephros (or opisthonephros), and that of adult amniotes is the **metanephros**. The nephrons of the various groups:

- The glomeruli of hagfish are large and arranged segmentally, primitive and simple; lampreys and most fish have typical glomeruli.
- The kidney of a few marine teleosts (toadfish, anglerfish, pipefish and seahorses, about 30 species) has no glomeruli, and urine is formed entirely by tubular secretion. In seawater fish must conserve water and filtration is rather a burden; aglomerular kidneys have arisen independently in several groups, showing that glomeruli can be dispensed with in seawater. This fact was also used by Homer Smith to argue that glomeruli originated in freshwater ancestors (to excrete excess water), but fossil and phylogenetic evidence does not support a freshwater origin of vertebrates, and the hypothesis remains controversial.
- Amphibians can regulate the number of glomeruli taking part in filtration: in dehydration arginine vasotocin constricts the afferent arterioles of some glomeruli and stops their filtration ("glomerular intermittency"), while the GFR of a single glomerulus does not change much.
- The nephrons of reptiles have no loop of Henle and cannot produce hyperosmotic urine; the kidney of birds has both loopless "reptilian-type" nephrons (the majority, in the cortex) and "mammalian-type" nephrons with loops of Henle, and can produce urine at most about 2–2.5 times as concentrated as plasma (Chapter 24).
- All mammalian nephrons have a loop of Henle, and the kidney has a distinct cortex and medulla; the relative thickness of the medulla is positively correlated with the ability to concentrate urine (Chapter 24).

From this comparison we can see how the design of glomerular filtration has been conserved and modified: filtration makes excretion "clear everything by default", and the tubule then decides what to keep according to the needs of each group, whereas the loop of Henle appears only in birds and mammals that need to produce hyperosmotic urine.

## Questions

1. A person has plasma urea of $5\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$, urine urea of $300\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$, a urine flow of $1\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}}$ and a GFR of $120\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}}$. Find the clearance and fractional excretion of urea, and state whether urea is net reabsorbed or net secreted in the kidney.

   **Answer** $C_{\mathrm{urea}} = 300 \times 1/5 = 60\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}}$, fractional excretion $= 60/120 = 50\%$. The clearance is less than GFR, so half of the filtered urea is net reabsorbed (mainly passive reabsorption in the proximal tubule, and recycling in the inner medullary collecting duct, Chapters 23 and 24). When urine flow increases, urea reabsorption decreases and clearance rises with urine flow.
2. Why can the clearance of inulin represent GFR, while the clearance of glucose (about zero normally) represents nothing? Why is the clearance of PAH markedly below RPF when plasma PAH concentration is very high?

   **Answer** Inulin is freely filtered and neither reabsorbed nor secreted, so the amount excreted equals the amount filtered and $C = \mathrm{GFR}$. Glucose is completely reabsorbed, and a clearance of zero only shows that reabsorption is complete and does not correspond to any volume. The secretion of PAH depends on a limited number of transporters; at a very high plasma concentration secretion saturates (reaching its $T_{\mathrm{m}}$), the PAH flowing through the kidney is no longer cleared in one pass, the extraction ratio falls, and $C_{\mathrm{PAH}}$ is markedly below RPF; so when RPF is measured, plasma PAH must be kept low.
3. Calculate the net filtration pressure with the human estimates of Section 22.5.2. If a ureteral stone raises $P_{\mathrm{BS}}$ to $30\,\mathrm{mmHg}$, with $K_{\mathrm{f}}$ and the other pressures unchanged, about what does GFR become?

   **Answer** Normally $\bar P_{\mathrm{UF}} = 60 - 18 - 32 = 10\,\mathrm{mmHg}$ and $\mathrm{GFR} = 12.5 \times 10 = 125\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}}$. With $P_{\mathrm{BS}} = 30$, $\bar P_{\mathrm{UF}} \approx 60 - 30 - 32 < 0$: estimated from the mean values, filtration nearly stops. In reality, once filtration is reduced the protein is concentrated less, the mean of $\pi_{\mathrm{GC}}$ falls to near the afferent value of $28\,\mathrm{mmHg}$, the net filtration pressure is about $2\,\mathrm{mmHg}$, and GFR falls to about one fifth of normal. This shows that the net filtration pressure is a small difference of several large numbers, so a modest change in any one of them changes GFR greatly.
4. Why may a patient with bilateral renal artery stenosis who takes an angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor develop acute renal failure?

   **Answer** Renal artery stenosis lowers the perfusion pressure at the afferent end, and the kidney maintains $P_{\mathrm{GC}}$ and GFR by constricting the efferent arteriole with angiotensin II. After formation of angiotensin II is inhibited, the efferent arteriole dilates, $P_{\mathrm{GC}}$ falls, and since the net filtration pressure is small to begin with, GFR falls abruptly. With unilateral stenosis the other kidney can compensate, but with bilateral stenosis (or a solitary kidney) it cannot.
5. Tubuloglomerular feedback is negative feedback. What may happen if its gain is high and its delay long? How can this be tested experimentally?

   **Answer** Negative feedback with high gain and long delay produces oscillation ([7.9.16](/7.9.16)): the proximal tubule pressure, flow and afferent arteriolar diameter of a single nephron oscillate with a period of about 30–50 s, and micropuncture experiments in the rat do observe this oscillation, which becomes irregular in spontaneously hypertensive rats. Test: change the flow of tubular fluid to the macula densa by microperfusion and measure the change of pre-glomerular pressure or filtration rate in the same nephron, giving the gain and time constant of the feedback; blocking NKCC2 of the macula densa (for example with furosemide) or knocking out the $\mathrm{A}_1$ receptor abolishes both feedback and oscillation.
6. The Malpighian tubules of insects produce the primary urine by secretion rather than ultrafiltration. Compare the advantages and disadvantages of the two schemes in clearing foreign toxins.

   **Answer** Ultrafiltration lets all sufficiently small molecules enter the primary urine by default, and anything not reabsorbed is excreted, so compounds never encountered before can be cleared; the cost is that large amounts of useful substances must be reabsorbed, which costs much energy. In secretory urine formation only substances recognized by transporters enter the primary urine (water and small molecules also enter to some extent paracellularly with the $\ce{K+}$-driven fluid flow), and clearance of foreign substances depends on broad-specificity organic anion and cation transporters and ABC transporters; it needs no high-pressure circulation and suits insects with an open circulation, but is less able to clear toxins for which no corresponding transporter exists. In practice the epithelium of the Malpighian tubule is fairly permeable, and together with the high rate of fluid secretion this lets many small molecules enter the lumen fairly quickly, so the difference between the two schemes is not as absolute as in principle.
