The previous chapter described how nutrients are absorbed; after absorption they must be distributed throughout the body, stored, and mobilized as needed between meals. The chemical transformations of these substances (glycolysis, gluconeogenesis, oxidation and synthesis of fatty acids, breakdown of amino acids and formation of urea) have been covered in biochemistry ([7.1.18](/7.1.18)–[7.1.26](/7.1.26)), and the hormonal integration of fuel metabolism in [7.1.29](/7.1.29). This chapter takes the viewpoint of physiology and looks only at the flows of matter among organs and the feedback that governs them: between which organs glucose, amino acids, fatty acids and lipoproteins flow, how large the flows are, how large the pools are and how fast they turn over (Sections 29.1–29.5); then the liver, which sits at the center of this network: its structure, blood flow and metabolic zonation (Sections 29.6–29.8), and how hepatocytes take up, process and secrete substances, including the formation of bile and the **enterohepatic circulation** of bile acids (Section 29.9). Energy metabolism as a whole (metabolic rate, energy balance and food intake) is treated in Chapter 30, and the endocrinology of insulin and glucagon in Chapter 35.

## 29.1. Energy Coupling and Enzymes

### 29.1.1. ATP Couples Energy to Function

The chemical energy of food is released by oxidation, part of it is transferred to ATP, and ATP in turn drives the various physiological functions: muscle contraction, ion pumps, biosynthesis, secretion and neural activity. ATP is the universal intermediate that carries energy from "formation" to "use". The body contains only on the order of $100\,\mathrm{g}$ of ATP (estimates range from about $50\,\mathrm{g}$ to $250\,\mathrm{g}$), but the amount of ATP turned over each day is comparable to body mass (tens of kilograms), each ATP molecule being synthesized and hydrolyzed hundreds to thousands of times a day.

Textbooks often say that ATP contains two "high-energy phosphate bonds" and that "each mole of high-energy bond contains about 12000 cal". This statement needs two clarifications. First, the value and the unit: 12000 cal (that is, $12\,\mathrm{kcal}$, about $50\,\mathrm{kJ}$) is the magnitude of the **Gibbs free energy** change for hydrolysis of ATP under actual intracellular conditions, $\Delta G \approx -50$ to $-65\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$; in the biochemical standard state (each species at $1\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$, pH 7), $\Delta G'^\circ = -30.5\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$ ($-7.3\,\mathrm{kcal\,mol^{-1}}$). The value in cells is more negative because $[\mathrm{ATP}]/([\mathrm{ADP}][\mathrm{P_i}])$ is far above its equilibrium value (the **phosphorylation potential**, [7.1.16](/7.1.16#sec-16.3)). Second, the meaning: this energy is not "stored in a particular bond", since breaking any chemical bond requires energy. The free energy released on hydrolysis of ATP comes from the whole reaction system: greater resonance stabilization of the products, relief of charge repulsion, better solvation of the products, and the cell's maintenance of ATP at a concentration far from equilibrium. "High energy" refers to a high **phosphoryl group transfer potential**, not to a large bond energy.

In muscle, the molecule that couples energy to function is ATP (which directly drives the cross-bridges through the myosin ATPase cycle, [Chapter 9](/7.14.9)), backed up by **phosphocreatine**, which rephosphorylates ADP within seconds through creatine kinase. In the kidney, the coupler is the basolateral **sodium pump** (Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase): it converts the energy of ATP into the electrochemical gradient of $\ce{Na+}$, which in turn drives the reabsorption of glucose, amino acids and phosphate and the secretion of $\ce{H+}$ ([Chapter 23](/7.14.23)). The cell-biological conditions for such coupling are that the energy-transducing proteins be localized to the correct membrane domain (polarity), that the energy currency be kept at a concentration far from equilibrium, and that feedback with the products (ADP, AMP) as signals make ATP production rise and fall automatically with consumption. The last point is clearest in **glycolysis**: ATP inhibits and ADP and AMP activate phosphofructokinase, and citrate inhibits it, so that the rate of glycolysis matches the capacity of the citric acid cycle to use pyruvate and the cell's ATP consumption; when all ADP has been phosphorylated to ATP, oxidative phosphorylation slows automatically for lack of substrate (**respiratory control**, [7.1.23](/7.1.23)). Complete oxidation of one molecule of glucose yields about 30–32 ATP.

### 29.1.2. A Brief History of Enzymology

- In the 1780s, Lavoisier proposed that respiration is a slow combustion, for the first time connecting vital activity with chemical processes; with Laplace he used an ice calorimeter to show that the heat produced by a guinea pig and the amount of $\ce{CO2}$ it exhaled were comparable to those of combustion (1783).
- In 1833, Payen and Persoz isolated from malt a substance that converts starch to sugar (amylase, which they called diastase), the first enzyme to be isolated, although its chemical nature was unknown.
- In 1897, Buchner used a cell-free extract of yeast to accomplish the fermentation of sugar to ethanol, proving that enzymes can catalyze independently of living cells and ending the vitalist argument that fermentation requires living cells (Nobel Prize in Chemistry, 1907).
- In 1926, Sumner crystallized urease and showed that it is a protein, establishing for the first time the chemical nature of enzymes (Nobel Prize in Chemistry, 1946). It was later found that some RNAs also have catalytic ability (**ribozymes**).

## 29.2. Flow of Sugar Among Organs

### 29.2.1. Glucose Is the Center of Sugar Metabolism

Absorbed monosaccharides (glucose, fructose, galactose) first reach the liver through the portal vein. GLUT2 on the hepatocyte membrane is a high-capacity, low-affinity carrier for **facilitated diffusion** that rapidly equilibrates the glucose concentration inside hepatocytes with that of portal blood; fructose and galactose are phosphorylated in the liver by their own kinases, and most are converted to glucose (or glycogen, or lactate). Whatever sugar is eaten, therefore, what enters the **systemic circulation** is almost entirely glucose: glucose is the circulating form of carbohydrate in the blood.

Entry of glucose into most cells is facilitated diffusion down its concentration gradient; the carrier GLUT4 of skeletal muscle, cardiac muscle and adipose tissue is translocated from intracellular vesicles to the membrane under the action of insulin, so insulin increases glucose uptake in these tissues ("insulin helps glucose diffuse into cells"). Glucose entering a cell is immediately phosphorylated by hexokinase (in the liver, **glucokinase**) to glucose-6-phosphate, which cannot pass through GLUT and is "locked" inside the cell.

Only tissues that express glucose-6-phosphatase can hydrolyze glucose-6-phosphate to glucose and release it into the blood: mainly the liver, then the renal cortex (proximal tubule), and the small-intestinal epithelium (a little). Muscle lacks this enzyme, so muscle **glycogen** can be used only locally in muscle (after breakdown to lactate, the lactate can travel in the blood to the liver to be resynthesized into glucose, the **Cori cycle**). The distribution of this single enzyme determines the division of labor among organs: the liver and kidney are the "exporters" of glucose, and the other tissues are the "users".

### 29.2.2. Glycogen: Storage and Mobilization

Almost all cells can store some glycogen, but liver and muscle store the most:

| | Liver | Skeletal muscle |
|---|---|---|
| Content | About $100\,\mathrm{g}$ (5%–8% of liver wet weight) | About $400\,\mathrm{g}$ (1%–2% of muscle wet weight) |
| Use | Maintains blood glucose, supplies the whole body (especially the brain) | Supplies the muscle's own contraction |
| Signals for mobilization | Glucagon and epinephrine (cAMP), low blood glucose | Epinephrine (cAMP), $\ce{Ca^2+}$ and AMP during contraction |
| How long it lasts | About $12$–$24\,\mathrm{h}$ of fasting | About $1$–$2\,\mathrm{h}$ of vigorous exercise |

Glycogen is synthesized by the pathway of glycogenesis and mobilized by that of glycogenolysis; the main enzyme of mobilization is **glycogen phosphorylase**. **Epinephrine** (secreted by the adrenal medulla) and **glucagon** (secreted by pancreatic islet A cells) both activate it through the **G protein–coupled receptor**–cAMP pathway, and both exert their effects mainly in hepatocytes (in muscle only epinephrine acts; muscle cells have no glucagon receptor). After glycogen stores are full, surplus glucose is synthesized in the liver to fatty acids and triglycerides and carried as VLDL to adipose tissue for storage; adipocytes themselves can also synthesize a little fat from glucose.

**Example 29.2.1 (The Blood Glucose Pool and the Turnover of Glucose)** Plasma glucose is about $5\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$ ($0.9\,\mathrm{g\,L^{-1}}$), distributed in about $15\,\mathrm{L}$ of **extracellular fluid** (and partly intracellular), so the rapidly exchangeable glucose pool is only about $15\,\mathrm{g}$. After an overnight fast, the rate of glucose output by the liver (and kidney) is about $2\,\mathrm{mg\,kg^{-1}\,min^{-1}}$, about $8\,\mathrm{g\,h^{-1}}$ for a 70 kg person, that is about $200\,\mathrm{g}$ per day, equivalent to turning this pool over every 2 h. The brain consumes about $120\,\mathrm{g}$ of glucose per day and erythrocytes about $40\,\mathrm{g}$ (all by anaerobic glycolysis). If the liver suddenly stopped exporting glucose, blood glucose would therefore fall to a dangerous level within an hour or two; the hepatic glycogen reserve (about 100 g) lasts only about half a day. This is why **gluconeogenesis** must be started in prolonged fasting and the brain must switch to **ketone bodies** (Section 29.5.2).

## 29.3. Proteins and Amino Acids

### 29.3.1. The Amino Acid Pool and Plasma Proteins

The body has no special "warehouse" for amino acids: amino acids exist as protein in cells of all kinds (mainly in muscle), and in blood as free amino acids and as plasma proteins. The total concentration of free amino acids in plasma is about $0.35$–$0.65\,\mathrm{g\,L^{-1}}$ (about $2.5$–$4\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$), on average about $0.02\,\mathrm{g\,L^{-1}}$ for each; after a protein meal it rises only a few milligrams per deciliter, because the absorbed amino acids are taken up by the liver and other tissues within minutes to an hour. Amino acids enter cells by **active transport** and are almost completely reabsorbed in the renal proximal tubule. The turnover of amino acids among tissues is high, with grams of amino acids transported among tissues every hour.

Plasma proteins (albumin $40$–$50\,\mathrm{g\,L^{-1}}$, globulins, fibrinogen, [Chapter 11](/7.14.11)) are almost all synthesized by the liver (antibodies excepted); the liver synthesizes about $10$–$15\,\mathrm{g}$ of albumin per day, and the **half-life** of albumin is about 20 days. Plasma proteins are also a reserve of amino acids: tissue macrophages take up plasma proteins by **pinocytosis**, break them down to amino acids in **lysosomes**, and release the amino acids back into the blood to compensate when amino acid levels fall; conversely, when amino acids are plentiful, the liver synthesizes more plasma proteins. In starvation, plasma proteins fall in proportion to body protein, and in severe cases the **colloid osmotic pressure** falls and **edema** appears ([Chapter 15](/7.14.15)).

When cells have more amino acids than they need, the amino group is removed in the liver (the ammonia being used to synthesize urea, [7.1.22](/7.1.22)), and the remaining carbon skeleton (α-keto acid) is either oxidized in the **citric acid cycle** for energy, or converted to glucose by **gluconeogenesis**, or converted to fat and stored by fat synthesis. In fasting, muscle breaks down protein and sends nitrogen to the liver and kidney as alanine and glutamine (the **glucose–alanine cycle**), and the carbon skeletons become raw material for gluconeogenesis.

### 29.3.2. Hormonal Regulation of Protein Metabolism

| Hormone | Action |
|---|---|
| **Growth hormone** | Promotes protein synthesis (increases amino acid uptake and translation) and reduces breakdown |
| **Insulin** | Promotes amino acid entry into cells and protein synthesis, inhibits breakdown; without insulin protein synthesis almost ceases |
| Androgens | Increase the protein content of muscle (anabolic action) |
| Estrogens | Increase the protein content of tissues, to a lesser extent than androgens |
| **Glucocorticoids** | Promote protein breakdown in extrahepatic tissues (especially muscle; activating the **ubiquitin–proteasome system**) and inhibit their protein synthesis and amino acid uptake, raising plasma amino acids and hepatic protein (including plasma proteins) and supplying raw material for hepatic gluconeogenesis |
| **Thyroid hormone** | Raises the metabolic rate of cells throughout the body, with a biphasic action: accelerates protein breakdown for energy when sugar and fat are lacking; promotes protein synthesis when sugar and fat are plentiful and amino acids are in excess (essential during growth) |

## 29.4. Lipid Transport and Adipose Tissue

### 29.4.1. Chylomicrons and Lipoprotein Lipase

The lipids of food and of the body are of three main kinds: **triglycerides** are the main store and source of energy; phospholipids and **cholesterol** make up cell membranes, and cholesterol is also the precursor of **steroid hormones** and **bile acids**.

After a fatty meal, **chylomicrons** secreted by enterocytes (Chapter 28) enter the veins through intestinal lymph and the thoracic duct. The composition of chylomicrons is approximately phospholipid 9%, cholesterol 3%, **apolipoprotein** B 1%, and the rest (about 87%) triglyceride; the diameter is $80$–$600\,\mathrm{nm}$. Their fate in the blood is as follows:

1. Localization of the enzyme: **lipoprotein lipase** is synthesized by adipose tissue, skeletal muscle, cardiac muscle and mammary gland, transported to the luminal surface of the endothelium of neighboring capillaries, and anchored there (by the protein GPIHBP1);
2. Hydrolysis: as chylomicrons flow through the capillaries of these tissues, their triglycerides are hydrolyzed by lipoprotein lipase, releasing fatty acids and glycerol; the half-life of triglyceride in chylomicrons is only a few minutes;
3. Uptake: fatty acids enter adipocytes or muscle cells by diffusion (and via CD36 and others);
4. Intracellular fate: in muscle they are oxidized for energy, and in adipocytes they are resynthesized to triglyceride for storage (the glycerol-3-phosphate needed comes from glucose);
5. Clearance of the remnants: chylomicron remnants (rich in cholesterol) that have lost most of their triglyceride enter the hepatic sinusoids and are taken up by the liver through binding of apolipoprotein E to hepatocyte receptors (the LDL receptor and LRP1).

The activity of lipoprotein lipase varies with tissue and state: after a meal insulin increases lipoprotein lipase in adipose tissue and fat is sent to storage; in fasting and exercise muscle lipoprotein lipase increases and that of adipose tissue decreases, and fat is sent to oxidation; in lactation lipoprotein lipase of the mammary gland increases greatly and fat is sent into the milk. Differential regulation of the same enzyme in different tissues thus determines the "direction of flow" of fat.

### 29.4.2. Rapid Turnover of the Free Fatty Acid Pool

Triglyceride stored in adipocytes is hydrolyzed when needed by **hormone-sensitive lipase** (and adipose triglyceride lipase) to free fatty acids (FFA) and glycerol, which are released into the blood. FFA are transported in plasma bound to albumin: one albumin molecule usually binds about 3 fatty acids and can bind up to 30 when needed.

At rest the plasma FFA concentration is only about $0.15\,\mathrm{g\,L^{-1}}$ (about $0.5\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$), and the whole plasma contains only about $0.45\,\mathrm{g}$; but turnover is extremely rapid, half being replaced every $2$–$3\,\mathrm{min}$.

**Example 29.4.1 (How a Very Small Pool Supplies a Very Large Flux)** Treat plasma FFA as a single compartment: $\mathrm{d}F/\mathrm{d}t = R_{\mathrm{a}} - kF$, $k = \ln 2/t_{1/2}$. With $t_{1/2} = 2.5\,\mathrm{min}$, $k = 0.28\,\mathrm{min^{-1}}$, and the steady-state flux is $R_{\mathrm{a}} = kF = 0.28 \times 0.45 \approx 0.125\,\mathrm{g\,min^{-1}}$, about $180\,\mathrm{g}$ per day. If all these fatty acids were oxidized, they would supply about $180 \times 37\,\mathrm{kJ} \approx 6.7\,\mathrm{MJ}$, comparable to a person's resting energy expenditure for a day (in reality a considerable fraction is re-esterified in the tissues, forming a "triglyceride–fatty acid cycle"). So although the FFA concentration is low, it is enough to meet the basal energy needs of the body. Rapid turnover also means rapid regulation: the FFA concentration reaches a new steady state within $5$ half-lives (about $10$–$15\,\mathrm{min}$). In starvation, diabetes and vigorous exercise, fat mobilization is enhanced and plasma FFA can rise 5–8-fold.

### 29.4.3. Lipoproteins

Plasma lipids are transported mainly in the form of **lipoproteins**. Apart from chylomicrons synthesized by the intestine, the great majority of lipoproteins and their lipids are synthesized by the liver, and the intestinal epithelium synthesizes a little HDL.

| Type | Origin and features | Main lipids | Function |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Very-low-density lipoprotein** (VLDL) | Secreted by the liver, high triglyceride content | Mainly triglyceride, moderate cholesterol and phospholipid | Carries triglyceride synthesized by the liver to adipose tissue and muscle |
| Intermediate-density lipoprotein (IDL) | VLDL after part of its triglyceride is removed by lipoprotein lipase | Higher proportion of cholesterol and phospholipid | Partly taken up by the liver, the rest converted to LDL |
| **Low-density lipoprotein** (LDL) | IDL after almost all triglyceride is removed | Very high cholesterol, moderate phospholipid | Carries cholesterol to peripheral tissues (by endocytosis via the **LDL receptor**) |
| **High-density lipoprotein** (HDL) | Synthesized by liver and intestine; protein about 50% | Low cholesterol and phospholipid | Collects cholesterol from peripheral tissues and returns it to the liver (**reverse cholesterol transport**) |

The conversion VLDL → IDL → LDL is a process in which the proportion of triglyceride falls and that of cholesterol rises in succession. The molecular structure and metabolism of lipoproteins and atherosclerosis are covered in [7.1.26](/7.1.26).

### 29.4.4. Adipose Tissue

The function of adipose tissue is to store triglyceride for other tissues to use when needed; it also insulates, cushions against mechanical shock, and secretes hormones (**leptin**, **adiponectin** and others, which regulate appetite and energy expenditure, Chapter 30). White adipocytes differentiate from mesenchymal precursor cells, and triglyceride makes up 80%–95% of the cell volume. Stored triglyceride is liquid at body temperature, a necessary condition for its hydrolysis and transport; under prolonged cold, the fatty acid chains of subcutaneous fat become shorter or more unsaturated, lowering the melting point so that the fat remains liquid at lower tissue temperatures (the same gradient exists in the legs of reindeer and the blubber of whales).

Stored fat is in dynamic equilibrium, but the rate of turnover differs among species. From isotope experiments in rodents, classical textbooks state that "the triglyceride in adipocytes is completely renewed about every 2–3 weeks"; in humans, measurements using the change in atmospheric $^{14}\mathrm{C}$ caused by nuclear testing give a mean "age" of lipid in adipocytes of about 1.6 years (Arner et al., 2011), that is, the storage turnover of human fat is much slower. This does not contradict the rapid FFA turnover of Section 29.4.2: the flux of fatty acids entering and leaving adipose tissue each day is large, but most of it is a "re-esterification" flux cycling within and outside the cells, and the time to replace the whole store in net terms is measured in years.

### 29.4.5. The Fat-Sparing Action of Sugar and Hormonal Regulation of Fat Mobilization

When sugar is plentiful, the body preferentially uses sugar for energy rather than fat. This "fat-sparing effect" is achieved mainly by two mechanisms: first, glycerol-3-phosphate (α-glycerophosphate) produced by glucose metabolism increases, combines with FFA in adipocytes, and re-esterifies them to triglyceride, reducing the FFA available for release; second, when sugar is in excess, acetyl-CoA is produced in large amounts and the FFA concentration falls, citrate activates acetyl-CoA carboxylase, the rate-limiting enzyme of fatty acid synthesis, and the excess sugar is converted to fat for storage. Insulin at the same time inhibits hormone-sensitive lipase. When sugar is lacking, these effects are completely reversed: insulin secretion falls markedly, adipocytes release large amounts of FFA, and fat replaces sugar as the main fuel.

Hormones that mobilize fat:

- Catecholamines: in vigorous exercise and stress, norepinephrine and epinephrine released by the sympathetic nerves and adrenal medulla directly activate the hormone-sensitive lipase of adipocytes through $\beta$ receptors and cAMP; plasma FFA can rise about 8-fold, and muscle use of fatty acids increases correspondingly;
- Glucocorticoids and ACTH: both can activate fat mobilization; prolonged excess (as in Cushing syndrome) can lead to massive fat mobilization and even **ketosis**, hence the "ketogenic action" (although fat is at the same time redistributed to the trunk);
- Growth hormone: similar but weaker action, also with a mild ketogenic effect;
- Thyroid hormone: promotes fat mobilization indirectly by raising the metabolic rate of cells throughout the body.

## 29.5. The Absorptive and Postabsorptive States

### 29.5.1. Two States

**Definition 29.5.1 (Absorptive and Postabsorptive States)** The period after a meal during which nutrients are being absorbed from the digestive tract into the blood (about $3$–$4\,\mathrm{h}$ after a mixed meal) is called the **absorptive state**. The absorbed nutrients then meet the body's current energy needs, and the surplus is stored. The period after absorption of nutrients from the digestive tract is complete, when the body relies on stored fuel to maintain blood glucose and energy supply, is called the **postabsorptive state**.

A person eating three meals a day spends about half of the day in the postabsorptive state. The transition between the two states is controlled mainly by the ratio of plasma insulin to glucagon ([7.1.29](/7.1.29#sec-29.3)); only the flows of matter among organs are listed here:

| Organ | Absorptive state | Postabsorptive state |
|---|---|---|
| Liver | Takes up glucose from the portal vein to synthesize glycogen; synthesizes fat from surplus glucose and amino acids and exports it as VLDL; synthesizes plasma proteins; clears chylomicron remnants | Glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis (raw materials: lactate and alanine from muscle, glycerol from fat) export glucose; oxidizes FFA, and after 1–2 days of fasting produces large amounts of **ketone bodies** |
| Skeletal muscle | Takes up glucose under the action of insulin (the main fate of postprandial glucose) to synthesize glycogen; takes up amino acids to synthesize protein | Mainly oxidizes FFA and ketone bodies, sparing glucose; breaks down protein and exports alanine and glutamine; glycogen is for its own use only |
| Adipose tissue | Lipoprotein lipase hydrolyzes chylomicrons and VLDL, triglyceride is stored; takes up glucose to provide glycerol-3-phosphate | Lipolysis, exports FFA and glycerol |
| Brain | Uses only glucose (about 120 g/d) | Still uses glucose; after several days of fasting about 2/3 of its energy comes from ketone bodies |
| Erythrocytes, renal medulla | Use only glucose (anaerobic glycolysis), export lactate | Same; lactate returns to the liver (Cori cycle) |
| Kidney | — | In prolonged fasting gluconeogenesis (from glutamine) can account for 40% of the whole body's; excretes $\ce{NH4+}$ |

This table can be summarized in three rules: glucose flows only to tissues that must use it; tissues that can use fat all switch to fat in the postabsorptive state; and protein is the last fuel to be drawn on, flowing between muscle and liver as amino acids.

### 29.5.2. From the Postabsorptive State to Starvation

As the postabsorptive state is prolonged into starvation, the fuel supply switches in sequence: for about the first day, hepatic glycogenolysis supplies most of the glucose; after that, **gluconeogenesis** becomes the only source of glucose, the raw material coming first from the breakdown of muscle protein, about $75\,\mathrm{g}$ of protein per day; after a few days the liver uses large amounts of FFA to produce **ketone bodies**, plasma ketone bodies rise from less than $0.1$ to $5$–$7\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$, and the brain switches to ketone bodies as its main fuel, its glucose requirement falling from about $120\,\mathrm{g\,d^{-1}}$ to about $40\,\mathrm{g\,d^{-1}}$ and protein breakdown falling accordingly to about $20\,\mathrm{g}$ per day, which protects muscle (including respiratory muscle and the heart). For a person weighing 70 kg with 15 kg of fat, at an expenditure of about $8\,\mathrm{MJ}$ per day, the fat lasts about two months; finally, when fat is exhausted and protein breakdown accelerates again, death follows. The hormonal and molecular mechanisms of this process are described in [7.1.29](/7.1.29#sec-29.3.4).

## 29.6. The Liver: Function and Structure

### 29.6.1. Functions of the Liver

The adult liver weighs about $1.5\,\mathrm{kg}$, about 2% of body weight, and is the largest gland in the body. It is at the same time:

1. A chemical factory: metabolism and interconversion of sugars, lipids and amino acids (earlier sections of this chapter);
2. An excretory organ: converts ammonia to urea ([7.1.22](/7.1.22)) and excretes bilirubin, cholesterol, and metabolites of drugs and toxins into bile;
3. An **exocrine gland**: secretes bile (bile salts, Chapter 27);
4. An **endocrine gland**: secretes **insulin-like growth factor 1** (IGF-1, Chapter 34), angiotensinogen (Chapter 24), hepcidin (Chapter 28) and thrombopoietin;
5. A logistics center: exports glucose between meals and, after a meal, converts surplus sugar to triglyceride for export;
6. A manufacturing plant: synthesizes albumin, clotting factors (fibrinogen, prothrombin and factors V, VII, IX, X and others, [Chapter 11](/7.14.11)), complement, apolipoproteins, transferrin and cholesterol;
7. A storehouse: stores glycogen, vitamins A, D and $\mathrm{B_{12}}$, and iron (ferritin);
8. A defense: clears bacteria and endotoxin that come from the gut.

Because the liver performs multiple functions, liver disease produces symptoms in many respects at once: jaundice (impaired bilirubin excretion), edema and ascites (decreased albumin and portal hypertension), bleeding tendency (decreased clotting factors), hypoglycemia, hyperammonemia and hepatic encephalopathy (impaired urea synthesis), prolonged drug action, and gynecomastia in men (reduced inactivation of estrogens).

### 29.6.2. The Hepatic Lobule and Acinus

The basic structural unit of the liver is the hepatic lobule: a hexagonal prism about $1$–$2\,\mathrm{mm}$ in diameter, of which the human liver has some 50,000 to 100,000. The axis of the lobule is a central vein; hepatocytes are arranged in plates one cell thick that radiate from the central vein to the periphery; between adjacent hepatocyte plates are the sinusoids, the capillaries of the liver. In the connective tissue between lobules (the portal tract) three kinds of vessels run in parallel: the interlobular artery (a branch of the hepatic artery), the interlobular vein (a branch of the portal vein) and the interlobular bile duct, together with lymphatics and nerves. Blood flows from the arterial and portal branches of the portal tract into the sinusoids, flows centripetally past the hepatocyte plates, and empties into the central vein, then through the sublobular veins and hepatic veins into the inferior vena cava. Bile runs the opposite way: bile secreted by hepatocytes enters the bile canaliculi between adjacent hepatocytes, which are sealed by **tight junctions**, and flows centrifugally to the bile ducts of the portal tract. The countercurrent flow of blood and bile keeps the two from mixing in the lobule.

A more useful unit from the functional viewpoint is the hepatic acinus proposed by Rappaport: a mass of tissue with a terminal vessel of the portal tract as its axis, extending on both sides toward the central veins. The hepatocytes nearest the terminal vessel (zone 1, the periportal zone) are the first to receive blood rich in oxygen and nutrients, those farthest away (zone 3, the pericentral zone) receive it last, and zone 2 lies in between. This structure is the basis of the metabolic zonation of Section 29.8.

### 29.6.3. Sinusoids, the Space of Disse and Kupffer Cells

**Definition 29.6.1 (Space of Disse)** The extracellular space between the sinusoidal endothelial cells and the basolateral (sinusoidal) membrane of hepatocytes is called the **space of Disse** (perisinusoidal space).

The endothelium of the hepatic sinusoid is a discontinuous, fenestrated endothelium: the fenestrae are about $0.1$–$0.2\,\mathrm{\mu m}$ in diameter, arranged in groups as sieve plates, and there is no **basement membrane** ([Chapter 15](/7.14.15)). Plasma and its proteins and smaller lipoproteins (including chylomicron remnants, but not intact large chylomicrons) can enter the space of Disse freely and come into direct contact with the numerous microvilli on the sinusoidal face of the hepatocytes. This allows hepatocytes to take up protein-bound substances from plasma efficiently and to secrete the plasma proteins they synthesize directly into the blood. (Some textbooks write that "the gaps between cells are as large as $1\,\mathrm{\mu m}$"; the measured fenestrae are an order of magnitude smaller, but still much larger than the pores of other capillaries.)

Fluid and protein in the space of Disse flow into the lymphatics of the portal tract. Because the sinusoid is almost no barrier to protein, the protein concentration of hepatic **lymph** is about $60\,\mathrm{g\,L^{-1}}$, close to that of plasma; at rest the liver produces about 1/3–1/2 of all body lymph. When hepatic venous pressure rises (right heart failure, hepatic vein obstruction), fluid formation in the space of Disse increases beyond the drainage capacity of the lymphatics, and fluid leaks from the surface of the liver into the peritoneal cavity, forming ascites.

There are two kinds of nonparenchymal cells in the sinusoid: Kupffer cells are macrophages resident in the sinusoid, making up about 80% of all tissue macrophages in the body, which phagocytose and clear bacteria, **endotoxin**, particles and aged erythrocytes entering from the portal vein; fewer than 1% of the bacteria that enter portal blood from the gut pass through the liver into the systemic circulation. Hepatic stellate cells (Ito cells) lie in the space of Disse and normally store vitamin A (about 80% of the body's vitamin A is here); in chronic liver injury they are activated into fibroblast-like cells that secrete **collagen**, causing hepatic fibrosis and cirrhosis.

## 29.7. Hepatic Blood Flow

The liver has a dual blood supply: the portal vein accounts for about 75% of hepatic blood flow (about $1050\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}}$) and brings nutrients absorbed from the digestive tract (and pancreatic hormones such as insulin and glucagon); the hepatic artery accounts for about 25% (about $300\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}}$) and brings oxygen-rich blood that supports the aerobic metabolism of hepatocytes, nourishes the bile ducts and regulates the composition of biliary secretions. Portal blood has already flowed through the capillaries of the digestive tract and has an oxygen saturation of only about 70%–85%, so although the hepatic artery supplies only 1/4 of the flow, it supplies about half of the oxygen. Total hepatic blood flow is about $1350\,\mathrm{mL\,min^{-1}}$, about 27% of resting **cardiac output**.

The **vascular resistance** of the liver is very low: portal venous pressure is about $9\,\mathrm{mmHg}$ ($1.2\,\mathrm{kPa}$), and hepatic venous and inferior vena caval pressure about $0\,\mathrm{mmHg}$; this $9\,\mathrm{mmHg}$ pressure difference drives a portal flow of about $1\,\mathrm{L\,min^{-1}}$. With the "**Ohm's law**" of blood flow ([Chapter 14](/7.14.14)): the portal–sinusoidal resistance is $R = \Delta p/Q \approx 9/1.05 \approx 8.6\,\mathrm{mmHg\,min\,L^{-1}}$, only half of the resistance of the entire systemic circulation (about $90/5 = 18\,\mathrm{mmHg\,min\,L^{-1}}$), and the sinusoid is a second capillary bed in series with the systemic circulation after the digestive tract. Low resistance is essential for the portal system: the driving pressure of the portal vein is only a few millimeters of mercury, and a slight rise in resistance greatly reduces flow and raises pressure. In cirrhosis, fibrous tissue compresses the sinusoids, resistance rises several-fold, and the portal pressure rises above $20\,\mathrm{mmHg}$ (portal hypertension); blood bypasses the liver through collateral circulation (esophageal and gastric varices, which bleed massively when they rupture), and splenomegaly and ascites result.

There is a complementary regulation between the flows of the hepatic artery and the portal vein, called the hepatic arterial buffer response: when portal blood flow falls, the hepatic artery dilates within seconds to minutes and partly compensates for the reduction in total flow. The mechanism is adenosine washout: tissue in the portal tract continuously produces adenosine, which is normally washed away by portal blood flow; when portal flow falls, adenosine accumulates and dilates the arterioles of the hepatic artery. This is a **negative feedback** that maintains total hepatic blood flow (rather than oxygen supply).

The liver is also a blood reservoir. The liver ordinarily holds about $450\,\mathrm{mL}$ of blood, about 10% of the blood volume of the body; its vessels have a high **compliance** and can hold a further $0.5$–$1\,\mathrm{L}$ of blood when hepatic venous pressure rises (right heart failure, excessive infusion); in blood loss, the sympathetic nerves constrict the hepatic vessels and transfer a considerable part of the blood to the systemic circulation (a blood storage organ in [Chapter 14](/7.14.14)).

## 29.8. Metabolic Zonation

From zone 1 to zone 3 along the hepatic acinus, the oxygen partial pressure of the blood falls from about $60$–$65\,\mathrm{mmHg}$ to about $30$–$35\,\mathrm{mmHg}$, and the concentrations of hormones and nutrients change in turn. The gene expression of hepatocytes differs with position, forming metabolic zonation:

| Periportal (zone 1): oxygen-rich | Pericentral (zone 3): low oxygen |
|---|---|
| Gluconeogenesis, glycogen breakdown (export of glucose to blood) | Glycolysis, glycogen synthesis (from glucose) |
| β-oxidation of fatty acids | Synthesis of fatty acids and triglycerides |
| **Urea cycle** (bulk clearance of ammonia) | **Glutamine synthetase** (high-affinity clearance of ammonia) |
| Bile acid–dependent bile secretion, cholesterol synthesis | Bile acid synthesis (CYP7A1), drug-metabolizing CYPs (such as CYP2E1) |
| Oxidative metabolism, protein synthesis and secretion | Conjugation reactions (glucuronidation) |

Metabolic zonation separates opposing processes (gluconeogenesis and glycolysis, β-oxidation and fat synthesis) in space, avoiding **futile cycles**, and places oxygen-demanding processes where oxygen is most plentiful. Wnt signals secreted by the endothelium of the central vein activate **β-catenin** in surrounding hepatocytes and maintain the zone 3 gene expression program; this is an example of **positional information** provided by blood vessels.

Clearance of ammonia is an elegant example of zonal design (Häussinger's "two-stage" model). The ammonia concentration of portal blood can reach $0.3$–$0.5\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$ (from the gut microbiota and from glutamine metabolism in the intestinal epithelium). The urea cycle in zone 1 has a large capacity but a low affinity for ammonia, and clears most of the ammonia but leaves a residue; a few (one or two layers of) hepatocytes in zone 3 express glutamine synthetase, which has a high affinity for ammonia and a small capacity, and "sweeps up" the residual ammonia to synthesize glutamine. The two stages in series reduce the ammonia concentration of blood leaving the liver to below about $0.03\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$. This design also explains pathology: zone 3 is most sensitive to hypoxia, and in shock zone 3 is the first to become necrotic; excess acetaminophen is converted by CYP2E1 in zone 3 to a toxic intermediate, also causing zone 3 necrosis, and then even though the urea cycle is intact, the residual ammonia escapes clearance and blood ammonia rises.

## 29.9. Transport Functions of the Hepatocyte

### 29.9.1. The Four Steps of Uptake, Processing and Secretion

The handling of substances by the hepatocyte follows a general four-step pattern:

1. Uptake: transporters in the basolateral (sinusoidal) membrane carry the substance from the space of Disse into the cell;
2. Intracellular transport: diffusion, or binding to intracellular binding proteins or transport in vesicles;
3. Modification or degradation: by various modifying enzymes (Section 29.9.4);
4. Secretion: transporters in the apical (canalicular) membrane secrete the substance into bile, or it returns to the blood through the basolateral membrane.

The basic ion homeostasis of hepatocytes is the same as that of other epithelial cells: the basolateral **sodium pump** maintains a low intracellular $[\ce{Na+}]$ and ion gradients, $\ce{K+}$ channels and $\ce{Cl-}$ channels together maintain the transmembrane potential (about $-30$ to $-40\,\mathrm{mV}$ at the sinusoidal face), and $\ce{Ca^2+}$ pumps maintain a low intracellular $[\ce{Ca^2+}]$. The $\ce{Na+}$ gradient drives the entry of many substances from the blood into the hepatocyte (symport of bile acids, amino acids and others); other substances enter by facilitated diffusion. Under stimulation by secretin, the bile duct epithelium also secretes an alkaline fluid into the bile ducts through CFTR and the $\ce{Cl-}$/$\ce{HCO3-}$ exchanger (Chapter 27).

### 29.9.2. Uptake and Secretion of Bile Acids

Bile acids are synthesized by hepatocytes from cholesterol (the rate-limiting enzyme is CYP7A1 in zone 3), are conjugated with glycine or taurine, and are secreted as bile salts; a small amount is modified by bacteria in the gut and absorbed. The handling of bile acids by hepatocytes is as follows:

- Basolateral membrane: bile salts returning to the liver are taken up mainly through the $\ce{Na+}$–taurocholate cotransporting polypeptide (NTCP, 2 $\ce{Na+}$ with 1 bile salt), and unconjugated bile acids are taken up together with other organic anions through organic anion transporting polypeptides (OATP). The liver extracts 80%–90% of the bile salts in portal blood on a single pass, so the concentration of bile acids in the systemic circulation is very low (a few $\mathrm{\mu mol\,L^{-1}}$). NTCP is also the receptor by which hepatitis B virus enters hepatocytes.
- Intracellular: bile acids are transported bound to binding proteins or in vesicles;
- Apical membrane: the bile salt export pump (BSEP, ABCB11) pumps bile salts into the bile canaliculus against their concentration gradient using ATP, and the bile salt concentration in the canaliculus can reach about 1000 times that in the cell. In the same membrane, MDR3 (ABCB4) flips phosphatidylcholine to the outer leaflet for secretion and ABCG5/G8 secrete cholesterol; the three assemble into mixed **micelles** in the canaliculus. Genetic defects of BSEP or MDR3 cause progressive familial intrahepatic cholestasis.

The formation of bile is not a filtration process like that of the glomerulus but a secretory process: BSEP and other pumps actively secrete osmotically active substances (bile salts, glutathione, $\ce{HCO3-}$) into the canaliculus, and water and electrolytes follow osmotically through the tight junctions and water channels. The more bile salt is secreted, the greater the bile flow (bile salt–dependent bile flow); secretion of glutathione and $\ce{HCO3-}$ produces bile salt–independent bile flow; the bile duct epithelium, under the action of secretin, adds a $\ce{HCO3-}$-rich fluid that accounts for about 1/3 of the total volume of bile. Hepatic bile is isotonic with plasma.

### 29.9.3. Quantitative Aspects of the Enterohepatic Circulation

The enterohepatic circulation of bile salts (Definition 27.5.1) is a loop of three parts: secretion by the liver, storage and release by the gallbladder, and reabsorption in the terminal ileum (the apical $\ce{Na+}$-dependent bile acid transporter ASBT and the basolateral OSTα/β), returning to the liver through the portal vein. A small amount of bile salt is deconjugated and 7α-dehydroxylated by bacteria in the terminal ileum and colon to form secondary bile acids (deoxycholic acid, lithocholic acid), part of which are absorbed passively and the rest excreted in the feces.

**Example 29.9.1 (The Bile Acid Pool Accounts)** The human bile acid pool is about $3\,\mathrm{g}$, and about $12$–$36\,\mathrm{g}$ of bile acids are secreted into bile each day (take $24\,\mathrm{g}$), so the pool circulates about 8 times a day (2–3 times per meal). About $0.5$–$0.6\,\mathrm{g}$ are lost in the feces each day, which at steady state is exactly equal to hepatic synthesis. The fraction recovered on each pass through the ileum is therefore

$$
f = 1 - \frac{0.6\,\mathrm{g\,d^{-1}}}{24\,\mathrm{g\,d^{-1}}} \approx 0.975.
$$

A bile acid molecule on average circulates $1/(1-f) \approx 40$ times before it is lost and stays in the body about $40/8 = 5$ days. If the terminal ileum is resected, $f$ falls below 0.5, and even if hepatic synthesis rises to its maximum ($4$–$6\,\mathrm{g}$ per day, about 5–8 times normal) it is far from enough to compensate the secretory requirement of tens of grams per day; the bile acid pool shrinks and steatorrhea results (Chapter 28).

:::

The synthesis of bile acids is regulated by negative feedback from the enterohepatic circulation: bile acids returning to the liver activate the **nuclear receptor** FXR in hepatocytes, which induces the repressor protein SHP and inhibits transcription of CYP7A1; when the ileum absorbs bile acids, FXR in the enterocytes induces secretion of fibroblast growth factor 19 (FGF19), which travels through the portal vein to the liver and likewise inhibits CYP7A1. This is a **negative feedback** in which the returning flux of bile acids is the regulated variable: when return is reduced (ileal resection, oral bile acid sequestrants such as cholestyramine), synthesis increases. Because synthesizing bile acids consumes cholesterol, bile acid sequestrants are used as cholesterol-lowering drugs: the liver consumes more cholesterol to replace bile acids, increases expression of the **LDL receptor**, and plasma LDL falls.

### 29.9.4. Organic Anions, Cations and Biotransformation

Besides bile acids, hepatocytes take up and clear many other organic substances from the blood: endogenous bilirubin, steroid hormones and thyroid hormones, and exogenous drugs and toxins.

- Uptake: organic anions are taken up mainly through OATP of the basolateral membrane (OATP1B1, OATP1B3, exchanging with intracellular anions such as glutathione or $\ce{HCO3-}$; some literature writes exchange with $\ce{Cl-}$); organic cations enter the cell through OCT1 (and OCT2 in the kidney) by membrane potential–driven facilitated diffusion. **Statins** enter the liver through OATP1B1, and polymorphisms of its gene influence the muscle toxicity of statins.
- Modification (biotransformation): lipid-soluble substances are difficult to excrete in bile or urine (they would be reabsorbed), and the liver raises their water solubility by chemical modification. Phase I reactions are mainly catalyzed by **cytochrome P-450** (CYP) monooxygenases, which introduce or expose polar groups such as hydroxyl on the molecule (humans have 57 CYP genes, of which CYP3A4 takes part in the metabolism of about half of clinical drugs); phase II reactions conjugate the polar group with glucuronic acid (UGT), sulfate (SULT) or glutathione (GST), giving products that are negatively charged and highly water-soluble. Biotransformation does not always detoxify: some substances become more active or toxic intermediates after phase I reactions (acetaminophen, benzo[a]pyrene), and some prodrugs need hepatic conversion to become active (codeine is converted to morphine by CYP2D6).
- Secretion: conjugated organic anions are secreted into bile through apical MRP2 (ABCC2) and positively charged drugs through MDR1 (P-glycoprotein); some also return to the blood through basolateral MRP3 and are excreted by the kidney.

Orally administered drugs reach the liver first through the portal vein, and a considerable fraction is taken up and metabolized by the liver before entering the systemic circulation (the **first-pass effect**, Chapter 33), so oral doses often need to be much larger than intravenous doses; liver disease and competition between drugs (for example two drugs both metabolized by CYP3A4) can markedly change drug blood concentrations.

### 29.9.5. Bilirubin

About $250$–$350\,\mathrm{mg}$ of **bilirubin** are produced each day, about 80% from the **heme** of hemoglobin in aged erythrocytes ([Chapter 11](/7.14.11)) and the rest from myoglobin, cytochromes and ineffective erythropoiesis. The handling of bilirubin is a typical example of the four steps of Section 29.9.4:

1. Unconjugated bilirubin is insoluble in water and is carried in plasma tightly bound to albumin (and therefore cannot be filtered by the glomerulus);
2. In the space of Disse it dissociates from albumin, enters the hepatocyte through OATP, and binds an intracellular binding protein (glutathione S-transferase);
3. In the **endoplasmic reticulum** it is conjugated with 1–2 glucuronic acid residues by UGT1A1, becoming water-soluble conjugated bilirubin;
4. It is secreted into bile through MRP2. In the gut, bacteria deconjugate conjugated bilirubin and reduce it to urobilinogen; most is excreted in the feces and oxidized to stercobilin (the color of feces); about 10%–20% of urobilinogen is reabsorbed, most of it is excreted into bile again by the liver (enterohepatic circulation), and a small amount is excreted by the kidney and oxidized to urobilin (the yellow of urine).

When plasma bilirubin exceeds about $34\,\mathrm{\mu mol\,L^{-1}}$ ($2\,\mathrm{mg\,dL^{-1}}$), the sclerae and skin turn yellow, called jaundice. From the four steps above the cause of jaundice can be inferred: with overproduction (hemolysis) or a defect of uptake or conjugation (immaturity of UGT1A1 in the newborn, Gilbert syndrome, Crigler–Najjar syndrome), unconjugated bilirubin is elevated and there is no bilirubin in the urine; with hepatocyte injury or biliary obstruction, conjugated bilirubin refluxes into the blood through MRP3, the elevated fraction is mainly conjugated bilirubin, which can be excreted in the urine (dark urine); with complete obstruction bilirubin cannot enter the gut, the feces are clay-colored, and urobilinogen disappears. In the newborn, unconjugated bilirubin can cross the **blood–brain barrier** and, if too high, damages the **basal ganglia** (kernicterus); irradiating the skin with blue light photoisomerizes bilirubin into water-soluble isomers that are excreted in bile and urine, and is the main treatment of neonatal jaundice.

## 29.10. Evolution and Comparison

### 29.10.1. Fat as Fuel: Migrating Birds and Fish

The energy stored per unit mass of fat (about $39\,\mathrm{kJ\,g^{-1}}$, and stored without water) is about 8 times that of glycogen (about $17\,\mathrm{kJ\,g^{-1}}$, but each gram of glycogen binds about 3 g of water, so the actual value is only $4$–$5\,\mathrm{kJ}$ per gram wet weight). For an animal that must carry its fuel on long journeys, fat is therefore the main fuel. Before migration, birds eat heavily for several weeks (hyperphagia), and fat can make up 30%–50% of body mass; the bar-tailed godwit flies nonstop from Alaska across the Pacific to New Zealand, about $11000\,\mathrm{km}$ in 8–11 days, with fat making up more than half of its body mass at takeoff, and it lets the digestive tract and liver atrophy before departure to reduce its weight ([Chapter 28](/7.14.28)). Unlike mammals, birds carry out fat synthesis mainly in the liver (de novo synthesis in adipose tissue is very low), and the triglyceride synthesized in the liver is carried to adipose tissue as VLDL; the flight muscles oxidize mainly fatty acids from the blood, and the high flux required is supported by a particularly high plasma FFA concentration, abundant fatty acid transport proteins in the muscle cell membrane and cytosolic fatty acid binding proteins. The muscles of mammals rely mainly on sugar during high-intensity exercise, whereas migrating birds can oxidize fat continuously at an intensity close to their maximal aerobic capacity, which is considered a special adaptation of avian muscle metabolism.

Fish likewise use lipid and protein as their main fuels and utilize sugar poorly (many carnivorous fish show prolonged hyperglycemia resembling diabetes when fed high-sugar diets). Migrating salmon do not eat during the weeks of their upstream journey and rely on fat and protein stored in muscle and viscera to complete a swim of thousands of kilometers and to breed.

### 29.10.2. Metabolic Switching in Fasting and Hibernation

Some animals fast for long periods at regular points of their life history: male emperor penguins fast for about 4 months during incubation, northern elephant seal pups fast for 2–3 months after weaning, and bears in **hibernation** neither eat nor drink for months. The fasting of these animals can be divided into three phases: in the first (short) phase glycogen is used and protein breakdown is relatively high; in the second (long) phase fat is the main fuel, protein breakdown falls very low and ketone bodies rise, which resembles prolonged starvation in humans (Section 29.5.2); when fat falls to a threshold, the third phase begins, protein breakdown accelerates again, and at the same time the animal's behavior changes: the emperor penguin abandons its egg and leaves to forage. This switch shows that the body monitors the remaining fat reserve and uses it as a signal for changing behavior and metabolism (leptin and others may take part, Chapter 30).

Hibernating bears hardly urinate for 5–7 months yet do not develop uremia and lose almost no muscle: the urea they produce is hydrolyzed to ammonia by the gut microbiota, and the ammonia returns to the liver and is used to synthesize amino acids and protein (urea–nitrogen recycling), the metabolism of the liver turning from urea synthesis to nitrogen reuse. Small hibernating mammals (ground squirrels) oxidize almost only fat during hibernation, with a **respiratory quotient** close to 0.7, and hepatic gluconeogenesis from glycerol maintains blood glucose at a low level.

### 29.10.3. The Shark Liver: An Organ of Buoyancy

Teleost fishes regulate buoyancy with a swim bladder; cartilaginous fishes (sharks and rays) have no swim bladder and obtain buoyancy from an enormous, oil-rich liver. The liver of a deep-sea shark can make up 20%–25% of body mass, of which 70%–80% is oil, mainly squalene (an unsaturated hydrocarbon that is not metabolized) with a density of about $0.86\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$ and alkyldiacylglycerols; their density is lower than that of triglyceride (about $0.93\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$), so they provide more lift (the density of seawater is about $1.026\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$). Using squalene, which is not metabolized, rather than triglyceride means that buoyancy is not "eaten up" during starvation. Compared with a gas-filled swim bladder, oil is hardly compressible and buoyancy hardly varies with depth, which suits deep-sea sharks that swim up and down over a large depth range; the price is large mass and slow adjustment. The liver here is "borrowed" for a function unrelated to metabolism, showing that the function of an organ can be reassigned in evolution.

## Questions

1. Of a patient with a defect of muscle glycogen phosphorylase (McArdle disease) and a patient with a defect of glucose-6-phosphatase (von Gierke disease), which develops severe hypoglycemia in fasting? Which gets muscle cramps in vigorous exercise? Give reasons.

   **Answer** The patient with glucose-6-phosphatase deficiency develops severe hypoglycemia in fasting: the last step of both hepatic glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis requires this enzyme to convert glucose-6-phosphate to glucose for release into the blood, and without it the liver cannot export glucose; glycogen accumulates massively in the liver (hepatomegaly), and glucose-6-phosphate is diverted to glycolysis and fat synthesis, causing lactic acidosis and hyperlipidemia. The patient with a defect of muscle glycogen phosphorylase has normal blood glucose (hepatic phosphorylase is a different isozyme), but in vigorous exercise the muscle cannot mobilize glycogen, lacks energy, and cramps and aches; blood lactate does not rise after exercise (because there is no glycogen breakdown to lactate), which corresponds exactly to the division of labor in which muscle glycogen is "for its own use only".

2. Using the model of Example 29.4.1, if the half-life of plasma FFA is 2.5 min and after a meal insulin reduces the rate of lipolysis to 1/5 of its former value, to what will plasma FFA fall, and how long will it take? What does this mean for fuel selection after a meal?

   **Answer** At steady state $F = R_{\mathrm{a}}/k$; $R_{\mathrm{a}}$ falls to 1/5, so $F$ also falls to 1/5, that is from $0.15$ to about $0.03\,\mathrm{g\,L^{-1}}$; the change proceeds exponentially with time constant $1/k \approx 3.6\,\mathrm{min}$ and essentially reaches the new steady state within about $15\,\mathrm{min}$ (5 half-lives). Significance: after a meal, blood glucose rises and insulin is secreted, FFA fall rapidly within minutes, the supply of fatty acids to muscle and heart decreases, and they turn to heavy uptake and oxidation of glucose (insulin at the same time increases GLUT4); this is the physiological basis of the glucose–fatty acid cycle (Randle cycle); fuel switching is fast precisely because the FFA pool is small and turns over rapidly.

3. Portal blood flow suddenly falls by half (for example, portal vein thrombosis). Describe the changes that will occur in hepatic blood flow, oxygen supply and metabolism, and the compensations of the body.

   **Answer** Total hepatic blood flow at first falls by about 1/3 (the portal vein accounts for 75%, and halving it is a 37% reduction); adenosine in the portal tract is no longer washed away and accumulates, and the hepatic artery dilates (hepatic arterial buffer response), partially restoring total flow. Because hepatic arterial blood is rich in oxygen, oxygen supply is less affected. Portal pressure rises, and blood of the portal system bypasses the liver into the systemic circulation through collaterals (esophageal, gastric, periumbilical and rectal veins); some of the nutrients, ammonia and drugs absorbed from the gut escape first-pass processing by the liver, which may cause postprandial hyperglycemia, elevated blood ammonia and enhanced drug action; splenomegaly and collateral varices are long-term consequences. The metabolic functions of the hepatocytes themselves (synthesis of albumin and clotting factors) can be maintained as long as the oxygen supply is adequate.

4. A patient has tea-dark urine, clay-colored stools and itching of the skin, with markedly elevated plasma conjugated bilirubin and alkaline phosphatase. Infer the site of the lesion, and explain each symptom in terms of the transport steps of bilirubin and bile acids.

   **Answer** This is cholestatic jaundice caused by biliary obstruction (for example a common bile duct stone or compression by a tumor of the head of the pancreas). Uptake and conjugation by the hepatocyte (OATP, UGT1A1) are normal, but conjugated bilirubin secreted through MRP2 cannot be discharged into the gut, the pressure in the bile ducts rises, and conjugated bilirubin refluxes into the blood through hepatocyte MRP3 and damaged tight junctions, so plasma conjugated bilirubin rises; it is water-soluble and can be filtered by the glomerulus, so the urine is dark. Bilirubin cannot enter the gut, so no urobilinogen or stercobilin is formed, the stools are clay-colored, and urobilinogen also disappears from the urine. Bile acids likewise reflux into the blood and deposit in the skin, causing itching; since bile acids cannot enter the gut, steatorrhea and deficiency of fat-soluble vitamins (especially vitamin K) also result, manifested as prolonged clotting time. Alkaline phosphatase is synthesized in large amounts by the bile duct epithelium in cholestasis and released into the blood.

5. Why does cholestyramine (a resin that binds bile acids in the gut so that they cannot be reabsorbed) lower plasma LDL cholesterol? Using the accounts of Example 29.9.1, explain why it usually does not cause marked steatorrhea whereas ileal resection does.

   **Answer** Cholestyramine increases the loss of bile acids in the feces and reduces the bile acids returning to the liver; the inhibition by FXR and FGF19 is released, CYP7A1 activity increases, and the liver uses more cholesterol to synthesize bile acids; the fall of cholesterol in hepatocytes activates SREBP2, which increases expression of the LDL receptor, so more LDL is taken up from plasma and plasma LDL cholesterol falls (hepatic cholesterol synthesis also increases, partly offsetting the effect, so it is often combined with a statin). Cholestyramine at therapeutic doses binds only a part of the bile acids; recovery falls from about 97.5% to, for example, 85%–90%, a loss of a few grams per day, within the maximal hepatic synthetic capacity (4–6 g/d), the pool shrinks somewhat but remains large enough for micelle formation, and fat digestion is not noticeably affected. Ileal resection reduces recovery below 50%; the amount that must be replaced each day (pool circulating 8 times a day × 3 g × 0.5 ≈ 12 g) far exceeds the maximal synthetic capacity, the pool shrinks, micelle formation is insufficient, and steatorrhea results.

6. (Open question) Birds synthesize fat in the liver, whereas the adipose tissue of mammals (especially rodents) also has a strong capacity for fat synthesis. Discuss possible reasons for this difference in the division of labor among organs from the standpoint of migratory flight and metabolic regulation.

   **Answer** Key points: (1) The scheme of synthesis in the liver with export as VLDL concentrates synthesis in one organ whose output can rise rapidly with hyperphagia, while adipose tissue is responsible only for storage and is lighter per unit mass (it does not need to maintain large amounts of synthetic enzymes), which favors flying animals that need to reduce body weight; (2) the yolk lipids of birds are also synthesized by the liver (vitellogenin and yolk-specific VLDL), so the capacity of the liver for fat synthesis may originally have been related to egg laying and later also served migration; (3) putting synthesis in the liver means that the interconversion of sugar, fat and protein is regulated in one place, which facilitates rapid switching during migration; (4) the costs are the need for very high lipoprotein and FFA flux in plasma and the burden on the liver. The synthetic capacity of mammalian adipose tissue (and in humans the division of labor between liver and adipose tissue that varies with diet) may be related to the predominantly carbohydrate diet of mammals and the need for long-term energy storage. This question has no definitive answer at present and can be tested by comparing data on birds and mammals with different diets and migratory habits.
