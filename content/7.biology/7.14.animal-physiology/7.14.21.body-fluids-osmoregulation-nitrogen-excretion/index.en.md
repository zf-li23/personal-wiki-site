Animal cells must work in a fluid of stable composition, yet the environments in which animals live differ enormously: seawater has an osmolality of about $1000\,\mathrm{mOsm\,kg^{-1}}$, fresh water only a few $\mathrm{mOsm\,kg^{-1}}$, and the land constantly draws water away. This chapter is the first of the urinary system and takes up the general problem that urine formation and excretion must solve: how an animal balances water and ions among three spaces, the external environment, the **internal environment** and the **intracellular fluid** (Section 21.1); how human body fluids are divided into compartments, measured, and exchanged with the outside in terms of water (Section 21.2); how changes in osmolality alter cell volume (Section 21.3); and how the ammonia produced by protein breakdown is excreted as ammonia, urea or uric acid (Section 21.4). Finally we compare the different solutions of animal groups in seawater, fresh water and on land (Section 21.5). The next four chapters deal with the kidney, the main regulating organ in humans: renal structure and glomerular filtration (Chapter 22), tubular reabsorption and secretion (Chapter 23), urinary concentration and water and sodium balance (Chapter 24), and acid–base balance (Chapter 25). The physics of water flow across membranes and of osmotic pressure is in Chapter 3, the cellular mechanisms of the pump–leak model and cell volume are in [7.3.5](/7.3.5), the biochemistry of the urea cycle is in [7.1.22](/7.1.22), and the development of the kidney is in [7.5.25](/7.5.25).

## 21.1. Three Spaces and Two Barriers

### 21.1.1. External Environment, Internal Environment and Intracellular Fluid

Maintaining the balance of water and ions requires coordination among three spaces:

| Space | Role | Typical composition |
|---|---|---|
| External environment | The environment in which the animal lives, the source of water, ions and food and the destination of wastes | Seawater, fresh water, air |
| Internal environment (**extracellular fluid**) | The environment in which cells live, kept in **homeostasis** under neural and humoral regulation | Rich in $\ce{Na+}$ and $\ce{Cl-}$, about $300\,\mathrm{mOsm\,kg^{-1}}$ (mammals) |
| **Intracellular fluid** | The site of biochemical reactions | Rich in $\ce{K+}$, proteins and organic phosphates |

Two barriers lie between the three spaces: the plasma membrane of each cell separates the intracellular fluid from the extracellular fluid, and epithelial tissue separates the extracellular fluid from the external environment. In animal evolution the division of labor between these two barriers has gradually changed. In sponges the tissue is loose, most cells face the seawater directly, and the plasma membrane is the only interface; flatworms have formed tissues but still lack a strict barrier, and the tissue fluid is still hyperosmotic like seawater. More complex animals have closed epithelia that can regulate the extracellular fluid to a composition different from that of the environment, which is the precondition for animals to enter fresh water and land ([Chapter 1](/7.14.1)).

### 21.1.2. Three Coupled Processes

**Definition 21.1.1 (Osmoregulation and Ionic Regulation)** The control by an animal of the osmolality of its body fluids, and thereby of the driving force for the movement of water across membranes, is called **osmoregulation**; the control of the concentrations and proportions of the various ions in the body fluids is called **ionic regulation**.

The two are related but not identical: the hemolymph of marine invertebrates is isosmotic with seawater (osmolality is not regulated), yet they generally regulate the ionic composition, for example keeping $\ce{Mg^2+}$ and $\ce{SO4^2-}$ low and $\ce{K+}$ high. The third process is the excretion of nitrogen: the ammonia produced by the breakdown of proteins and nucleic acids is toxic and must be removed; the form of excretion (ammonia, urea or uric acid) determines how much water is lost with it and how much energy is spent, and so it is coupled with osmoregulation and ionic regulation (Section 21.4). The three processes are often carried out by the same epithelium: the fish gill exchanges gases, transports ions and excretes ammonia; the mammalian kidney regulates water, ions and acid–base balance and excretes urea at the same time.

Different environments pose different problems:

| Environment | Relation of body fluid to environment | Problem to solve |
|---|---|---|
| Seawater | Ion concentration of body fluid lower than (teleosts) or comparable to that of the environment | Excrete ions against the electrochemical gradient and obtain water against the osmotic gradient |
| Fresh water | Ion concentration of body fluid far above that of the environment | Take up ions from ion-poor water and excrete excess water |
| Land | Dehydration by air | Prevent water loss; balance the ions in the diet |

### 21.1.3. The Epithelium: Interface between the Internal and External Environments

The epidermis and the epithelia of the airways, the digestive tract and the renal tubules are all interfaces between the internal and external environments, among which the kidney is devoted to regulating body fluids. Transporting epithelia share four features:

- Apical–basal polarity: the transport proteins of the apical membrane (facing the lumen or the external environment) and of the basolateral membrane (facing interstitial fluid and blood) differ, and the two act in series so that substances cross the cell in a defined direction (**transepithelial transport**, [7.3.5](/7.3.5)).
- Barrier: **tight junctions** between adjacent cells seal the intercellular space; the kinds of claudin determine the permeability of this paracellular pathway to water and ions, which ranges from the "leaky" proximal tubule to the "tight" collecting duct and bladder ([7.3.23](/7.3.23)).
- Multiple functions: the same epithelium consists of several differentiated cell types (for example the principal and intercalated cells of the collecting duct, the ionocytes and pavement cells of the fish gill), each responsible for different transport.
- High energy cost: transporting epithelia are rich in mitochondria, often concentrated near infoldings of the basolateral membrane, where they power the **sodium pump**. The kidneys make up only about $0.5\%$ of body mass but consume about $7\%$–$10\%$ of resting oxygen consumption, most of it for the transport of $\ce{Na+}$ (Chapter 23).

## 21.2. Human Body Fluids

### 21.2.1. Compartments and Their Measurement

About $60\%$ of the body mass of an adult is water ([Chapter 1](/7.14.1)). For a $70\,\mathrm{kg}$ man, body fluid is about $42\,\mathrm{L}$, of which about $28\,\mathrm{L}$ is intracellular fluid and about $14\,\mathrm{L}$ extracellular fluid; of the extracellular fluid, **interstitial fluid** is about $11\,\mathrm{L}$ and **blood plasma** about $3\,\mathrm{L}$, with small amounts of lymph and transcellular fluids (cerebrospinal fluid, peritoneal fluid, synovial fluid, aqueous humor and others).

The volumes of the compartments are measured by one principle:

**Definition 21.2.1 (Indicator-Dilution Method)** A known amount $A$ of an indicator is injected into the body, and after it has distributed uniformly in a compartment its concentration $c$ is measured; the volume of the compartment is then

$$
V = \frac{A - A_{\mathrm{lost}}}{c},
$$
where $A_{\mathrm{lost}}$ is the amount lost during mixing by urine or other routes. This method of measuring volume is called the **indicator-dilution method**.

The key is to choose an indicator that is confined to the target compartment:

| Compartment | Indicator | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Total body water | Heavy water $\ce{D2O}$, tritiated water, antipyrine | Cross all cell membranes |
| Extracellular fluid | Inulin, sucrose, mannitol, ${}^{22}\ce{Na+}$, $\ce{Br-}$ | Do not enter cells; ions enter cells slightly, so the measured value is too large |
| Plasma | Evans blue (bound to albumin), ${}^{125}\mathrm{I}$-labeled albumin | Do not leave the vessels |
| Blood volume | ${}^{51}\mathrm{Cr}$-labeled red cells, or calculated from plasma volume | Blood volume = plasma volume / (1 − **hematocrit**) |
| Intracellular fluid | Cannot be measured directly | = total body water − extracellular fluid |
| Interstitial fluid | Cannot be measured directly | = extracellular fluid − plasma |

**Example 21.2.1** A $60\,\mathrm{kg}$ subject is injected intravenously with $10\,\mathrm{mL}$ of Evans blue at $5\,\mathrm{mg\,mL^{-1}}$, and 10 minutes later the plasma concentration is $0.0185\,\mathrm{mg\,mL^{-1}}$; the hematocrit is measured as $0.42$. The plasma volume is $V = 50\,\mathrm{mg}/0.0185\,\mathrm{mg\,mL^{-1}} = 2.70\,\mathrm{L}$ and the blood volume $= 2.70/(1 - 0.42) = 4.66\,\mathrm{L}$, about $7.8\%$ of body mass. If $\ce{D2O}$ given at the same time shows total body water of $36\,\mathrm{L}$ and inulin shows an extracellular fluid volume of $12\,\mathrm{L}$, then intracellular fluid $= 36 - 12 = 24\,\mathrm{L}$ and interstitial fluid $= 12 - 2.70 = 9.3\,\mathrm{L}$.

### 21.2.2. Composition and Osmotic Balance of the Compartments

The composition of the three main compartments (in $\mathrm{mOsm\,L^{-1}}$ of water, approximate values):

| Component | Plasma | Interstitial fluid | Intracellular fluid |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\ce{Na+}$ | $142$ | $139$ | $14$ |
| $\ce{K+}$ | $4.2$ | $4.0$ | $140$ |
| $\ce{Ca^2+}$, $\ce{Mg^2+}$ | $1.3$, $0.8$ | $1.2$, $0.7$ | $0$, $20$ |
| $\ce{Cl-}$ | $106$ | $108$ | $4$ |
| $\ce{HCO3-}$ | $24$ | $28$ | $10$ |
| Phosphate, sulfate | $2.5$ | $2.5$ | $11 + 0.5$ |
| Small organic molecules (glucose, amino acids, urea and others) | $\approx 11$ | $\approx 11$ | Phosphocreatine, carnosine, amino acids and others $\approx 90$ |
| Protein | $1.2$ | $0.2$ | $4$ |
| Total | $\approx 300$ | $\approx 300$ | $\approx 300$ |

Several regularities:

- Plasma and interstitial fluid are separated by the highly permeable capillary wall, and their small-molecule composition is almost identical; the difference lies in protein, whose concentration is much higher in plasma and produces a **colloid osmotic pressure** of about $25\,\mathrm{mmHg}$ ($3.3\,\mathrm{kPa}$), one of the Starling forces of fluid exchange at the capillary ([Chapter 15](/7.14.15)). Because of the negatively charged plasma proteins, the **Donnan equilibrium** gives plasma slightly more cations and slightly fewer diffusible anions than interstitial fluid.
- Intracellular fluid is rich in $\ce{K+}$, $\ce{Mg^2+}$, phosphate and protein, and extracellular fluid in $\ce{Na+}$, $\ce{Cl-}$ and $\ce{HCO3-}$; this asymmetry is maintained by the **sodium pump**.
- The total **osmolarity** of all compartments is equal, because water crosses cell membranes and capillary walls freely and any osmotic difference is leveled out within minutes by the movement of water. What determines the distribution of water inside and outside cells is the solutes that cannot cross the cell membrane: in the extracellular fluid mainly $\ce{Na+}$ and its accompanying anions, in the intracellular fluid mainly $\ce{K+}$ and organic anions; the $[\ce{Na+}]$ of the extracellular fluid is therefore closely related to the osmolality of the extracellular fluid and thereby to cell volume (Chapter 24).

### 21.2.3. Sources, Losses and Balance of Water

Water in the human body has three sources: drinking (about $60\%$), water in food (about $30\%$) and water produced by metabolism (about $10\%$).

**Definition 21.2.2 (Metabolic Water)** The water formed when nutrients are oxidized is called **metabolic water**.

Oxidation of $1\,\mathrm{g}$ of fat yields about $1.07\,\mathrm{g}$ of water, $1\,\mathrm{g}$ of starch about $0.56\,\mathrm{g}$ and $1\,\mathrm{g}$ of protein about $0.40\,\mathrm{g}$ (oxidized as far as urea). A person produces about $200$–$300\,\mathrm{mL}$ of metabolic water per day, which is of only secondary importance to water balance; but some desert animals that do not drink depend on it almost entirely (Section 21.5.6).

**Definition 21.2.3 (Insensible Water Loss)** The continuous, unnoticed evaporation of water through the skin and the airways is called **insensible water loss**. Insensible water loss does not include sweating.

The approximate daily water intake and output of an adult at rest in a mild environment and during prolonged strenuous exercise in heat are as follows (in $\mathrm{mL\,d^{-1}}$):

| Item | Normal | Prolonged strenuous exercise |
|---|---|---|
| Intake: drinking and food | $2100$ | Must be increased to match output |
| Intake: metabolic water | $200$ | $200$ |
| Output: insensible loss, skin | $350$ | $350$ |
| Output: insensible loss, airways | $350$ | $650$ |
| Output: sweat | $100$ | $5000$ |
| Output: feces | $100$ | $100$ |
| Output: urine | $1400$ | $500$ |
| Total output | $2300$ | $6600$ |

Water is lost from the airways because exhaled gas is saturated with water vapor at body temperature (the saturation vapor pressure at $37\,{}^\circ\mathrm{C}$ is $47\,\mathrm{mmHg}$, that is, $6.3\,\mathrm{kPa}$) whereas inspired air is usually drier; cold, dry air and high ventilation increase respiratory water loss. Insensible evaporation from the skin is limited by the stratum corneum, and when extensive burns destroy it, water loss can increase more than tenfold; in diarrhea, fecal water loss can reach several liters per day; sweating varies the most with exercise and air temperature and can raise water loss severalfold. Of these routes, only urine volume can be actively adjusted to maintain water balance: under normal conditions intake ≈ output, and this is achieved precisely by the kidney changing urine volume and urine osmolality as needed (Chapter 24).

## 21.3. Osmosis and Cell Volume

### 21.3.1. Isosmotic, Hyposmotic and Hyperosmotic

When a cell is placed in a solution containing only solutes that cannot cross the cell membrane, water moves toward the side of higher osmolality until the two sides are equal: in a solution with the same osmolality as the intracellular fluid the cell volume is unchanged; in a hyposmotic solution the cell takes up water and swells, and a red blood cell may burst (hemolysis); in a hyperosmotic solution the cell loses water and shrinks. Whether the solute can cross the cell membrane determines whether an **isosmotic solution** is also an **isotonic solution**: a $300\,\mathrm{mOsm\,L^{-1}}$ urea solution is isosmotic, but urea can enter the cell and the cell still swells ([Chapter 3](/7.14.3)).

Cells contain large amounts of macromolecular anions that cannot cross the cell membrane, and if left uncontrolled the Donnan effect would cause ions and water to keep entering the cell. Animal cells have no cell wall and use the sodium–potassium pump to pump $\ce{Na+}$ out, making $\ce{Na+}$ functionally a solute that cannot enter the cell and so balancing the osmotic action of the intracellular macromolecules; this is the **pump–leak model** of volume control ([7.3.5](/7.3.5)). When the sodium–potassium pump is inhibited (for example when hypoxia depletes ATP), cells swell, which is one of the early signs of ischemic cell injury.

### 21.3.2. How a Change in the Extracellular Fluid Is Distributed between the Two Compartments

In the whole body, water and solute added to the extracellular fluid are redistributed between the inside and outside of cells. The principles of the calculation are: (1) solutes that cannot enter cells ($\ce{Na+}$, $\ce{Cl-}$) remain in the extracellular fluid; (2) water moves freely, and after equilibration the osmolality of the two compartments is equal; (3) the total amount of solute in each compartment is conserved.

**Example 21.3.1 (Infusion of Hypertonic Saline)** A $70\,\mathrm{kg}$ man has $42\,\mathrm{L}$ of body fluid ($28\,\mathrm{L}$ intracellular, $14\,\mathrm{L}$ extracellular) at an osmolality of $280\,\mathrm{mOsm\,L^{-1}}$ (osmotic activity is used in this example). After intravenous infusion of $2\,\mathrm{L}$ of $3\%$ NaCl solution, how much fluid is in each compartment?

$3\%$ NaCl is $30\,\mathrm{g\,L^{-1}}$, and $30/58.5 = 0.513\,\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}$, an osmolality of about $1026\,\mathrm{mOsm\,L^{-1}}$; $2\,\mathrm{L}$ brings in $2052\,\mathrm{mOsm}$, all of which stays in the extracellular fluid. The original solute: intracellular $28 \times 280 = 7840\,\mathrm{mOsm}$, extracellular $14 \times 280 = 3920\,\mathrm{mOsm}$. After equilibration the total body water is $44\,\mathrm{L}$ and the osmolality is $(7840 + 3920 + 2052)/44 = 314\,\mathrm{mOsm\,L^{-1}}$. Intracellular fluid is $7840/314 = 25.0\,\mathrm{L}$ and extracellular fluid $44 - 25.0 = 19.0\,\mathrm{L}$. Although $2\,\mathrm{L}$ of fluid was infused, the extracellular fluid has increased by $5\,\mathrm{L}$: $3\,\mathrm{L}$ of water has been drawn out of the cells.

The six basic cases follow in the same way (compared with the original state after equilibration):

| Change | Example | Extracellular volume | Intracellular volume | Osmolality |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Addition of isosmotic fluid | Infusion of physiological saline | ↑ | Unchanged | Unchanged |
| Addition of hyposmotic fluid (water) | Drinking large amounts of water, excess ADH secretion | ↑ | ↑ | ↓ |
| Addition of hyperosmotic fluid | Infusion of hypertonic saline, drinking seawater | ↑ | ↓ | ↑ |
| Loss of isosmotic fluid | Hemorrhage, diarrhea (early) | ↓ | Unchanged | Unchanged |
| Loss of hyposmotic fluid | Sweating, diabetes insipidus | ↓ | ↓ | ↑ |
| Loss of hyperosmotic fluid (more salt than water) | Adrenocortical insufficiency | ↓ | ↑ | ↓ |

The meaning of this table is that the volume of the **extracellular fluid** is determined by its total $\ce{Na+}$ and its osmolality by water. In the clinic, "dehydration" and "edema" must first be distinguished as a problem of volume or of osmolality, and the fluids used in treatment differ accordingly. The body copes with them by two different regulations: osmolality, through osmoreceptors, ADH and thirst, regulates water balance, and volume, through the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system and others, regulates $\ce{Na+}$ balance (Chapter 24).

### 21.3.3. Cell Volume Regulation

In an anisosmotic environment a cell first swells or shrinks passively like an osmometer and then, within minutes, actively recovers part of its original volume:

- **Regulatory volume decrease**: a swollen cell opens $\ce{K+}$ channels and $\ce{Cl-}$ channels (such as the volume-regulated anion channel VRAC, made of LRRC8 proteins), activates $\ce{K+}$–$\ce{Cl-}$ cotransport and extrudes organic osmolytes such as taurine, and water leaves the cell with them.
- **Regulatory volume increase**: a shrunken cell activates $\ce{Na+}$–$\ce{K+}$–$2\ce{Cl-}$ cotransport and $\ce{Na+}/\ce{H+}$ exchange (together with $\ce{Cl-}/\ce{HCO3-}$ exchange), taking up NaCl, and water enters with it.

Both regulations must satisfy osmotic balance and electroneutrality at the same time, so cations and anions always move in pairs, accomplished by different transport proteins. Cells that live long in a hyperosmotic environment also synthesize or take up organic osmolytes that do not affect protein function: cells of the renal medulla (interstitial osmolality up to $1200\,\mathrm{mOsm\,kg^{-1}}$) accumulate sorbitol, inositol, betaine and glycerophosphorylcholine, and their synthesis and transporters are induced by the transcription factor TonEBP/NFAT5. Brain cells lose organic osmolytes in chronic hyponatremia to limit cerebral edema, which is why chronic hyponatremia must be corrected slowly: raising plasma osmolality too quickly shrinks brain cells that have already adapted and causes osmotic demyelination.

## 21.4. Nitrogenous Wastes

### 21.4.1. Ammonia, Urea and Uric Acid

Deamination of amino acids produces ammonia ($\ce{NH3}$, which at the pH of body fluids exists mainly as $\ce{NH4+}$, $\mathrm{p}K_{\mathrm{a}} \approx 9.2$). Ammonia is highly toxic: plasma ammonia in mammals is usually below $0.05\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$, and a rise to about $1\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$ causes coma, mainly because it disturbs glutamate–glutamine metabolism and the balance of neurotransmitters in the brain. Animals excrete ammonia in three ways.

**Definition 21.4.1 (Ammonotelic, Ureotelic and Uricotelic Animals)** Animals whose main nitrogenous excretory product is ammonia are called **ammonotelic**, those in which it is mainly urea are called **ureotelic**, and those in which it is mainly uric acid (and its salts) are called **uricotelic**.

![Comparison of the three nitrogenous wastes (orders of magnitude). A: water needed to excrete 1 g of nitrogen: ammonia about 300–500 mL, urea about 50 mL, uric acid about 10 mL or less (excreted as a paste or solid). B: synthesis cost per nitrogen atom: zero for ammonia; about 2 ATP equivalents for urea (the urea cycle spends 4 high-energy phosphate bonds per molecule); about 2 for uric acid (an estimate from de novo purine synthesis and glutamine synthesis), and each nitrogen atom carries away 1.25 carbon atoms. C: concentrations that can be tolerated or reached in body fluids: ammonia about 0.05 mM (toxic above about 1 mM); urea reaches 350–400 mM in the plasma of elasmobranchs; uric acid saturates at about 0.4 mM](./plots/nitrogen-waste.py)

The choice among the three is a trade-off between water and energy:

| | Ammonia | Urea | Uric acid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Toxicity | High | Low | Low |
| Solubility | High | High (up to several hundred mM) | Very low, crystallizes easily |
| Water needed for excretion | Much; must be diluted heavily | Moderate; can be concentrated | Very little; can be excreted as a semisolid |
| Energy of synthesis | None | Required (**urea cycle**, mainly in the liver) | Required (purine synthesis pathway), and carries away more carbon |
| Route of excretion | Diffusion and transport across gills or body surface (Rh glycoproteins) | Kidney, concentrated in urine | Tubular secretion; cloaca and gut reabsorb water |
| Typical groups | Most aquatic invertebrates, teleosts, tadpoles | Mammals, adult amphibians, elasmobranchs, sea turtles | Birds, squamates and tortoises, insects, land snails |

Animals living in water can let ammonia diffuse directly into a large volume of water at no energy cost; land animals do not have this option and pay energy to convert ammonia into urea, which is of low toxicity and can be concentrated, or into **uric acid**, which is almost insoluble and carries away almost no water. Uric acid has one further special advantage: in the amniotic egg (the shelled egg of birds and reptiles) the embryo cannot excrete wastes out of the egg, and uric acid is deposited in the allantois as crystals, neither poisoning the embryo nor changing the osmolality of the fluid in the egg; mammalian embryos instead hand urea to the mother across the placenta. In fact most animals excrete several nitrogenous substances at the same time, only in different proportions; about $85\%$ of the nitrogen in human urine is urea, the rest being $\ce{NH4+}$, creatinine and uric acid (human uric acid comes from the breakdown of purines and is not the main excretory form of amino acid nitrogen).

### 21.4.2. Switches of Strategy

The form of excretion is not a fixed characteristic of a species but adjusts with development and environment:

- Development: tadpoles live in water and excrete mainly ammonia; at metamorphosis, under the action of thyroid hormone, the enzymes of the urea cycle in the liver (especially carbamoyl phosphate synthetase I) are strongly induced and the frog switches to excreting urea (the endocrine control of **metamorphosis** is in [7.5.33](/7.5.33)). The wholly aquatic *Xenopus* still excretes mainly ammonia.
- Drought: African lungfish normally excrete ammonia in water; in the dry season they burrow into mud and aestivate in a cocoon, stop excreting ammonia and accumulate nitrogen in the body as urea, with plasma urea rising to several hundred $\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$, and excrete it when they return to water in the rainy season.
- Other environmental constraints: the gulf toadfish switches to excreting urea when crowded or exposed to air; the tilapia *Alcolapia grahami*, which lives in Lake Magadi in East Africa at a pH of about 10, cannot excrete ammonia into strongly alkaline water (at high pH ammonia exists mainly as $\ce{NH3}$ and is not easily "trapped" in the water) and excretes urea throughout life.

## 21.5. Evolution and Comparison

### 21.5.1. Osmoconformers and Osmoregulators

**Definition 21.5.1 (Osmoconformers and Osmoregulators)** Animals whose body fluid osmolality varies with the environment and is roughly equal to it are called **osmoconformers**; animals that maintain the osmolality of their body fluids at a level different from that of the environment are called **osmoregulators**.

**Definition 21.5.2 (Euryhaline and Stenohaline)** Animals that tolerate a wide range of external salinity are called **euryhaline** animals, and those that can live only in a narrow range of salinity are called **stenohaline** animals.

Conforming versus regulating and euryhaline versus stenohaline are two independent dimensions: marine echinoderms are stenohaline conformers; the mussel can live in estuaries and is a euryhaline conformer (its cells withstand changes in body fluid osmolality by regulating organic osmolytes); the shore crab (*Carcinus*) keeps its hemolymph above the external level in dilute seawater and conforms in seawater, a euryhaline hyperosmotic regulator; salmon and eels migrate between seawater and fresh water and are euryhaline hyper–hypo-osmotic regulators; most freshwater and marine fish are stenohaline regulators. Most animals regulate water and ions to some degree, only the proportion borne by the two barriers (cell membrane and epithelium) differs.

The difference between conforming and regulating can be understood with a very simple model. Let the osmolality of the body fluid be $C_{\mathrm{i}}$ and that of the environment $C_{\mathrm{o}}$; passive exchange of water and solute across the body surface drives $C_{\mathrm{i}}$ toward $C_{\mathrm{o}}$, at a rate proportional to the permeability $P$ of the body surface; the flux of active transport $J_{\mathrm{a}}$ (positive for uptake of ions, negative for excretion) opposes the deviation of $C_{\mathrm{i}}$ from the set point $C_{\mathrm{set}}$ but has a maximum rate $J_{\max}$. At steady state

$$
P\,(C_{\mathrm{o}} - C_{\mathrm{i}}) + J_{\mathrm{a}}(C_{\mathrm{i}}) = 0, \qquad J_{\mathrm{a}} = J_{\max}\tanh\!\left(\frac{G\,(C_{\mathrm{set}} - C_{\mathrm{i}})}{J_{\max}}\right),
$$

where $G$ is the gain near the set point, corresponding to the **feedback gain** of **negative feedback**. For a conformer $J_{\mathrm{a}} = 0$ and $C_{\mathrm{i}} = C_{\mathrm{o}}$; the $C_{\mathrm{i}}$ of a regulator is pulled toward $C_{\mathrm{set}}$, but regulation fails when the environment deviates so far that the required flux exceeds $J_{\max}$. Transporting a solute against the concentration ratio $C_{\mathrm{o}}/C_{\mathrm{i}}$ requires work of at least $RT\,\lvert\ln(C_{\mathrm{o}}/C_{\mathrm{i}})\rvert$ per mole (the **electrochemical potential** of Chapter 3), so the minimum power of regulation is $\lvert J_{\mathrm{a}}\rvert\,RT\,\lvert\ln(C_{\mathrm{o}}/C_{\mathrm{i}})\rvert$: it is zero at the isosmotic point, increases rapidly with the gradient, and is proportional to the passive flux, so lowering the permeability of the body surface is the cheapest way of reducing the cost of regulation.

![Flux-balance model of osmoconformers and osmoregulators (parameters are illustrative). A: steady-state body fluid osmolality. The conformer (dotted line) has body fluid equal to the environment; the hyperosmotic regulator (modeled on the shore crab, able only to take up ions, set point 750 mOsm/kg) stays above the environment in dilute seawater, conforms in environments above the set point, and has insufficient capacity to regulate in fresh water; the hyper–hypo-osmotic regulator (modeled on a teleost, body-surface permeability one quarter of the former, able both to take up and to excrete ions, set point 320 mOsm/kg) holds the body fluid at about 320 mOsm/kg over the whole range from fresh water to seawater. B: minimum pump power (arbitrary units) in the teleost model: zero at the isosmotic point, rising rapidly toward both fresh water and seawater; with the body-surface permeability halved the cost is also halved](./plots/osmoregulation-model.py)

Measured data are consistent with this picture: in euryhaline fish the fraction of oxygen consumption devoted to osmoregulation is lowest near isosmotic salinity and increases away from it; the body-surface permeability (scales and mucus) of both freshwater and marine fish is far lower than that of marine invertebrates.

### 21.5.2. Marine Invertebrates and Hagfish

Most marine invertebrates are osmoconformers, with body fluids isosmotic with seawater, but most regulate ions: for example, they keep $\ce{K+}$ high and $\ce{SO4^2-}$ low; swimming squids and crustaceans lower the heavy $\ce{SO4^2-}$ in the body fluid and retain the light $\ce{NH4+}$ or $\ce{Cl-}$ to reduce density and gain buoyancy. Among vertebrates only the hagfish has body fluids isosmotic with seawater and an ionic composition close to it; the body fluid osmolality of all other vertebrates is about one third that of seawater or lower (the special case of elasmobranchs is in Section 21.5.4).

### 21.5.3. Teleosts: Seawater and Fresh Water

The plasma of teleosts is about $300$–$400\,\mathrm{mOsm\,kg^{-1}}$, lower than the environment in seawater and far above it in fresh water; the two opposite problems are solved by two opposite sets of mechanisms:

| | Marine teleost | Freshwater teleost |
|---|---|---|
| Passive water flow | Loses water osmotically across the gill | Gains water osmotically across the gill |
| Passive ion flow | Gains NaCl across the gill | Loses NaCl across the gill |
| Drinking | Drinks seawater in large amounts; the gut absorbs NaCl and water ($70\%$–$85\%$ of the water); the divalent ions $\ce{Mg^2+}$ and $\ce{SO4^2-}$ mostly remain in the gut or are excreted by the kidney | Hardly drinks |
| Gill ionocytes | Excrete NaCl: the sodium–potassium pump and $\ce{Na+}$–$\ce{K+}$–$2\ce{Cl-}$ cotransporter of the basolateral membrane cause $\ce{Cl-}$ to accumulate in the cell and it leaves through the CFTR channel of the apical membrane; $\ce{Na+}$ leaves down the potential gradient through leaky junctions between adjacent cells | Take up $\ce{Na+}$ and $\ce{Cl-}$: $\ce{Na+}/\ce{H+}$ exchange (or $\ce{Na+}$ channels driven by the $\ce{H+}$-ATPase) takes up $\ce{Na+}$, and $\ce{Cl-}/\ce{HCO3-}$ exchange or $\ce{Na+}$–$\ce{Cl-}$ cotransport takes up $\ce{Cl-}$ |

The cells of the gill that transport ions were formerly called "chloride cells" because they secrete $\ce{Cl-}$ in marine fish; they are now called ionocytes, rich in mitochondria and sodium–potassium pumps. Before migration (the "smoltification" preceding descent to the sea), salmon already increase secretory ionocytes in the gill, a switch promoted by cortisol and growth hormone; on entering fresh water, prolactin suppresses secretory cells, promotes uptake cells and lowers the permeability of the gill. This is an example of **humoral regulation** switching one epithelium between two opposite functions. The nitrogenous waste excreted by the gill is mainly $\ce{NH3}$, which diffuses out through Rh glycoprotein channels and is protonated to $\ce{NH4+}$ by $\ce{H+}$ at the gill surface and so "trapped" in the water (the same principle as excretion of $\ce{NH4+}$ by the collecting duct of the kidney in Chapter 25).

### 21.5.4. Elasmobranchs: Urea and the Rectal Gland

Marine elasmobranchs such as sharks and rays adopt another solution: plasma NaCl is only about half that of seawater, but about $350$–$400\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$ of urea and about $150\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$ of trimethylamine oxide (TMAO) are retained, making the total osmolality slightly higher than that of seawater (about $1000$–$1100\,\mathrm{mOsm\,kg^{-1}}$). Water therefore enters slowly across the gill, the shark need not drink seawater, and the excess water is excreted by the kidney; the renal tubule actively reabsorbs urea, and the gill epithelium has an extremely low permeability to urea so that this costly osmolyte is not lost.

A high concentration of urea destabilizes proteins and inhibits many enzymes, whereas TMAO stabilizes protein folding; at a ratio of about $2:1$ their effects on proteins roughly cancel, and this combination of "counteracting osmolytes" also appears in deep-sea fish (TMAO in deep-sea fish increases with depth, resisting the perturbation of proteins by hydrostatic pressure). The NaCl that enters through the gill and the food is excreted by the rectal gland at the end of the intestine: the rectal gland secretes a NaCl solution of about $500\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$, and the mechanism of its secretory cells (sodium–potassium pump, $\ce{Na+}$–$\ce{K+}$–$2\ce{Cl-}$ cotransporter and CFTR) is the same as that of the ionocytes of the marine teleost gill and of the secretory epithelia of the human airway and intestine, which makes the shark rectal gland classic material for studying the regulation of CFTR. The coelacanth also osmoregulates with urea, a solution that has arisen independently in two groups.

### 21.5.5. Salt Glands

The kidneys of seabirds, sea turtles, sea snakes and marine iguanas cannot produce urine much more concentrated than plasma (in birds at most about twice plasma, in reptiles no more than isosmotic), yet they can drink seawater or eat salty food because they have salt-excreting organs outside the kidney: salt glands. The salt gland of seabirds lies above the orbit and opens into the nasal cavity; that of sea turtles lies at the orbit ("weeping"), that of sea snakes under the tongue and that of marine iguanas at the nose. Salt glands secrete only under a salt load, and the NaCl concentration of the secretion can reach $600$–$1000\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$, higher than that of seawater; the mechanism of secretion is still sodium–potassium pump-driven $\ce{Cl-}$ secretion; secretion is controlled by parasympathetic nerves and triggered by a rise in plasma osmolality detected by the hypothalamus. When a gull drinks $1\,\mathrm{L}$ of seawater, the water needed to excrete the salt through the salt gland is clearly less than $1\,\mathrm{L}$, so there is a net gain of water. Mammals have no salt glands, and marine mammals obtain water mainly from food and metabolic water and rarely drink seawater (Chapter 24).

### 21.5.6. Water Conservation in Terrestrial Animals

The main threat to terrestrial animals is evaporative water loss, and each group has mechanisms for saving water along every route of loss:

- Body surface: insects have a wax layer outside the cuticle and lose very little water; reptiles have keratinized scales; amphibian skin is highly permeable and most can live only in moist environments, but the tree frog *Phyllomedusa* secretes wax and the skin of *Chiromantis* is almost impermeable to water, so they can live in arid regions.
- Respiration: countercurrent heat exchange in the nasal cavity cools exhaled gas before it leaves, and water vapor condenses on the nasal mucosa and is recovered; kangaroo rats recover most of their respiratory water loss in this way (countercurrent heat exchange of body temperature is in Chapter 31).
- Excretion: excretion of uric acid or highly concentrated urine (Chapter 24), and dry feces. The amphibian bladder stores water and reabsorbs it during dehydration under the action of ADH-like hormones (arginine vasotocin); many terrestrial amphibians can also absorb water from moist ground through a "seat patch" on the belly skin.
- Source: kangaroo rats eat dry seeds and do not drink, relying almost entirely on metabolic water and the small amount of water in seeds; their water balance depends on three savings: urine concentrated to about $5000\,\mathrm{mOsm\,kg^{-1}}$, dry feces and recovery of water in the nasal cavity.

These solutions on land use the same set of tools as those in the sea: epithelial transport proteins, junctions that regulate permeability, organs that concentrate or dilute body fluids, and the hormones that regulate them. The next four chapters describe the solution in humans that concentrates these tools in one organ: the kidney.

## Questions

1. A subject weighs $50\,\mathrm{kg}$. Three hours after oral $50\,\mathrm{g}$ of $\ce{D2O}$, the plasma concentration of $\ce{D2O}$ is $1.6\,\mathrm{mg\,mL^{-1}}$, and about $1\,\mathrm{g}$ was lost in urine and breath in the meantime. What is his total body water? Is its fraction of body mass normal?

   **Answer** $V = (50 - 1)\,\mathrm{g}/1.6\,\mathrm{g\,L^{-1}} = 30.6\,\mathrm{L}$, about $61\%$ of body mass, within the normal range for an adult man (lower in women and in those with more fat).
2. A $70\,\mathrm{kg}$ man (body fluid $42\,\mathrm{L}$, intracellular fluid $28\,\mathrm{L}$, osmolality $280\,\mathrm{mOsm\,L^{-1}}$) drinks $2\,\mathrm{L}$ of pure water at once. Before the kidney excretes any of it, what are the volumes and osmolality of the two compartments?

   **Answer** The total amount of solute $42 \times 280 = 11760\,\mathrm{mOsm}$ is unchanged, the total body water is $44\,\mathrm{L}$, and the osmolality is $11760/44 = 267\,\mathrm{mOsm\,L^{-1}}$. Intracellular fluid is $7840/267 = 29.3\,\mathrm{L}$ (an increase of $1.3\,\mathrm{L}$) and extracellular fluid $3920/267 = 14.7\,\mathrm{L}$ (an increase of $0.7\,\mathrm{L}$). Water is distributed according to the sizes of the two compartments (about $2:1$), and two thirds enters the cells. A fall in osmolality of less than $5\%$ suffices to nearly stop ADH secretion and cause water diuresis (Chapter 24).
3. Why is intravenous infusion of $5\%$ glucose solution (isosmotic) equivalent in the body to infusion of pure water?

   **Answer** $5\%$ glucose (about $278\,\mathrm{mmol\,L^{-1}}$) is isosmotic with plasma on infusion and does not immediately hemolyze red cells; but glucose is quickly taken up and metabolized by cells, leaving only water, so the effect in the body is the same as infusing a hyposmotic fluid: water is distributed to the two compartments and the osmolality of the extracellular fluid falls. It is isosmotic but not isotonic.
4. Using the flux-balance model (Section 21.5.1), explain why, when a euryhaline fish moves from seawater into fresh water, lowering the gill permeability is more "economical" than raising the capacity for ion uptake.

   **Answer** The active uptake flux needed to maintain $C_{\mathrm{i}}$ in fresh water equals the passive loss $P\,(C_{\mathrm{i}} - C_{\mathrm{o}})$, and the minimum power is $P\,(C_{\mathrm{i}} - C_{\mathrm{o}})\,RT\ln(C_{\mathrm{i}}/C_{\mathrm{o}})$, proportional to $P$. Lowering $P$ (fewer leaky junctions, more mucus) reduces the required flux and energy in proportion, whereas raising $J_{\max}$ merely permits a higher cost to be paid. Salmon lower gill permeability under the action of prolactin on entering fresh water, exactly this principle.
5. Birds excrete uric acid and mammals urea; can you give an explanation from the standpoint of reproductive mode? What are the limits of this explanation?

   **Answer** The amniotic egg is closed, and the nitrogenous waste of the embryo can only remain in the egg; urea is soluble, and its accumulation would raise the osmolality of the fluid in the egg and is somewhat toxic, whereas uric acid is deposited as crystals in the allantois, harmless and without osmotic effect. Mammalian embryos hand urea to the mother across the placenta for excretion and are not subject to this constraint. Limits: this explains only the selection pressure in the embryonic period, whereas the excretory form of adults is also influenced by the availability of water (for example tortoises and aquatic turtles differ in excretory form, and squamates also excrete uric acid in arid environments); moreover, uric acid synthesis is tied to purine metabolism, and the uricotelic groups are concentrated phylogenetically in the sauropsids, so an element of ancestral trait is also involved.
6. The plasma osmolality of elasmobranchs is slightly higher than that of seawater. What does this mean for their water balance and renal function? What would happen if a shark were put into fresh water?

   **Answer** With body fluids slightly hyperosmotic to seawater, water enters slowly across the gill and the shark need not drink seawater; the task of the kidney is to excrete the excess water and divalent ions while reabsorbing urea. In fresh water, the huge osmotic gradient causes water to pour in and urea and salt to be lost across the gill and in urine; the great majority of sharks are stenohaline and cannot survive. A few species (such as the bull shark) can enter rivers; in fresh water they lower plasma urea and NaCl and increase excretion of dilute urine, and rectal gland activity also weakens, which shows that the "set point" of urea osmoregulation can be adjusted.
